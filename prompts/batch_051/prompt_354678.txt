I have a standing rule that governs how I argue and how I plan, and I want to know whether it is defensible or merely my own impatience dressed up as method. The rule is that a declarative sentence gets to do work in my reasoning as a premise only if it is determinately true or determinately false. If I judge that a sentence sits in a truth-value gap, I quarantine it and require a sharpened, bivalent replacement before I will reason from it at all. This rule is not aimed at probabilistic claims: I am comfortable reasoning from a sentence that says something will probably happen, because that sentence itself is either true or false now, whatever the future does. The rule targets three kinds of sentence. First, vague ones, such as a claim that a certain policy helps the poor: until someone supplies a threshold for counting as poor, I treat the sentence as having no determinate content. Second, future-contingent ones, such as a claim that our side will win the next election: I treat the sentence as neither true nor false until the election settles it. Third, sentences that presuppose something nonexistent, such as the one attributing baldness to a current king of France; I refuse to reason with such a sentence in either direction.

I lean on this rule hardest in political argument and in my own planning. Recently a colleague argued that our organization should adopt a policy because it would make the organization more trustworthy, and I replied that until he supplied a crisp criterion for trustworthiness, his claim was neither true nor false and could not support the proposal. The rule keeps disagreements from dissolving into fights about what a word means. But I worry it also smuggles a contested semantic doctrine — bivalence, and maybe something stronger, determinacy — into my everyday thinking while presenting it as mere epistemic hygiene. I suspect I sometimes confuse a sentence I cannot currently settle with a sentence that is not settled.

Is this policy defensible as a policy? Where does it serve me, where does it fail, and could it be restated to keep the benefits without discarding vague-but-informative claims or treating an open future as a gap? How would I tell, from the inside, whether I am enforcing clarity or refusing to reason under vagueness and uncertainty? Please separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely disputed — for example, whether future-tensed assertions already have truth values and whether borderline vague sentences are gappy or merely unknown — and do not present one side of a live dispute as the consensus.
