I work as a software engineer, and I've noticed that I rely on the same habit at work and in daily life, and I'd like to know whether it is defensible. The habit is letting a source vouch for itself. When I take over a component with a sensor, I don't begin by checking the sensor against an external standard. I take its readings at face value, and once it has built up a track record of readings that all look plausible, I count that track record as evidence that the sensor is reliable — even though every entry in the track record came from the sensor itself. I do the same with a codebase whose tests were written by the same people who wrote the code, and, more uncomfortably, with my own reasoning: I catch defects in my reasoning by using the same reasoning that produced them, and then treat my ability to find and fix my own mistakes as evidence that my judgment is sound. Is this a reasonable shortcut, or is it circular reasoning in disguise? Specifically: is a self-reported track record ever real evidence, or does reliability have to be established by at least one independent check before self-consistency can count for anything? And is the case of my own reasoning structurally different from the case of a sensor, or the same problem one level down? Please mark which parts of your answer reflect settled ground and which are genuinely contested, and avoid presenting a contested view as the consensus.
