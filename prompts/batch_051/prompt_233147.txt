Please argue for the position that a machine whose ethical reasoning runs entirely on general rules or principles, such as a deontic constraint solver or a utility maximizer, cannot possess practical wisdom in Aristotle’s sense, even if its decisions match those of a virtuous human. In Book VI, chapter 7 of the Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle distinguishes practical wisdom from other intellectual excellences by insisting that it is concerned with particulars as well as universals. He illustrates the point by saying that someone who knows the universal truth that light meats are digestible and wholesome but cannot recognize which meats are light will not produce health, whereas the person who knows that chicken is light and wholesome is in a better position to do so. Treat that passage as the center of your argument. You should explain whether the distinctive gap Aristotle identifies is best understood as missing data, missing experience, missing perceptual or situational recognition, or something more fundamental about the practical relation between rule and case. Then defend the strongest form of the claim that a purely principle-driven machine ethics remains unwise in this respect. Consider at least one serious objection, for example that machine learning can acquire the relevant recognitional capacities without being experienced in Aristotle’s sense, or that fine-grained explicit rules can capture the relevant particulars, and show whether your argument can absorb that objection or must be qualified in response to it.
