I got into inverse reinforcement learning from blog posts and videos — the idea that instead of programming a reward function into an agent, you watch what it does and work backwards to figure out what reward would make those actions look optimal. The part that stuck with me is that the problem is underdetermined. If you only assume the agent is optimal with respect to some reward, then a whole family of reward functions fit the same behavior: the observed actions just have to be at least as good as the alternatives, and that's a set of inequalities, not a unique answer. The degenerate case — reward zero everywhere — makes every action equally optimal, so if that's allowed you haven't learned anything. Real IRL fixes this by adding extra assumptions: the reward is sparse, or simple, or built from a few features, or the agent is maximum-entropy subject to matching observed feature counts. Something has to break the tie.

Then I realized I do the same thing when I try to figure out what people want from what they do. My sister keeps taking extra shifts, and I can tell five stories that all fit the same behavior: she wants the money, she's avoiding home, she can't say no when asked, she likes being seen as reliable, or she's on autopilot and never actually decided. The actions alone dont distinguish them. And I only settle on one by importing the everyday equivalents of IRL priors: people mostly want approval, avoid pain, are roughly sincere, don't like effort for its own sake. The "zero reward everywhere" case even has a matching move — the explanation that she doesn't actually care either way and it's all constraint and inertia, which you can apply to anyone and which explains everything and nothing.

So here's the parallel I can't shake: reading preferences off behavior is the same inverse problem in both cases — given actions and the situation, infer the function that assigns value to outcomes — and in both cases the data leave the answer badly underdetermined, so you fix it with a prior. If that's right, reward ambiguity in IRL and the everyday fact that you can never directly observe why someone did something are the same problem with different labels.

Is that real, or am I just pattern matching on the word "infer"? I can see one place it might break: IRL assumes the environment and the agent's model of it are fixed and known, and that the agent is actually optimal with respect to some reward. People are inconsistent, act against what they'd say they value, don't know their own options, and also talk to you. So maybe reading people is a strictly harder and messier inverse problem, not the same one.
