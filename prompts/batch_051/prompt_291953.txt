The speaker at next month's PhD seminar on strategic rationality defends a superrationality-style answer to the one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and I'm chairing the Q&A. His line is that in a symmetric dilemma, two equally rational players should expect to reach the same conclusion, so each is really choosing between both cooperating and both defecting rather than between cooperating and defecting while holding the other player's move fixed; he tends to shrug off the standard dominance objection as parochialism. I need about ten questions that push past the usual restatement. Please build the sequence so it forces him to get precise on at least these: what actually justifies treating the other player as a near-copy of himself in his reasoning; whether on his picture the two choices are causally independent, evidence for each other, or connected in some acausal way; what he wants to say about asymmetric payoffs or about symmetric games with more than one decent equilibrium; how his position is meant to differ from Kantian universalisation and from team reasoning; and how a superrational player should behave in a population of mostly defectors, or with a partner who is equally superrational but simply mistaken about the payoffs. For each line of questioning, add the follow-up you would ask if he gives the response that is most natural for his view.
