I am a novelist at work on a book about a contemporary figure who decides to run an updated version of Benjamin Franklin's programme of moral perfection, using habit-tracking software and behavioural nudges rather than paper and ink. To make the novel psychologically credible, I need to understand Franklin's actual project as something more nuanced than the founding myth of the self-improvement industry, and I would welcome a careful analysis. As I understand it, Franklin describes in his Autobiography drawing up a list of thirteen virtues, taking them up one at a time rather than all at once, and keeping a little book in which he recorded his daily failures against each; his own assessment, as I gather, was that he never fully attained some of them, yet regarded the whole attempt as having improved both his character and his contentment.

Here is what I would like you to do. Treat Franklin's project as a historical case study and interpret it through the lens of modern habit-formation and self-regulation research: cue–routine–reward accounts of habit, implementation intentions, and the empirical work on self-monitoring. Be disciplined about the mapping. In that vocabulary, what exactly is the daily chart — a monitoring device, a feedback loop, a commitment device, or all of these at once? What would the theory identify as the cues, the routines, and the rewards, and does the one-virtue-at-a-time design make theoretical sense? Then say honestly which features of Franklin's project this framework explains well and which it strains to explain or positively misdescribes; the fact that he treated virtue as something one could fail at, and yet still found the endeavour worthwhile, seems to me to sit uneasily with a purely habit-based picture. After that, reverse direction and ask what the episode reveals about the limits of habit-formation theory, about the difference between a virtue and a habit, and about the assumptions encoded in today's self-tracking technology. Conclude by stating plainly where the framework gives out and where a different vocabulary — virtue ethics, for instance — is needed to make sense of him. I would be grateful if you would write the response in British English.
