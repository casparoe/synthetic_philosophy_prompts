I have recently had to choose between two treatment regimens for a condition with which I was diagnosed earlier this year. The first carries a near-certain moderate improvement. The second carries a small chance of a full recovery and a substantially larger chance of leaving me somewhat worse off than the first would. A relative in finance has been pressing me to treat the decision as a simple computation: assign each possible final state a utility, weight each by its probability, and take the option with the larger expected utility. When I replied that the shape of the risk itself mattered to me, and not only its probability-weighted average, he presented the following argument, which he says shows that my reluctance is not a legitimate difference of taste but a failure of coherence. I suspect the argument moves too quickly, but I cannot identify exactly where. Please go through it step by step and tell me whether it is valid.

(1) A rational person's preferences over possible outcomes are complete: for any two outcomes, she prefers one to the other or is indifferent between them.

(2) A rational person's preferences are transitive: if she prefers A to B and B to C, then she prefers A to C.

(3) From (1) and (2), each possible outcome can be assigned a real number — its utility — that represents the person's ranking of outcomes, with higher numbers assigned to preferred outcomes and equal numbers to outcomes ranked equally.

(4) An option with an uncertain outcome is nothing more than a device for delivering one of its possible outcomes with a certain probability. Its value must therefore be determined entirely by the utilities of its possible outcomes and their probabilities. The only non-arbitrary way to combine them is to multiply each outcome's utility by its probability and sum — that is, to use expected utility. Any rival rule would either ignore the utility of some possible outcome or allow an outcome's contribution to depend on which other outcomes are possible, which would be to treat its importance as arbitrary.

(5) Adding (4) to (1)-(3), a rational person's preferences over uncertain options must rank those options by expected utility. My shrinking from the higher-expected-utility option was therefore an incoherence of the same general kind as holding inconsistent beliefs, not a permissible expression of taste.

For each step, say whether it is true on its most natural reading and whether the inference to that step is warranted. If the argument is invalid, identify the first step at which it fails, give the strongest reading of that step under which the inference would go through, and say whether the argument would still establish its conclusion on that reading. If the argument is valid, say which premises could reasonably be denied.
