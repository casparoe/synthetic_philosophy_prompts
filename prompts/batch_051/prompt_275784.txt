I'm taking a seminar this term on reasons and normativity, and we have spent the past several sessions on what the literature calls the wrong kind of reasons problem. I find that I have fairly stable intuitions about individual cases but cannot yet bring them under a single principle, and I would like help doing exactly that. My working principle is a rough version of the object-given/state-given distinction we discussed in class: a consideration counts as a reason to have an attitude only if it bears on whether the object merits that attitude, while considerations about the desirability or costs of having the attitude are reasons to bring it about that one has it, not reasons for the attitude itself. I keep running into cases where that line blurs, so I want to test the principle rather than simply assert it.

Here are my cases. If a demon threatens to torture my family unless I fear a harmless house cat, I want to say that I have no reason to fear the cat; at most I have a reason to make myself fear it, if I can. If an eccentric billionaire offers me a fortune to believe that there is an even number of stars, likewise: I have a reason to acquire the belief, but no reason to believe it. The same holds when a demon offers me money to admire him for his supposed wit, when he is in fact dull and cruel: I have a reason to want to admire him and a reason to take steps to bring the admiration about, but no reason to admire him. By contrast, when a qualified doctor tells me that a snake in front of me is venomous, that does seem to be a reason to fear it, and when a trusted friend shows me that I have been lied to for years, that is a reason to distrust. Someone's actual generosity is a reason to admire her. So far the principle fits reasonably well. The trouble cases are these. Someone offers me five hundred dollars to take a pill that will make me admire a person who is in fact genuinely generous, clever, and honest: I am confident there is a reason to take the pill, but I go back and forth on whether the offer is itself a reason to admire the person in the same way her generosity is. And I do not know what to say about the fact that admiring a talented pianist would give me joy: that somehow feels like a reason to admire her, and not merely a reason to bring it about that I admire her, even though it appears to be a fact about my state rather than about her.

What I am asking for is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts as well as possible. Please state it as precisely as you can, indicate which clause is motivated by which of my cases, apply it to a few further cases I have not considered, and tell me honestly which of my reported verdicts, if any, it cannot accommodate without ad hoc contrivance.
