I write fiction for a living, and for the past three years I have also served on the literature panel of a public arts funder. The panel decides which literary projects receive public support, and I have kept a careful record of our decisions because I am trying to work out what principle, if any, underlies them. Our record so far is as follows.

1. A debut novel of unusual formal ambition, submitted by a small press that could cover only a fraction of the production costs. The outside readers called it the most striking unsolicited manuscript they had seen in years. Funded.
2. An application from a large trade publisher to underwrite the marketing and festival tour for the newest instalment in a highly profitable historical-romance series; the series is by general agreement competently made but formulaic. Declined.
3. A translation of a long-neglected modernist novel by a translator of real distinction. Projected sales were small, but the panel judged the work one of lasting importance. Funded.
4. A community storytelling project in three underserved districts, in which working novelists would lead workshops in public libraries and the resulting fiction would be issued as free chapbooks. The assessors judged the writing uneven, though it contained some striking pieces, and the project had no commercial sponsor. Funded.
5. A small-press collection of stories by a mid-career writer. The assessors described the collection as competent but unremarkable, and the press would not publish without a full subsidy. Declined.

Next week we must decide this application. A major trade publisher has asked for a grant to offset the first print run of the new novel by one of the country's most decorated novelists. By every account the novel is exceptional. The publisher has made clear that it will issue the book with or without our support and that it expects substantial sales; the application includes no public-access or community component.

I am less interested in the bare prediction than in the reasoning behind it. If the panel is consistent with its own record, how should it decide this new application? State which rule or rules fit the decisions we have already made, what each of those rules predicts for the new application, which rule you think best explains our practice and why, and what further case would best settle the question.
