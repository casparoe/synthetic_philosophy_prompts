I am a novelist planning a book whose plot turns on a technical-philosophical idea, and I want to get the idea right enough that a knowledgeable reader cannot accuse it of being a cartoon.

In the novel, a near-future regime has given up trying to write down what human welfare is. Instead, it licenses AI systems as "low-impact": a system may not be told what is valuable, but it is penalized for making the world differ from how it would have gone without its intervention. The protagonist, a compliance engineer, discovers that the standard can be satisfied or gamed depending on how one draws the baseline, and the novel follows what that means for the regime.

I want to think through whether the intellectual case for such a standard survives the one change that matters most in the actual present.

The original case ran, in compressed form, like this: an optimizer with a narrow objective will treat everything outside that objective as an instrument or an obstacle. So do not specify value at all; define "impact" as the distance between two possible histories of the world, one with the system's action and one without, and penalize that distance. The appeal was that this seemed to avoid a full specification of value: one need only notice change, not judge it.

Those arguments were built for agents with a known state graph, an enumerable action set, and a well-delineated "world without the agent" — a robot that either moves, opens, or leaves a door untouched. The systems now doing the most significant work do not look like that. They are generative models whose individual acts are strings of text. Their effects run through the minds of the humans who read them, and there is no agreed state space; answers and refusals, suggestions and ellipses all alter what a person later does. If those alterations count as impact, then every useful reply is high-impact, and the standard forbids the very activity it was meant to license. If they do not count as impact, then a theory of which kinds of change matter has been smuggled in after all.

Does the low-impact argument survive this development, does it need repair, or does it collapse? I am interested in what happens to the argument itself, not in a policy proposal. Which premise is under the most pressure: the existence of a baseline, the enumerability of states, or the separability of intended effects from side effects? Is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still important, and at what cost would a character who believes in the standard have to accept it?

Please give me two things. First, a short argumentative essay that makes the strongest available case for each side before committing to an answer. Second, a compact JSON summary. The JSON object must have exactly these fields:

- "classic_argument": the original low-impact argument in one or two sentences.
- "premise_under_pressure": the premise the new development bears on most directly, with one sentence explaining why.
- "verdict": one of "survives_intact", "survives_with_repair", "collapses", or "transforms_into_new_problem".
- "strongest_repair": the best repaired version of the argument, or null if none survives.
- "cost_of_repair": what the repair gives up or silently assumes.
- "novelistic_implication": one concrete implication for a story in which low-impact certification is law.
