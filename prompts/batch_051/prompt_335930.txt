I am a sixth-form student, and I have drafted the following proposal for a school essay competition. I would like it treated strictly as a mechanism: rather than judging whether English spelling is good or bad, please analyse how this institution would actually behave if it were established.

Every twenty-five years, a Spelling Resynchronisation Board is required to publish a revised standard spelling of English, which public examinations, school textbooks, and government documents must adopt. The Board works from a fixed key in which each phoneme of a designated standard accent corresponds to exactly one written unit (a letter or digraph), and each written unit corresponds to exactly one phoneme. At each revision, any word whose current standard spelling does not match the transcription this key assigns to its citation-form pronunciation must be re-spelled accordingly, with two exceptions. First, where re-spelling would make two or more words identical in print, the Board may keep the old spelling for one member of each such set, provided the set and the protected spelling are recorded in a public register. Second, if two related words share the written form of a morpheme they pronounce differently (as in sign and signature), the Board may defer re-spelling that morpheme for one further cycle. All protections and deferrals lapse after twenty-five years unless renewed.

Analyse how this mechanism would play out. What information would writing gain and lose, and who would be empowered, burdened, or tempted to game the rules? What second-order effects would follow for early literacy, literary texts, dialect communities, and the unity of the written language across generations and regions? Are the failure modes fixable, or are the exceptions themselves where the system would break down?
