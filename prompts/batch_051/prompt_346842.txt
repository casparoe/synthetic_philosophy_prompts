I've been going back and forth on an idea I came across a while ago: that the badness of human extinction isn't just a matter of all the deaths and suffering it involves. The thought is that a surprising amount of what we do only has its point because we take for granted that there will be people after us — finishing a novel, working on a slow-moving scientific problem, planting an oak, trying to reform an institution. The argument goes that if I were told I would die next Tuesday and everyone else would die the following month, some of these projects would lose their point even though my own remaining days are exactly the same. That's meant to show their value depends on the assumption that the species continues after me, not just on whether I live to see them finished. But I definately can't tell if this is a claim about the structure of value or just a mood efffect: thinking about extinction makes everything feel pointless, the way a spell of grief does, without revealing anything deep.

So treat this as a claim that could be partly tested: that the value of many ordinary projects is really dependent on the belief that humanity will continue after one's own death, rather than merely on the unpleasantness of imagining extinction. Design the strongest concrete test you can — an experiment, a survey, a behavioral measure, or a natural experiment using people whose beliefs about the future already vary. Tell me exactly what result would count in favour of the claim and what would count against it, and how you would control for the confound where thinking about death just makes everything feel pointless for reasons unrelated to the projects themselves. Then be honest about what even a clean positive result would not settle: whether the dependence is rational or just a quirk of our psychology, whether it concerns the value of the projects or only the motivation to pursue them, and how far it generalizes beyond the people you test.
