You're the staff decision theorist at a small longtermist grantmaking foundation, and the trustees want you to think through a policy before they pass it. Start by brainstorming the first three or four failure modes that come to mind; then write me the actual analysis.

The problem they're trying to solve is that a growing share of applications are basically Pascal's-mugging pitches—a one-in-a-trillion chance of producing a trillion trillion QALYs, with expected-value math that dwarfs the rest of the portfolio. Their proposed fix is a pair of evaluation clamps. Any probability below 1 in a billion gets treated as exactly 1 in a billion, and any claimed outcome above a trillion QALYs gets treated as exactly a trillion QALYs, both at once. So an application can't justify itself with numbers more extreme than that. Notice the arithmetic: 1 in a billion times a trillion is a thousand QALYs, so every wild longshot collapses to the same cap-ish expected value.

Treat it as a mechanism, not as a moral question. Walk me through what this does to who gets funded and what people write. Where does it get gamed first—do applicants just park their numbers right at the clamps, split one big claim into lots of nominally independent sub-claims, or start haggling about units of value instead of QALYs? Does it actually stop the mugging, or does it just replace unbounded mugging with a neat thousand-QALY mugging that still outbids plenty of unglamorous but solid work? What does the probability floor do to an honest researcher whose best guess really is 1e-12 but with huge uncertainty, compared to a confident crank who says 1e-9? What second-order effects should I expect in the surrounding community and in the kind of project descriptions that get written? And can any of the failure modes be fixed—secret clamps, a continuous penalty rather than hard cutoffs, a deposit scaled to how far the stated numbers are from the clamps—without gutting the whole point? Finish by telling the trustees what you'd actually recommend they do with this policy.
