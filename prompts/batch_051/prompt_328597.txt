I hold a two-part position about the moral status of digital minds, and I want a systematic account of what would count against it rather than a defense of it. The first part: moral considerability supervenes on a mind's causal-functional profile at the right level of abstraction; substrate and causal history are not themselves morally relevant. The second part: the right level of abstraction is fixed by which fine-grained implementation differences could in principle register in the mind's own ongoing experience, deliberation, or felt reasons. A difference that is invisible from the inside is, on my view, morally inert. I am not claiming that any present or near-term system clears the threshold; the view is a conditional claim about whatever does clear it.

What I want is an exposure map for this view: the places where it risks being false. Please sort candidate defeaters by how decisively they would bear on the position. Which arguments or thought experiments would, if accepted, flatly refute the first part, the second part, or the conjunction — and which would merely be awkward, such as puzzles about copies, rewinds, or fusion? Distinguish challenges to the sufficiency of functional structure from challenges to my definition of the relevant level of abstraction, since those are different joints. Where possible, separate a defender's own concessions from an outsider's objections: what would someone who holds this view have to admit counts against it on its own terms?

I also want to know what this position predicts that its rivals do not — for example, about incremental replacement of biological neurons, high-level versus low-level emulation of a valenced system, or the duplication of a mind. Which of those predictions are genuine tests, and which are just the view restated in exotic cases? Then address whether the inside-perspective criterion has insulated the position into unfalsifiability, and whether that insulation is a defect or simply the right shape for a claim of this metaphysical kind.

My own prior is that I would be most moved by a principled account of why the inside-perspective filter is the wrong filter, one that does not quietly assume that biology matters. But do not bias the answer toward that; tell me where the view would actually break first. If the honest conclusion is that the view is well insulated, say so explicitly, and say whether that is a reason to keep it or to distrust it. Please write the answer in American English.
