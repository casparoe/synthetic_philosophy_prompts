I'm teaching a course segment on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness—the view, associated especially with Keith Frankish, that the idea of private, non-physical qualitative properties is generated by introspective mechanisms rather than discovered by them, and that what we call "phenomenal consciousness" is an illusion in something like the way a seemingly unified self is constructed by a story. I'd like to use Stanisław Lem's *Solaris* as our fiction entry point, with Tarkovsky's film allowed as a comparison but the novel as the anchor. The setup seems built for it: the planet's "guests" are produced from the scientists' own memories and appear to be fully conscious beings, with grief, desire, and apparent inner lives, even though the scientists know they are material constructions generated by a non-conscious ocean. I want a close reading of *Solaris* through the lens of illusionism, but one that is genuinely faithful in both directions. In particular: does the novel dramatize the illusionist's claim that our confident sense of someone else's inner life is a projection generated by our cognitive machinery, or does it show the limits of that deflationary move? Consider Kelvin's growing relationship with the Hari replica: he knows what she is, but the normative pull of treating her as a subject does not go away. Is that fact, in the novel, a revelation of something real that illusionism misses, a symptom of the illusion's grip, or something more ambiguous? What scenes or details would you point a class to, and where does *Solaris* resist a clean illusionist reading? The students will have read the novel, so specific scene references are welcome, but please avoid verbatim quotation. Finally, I'd like you to end by saying how confident you are in your central interpretive claims, and to single out the point you are least sure of.
