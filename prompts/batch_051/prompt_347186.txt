Recently I sat in on a long argument between two members of my reading group, Mara and Theo, about what Nozick's experience-machine thought experiment really shows for theories of well-being. Both had thought carefully about the material, and the argument moved through several distinct stages. I have reconstructed it below so as to preserve the substance of each move. I honestly could not determine who had the better of it, and I would like the exchange adjudicated as it actually unfolded.

1. Mara opened by challenging the standard inference. She said the experiment is usually read as refuting hedonism because people would not plug in. But the question is put to us from outside the machine, and from that standpoint the choice is already full of anticipated bad experiences: guilt at making a settled plan to abandon one's actual family, anxiety about the machine failing, unease at the thought of a body kept alive in a tank. Each of these is an unpleasant felt state that a hedonist can count. So the refusal to plug in does not by itself establish that we value anything beyond felt experience.

2. Theo replied that this mislocates the thought experiment. The machine is specified as able to deliver any experience one would want, including the experience of having no lingering suspicion, guilt, or unease about the choice. The real comparison is between two complete lives: one spent in the machine with a flawless stream of experiences, and a matching real life with actual pursuits and actual contact with others. No difference in the felt stream explains why the simulated life should rank lower. If Mara must fall back on an anticipatory unease that is itself a response to the thought that the simulated life is somehow worse, then the unease is carrying the non-hedonic judgment rather than explaining it; otherwise the view merely protects itself by postulating new bad feelings whenever it is threatened.

3. Mara conceded that her first point was weaker than she had made it sound, but said the experiment has a different defect. Deciding not to plug in can be motivated by duties to actual people — a dependent spouse, children, aging parents — who would be left behind. Those are moral or relational reasons, not reasons about one's own welfare. Since the standard reading takes our refusal as evidence about what makes a life good for the person living it, the usual experiment conflates prudential value with obligations to others. It is accordingly contaminated evidence.

4. Theo answered that this is a correctable confound rather than an objection in principle. Run the case with no living dependents, or suppose that one's obligations would be discharged by others; the residual unwillingness to plug in remains, and it can no longer be attributed to identifiable third parties. The persistence of the refusal across such variations makes a standing appeal to some independent moral prohibition look strained. He added that even the parent's refusal is not normally a bare citation of duty: the parent wants to be the kind of person who stays with their child, which is itself an example of a non-experiential element in a good life, of exactly the sort the experiment is meant to expose.

5. Mara then moved to the perspective of someone already inside the machine. Such a person, on the machine's own specification, feels no suspicion that their world is simulated and experiences no lack. If we say this person is worse off than someone living a matching real life, we make well-being independent of anything the person can recognize in their own experience; if we say they are not worse off, then the outsider's refusal to plug in was never a judgment about the machine-dweller's welfare at all, but only a preference for reality on the outsider's part. Either way, she argued, the experiment fails to show that the simulated life is worse for the one who lives it.

6. Theo said this dilemma is built on contested ground. It presupposes that a person's well-being must be evident from within experience, which is the very thesis in dispute. People are harmed in ways they never feel: a spouse is quietly despised by the person they trust; a writer believes her books are admired when in fact no one has read them. If those lives can go badly without a felt deficit, then the machine-dweller's having no felt deficit proves nothing. What this shows, he said, is that a life's seeming good from the inside is not the same as its going well for the person, and the machine can hide the distance between the two without closing it.

7. Mara replied that the hidden-betrayal and unread-author examples are not pure. In ordinary cases the hidden fact is entangled with a standing risk of felt consequences: exposure, mockery, exploitation, or lost opportunities. Once those are stripped away, it is no longer obvious that the bare unexperienced fact itself is a harm. The experience machine is exactly that stripped-down case — an illusion guaranteed never to leave a felt trace. So she thought the machine does not settle the dispute between experiential and non-experiential theories of well-being; it presents the pure case on which those two theories disagree, and their disagreement about that pure case is the thing that needed to be argued, not assumed.

At that point neither would budge. I could not see who was winning, and I suspect both were partly right about different parts of the exchange.

Please work through the argument move by move. For each move, say what is right in it, what is overstated, and whether the move succeeds on its own terms. Then say which of the two has the stronger overall position by the end, and identify anything important that both of them are missing or conflating.
