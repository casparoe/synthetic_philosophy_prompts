I'm a retired geologist — forty years in igneous petrology — and I've recently fallen into philosophy of law, mostly because old rules remind me of old strata: the original conditions are gone but the structure carries on. A local dispute has turned into a nice test case, and I'd like you to work through it with me.

The rule. Article IX of the 1703 Charter of the Meridian Sea Company reads:

'The said Company shall and may make, ordain, and establish such Laws, Ordinances, and Constitutions as shall be thought meet and convenient for the good Government of the said Port, Plantation, and Factories, and of the Officers, Servants, and Inhabitants thereof, and shall have full Power and Authority to appoint Judges and Magistrates for the hearing and determining of all Causes, as well Civil as Criminal, arising within the same.'

(That's copied from our local archive's transcription; I'd rather not argue about the commas.)

The case. The Company was chartered to run a trading post on a small island. It never surrendered the charter, and the old Port of Meridian is now a town of about sixty thousand people. Most residents are not Company employees and never signed anything with the Company; they work for the university, the hospital, some tech firms. The Company still owns much of the land and runs the port, the water utility, and the fiber network. Citing Article IX, the Company's board has just set up a 'Charter Court' with a paid magistrate and claims civil and criminal jurisdiction over everyone residing within the old settlement boundaries, employee or not. The town council says that's absurd; the Company says the clause means what it says and was never repealed. I suspect the Company is confusing ownership with government, but that might just be my twenty-first-century prejudice, and I'd rather see the question argued seperately.

The drafters in 1703 could only have imagined a few hundred officers, sailors, and servants in a stockade, not a city of non-employees with the internet. So this is a rule that has outrun its authors, and I want to know what it actually requires.

Please act as a philosopher of law who also knows the history of chartered companies and corporate sovereignty. Think carefully, and before committing, brainstorm the interpretive moves you might make and the weakest point of each. Then answer: (i) what does the plain text of Article IX give? (ii) what does the purpose of the clause, in a 1703 trading charter, give? (iii) what would the drafters have said in 1703 if asked about a city of sixty thousand non-employees? (iv) what would a reasonable present-day resident reading the clause take it to mean? Finally, which approach should control, and does Article IX authorize the Charter Court over non-employees?

I want a committed answer, not a menu; if the four approaches split, pick the winner and say why. Give me a JSON object with exactly these fields:
- "verdict": one of "authorizes", "does_not_authorize", or "partially_authorizes"
- "plain_text_reading": one sentence on (i)
- "purpose_reading": one sentence on (ii)
- "drafters_counterfactual": one sentence on (iii)
- "reasonable_reader_today": one sentence on (iv)
- "decisive_consideration": the single strongest reason for the verdict
- "reasoning": a short paragraph defending the verdict, including the strongest opposing reading and why it loses
- "confidence": a number from 0 to 1

The JSON is all I need; don't add anything after it.
