Two friends of mine got into it at a party over whether a ferromagnet's magnetization is "just" its atoms aligning. Mina is a condensed-matter PhD student and Jonah is in philosophy of science, so it got pretty far into the weeds.

Mina said this is as clean a reduction as you could ask for: below the Curie temperature the atomic spins align and the magnetization is just the summed contribution of those spins. You can put the Ising Hamiltonian on a lattice, run a simulation, and watch the ordering appear. No extra ingredient.

Jonah said that skips the actual transition. A real magnet has finitely many atoms, and in a finite system there is no sharp phase transition: the magnetization just fluctuates. A genuine discontinuity only exists in the thermodynamic limit, an idealization we impose. That limit isnt entailed by any finite collection of atoms, so the thing we call a phase transition isnt the kind of fact you get from the atoms alone.

Mina said that old complaint doesnt buy you emergence. Physicists know the limit is an idealization. Idealizations are how you derive sharp real behavior from the microdynamics, like treating a gas as a continuum. An idealization inside a derivation is not some new kind of stuff.

Jonah said fine, but universality makes the same point another way. The same critical exponents show up in magnets, fluids near their critical points, and binary mixtures, whose microscopic bits are completely different. If you can swap out the microdetails and keep the macrobehavior, then "nothing but atoms" is the wrong explanatory story. The explaining is done by scaling relations and fixed points, which live at the coarse-grained level, not at the level of particular atoms.

Mina said that's backwards. Universality means many microscopic theories flow to the same fixed point under coarse-graining. You still need the microscopic Hamiltonian to know which universality class the system is in, and to define the renormalization transformation in the first place. Without the microtheory you have no derivation, just a catalogue of exponents.

Jonah said that is more than his point needs. He isnt saying you cant get the exponents out of a model. He's saying the derivation only works by showing that the microdetails are irrelevant to the transition. So the honest description is that the phase transition belongs to a high-level scaling pattern that many microphysics happen to realize. That is what he means by emergence.

Mina said "many microphysics happen to realize it" is just multiple realizability, and multiple realizability is compatible with reducing this particular magnet: this magnet's behavior is still wholly constituted by these atoms and their couplings, even if other atoms could have played the same role.

Jonah said that token-level concession comes after the fact. The question is whether the physics of the transition is contained in atomic physics, or whether it is a pattern that only appears once you coarse-grain. Order parameters, domain walls, correlation lengths — none of these even show up unless you change the level of description. If the thing you are trying to explain literally doesnt exist at the atomic level, then calling it "just atoms" is wrong about the explanation, even if it is right about the building materials.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldnt. Can you go through the exchange step by step and say who's right about which point, where each of them overstates, and who has the better overall case if either does?
