I'm in a philosophy of mind course, and we've been working through what physicalism could even mean — and the more I sit with Hempel's dilemma, the more it looks like the position is left with no formulation at all. As I understand the dilemma: if 'physical' means the ontology of current physics, then physicalism is almost certainly false, because current physics is incomplete and its posits are likely to be revised, possibly beyond recognition. But if 'physical' means whatever the ontology of a completed, ideal future physics turns out to be, then physicalism seems to say only that everything is whatever the final physics ends up describing. And if that final physics were to include consciousness, qualia, or protomental properties as fundamental, then the second reading wouldn't rule out views that everyone would have classified as nonphysicalist. So the thesis appears to be either false or empty.

What I don't understand is why this isn't simply treated as a refutation. Whenever the dilemma comes up in discussion, it's introduced as a difficulty the physicalist must handle, not as the point at which the view is abandoned — yet I can't see what's left to handle it with. Could you explain what a working physicalist means by 'the physical' once both horns are on the table? Is there a formulation that is neither hostage to how current physics happens to fare nor so permissive that it admits dualism by default, and what keeps that formulation from collapsing the distinction between physicalism and the views that are supposed to be its rivals?
