I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I keep coming back to a parallel that I cannot tell apart from a loose analogy.

A pseudorandom number generator is a deterministic state machine: given the seed, the entire output stream is fixed, and after seeding there is no randomness anywhere in the system. Yet the output can be indistinguishable, for any efficient observer, from a truly uniform random source. The standard cryptographic definition of a secure pseudorandom generator is precisely the claim that no computationally bounded test can tell its output from uniform randomness with non-negligible advantage. The randomness, then, is a property of the observer's information and computational limits, not of the generator itself.

Chaotic physical systems seem to have the same shape. They are deterministic in the sense that a complete state plus the dynamics fixes the future, but sensitive dependence on initial conditions means that any finite-precision measurement leaves enough uncertainty for errors to grow exponentially, so a bounded observer faces a prediction horizon. The system is deterministic at the level of its full state while being unpredictable, even random-looking, at the level of what a bounded observer can measure.

I think I am looking at the same structure in both cases: determinism at one level, observer-relative unpredictability at another, and randomness emerging from the gap.

Where does this parallel hold, and where does it break? Is the PRNG case deterministic in the relevant sense, or does its finite state space and known transition function make it a misleading model? In the chaotic case, is the unpredictability fundamentally about measurement precision, or does it also have a computational component — for example, if one had unlimited computation but finite measurement precision? If the analogy is deep, then the way computer science treats randomness as inability-to-distinguish would suggest that the question whether the universe is deterministic or genuinely chancy is less crisp than it looks once the class of observers is fixed. Is that a real implication, or does physics retain a fact of the matter that the analogy misses?
