Consider the following argument, which is sometimes offered as a parity argument against the idea that epistemic dependence on AI erodes human understanding. Its conclusion seems grossly inflated, yet none of the steps is obviously false.

(1) If a person can reliably answer and explain the questions of a given domain by consulting a cognitive tool, then the person–tool pair possesses the domain-specific competence that constitutes understanding.

(2) There is no principled epistemic difference between a cognitive capacity realized inside the skull and one that depends on a stable, transparent, immediately available external tool: both are resources the person draws on without checking every operation.

(3) If the person–tool pair possesses the competence that constitutes understanding, and the tool stands to the person in the same epistemic relation as memory or an acquired routine, then the person understands the domain.

(4) A student who has reliable access to a capable AI and has learned which prompts elicit correct answers and explanations satisfies the condition in (1).

(5) Therefore the student understands whatever the AI can answer about — including, for example, the theory of stochastic partial differential equations, if the AI can answer questions about it.

(6) But that is false: bare, unvetted dependence on an oracle is not understanding.

The argument appears to prove too much, since it makes understanding depend only on the reliability of the system and the student's skill at prompting, not on anything the student grasps or could reconstruct. Diagnose the argument. If it contains a flawed step, identify it; if it is valid, state which premise is least costly to reject. Explain why the rejected step initially looked true, and give the strongest repair available to someone who wants to retain the view that well-integrated cognitive tools can extend understanding without licensing oracle-driven understanding.
