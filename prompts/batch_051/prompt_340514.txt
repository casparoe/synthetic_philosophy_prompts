Consider a constitutional amendment under debate in a country with a unicameral legislature. The amendment creates a second, non-amending chamber, the Chamber of Foundations, made up of six caucuses of one hundred members each, corresponding to the six foundations of Moral Foundations Theory: care/harm, fairness/cheating, liberty/oppression, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and sanctity/degradation. Every citizen, on reaching adulthood, takes a machine-scored Moral Foundations Questionnaire, and their six standardized foundation scores are recorded in a national register. A citizen qualifies for a caucus if their score on that foundation is in the top fifth of the population and higher than each of their other five scores; ties at the top are broken by random assignment before the draw. Members are then selected by sortition from the six eligibility pools, are paid, serve staggered four-year terms, may not organize by party, and may serve only once. A bill passed by the lower house must win at least 60 percent approval within every one of the six caucuses to become law; otherwise it dies for the session, and the lower house may not reintroduce a substantially identical bill until the next session. The Chamber cannot amend bills. It only votes, and after every vote it publishes each caucus's tally together with a short statement of the reasons that moved it.

Treat the Chamber as a mechanism rather than a moral position to be praised or condemned. I want a reverse-engineering of how it would actually behave. Which kinds of legislation would clear six foundation vetoes, and which kinds would systematically fail? What would the rule incentivize among parties drafting bills in the lower house, among citizens filling out the questionnaire, and among the citizens seated in the caucuses? Where would it be gamed first, and what would the first visible distortion be? What pressure would build up over the first few sessions, and how would an enterprising government route around the Chamber? Pay particular attention to what the rule does with the fact that the six foundations are unequally distributed, correlated with one another, and variable in intensity across the population, and to what it quietly assumes when it gives each foundation an equal veto.

I would like a committed analysis, not a survey of possible outcomes: take a definite position on which two or three effects would dominate and in what order they would appear, give the reasoning, and then flag the points where you are least confident.
