A colleague in a jurisprudence seminar has been defending the void-for-vagueness doctrine, and he produced an argument that seems to show the doctrine swallows all of criminal law. He will not give up any step, and he will not accept the conclusion. Here is the argument as he lays it out:

(1) A criminal law is void for vagueness if it fails to give fair notice: if there are cases in which a person of ordinary understanding cannot determine, even after reasonable effort, whether some contemplated conduct falls under the law.

(2) Every actual criminal statute contains at least one general term — something like "vehicle," "recklessly," "residence," "threatens," "obscene," or "deadly weapon" — and every such general term admits genuine borderline instances, cases in which competent speakers cannot settle whether the term applies.

(3) Therefore, for any real criminal statute, there is some possible conduct such that a person of ordinary understanding cannot determine whether the statute applies to it.

(4) Therefore, by the standard in (1), every criminal statute is void for vagueness.

(5) But that cannot be right: it would mean that a prohibition on, say, murder or arson is unconstitutional even though its central applications are perfectly clear.

(6) So at least one premise must be rejected, or the move from (1) and (2) to (4) must be invalid.

I would prefer to reject (1), because accepting sharp boundaries in ordinary language seems far too costly. But another colleague says rejecting (1) is the question-begging move: it merely restates the doctrine as requiring a statute to be "so vague" that it fails in its central applications rather than "vague in some respect," which just relocates the problem, since the threshold between tolerable and intolerable vagueness will itself be vague. Which step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it commit me to? If (1) is the culprit, give the strongest non-ad-hoc restatement of the doctrine and say whether its boundary is any less exposed to the same argument. If (2) is the culprit, specify the theory of vagueness that blocks it, and what that theory must say about the borderline cases above.
