As a journalist covering consciousness science and the welfare debates that now run from animal research to artificial systems, I have been carrying around a rule of thumb for comparing experiences across different kinds of minds, and it has started to break against my own judgments. I want the principle reconstructed from my verdicts rather than a survey of positions.

The rule of thumb was multiplicative. Assign each state two numbers: its degree of phenomenal consciousness, from zero to one, and its hedonic intensity, how good or bad the experience is. The moral weight of the state is the product of the two. The rule has sharp consequences: zero consciousness means zero weight no matter how intense the displayed state; a large enough difference in intensity can compensate for a difference in consciousness; and a state that is half as conscious and half as intense counts a quarter as much.

Here are five cases and my verdicts on them.

1. Maximal reward with no consciousness. Suppose an intervention could hold a patient with no consciousness at all — stipulate certainty, whatever the real clinical uncertainties — in a state of maximal neural reward for a year. I judge that no length of such a state is worth the loss of a single pleasant afternoon for a fully conscious person. It counts for nothing. The product rule gets this right.

2. The severely pained mouse and the mildly pained human. Assume for argument that a mouse is less richly conscious than a human, though plainly conscious. I judge that a mouse in severe pain should be helped before a human with a mild tension headache; the intensity gap outweighs the consciousness gap. If someone said human-level consciousness has lexical priority over all animal suffering, I would reject that rule on the basis of this case.

3. The many mildly uncomfortable fish. Assume a fish is conscious but only dimly so. Compare an arbitrarily large number of fish, each in a mildly unpleasant state, with one alert human in substantial but bearable anxiety. I judge that no finite number of the mildly unpleasant fish states outweighs the human's anxiety, even though the product rule implies that with enough fish the summed, discounted suffering must eventually win. But the obvious repair — giving human-level consciousness lexical priority — would contradict my verdict in the mouse case.

4. Double discounting. A drowsy, half-present person has a pleasant sensation that is half as intense as a baseline, and the drowsy state itself is half as conscious as full wakefulness. Compare that with an alert person whose pleasant sensation has the baseline intensity. The product rule says the first is a quarter as valuable. I judge the first is only somewhat less valuable; the rule seems to discount the same deficit twice.

5. The discontinuity at zero. I hold that a state with no consciousness at all has no intrinsic value even if its behavior matches a conscious state of enjoyment or distress, but I also hold that any positive amount of consciousness, however small, can still support great value when the intensity is great enough. I want a sharp zero and no other thresholds, and I cannot tell whether the product rule ultimately delivers that or merely approximates it.

What I am after is the principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that best captures these verdicts. Consider at least the additive, multiplicative, and lexical structures, where lexical priority means one dimension entirely dominates the other, as well as any hybrid or thresholded variants they suggest. State the principle precisely enough to yield verdicts on cases I have not considered. Then say honestly which of my five verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, whether the small harms in case 3 should aggregate, and what the final form of the principle is.
