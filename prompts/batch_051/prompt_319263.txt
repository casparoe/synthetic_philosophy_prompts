I joined the trust-and-safety appeals panel of a company that runs four persistent social virtual worlds six months ago, and I keep finding that our hardest problem is conceptual rather than technical: we have a record of rulings about when damage to virtual objects, or distress caused through a virtual world, counts as a real harm, and users now cite those rulings back at us when they appeal. I have been asked to prepare the recommendation for our next meeting, and I want it to stand on our own precedents rather than on whichever moral intuition I happen to have that week. Here are the rulings we are all committed to. In the auction-house case we restored drained funds and banned the offender: a script exploit siphoned another account's tradable gold, which is exchangeable for real money on our platform-run exchange. In the arena case we took no action: a player killed another player's avatar repeatedly inside a clearly marked player-versus-player zone, despite an anguished appeal from the target, because the world's own published rules say not to enter the zone with anything you would mind losing. In the memorial case we imposed a permanent ban on a user who drove a bulldozing script through a player-built memorial garden for a deceased guildmate; the garden had no cash-out value and stood in an unzoned area with no formal building protections, but the destruction was aimed at identifiable mourners, breached our platform-wide anti-griefing policy, and caused documented severe distress. In the shoreline case we took no action: an owner rebuilt their paid-for private island estate into something the neighbors found ugly, spoiling the view from a popular public beach, but we reasoned that no common facility had been destroyed and nobody had been targeted.

The case now in front of us is the plaza case. A player has used only legitimate purchase mechanics to acquire ownership of Founder's Plaza, a fifteen-year-old communal meeting space that predates the current property system. The plaza has no real-money exchange value, but it has hosted new-player orientation, weddings, holiday celebrations, and a widely attended memorial vigil, and one community group has shown us that deleting it would end their decade-old tradition of holding a weekly open house there. The owner has not broken any written rule: the property code gives plot owners full editing rights, and our anti-griefing policy has never been applied to what a landowner does on their own plot. The owner now announces they will delete the plaza and build an invitation-only casino. No tradable assets are at stake, but many long-time users are distraught, and the loss they describe is real to them.

What should the panel decide if it wants to be consistent with its own record? I need a decision memorandum prepared as if for my fellow panel members. Identify the candidate principles that would fit our four earlier rulings, say what each principle would dictate in the plaza case, and tell me which precedent is the closest analog and why. Then give your own recommendation, and be explicit about the point where consistency runs out: if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the plaza case, say so, and state which new principle each possible ruling would commit us to. Finally, name the single further case whose outcome would do the most to tell those principles apart. Keep the memorandum to no more than 900 words, with the recommended decision in the opening sentence.
