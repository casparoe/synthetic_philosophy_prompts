I retired a few years ago from an environmental science lab and got into philosophy of action, mainly because I keep setting myself a schedule for writing up old data and then blowing it in the evenings in ways that don't fit my own planning. That got me reading about weakness of will, and then a friend pointed me at the hyperbolic discounting work from behavioral economics, which she said explains exactly that. I've now built an argument out of the two literatures that seems to show too much, and I can't find the flaw—maybe because my training makes me too willing to treat a "preference" as just whatever a person ends up choosing. Here is the argument:

(1) Weakness of will, as I'm understanding it, is when a person judges that X is better all-things-considered than Y, and then freely does Y anyway. (2) Hyperbolic discounting is supposed to explain this: a reward's subjective value falls off steeply with delay at first and then flattens out, roughly value = reward / (1 + k·delay), so on Tuesday a big reward due in a month ranks higher than a smaller reward due Friday, but by Friday evening the smaller one, being right there, ranks higher. (3) So at the moment the person picks the drink or the phone, she is not acting against her own evaluation: the smaller sooner reward now really ranks higher for her, and all that has happened is that she changed her mind about which option is better as the delays changed. (4) But changing one's mind over time is not weakness of will; it's just an unstable preference, like a kid who wants the red crayon on Monday and the blue one on Tuesday. (5) If instead she still judges at the moment of choice that the larger later reward is better all-things-considered, then the discounting story doesn't explain why that judgment loses—it only gives a curve that describes the temptation, which is just a redescription of the very fact we wanted explained. (6) So either what we call weakness of will is really just preference reversal, with no genuine acting against one's own better judgment, or hyperbolic discounting leaves the actual phenomenon unexplained. Since the phenomenon is real, I'm forced to throw out the hyperbolic discounting explanation entirely. But that seems to prove to much, because those discounting models fit an awful lot of behavior. Where does the argument go wrong?
