I've been going round in circles on a thought experiment I half-remember from Hofstadter – I think he calls it the careenium. The setup is roughly this: imagine a perfectly frictionless billiard table populated by little simulated balls (simmballs) that bounce around forever. After a while, you stop tracking individual trajectories and start describing the table in terms of larger patterns – streams, vortices, maybe even things that look like they're steering the balls. Hofstadter's question was whether those high-level patterns can genuinely push the lower-level balls around, or whether that's just a way of talking. I might be mangling the details, but the general idea is what I care about.

I'm trying to work out the strongest version of each side. On one hand, you could say the patterns are real causal structures, not mere summaries, because if you intervene on the pattern (say you block a vortex) you change the micro-trajectories, and no one finds that mysterious. On the other hand, you could say the patterns are epiphenomenal shadows of the microstate; the simmballs don't care about the pattern, they just obey the local physics. The thing I really care about is less the thought experiment itself and more the general claim about whether symbols push the substrate around. I'd like a comapre between the epiphenomenalist reading and the 'patterns as real causes' reading. Specifically, which reading does the careenium actually support, and does the answer transfer to mental causation – e.g. a belief or a symbol causing a neuron to fire? I'm writing something and I want to avoid the usual handwavy conclusion that 'it's both'. So make a case for the reading you think is more defensible, and point out where the other one goes wrong.
