For the past few years my decision theory reading group has been stuck on the same dispute about Quinn's self-torturer, and I've started to suspect the impasse has less to do with anyone being confused than with the structure of the disagreement itself. The setup is familiar: someone can increase a device a thousand times, each increment gives them a meaningful amount of money and adds a pain increase so small that no single increment feels any different, but after all thousand increments they're in agony and would pay everything back to undo the whole sequence. One camp says the rational agent should refuse the series somewhere: each step is locally tempting, but the end state is obviously worse than the start, so either some step wasn't actually rational, or the agent is failing to see how choices compose over time. The other camp says the case shows rational preference can be nontransitive across barely discriminable differences; transitivity, they say, is a tidy mathematical assumption, not a requirement of rationality, and the fact that someone with cyclic preferences can be money-pumped shows only that they are exploitable, not that they are irrational. Everyone in the group knows the arguments, nobody's moved in years, and I honestly don't want a verdict on who's right. What I want is a diagnosis: is this a verbal dispute about what 'preference' or 'rational' means, is it a disagreement about whether pairwise choices or overall rankings are the real data, is it a bedrock clash of intuitions, or is there an unnoticed formal or empirical question doing the work? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would actually count as progress—a distinction, an experiment, a theorem, a change of question—and why?
