Consider a proposal for municipal planning disputes. A city government, impressed by evidence on loss aversion and the endowment effect, decides that long-standing residents should be treated as the rightful holders of the status quo: their resistance to change should be taken at face value rather than discounted as a bias. It therefore replaces ordinary planning objections with a reference-point register, run as follows.

Whenever a project passes technical review, every household within a fixed radius of the site must lodge one of three declarations. An objection states a single cash amount the household is willing to accept in exchange for consenting to the project; the objection is binding, so if the project proceeds the objector is paid exactly that amount and may not oppose the project later. A support states a single cash amount the household will pay toward the project if it proceeds, and is likewise binding. A declaration of indifference is free and carries no obligation. All declarations are public.

The project proceeds if and only if total support prices, plus any developer contribution committed in advance, at least equal total objection prices. If it proceeds, every objector is paid in full from the resulting fund; any surplus goes to a district maintenance fund; supporters pay their stated prices regardless of the surplus. If the fund falls short, the project is refused unless the developer privately tops up the shortfall within thirty days.

After two years the city adds two anti-gaming rules: objection prices are capped at 25 per cent above the district median for settled objections in the same category over the previous three years, and households that declared indifference may not later sue for nuisance or claim compensation in court.

Walk through how this system would actually behave. Who would declare as an objector, a supporter, or indifferent, and what prices would they name? Would the scheme succeed in converting loss aversion from something that distorts planning into a legitimate property right, or would it simply turn loss aversion into an entitlement that freezes development? Then analyse the second-order effects: strategic developer contributions designed to trigger top-ups, collusion among neighbours to raise the local median, the effect of publicity on price-setting, and the distributional shift among renters, owners, and developers. Which of these failure modes can be fixed by amendment, and which are baked into treating the status quo as property? Finally, say whether, on your analysis, the scheme mostly vindicates or mostly undermines the view that the endowment effect is a legitimate preference rather than a bias. Please write your answer in American English.
