I have a policy thats served me for years: when someone argues I should do something costly because there is a small chance of an enormous payoff, I treat the argument as presumptively bad unless they can give me an actual, checkable way to estimate the chance. I don't mean I demand precision; I mean if the probability is basically unknowable and the payoff is defined as infinite or astronomically large, I dismiss the argument as expected-value fanaticism. I apply this to Pascal's wager too. I was rereading Pascal's Pensées, in the wager section, and he says something close to: if God might exist, the gain from wagering for God is infinite, and what you give up is finite, so as long as the chance is any positive number, the best bet is obvious. I find myself wanting to say that this is exactly the mistake my policy protects me against. But I also worry my policy is just a way of refusing to think about arguments that have unpleasant conclusions. So: is my policy defensible, or is it bias dressed up as a rule? I want a definite verdict, not a survey of possible positions. If it is defensible, tell me the cleanest version of the rule; if it is not, tell me what I should replace it with and why Pascal's reasoning should not be dismissed the way I dismiss other fanatical bets.
