I'm working on a piece about what actually makes scientific communities update their minds, and a source — a pragmatist philosopher who lives and breathes Peirce — kept leaning on an analogy: inquiry is natural selection for claims. Ideas vary, experience does the selecting, and the beliefs that survive contact with facts that refuse to cooperate are the 'fit' ones. He used it to brush off the worry that science gets shaped by funding, status, and fads, on the grounds that those are just selection pressures, and nobody thinks selection pressures discredit evolution. That feels too neat to me. How far does this analogy actually hold? Give me the correspondence table — what's the population, the mutation, the enviroment, the fitness, the inheritance — and then show me where it snaps. In particular: biological fitness is just differential reproduction, whereas 'survival' in inquiry already seems to smuggle in the idea that the surviving belief is warranted or truth-tracking; and ideas don't replicate the way genes do, so the mutation half of the analogy looks shaky too. Do those disanalogies actually kill his conclusion that funding and fads don't corrupt inquiry, or does the conclusion survive even if the analogy is only rough? And if the natural-selection picture breaks in the wrong place, is there a better analogy for what evidence does to a community of inquirers, or should I drop the analogy entirely?
