Okay, so in my constitutionalism class we've been doing precommitment stuff — Ulysses binding himself to the mast so he can hear the Sirens without wrecking the ship, a gambler putting himself on a self-exclusion list, someone setting up automatic savings so later-them can't spend it. Everyone treats those individual self-bindings as, like, the paradigm of rationality. But bring the same structure into constitutions — a founding generation entrenches a right, or requires a supermajority to amend something, or throws in an actual unamendable eternity clause — and the mood flips: people call it a dead hand, anti-democratic, the past hijacking the future. So we've got this asymmetry: individually binding your own future self is fine, but a democracy binding its later generations is suspect. The obvious reply is that future citizens never consented, whereas tomorrow-you is still you. But does that actually carry the weight? Trow a few nearby cases at it: advance directives for dementia patients (we tend to honor them, but people argue hard about whether the later person is 'you' in the relevant sense), ordinary contracts that bind a future self who now wants out (we still enforce a lot of those), and the thought that constitutions are less dead-hand than rules of the game that make politics possible at all. I'm trying to figure out whether this asymmetry should be kept, reversed (maybe we're too relaxed about self-binding and too harsh on constitutional precommitment), or just given up as an inconsistency. Also, please seperate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. At the very end, put your bottom line in fixed form: \answer{keep} or \answer{reverse} or \answer{give up}.
