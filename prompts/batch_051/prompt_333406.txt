I'm a novelist, so I'm outside my lane here, but I've been following the logical induction stuff for years and the argument feels stuck in a way I cant quite name. Maybe you can name it.

The standoff, as I understand it. One camp says ideal Bayesian reasoning under empirical uncertainty is the one clear success story—avoiding Dutch books pins down the probabilities—and the only thing wrong with it was logical omniscience. Logical induction fixes exactly that: a computable reasoner can assign probabilities to every sentence of arithmetic, keep updating over time, be unexploitable by any computable trading strategy, and still converge to probability one on the provable stuff and zero on the refutable stuff. For that camp, the remaining choices (which traders, what time-discounting, how much compute) are just detail.

The second camp says those details are the whole problem, because the guarantees don't tell a bounded agent what to think about Goldbach's conjecture on any given afternoon; the answer can be postponed forever. Choosing the traders and the discount schedule is doing all the normative work, but nobody will own it. A third camp says the whole enterprise mistakes logic for weather: a sentence of arithmetic is already true or false, so assigning it a credence of 0.7 isn't an estimate of anything real, just a description of confusion.

My novelist's hypothesis: the first camp wants a theory of rational performance, the second a theory of rational belief, and the third a theory of what probability-talk means. Under one word—rational—three different questions.

Is that actually what has stalled the debate, or is it something else—a hidden mathematical question, a difference about which cases count as data, an ill-posed target? Tell me what kind of disagreement this is, with reasons, and what would count as progress given the diagnosis. Or whether I should stop waiting for a winner and just use all three as characters.
