I've spent the past month trying to make sense of a running disagreement with a co-author. We have read the same studies and yet assign very different probabilities to the same empirical claim. I kept returning to the Bayesian convergence results — the idea that priors wash out in the long run — and I ended up with an argument that now looks as if it proves too much.

(1) Take a finite set of mutually distinguishable hypotheses and two Bayesian agents whose priors give positive probability to every hypothesis in the set. If both update by conditionalization on the same sequence of observations, then, almost surely, each agent's posterior probability in the true hypothesis tends to one as the sequence grows; their posterior gap therefore tends to zero.

(2) So there is a finite amount of shared evidence — some fixed number of observations — after which any two such priors must yield posteriors that agree to any ordinary standard of precision.

(3) In mature sciences, rival hypotheses are routinely modeled as a finite list, and the shared evidence has long since exceeded that amount.

(4) Yet careful, well-informed researchers continue to disagree on many empirical questions.

(5) Therefore, by (2) and (3), in any such dispute at least one side must fail to be a non-dogmatic Bayesian: they assigned zero probability to a live hypothesis, failed to update on the shared evidence, or never really shared it.

(6) But that is an implausible description of many live disputes. So either those disputes are much less rational than they look, or the convergence results — or Bayesianism itself — must be rejected.

I would rather keep both Bayesianism and the thought that my disagreement with my co-author is rational; this argument says I cannot. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
