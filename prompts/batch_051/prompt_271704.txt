I'm reading up on the socialist calculation debate and I keep running into the same one-paragraph version of it, from an economics blog I wont name that's otherwise pretty careful. Here's the gist:

"The socialist calculation debate was about whether a centrally planned economy could be rational. The Austrian side—Mises first, then Hayek—claimed it couldn't: without private ownership of the means of production you get no genuine prices for capital goods, and in any case a planning board would have to solve an impossibly large system of simultaneous equations to know the right quantities to produce. Oskar Lange replied that this was just wrong. A central planning board can announce provisional prices, tell managers to treat those prices as parametric and minimize cost, then bump prices up when there are shortages and down when there are surpluses. This trial-and-error procedure converges on the same equilibrium a real market would reach, so 'calculation' under socialism is only a computational burden—one that modern computers have effectively removed. Having lost, the Austrians retreated to ideological objections about planners' arrogance and individual liberty."

Then I actually sat down with Hayek's "The Use of Knowledge in Society" and hit this passage:

"In ordinary language we describe by the word “planning” the complex of interrelated decisions about the allocation of our available resources. All economic activity is in this sense planning; and in any society in which many people collaborate, this planning, whoever does it, will in some measure have to be based on knowledge which, in the first instance, is not given to the planner but to somebody else, which somehow will have to be conveyed to the planner."

That passage made me suspect the one-paragraph version is aiming at a strawman, but I'm not sure where.
