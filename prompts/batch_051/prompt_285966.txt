I keep turning over a disagreement I had with a friend about whether a life spent perfecting something in complete obscurity is wasted, and I have ended up stuck between a view I cannot easily refute and an intuition I cannot shake. The view is this: an achievement is good only to the extent that it benefits someone — the person who achieves it, other people, or other living things. A performance is good because it moves an audience, a proof because it helps someone understand or build, a made object because someone uses and enjoys it. Once you remove every such benefit, including any satisfaction for the maker, what remains is just spent time and arranged material, and that has no value. I am drawn to this view because it is economical, it never asks me to count goodness with no one to receive it, and it gives me one clear test for whether something impressive is actually good: trace the benefits.

But the intuition will not leave me alone. Imagine a person who spends thirty years learning to play the violin to a standard that would be called exceptional anywhere in the world, and does so entirely alone, in a soundproof room: no audience, no recordings, no students, no one ever hears a note, and no enjoyment for the player that could quietly count as a benefit. When the person dies, the skill is simply gone. The view I just described says that, absent every benefit, all those decades are worth nothing: the world is no better than if the person had sat idle, and the person has done nothing good. I find that conclusion impossible to accept. I think the world is better for having contained that private excellence, or at least that the person did something genuinely good even though no one, not even the person, got anything out of it. But I cannot point to the beneficiary, and that is exactly what my own theory demands.

This looks to me like the classic collision between a tidy theory and a stubborn intuition, but I do not want a survey. Give me the strongest case for each side: the case that my intuition is an illusion produced by imagining an audience and confusing my own admiration with real value, and the case that the benefit-only view has defined goodness too narrowly and must make room for excellence itself. Then take a definite position. Which should yield, the intuition or the theory, and what would specifically justify that move rather than merely make it comfortable? If both can survive on some reading, argue for that reading concretely. Be committed: acknowledge the costs of your verdict, but do not leave me with a menu.
