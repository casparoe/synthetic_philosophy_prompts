I'm a PhD student in ancient history (late Bronze/early Iron Age eastern Mediterranean, so the Exodus debates are kind of unavoidable), and I'd like your read on a methodological problem I keep hitting. Act as a specialist in historical method and the historiography of ancient Israel, and take your time with this — maybe start by brainstorming before you settle on an answer, and feel free to push back if you think I've got the dialectic wrong.

The background, quickly: one of the classic moves in arguing that some core of the Exodus tradition goes back to real events is what people call the criterion of embarrassment. The idea is that when a community preserves and transmits a story that makes itself look bad — here, Israel as slaves in Egypt, as rebels, as a people that keep messing up — that's a reason to think the story wasn't simply invented, because nobody fabricates traditions that undermine their own prestige or flatter their enemies. So the embarrassing material gets read as a trace of genuine memory rather than later ideological construction.

Here's where I've gotten stuck. Some of the more recent work on collective memory and identity has convinced me that groups actually do invent, elaborate, and aggressively preserve lowly or shameful origin stories, precisely because such stories do work: they make membership costly, bind people through shared abasement, create a redemption arc, or give the group a distinctive identity against its neighbors. If that's right, then the premise "they would never have made this up" looks false for exactly the kind of collective, identity-defining embarassment the Exodus story trades in. The fact that the story casts Israel as slaves isn't obviously evidence of memory; it might be evidence of a very successful piece of identity engineering.

So my question: does the criterion of embarrassment survive this development, and if so in what form? Is it dead, does it need to be repaired, or does it only retain force for a narrower class of cases (say, details that embarrass an individual leader rather than the group as a whole)? If it survives for some cases and not others, I'd want a principled way of telling them apart, not just an ad hoc line. And I'm less interested in a bibliography than in whether the underlying inference can be reconstructed at all.
