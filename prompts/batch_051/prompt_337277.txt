I'm putting together a list of objections to the view that a holobiont — a host organism together with its persistent associated microbes, such as a coral with its zooxanthellae or a mammal with its gut microbiota — is best classified as an organism rather than as a community or an ecosystem. So far I have the following six, which I take to be the standard objections:

(1) Lability of membership. The composition of a host's microbial community shifts with diet, season, age, and local environment, and even stable partnerships show considerable species or strain turnover. That fluidity is more characteristic of an ecological community than of an organism's fixed tissues.

(2) No holobiont germ line. Although some symbionts are transmitted vertically from parent to offspring, many members of a holobiont are acquired afresh from the environment each generation. Without faithful transmission of the whole community, the holobiont is not a reproductive unit that passes itself on as a holobiont.

(3) No reproductive bottleneck. Multicellular organisms pass through a single-cell stage that removes most within-organism genetic variation and aligns the fitness interests of cells. A holobiont has no analogous bottleneck, so its constituent lineages retain partially conflicting fitness interests, and the ensemble looks more like a coalition than a fused unit.

(4) Nonconstitutive dependency. Laboratory mammals can be raised germ-free and still survive and reproduce, and many particular microbial partnerships are dispensable or replaceable by different strains or species. The integration, though real, is therefore contingent rather than constitutive of a single living thing.

(5) Arbitrariness of the boundary. It is unclear which of the thousands of associated microbes are parts of the holobiont and which are environmental transients, pathogens, or merely gut contents. Without a principled way to draw that boundary, the holobiont is not a well-individuated object of the kind that "organism" is supposed to pick out.

(6) Equivocation on "organism." The holobiont view tends to slide between distinct criteria — physiological integration, reproductive or evolutionary individuality, metabolic autonomy, and so on. A holobiont may satisfy one criterion while failing others, and the conclusion that it is an organism follows only if those criteria are allowed to blur together.

I'm looking for one more objection that is genuinely different: not a rewording, special case, or combination of the six above, and ideally one that attacks the holobiont-as-organism thesis on substantially different grounds. Please explain how your proposed objection relates to the six, how it avoids merely restating one of them, and whether you think it is a good objection. Then tell me how confident you are in the objection you propose and in its claim to be distinct from the six, and identify the single point in your answer that you are least sure about.
