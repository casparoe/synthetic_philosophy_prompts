In the novel I am drafting, a charitable trust's governing deed contains a clause I want to take seriously: "Every recipient of the Foundation's bounty shall remain bound by gratitude to the Founder, and to the Founder's memory after his death, for as long as the benefit shall endure." A central character is a woman awarded a medical scholarship through a clerical error: her file was exchanged with a male candidate's, so neither the Founder nor any trustee ever intended to benefit her, and the Founder's private papers make plain that he would have regarded her as disqualified for the profession she entered with the money. The Founder is long dead, and the benefit has obviously endured—she has practiced surgery for twenty years. The present trustees, having discovered the mix-up, nevertheless honor her and ask her to speak at a dinner celebrating the Founder's memory, as a recipient bound by the clause. I want her refusal or acceptance to have the right moral grain. Does the clause reach her on its plain wording? Does it reach her on its evident purpose? What would the Founder have said if asked? What would a reasonable reader of the deed take it to mean today? If these readings diverge, which should govern—and is she bound by gratitude, or only by a document?
