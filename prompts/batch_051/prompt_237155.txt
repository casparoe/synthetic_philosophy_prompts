I want to sort out my views about moral worth — the difference between doing the right thing and doing it for the right reason. Going by my gut on particular cases:

- A colleague helps me meet a deadline because she believes it is her duty, though she feels no particular sympathy for me. I consider that morally worthy.
- Another colleague helps because seeing me stressed upsets him and he wants to relieve that feeling. I also consider that worthy, and I do not regard it as second-rate.
- A third colleague helps mainly because it will make him look like a team player. Not worthy.
- A person returns a lost wallet because he expects a generous reward. Not worthy.
- A person raised in a deeply prejudiced community believes, as a matter of principle, that members of some group are not owed help. Confronted with a member of that group in urgent distress, he finds himself unable to follow the rule he professes and helps, while later insisting he was weak and irrational. I lean toward calling that worthy, even though by his own lights he did the wrong thing.
- A person does the right thing solely because he wants to do whatever turns out to be right, without yet knowing which features of the act make it right. I want to say this is worthy, but only in a derivative way.

Is there a principle about the content of the reasons for which a person acts that captures all of these verdicts, or nearly all of them? What does that principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on — a person who helps from sympathy, but who would not have helped had he not also believed helping was his duty, so the two motives overdetermine the act? And which of my verdicts, if any, does the principle force me to give up?
