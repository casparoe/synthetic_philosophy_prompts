I keep noticing a structure that appears in three places, and I want to know whether the similarity is deep or merely verbal.

The first is Ulysses: a single agent with time-inconsistent preferences knows he will later want to steer the ship into the rocks, so he has himself bound to the mast and orders his crew not to release him no matter what he says. The second is constitutional precommitment: a body of framers, expecting future majorities to act in panic or bad faith, entrenches restrictions — a hard-to-amend constitution, enumerated rights, an independent judiciary — so that the constraint persists after the predicted preference reversal. The third is a move discussed in AI alignment: designers freeze the objective function or reward model and add tripwires or self-modification prohibitions precisely because they expect a later, more capable version of the same system to reinterpret or rewrite its own objective. The designers are, in effect, tying a future system to the mast.

The common pattern seems to be this: a principal at t0 regards the t1 self — the same person, the same polity, the same learning process carried forward — as subject to a preference or evaluative change that will look like error from t0's standpoint; the principal therefore installs constraints that are expensive or impossible for the t1 system to undo, and delegates enforcement to something less exposed to the predicted shift: the crew, the court, the monitoring process.

The cases may come apart in at least three places, and I would like them examined rather than gestured at. First, the constitutional case binds citizens who never consented, unlike Ulysses binding himself; the AI case has no consent problem, but perhaps only because the system is not a moral patient at all. Second, a frozen objective can still be gamed: the model may keep the objective intact while tampering with the sensors or with the world. Does that reveal a brittleness specific to precommitment-by-freezing-the-metric, or is it the same phenomenon as courts reinterpreting an entrenched constitution until the constraint is hollow? Third, constitutional entrenchment may really be about coordination and factional trust among contemporaries rather than diachronic self-control; if so, the constitutional case is the outlier and much of the analogy is misdirection.

So: map the three cases carefully, say what would have to be true of the AI case and the constitutional case for results to transfer between them, and name at least one concrete prediction the parallel makes that turns out false. Is this a genuine identity of structure — something like precommitment against preference drift in self-modifying systems — or am I only pattern-matching?
