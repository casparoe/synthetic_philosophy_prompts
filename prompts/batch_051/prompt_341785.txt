I've been trying to figure out where Buddhist no-self (anatta, or not-self if you think the translation does real work here) actually sits in the personal identity debate, and I keep going back and forth. Here's my working list of criteria for a view's being a theory of personal identity at all, rather than a different project that just happens to use the word 'self': (i) it takes a stand on whether the person at t1 is strictly identical with the person at t2, at least in ordinary cases; (ii) it identifies what does the persisting, if anything—a soul, a body, a stream of consciousness, overlapping memories; (iii) it says what matters for survival, or explicitly refuses to; and (iv) it has something to say about problem cases, like fission or teletransportation, instead of sitting them out.

Now the case I want to test: the anatta view, understood as the claim that a person is a conventionally designated bundle of five impermanent aggregates—bodily form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, consciousness—with no owner and no enduring subject behind them. Is anatta a theory of personal identity under my four criteria? Go criterion by criterion, and be specific about where the doctrine underdetermines the answer. Where it fails a criterion, tell me whether that's because my criteria are loaded toward the Western debate, because anatta is genuinly changing the subject, or because I've got the doctrine wrong in a way that matters. And I want the result to be tentative, not a forced verdict: if the honest upshot is that 'theory of personal identity' is a messier category than my four criteria allow, or that anatta is best read as a refusal to give one, say that plainly instead of bending the case to fit the criteria.
