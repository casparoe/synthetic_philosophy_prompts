A standing puzzle in the study of language is how natural languages remain workable while saturated with lexical ambiguity: one form serves for both a financial institution and a riverbank, and a word like pitch can mark a sound frequency, a throw, a black residue, or a sales talk. Context, intonation, tone of voice, and shared background do much of the disambiguation, which pushes the interesting question onto the residue — the ambiguity that persists and is even cultivated, rather than engineered away.

Consider a thought experiment, set today and spanning all spoken and signed languages. Starting tomorrow, every person over sixteen acquires a stable, involuntary comprehension ability with this exact scope: whenever they hear, see, or read a word or fixed phrase with more than one established lexical sense, they reliably register which established sense or senses the speaker or writer intended. If a speaker intends only one sense of sanction — to authorize rather than to penalize — the hearer is never so much as briefly drawn to the unmeant sense; if a speaker deliberately intends two senses at once, as in a pun, the hearer registers exactly those two and no others. The ability has no effect on production, so speakers must still decide word by word whether to choose an ambiguous form, and a transcript or recording remains exactly as ambiguous as before to anyone not present. It does not apply to children under sixteen, whose comprehension remains as it is now. It also excludes structural ambiguity (how the grammar groups the words, as opposed to what the words themselves mean), pronouns and other context-dependent expressions, and one-off metaphorical extensions, and it gives no access to speaker meaning beyond the words chosen, so irony, insinuation, and hinting are untouched.

I would like a reasoned prediction rather than a narrative: trace mechanisms, incentives, and constraints. Over the next thirty years, what should we expect to happen to each of the following? First, the inventory of ambiguous words and fixed phrases in languages: will it shrink, grow, or stay broadly stable, through which processes of language change and lexical innovation, and why? Second, the way speakers and writers exploit, avoid, or engineer ambiguity in communication, given that the listener's burden of resolving lexical ambiguity has dropped to zero while the burden on absent third parties and on transcripts or recordings has not. Third, the social practices that currently trade on lexical ambiguity: scripted and improvised humor, diplomatic and corporate evasion, advertising and political speech, and the face-saving indirection common in flirtation and among acquaintances. Throughout, separate the predictions that follow nearly unavoidably from the stated change from those that depend on contestable assumptions about how speakers weigh effort, what they believe about their audiences, and how much they care about their own later characterizations of what they said; where a prediction would fail if one of those assumptions were false, say so explicitly.
