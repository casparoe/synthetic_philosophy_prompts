I'm writing an essay and keep returning to *Groundhog Day* as a way of thinking through a specific philosophical worry about finite minds and whether experience can be exhausted. The worry, roughly, is that a mind with finite capacities has only a finite repertoire of possible experiences, so a sufficiently long life — or an immortal one — would eventually run through everything it could feel, think, or want and settle into bare repetition. Bernard Williams gives the best-known version of this: an endless life becomes unbearable because a finite character eventually exhausts the categorical desires that give it reasons to go on at all. I want a serious philosophical reading of the film through this lens, and I want it to work critically in both directions rather than treating the film as a mere illustration. Where does Phil Connors actually live out the exhaustibility problem — the stretch where he has enumerated the day's possibilities, manipulated or seduced everyone available, and sinks into suicidal despair? Does the later turn, when he learns piano, carves ice, and starts noticing which townspeople need help, refute the worry that the day's experiences could be used up, or does it evade it? I am struck by the ways the film cheats in philosophically interesting ways: Phil retains his memories across resets, so he is never truly living the same day twice; the accumulation of skill and reputation changes what the day affords him; and the premise hands him literally unlimited time, which is exactly what a finite life does not have. Does this mean the film shows that experience becomes inexhaustible only for a mind allowed to become a different mind, and is that a genuine answer to Williams's challenge, or a dodge? I want a reading that is faithful to the actual structure of the film and to the philosophical debate, and that ends with what the film adds to the abstract argument that the argument cannot supply on its own.
