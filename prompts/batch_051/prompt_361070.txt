I chair a seven-member search committee in my department, and a round of voting this week has left me unsure whether our procedure is coherent. The post requires both strong research and strong teaching, and our usual practice is to deliberate, then vote on each requirement separately, and finally vote on the overall recommendation.

Our last vote went like this. Four of the seven members accepted the proposition that the candidate's research is excellent, and three rejected it. Four accepted the proposition that the candidate's teaching is adequate, and three rejected it. Yet the final motion to recommend hiring failed, four votes to three. I checked the ballots, and nobody voted inconsistently: no one changed his or her mind between votes, every member who accepted both propositions voted to hire, and every member who rejected at least one voted against. The four who accepted the research proposition and the four who accepted the teaching proposition were simply not the same four people.

The upshot is that the committee collectively appears to have concluded: the research is excellent, the teaching is adequate, and the candidate should not be hired, even though our own rule requires hiring anyone who satisfies both criteria. No individual member contradicted himself or herself, but the committee seems to have contradicted itself.

I am having trouble saying what went wrong, or whether anything did. A colleague argues that only the direct vote on hiring should count, since that is the question we actually had to answer, and the criterion votes were merely preparatory. Another argues the opposite: the two criterion votes are where the committee's real commitments lie, and once a majority has accepted both requirements, a hiring recommendation should follow automatically. These two proposals yield different outcomes from the same underlying opinions, and I do not know which better represents the committee's judgment, or whether it is a mistake to think the committee has a judgment at all over and above the members' individual votes.

I would be grateful for an analysis that helps me see what our committee should do. Is the collective inconsistency genuine, or a category mistake? Is there a principled basis for privileging one kind of vote over the other? And is there any aggregation procedure that could guarantee a consistent set of collective judgments while treating every member fairly, or is some such tension unavoidable? Please spell out the principles that should guide the choice of procedure, rather than just resolving this particular tally.
