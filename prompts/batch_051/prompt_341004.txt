I run a reading group on the conceptual foundations of AI safety, and at our last meeting two of its members spent most of the evening locked in an argument; they have asked me to referee, but I was only half-following and would prefer an outside judgement. The background, as I understand it, is this. Interpretability is the project of making the internal computation of a trained model intelligible to humans. Recursive interpretability is the increasingly common practice of using one AI system as the interpreter of another, for instance to produce or evaluate explanations of a target model's features, circuits, or reasoning. Mina opened with the complaint that recursive interpretability simply defers the problem. If a model I explains a target model T, our entitlement to trust I's explanation depends on our entitlement to trust I; but I is itself opaque, so we must interpret I with another model I2, which must itself be interpreted by I3, and so on. Nothing ever grounds the chain, so recursive interpretability cannot supply a foundation for trusting any explanation (1). Ravi replied that this is only the familiar justificatory regress, and it is fatal only if one demands foundationalist certainty. We do not require a self-validating interpreter any more than physics requires a self-validating voltmeter. We validate interpreters as we validate instruments: by whether their explanations support successful interventions, by whether the features they identify still matter when ablated, and by whether they make correct predictions about what the target will do. Convergent checks across independent interpreters are justification enough, and the regress never needs to be run to infinity (2). Mina came back that this misses the force of the objection. The checks themselves must be run and evaluated, and the evaluator is either another opaque system or a human who cannot independently generate the counterexamples that would expose a plausible-but-false explanation; small, transparent interpreters are checkable but too weak to understand a larger target, while capable interpreters are as opaque as the target itself. So recursive interpretability faces a dilemma, not a solvable regress (3). Ravi rejoined that understanding does not require matching capability: a mathematician can explain why an automated theorem-prover found a proof without replicating its search. An interpreter can be smaller and more constrained than the target — for example, required to output predictions or executable summaries a human can test — and still be explanatory about a restricted subspace of the target's behaviour; agreement among several such interpreters trained on different data and architectures is real evidence. Fallibilism is enough, and the regress looks vicious only because Mina keeps demanding a self-certifying terminal interpreter (4).

I would like you to adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four numbered moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justifying each rating; say what each side is right about and where each overreaches; and then say what, if anything, both parties are missing about how to distinguish a vicious regress from a benign chain of fallible instruments. Please write your answer in British English.
