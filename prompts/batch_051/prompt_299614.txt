In my seminar on Hume we have just finished the sections on causation in the Treatise and the first Enquiry, and when I went to the secondary literature I found what appears to be a decades-long standoff. Two broad camps read the same passages and draw opposite conclusions. The traditional regularity reading takes Hume to hold that causation, as far as we can coherently think or say it, is nothing more than regular succession: experience supplies contiguity, temporal priority, and constant conjunction, and the idea of necessary connection is something the mind contributes after repeated observation. On this reading Hume denies that there is any power or necessary tie in the objects themselves, and his two definitions of cause are meant to say everything that can be said about what causation is. The New Hume reading replies that Hume is not a reductionist but a skeptical realist: he denies only that we can know or form a clear idea of the tie between cause and effect, while leaving open and perhaps taking seriously the possibility that there are hidden powers or connections in nature that lie beyond our faculties. This camp emphasizes the passages where Hume says that the fundamental workings of nature lie beyond human investigation, or that his own definitions of cause capture only the outward circumstances of causation; the traditional camp emphasizes the passages where he says that necessity lies in the mind rather than in the objects, and that we have no impression from which a genuine idea of power could be derived. Each side appears able to account for the other side's favored passages by treating them as rhetoric, irony, or loose speech, and after reading several of these exchanges I cannot see that anybody has moved.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is less about what Hume asserted in any particular sentence than about what an interpretation is supposed to accomplish: whether it should reconstruct the position Hume was most willing to assert in his own voice, or the position that best makes sense of why he thinks causal inference is rationally vindicated in ordinary life while insisting that the tie itself is inaccessible. It may also be partly verbal, with one side using cause to mean what experience can disclose and the other using it to mean whatever in nature does the producing. I would like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement about the text, a verbal dispute about terms, or a difference in interpretive methodology, or some combination? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a sharper taxonomy of the disputed passages, a formal reconstruction of the two positions, or an argument that the text is simply underdetermined between them?
