Me and a couple friends in a philosophy of mind / animal cognition reading group keep going in circles on this one, and I want a neutral diagnosis. The argument is about Morgan's canon, the old rule of thumb from comparative psychology that you shouldn't interpret an animal's behavior by appealing to a "higher" psychological faculty when a "lower" one would account for it. (So, don't say the dog reasoned about where you hide the treat if simple associative learning covers the same data.) We all basically like the canon, but one friend has built an argument that it proves way too much:

1. Morgan's canon says: if a lower psychological process can account for the behavior, don't attribute a higher one.
2. For basically any animal behavior, you can tell some lower story everytime you try — associative learning, habit, fixed action patterns, stimulus–response, and if you go low enough, a purely mechanistic or neural description.
3. So if we followed the canon strictly, we'd almost never be licensed to attribute planning, beliefs, or causal reasoning to nonhuman animals.
4. But parity: the same trick works on humans. For any human behavior (your roommate skipping the party to study, say) there's also a lower story available — past reinforcement, habit, avoiding a bad feeling, whatever.
5. So a strict canon would also strip humans of planning and beliefs, at least much of the time.
6. But that's absurd. We do plan, and we do have beliefs.
7. So either the canon is just a piece of rhetoric, or step 4 has to be blocked some other way, but than the canon isn't doing evenhanded work.

We'd like to keep step 1 and step 6 if we can, and we're open to sacrificing whatever else needs to go. Which premise or step is the culprit? Why did it look so fine? Then spell out what the main ways of responding each have to give up, and whether any of them leave the canon with actual teeth.
