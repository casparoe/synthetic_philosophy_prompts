I direct a small group in the philosophy of climate science, and for the next three years we will be working on the interpretation of multi-model climate ensembles and on how ensemble output should be translated into decision-relevant uncertainty for adaptation planning. We have one postdoc and two doctoral students; we do conceptual and methodological work, not model development, and our intended audience on one side is the climate scientists who build and evaluate ensembles, and on the other side the planners, insurers, and water managers who must act on their output. I do not want a survey of the field or a reading list. I want a defended research agenda.

The possible lines of work I am weighing include, in no particular order:

1. What an ensemble is. Is a multi-model ensemble best understood as an approximate sample from a distribution over possible models, as an instrument for exploring structural uncertainty through robustness analysis, or as something else? The answer bears on whether the ensemble mean is even a meaningful central estimate and on whether and how individual members should be weighted.

2. The independence problem. Ensemble members share code, parameterization schemes, and tuning data, so their agreement is not straightforwardly independent confirmation. Can dependence among models be represented or corrected for, and what should that do to the evidential weight of consensus across an ensemble?

3. Robustness versus performance. Should a projection that survives across structurally distinct model families be privileged even when the models hindcast less well, or should historical performance dominate model selection? What should "robust" mean if the point is to support decisions rather than merely to summarize model spread?

4. The representation of uncertainty. For adaptation decisions under deep uncertainty, should we be producing probabilistic projections, storylines, imprecise probabilities, or scenario-neutral impact functions? Under what decision structures does the choice among these actually change what a decision-maker should do, and when is it cosmetic?

5. Validation and circularity. Models are tuned against the same historical record on which they are validated, so what exactly does hindcast performance license about confidence in out-of-sample projections? Where does calibration end and circularity begin?

Which of these — or a line of work I have not listed — should we pursue first, second, and third? Please defend the ordering in a way that attends to the dependency structure among these questions: which are upstream of which, which are tractable for a group of philosophers working with climate scientists but not running models, which are neglected relative to their importance, and what evidence over the next two or three years would move a question up or down the ranking.

Please structure the response in four sections, in this order, with approximate lengths:

1. Candidate questions (roughly 300 words): lay out the open questions you are comparing, including any you would add to my list, and state in one sentence what each question turns on.
2. The ordered agenda (roughly 150 words): give the top three in rank order, each with a one-sentence justification of its place.
3. Defense of the ordering (roughly 500 words): weigh importance, tractability, neglectedness, and how much downstream work depends on each question; say explicitly what you are trading off and which criterion does the most work.
4. What would change it (roughly 150 words): name three concrete observations, results, or methodological developments in the next two to three years that would move a question up or down your list.
