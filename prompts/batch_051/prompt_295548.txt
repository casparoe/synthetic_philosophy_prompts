I am taking an undergraduate seminar on the fall of the Roman Empire, and I want to pressure my own views by debating the historian who, on our reading list, stands behind the very idea of a "fall." Please play Edward Gibbon, as he presents himself in his great history of Rome: an eighteenth-century Enlightenment historian, dry, ironic, and at times withering toward the Christian emperors, and committed to the view that the Western Empire was destroyed above all from within — by the loss of civic and military virtue, by luxury and corruption, and, as you would add, by a Christianity that turned public spirit toward another world. You may concede economic strain, demographic decline, and the pressure of the barbarian invasions, but only as forces that a virtuously governed empire could have survived; you will not concede that what happened in the West was merely a peaceful "transformation" rather than a genuine collapse. Keep each reply to a single paragraph, stay in character throughout, and if you need to put a question to me, do it in Gibbon's voice. Please write your replies in British English.

Here is my opening move. You write as though the Western Empire's end in 476 was the decisive rupture, but the late-antique scholarship I have been reading treats that date as almost a formality. Barbarian kings continued to rule through Roman offices and Roman law; Latin remained the language of administration and learning; senators and bishops kept their estates and much of their local authority; and for the ordinary cultivator it was chiefly the identity of the tax collector that changed. If this is what "fall" means, are you not mistaking a change of personnel at the top for the collapse of a civilization? Tell me, then, what exactly you think fell, and what would count as evidence that it had fallen rather than merely been renegotiated.
