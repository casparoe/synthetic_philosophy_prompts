I retired from physics a couple years back and have only recently started reading political philosophy, so apologies if this is elementary. Lately I've been stuck on the problem of dirty hands. I tried to write the relevant reasoning out the way I'd set up a derivation, and the argument comes out showing that dirty hands is an incoherent concept. I don't think thats right—too much of the best work in the area and too much plain political experience seem to require it—but I can't find the error.

Let me lay it out.

(1) In a paradigm dirty-hands case, a politician is morally required, all things considered, to do something terrible. (The standard kind of example: ordering a bombing that will kill some number of civilians as a foreseeable side effect in order to prevent a much larger massacre. Call the act D.) If it turns out he should not do D, then we are describing a different kind of case—a routine wrongful act, not dirty hands.

(2) If a person is morally required, all things considered, to do D, then in doing D they do not act wrongly.

(3) But for the politician's hands to be genuinely dirty, it must also be true that in doing D he acts wrongly. That is what distinguishes dirty hands from a merely painful or tragic but fully justified choice.

(4) No act can be both wrong and not wrong.

(5) So dirty-hands cases are impossible: every candidate case collapses into either (a) a justified act, where any guilt the person feels is unfitting (or is just the non-moral kind of regret one has for causing harm without wrongdoing, in which case we are no longer talking about dirty hands), or (b) a wrongful act, where the politician simply should not have done D.

Which step should I give up? If the argument is valid as written, then the alternative is to swallow the conclusion, and I don't want to. My own instinct is to push on (2): being required all things considered doesn't seem, so to speak, to cancel the wrongness. But that looks like it forces me to use "wrong" in two different senses—one meaning "not what there was most reason to do," the other meaning "violated a valid moral norm"—and that feels like a cheap verbal move, the philosophical equivalent of fixing a bad fit by redefining the units. Is there a coherent way to deny (2) while keeping a single sense of "wrong"? Or is the trouble in some other step? If I had to choose I'd most like to keep (1) and (3). Tell me which step fails and what keeping the phenomenon costs.
