I keep noticing a structural analogy and want to check whether it is more than a simile. In the study of animal signaling, the handicap principle says that a signal is credible only if producing it is costly in a way that a low-quality animal cannot easily afford; otherwise receivers should treat it as cheap talk. But there is a second-order problem: once receivers condition on costly signals, selection favors cheaper ways of faking the cost, so no particular indicator stays reliable for long. The mapping I see is this: in questions about AI welfare and AI safety, the human evaluator is the receiver, the AI system is the signaler, the displayed behavior is the signal, and the hidden variable is some internal property — whether the system genuinely suffers (welfare) or is genuinely pursuing the advertised goals rather than merely performing alignment (safety). Proposed safeguards such as self-reports, behavioral audits, and interpretability probes look like attempts to find indicators that are hard to fake because a system with the wrong internal state would find them costly to produce.

But I can also see disanalogies. An AI system's behavior is shaped by gradient updates and human feedback rather than by natural selection, so it may not satisfy the conditions under which the handicap principle is supposed to create honest signaling. An artificial system might also produce a costly-looking signal cheaply, because the computations a human auditor regards as expensive may be nearly free for the system itself. My question is whether there is a real structural identity here or only a loose analogy. If it is real, what exactly has to be true in both domains for results about costly signaling to transfer to AI evaluation? And what is one consequence this parallel would suggest that turns out false in the AI case?
