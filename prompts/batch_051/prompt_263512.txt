I'm a retired ecologist who's recently got into philosophy of science, and I keep running into a move that bothers me. In my old field, when someone complains that a model is full of false assumptions, the standard reply is the map analogy: a model is like a map, the map is not the territory, and a good map is good precisely because it simplifies — the Tube map is geographically hopeless but gets you across London better than a satellite photograph would. From this people conclude that the unrealistic bits in, say, an ecosystem model that treats predators as perfectly informed optimal foragers don't matter so long as the model predicts well. I've now seen Friedman make much the same point in economics — something about the realism of a theory's assumptions being irrelevant if its predictions are good.

What I'd like is an audit of how far this analogy actually carries. Please lay out the mapping explicitly: what in a scientific model corresponds to the territory, the projection, the scale, the legend, and the things deliberately left off the map. Then say where the mapping holds up and where it breaks down. Which disanalogies matter for the Friedman-style conclusion that predictive success washes out false assumptions — for instance, mapmakers know their distortions, whereas modellers often can't tell whether an assumption is a harmless simplification or is doing real work — and which disanalogies are only superficial? Finally, does the conclusion survive once those disanalogies are on the table, and is there a better analogy for what a model with unrealistic assumptions is doing? I'd be greatful if the answer could be in British English.
