I'm a novelist writing a political thriller. In the story, a decapitation strike kills most of the leadership of a large constitutional republic in a single afternoon, and the presidency passes by the order of succession to an obscure official who was meant to be the designated survivor. A character — the survivor's former constitutional-law professor — insists that the new president cannot possess legitimate authority at the very moment it matters. I want the protagonist's answer to be intellectually honest rather than patriotic, so I need to know which step of the professor's argument to reject and what rejecting it costs.

Here is the argument:

(1) A person has legitimate authority — the right to issue commands and a correlative duty on others to obey — only if that authority is conferred by a valid legal rule within a functioning legal system.

(2) The rules of a legal system are valid only while the system as a whole is generally effective: its officials and the public in fact accept and apply it, and its institutions actually operate.

(3) After the strike, the old system is no longer generally effective in any substantive sense. Most senior officials are dead, the lower levels obey from shock rather than settled acceptance, and the public has not yet accepted the survivor as lawful president.

(4) The survivor's only claim to office comes from the old system's rule of succession.

(5) Therefore, when the succession rule is triggered, the old system cannot confer valid authority on the survivor (from 1–4).

(6) If the survivor acts anyway and manages to govern, whatever authority they later have can come only from a new post-strike fact: enough officials and citizens now accept their rule.

(7) But that is the very basis on which any successful coup leader rules — de facto acceptance after taking power — and the legal pedigree adds nothing beyond its role in producing that acceptance.

(8) So either the designated survivor is a usurper with better branding, or constitutional legitimacy simply is effective power.

The protagonist wants to keep constitutional continuity real and is inclined to keep (1), that legitimate authority must be lawful, but cannot find a cheap way out. Which step is the weakest, and what does rejecting it cost for the story's deeper assumptions about legitimate government? Please end your answer with a final line of the form \answer{...} giving the number of the step you reject and a short phrase for what rejecting it costs.
