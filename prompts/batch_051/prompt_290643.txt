I am a PhD student in the philosophy of information working on data governance, and I am trying to get clear on what theory of ownership is actually presupposed by how consumer data is governed. The practice looks inconsistent. When a person signs up for a free online service, the terms let the company collect detailed information about her, infer further traits, and sell or license the resulting profiles; brokers trade these profiles as corporate assets, and firms routinely describe consumer data as something they own. Yet much of the same material is regulated as her personal data: under European data-protection law she has rights of access, correction, deletion, and portability, framed as fundamental rights rather than property rights. She cannot, in practice, simply sell her own data to a broker at its market price, and when a company misuses or loses it, the main remedies are a regulatory penalty and, for her, modest compensation—not the remedies an owner gets for theft or conversion. What conception of ownership, or of what it means for information about a person to belong to someone, does this mixed regime presuppose? Which rival views does it quietly rule out—for example, that personal data is fully ownable and alienable, that it is an inalienable aspect of personhood, or that it belongs to no one? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably, say where the existing rules leave the matter underdetermined, and indicate what would have to change in practice if one of the rival views were adopted instead. Please keep the answer under 800 words and open with a single sentence stating the implicit view you will defend.
