Write a dialogue between two moral philosophers who disagree about whether there can be genuine moral dilemmas. Let a genuine dilemma be a situation in which an agent faces two or more non-overridden moral requirements that cannot all be satisfied, so that whatever she does she violates at least one requirement, not merely incurs a foreseeable loss. One philosopher—the Defender—should argue that some such dilemmas are genuine, drawing on cases such as incompatible promises or a forced choice about which of two people to rescue. The other—the Critic—should argue that apparent dilemmas are conflicts among merely prima facie considerations, that every case is resolved into a single all-things-considered obligation, and that lingering guilt or regret need not be explained by a violated requirement. Neither side should be a strawman. The Defender should challenge the Critic to justify a unique ranking in the hardest cases, while the Critic should press the ought-implies-can principle and the agglomeration principle (if you ought to do each of two things, then you ought to do both). They should also engage the moral-remainder argument: the Defender claims that the felt need to apologize or compensate after acting rightly in a hard case is best explained by the violation of a genuine requirement, and the Critic offers an alternative explanation of that remainder. Present the dialogue as exactly five numbered exchanges, each consisting of one speech by each philosopher, with each speech one to two sentences. Use the labels Defender and Critic, write in American English, and include no introduction, conclusion, or narrative stage directions.
