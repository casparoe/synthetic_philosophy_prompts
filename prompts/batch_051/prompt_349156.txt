I have been trying to settle on an account of graded moral status, and my verdicts on particular cases are easier to state than the principle behind them. Stepping on an ant for the sensation of crushing it is wrong but only slightly; swatting a fly that has been disrupting my work is fine; using a pesticide that kills billions of insects to save a staple crop people depend on is justified; a student project that breeds and kills thousands of fruit flies with no real research question strikes me as wrong, though mildly. Causing a mouse a few minutes of pain to test a cancer drug is acceptable; doing the same to test a new shade of lipstick is not. Overriding a thermostat's set point by opening a window is not wrong in any thin sense, since there is no frustrated interest to regret. My tentative principle is that moral status tracks something like sentience or capacity for valenced experience, so humans, mice, and flies count, but flies count for very little and thermostats for exactly zero. I also want to insist on a floor: no number of flies whose interests are slightly frustrated could justify torturing one human. I suspect these commitments conflict, because a positive moral weight for flies should, at large enough numbers, eventually outweigh anything. Please tell me which principle best captures these verdicts, which verdicts it forces me to abandon, and what it says about a case I cannot decide: whether there is a large enough number of fruit flies whose brief suffering in an experiment would outweigh the benefit of a cosmetic test, or even a single human headache.
