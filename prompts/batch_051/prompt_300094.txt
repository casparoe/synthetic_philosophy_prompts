I'm putting together a historiography discussion for a graduate seminar on environmental history, and I keep returning to Braudel's preface to The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II. He distinguishes, as I read him, between a history that moves so slowly it feels almost stationary—coasts, climate, routes, crops—and a faster history of institutions, economies, and social forms, and finally the rapid, noisy history of battles, treaties, and rulers. He suggests that the second and third levels can mislead if they are detached from the first, because the deep structures set the limits within which faster-moving events take place. I don't want to rehearse the usual textbook criticisms about determinism. Instead, I want a concrete case that exposes where the scheme is genuinely unstable: a social or political outcome that looks like it belongs to event-time or institutional-time, but where a longue durée explanation and a more event-centered explanation would give sharply different answers about why it happened. Give an example of a specific outcome—say a revolution, a famine, a city's success, or a state's collapse—where the explanatory priority of the slow geographical and climatic register over the faster political register is hardest to settle, and explain what exactly is at stake in choosing between them.
