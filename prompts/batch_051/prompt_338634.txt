I'm writing a short book on whether the last ten thousand years should be told as a story of progress, and my editor keeps warning me about Whiggishness — the habit of writing the past as if it were an escalator that was always going to end at us. I need a working definition of progress that means more than "change towards whatever we happen to like at the moment." You're a philosopher of history; think carefully and, before you settle on a definition, brainstorm the features that would have to be doing the actual work.

Then run your definition through these cases, with a sentence or two on each:

- the adoption of farming, which probably made most individuals shorter, sicker and more overworked, but supported cities, writing and states
- the early industrial revolution, when life expectancy and average height fell in factory districts for a generation or two
- the spread of antibiotics
- the abolition of a chattel slavery regime that freed the enslaved into forced apprenticeship while compensating the enslavers
- the global spread of English at the cost of thousands of smaller languages
- a ruler who builds reliable roads, raises life expectancy, and runs secret prisons
- the spread of the internet

For what it's worth, I'd count antibiotics and, more reluctantly, the internet; I wouldn't count the secret-prison ruler; the farming and industrial cases seem to be both progress and its opposite depending on whose life I look at, and the language case I keep flip-flopping on.

My hunch is that "progress" gets used in two ways that normally travel together but come apart in these cases: a descriptive sense (something like greater powers, longer lives, more accurate information) and a verdictive sense (something like better all things considered). If you think that's right, give me the two definitions and say which of the seven cases change their classification between them. If you think it's one thing, defend a single definition and show me where it fails to give a determinate verdict.

Finally, the thing I actually care about: can a historian say "this was progress" about any of those cases without just congratulating ourselves for being the present? Or is Whiggishness a flaw in every progress story, not only the complacent ones? Please give the answer in British English.
