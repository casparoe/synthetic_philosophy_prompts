As a novelist researching a book about how communities remember their defeats, I keep noticing something I don't have a word for. When a people suffers a total defeat, the defeat often becomes the very center of its self-understanding rather than a memory it wants to set aside: a failed revolt is remembered as the founding sacrifice that made the people who they are; an evacuation under fire is commemorated as the nation's finest hour; a lost war fought for an ignoble cause is handed down as a defense of home and honor. The defeat is not denied or softened — it stays vivid and central — but its significance is inverted, so that military failure reads as proof of moral character, and the more complete the loss, the more sacred the story becomes. This is not simply a case of the defeated controlling the telling, and it is not ordinary consolation or revisionism, because the fact of defeat is preserved rather than erased. I wonder whether there is an established concept for exactly this pattern; if the nearest concepts fit only imperfectly, I would like to know how each falls short and which fits best, and if none fits, I would want a name and definition that mark its boundaries against those neighbors. A bare label is not really what I am after — I want the phenomenon itself carved cleanly at the edges.
