Two people in my moral philosophy seminar, Ben and Maya, got locked into an argument after class on Tuesday about Ben's habit of skipping the required Friday discussion sections to go to a peer reading group instead, which he claims teaches him more. I sat there and couldn't settle it, I've replayed it a few times since, and I genuinely can't tell whose more right. We have to write a short reflection on it for the seminar, so I'd love someone to referee it properly.

For context: the course has been doing Kant's formula of universal law, then Hare's prescriptivism and Singer's generalization argument, so the argument kept sliding between the ordinary "what if everyone did that?" and the more technical versions. Here's the argument, numbered as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Maya: You can't will a world where everyone skips required sections whenever they have something they'd rather do. If everyone actually did that, sections would stop functioning, and you'd be free-riding on the rest of us who show up.

(2) Ben: But that's a different act from mine. One person skipping doesn't change how the section functions; it only changes my own experience. You're evaluating the collective action "everyone skips," and that's a threshold thing — my individual act is below the threshold.

(3) Maya: You're reading the test as a prediction of consequences. The universal law test isn't "what would happen if everyone did it." It's about whether you can will your maxim as a universal law. Your maxim is something like "skip required sections whenever you judge an alternative is better." I don't think you can will that as a practical law, because the institution you're relying on for your grade would be undermined.

(4) Ben: But I don't have to will that maxim. My maxim is narrower: "anyone who finds the section unhelpful and has a reading group covering the material better may skip." Universalize that, and sections still exist for people who want them. No contradiction.

(5) Maya: Right, this is the maxim-loading move from the lecture. You can always add details to your maxim until the contradiction disappears, which makes the test toothless. There has to be a right level of description. If "anyone in my exact situation may skip" is allowed, everyone could smuggle in "unless it's me" clauses.

(6) Ben: But that problem is internal to your test, not a reason why my action is wrong. If skipping is wrong, it's wrong because I committed to attending when I enrolled, or because I'm treating the section as a resource I don't contribute to, or because I'm deluded about how much I'm learning. Any of those can be said without pretending a world of skippers is the thing being evaluated.

(7) Maya: The Hare-style version avoids your threshold objection, though. The test isn't an empirical prophecy at all. It's whether you can prescribe your action for all relevantly similar cases, including cases with the roles swapped — you as the instructor counting on attendance, or as a student who can't form a reading group. You couldn't sincerely prescribe "required means optional whenever it doesn't suit me," because you'd reject it from those positions.

(8) Ben: But now the test has stopped being a logical consistency check and started being a fairness judgment, which is fine — that's the real argument against me. And notice the formal version gets false positives and negatives either way: there are trivial maxims everyone can will ("everyone should tie their left shoe first") that aren't moral laws, and innocuous maxims that fail the literal generalization test ("everyone becomes a farmer" would wreck the economy, but farming isn't wrong). So universalizability needs a relevance criterion; once you supply one, the test is doing very little of the work.

They went around for a while after this, but that's the shape of it.
