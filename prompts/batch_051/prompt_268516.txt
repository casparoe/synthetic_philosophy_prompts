I spent my career as a comparative neuroanatomist, studying how brain mass scales with body mass across birds and mammals, and since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy, where I keep meeting versions of the claim that brain size, encephalization quotient, or cortical neuron count should guide judgments about the moral status of animals. That worries me. In my old field, those quantities were treated as fallible proxies: useful where behavioral evidence was thin, always hostage to method—fixation, counting rules, tissue shrinkage—and known to mislead across clades. A raven has a small brain in absolute terms but a densely packed and differently organized one, and no comparative anatomist would read its moral standing off its mass. Yet the philosophical arguments I read often seem unsure whether such measures are supposed to be the thing that matters or merely a rough sign of the thing that matters. That ambiguity looks like a genuine gap rather than a quibble over words.

I am applying to a small foundation for a six-month independent project and need a two-page proposal organized around the question: is brain size—taken broadly to include absolute mass, body-relative size, and total neuron number—morally relevant in its own right, or only evidentially relevant as a revisable indicator of the capacities that actually ground moral status? One test case I keep returning to: if two species performed indistinguishably on every behavioral assay we have, but one had twice the brain mass or neuron count of the other, should any moral difference follow? If not, then the anatomy is surplus to the morality and we should say so; if yes, then we owe an account of why a measure that tracks no psychological difference should matter. My half-formed suspicion is that the usual positions conflate three questions—which neural measure, if any, validly tracks the morally relevant capacity; why that capacity matters morally at all; and whether equal measured capacity entails equal status across very different kinds of mind—and that keeping them separate would dissolve some of the standard stalemates.

Please write the proposal: a precise statement of the question, why the available answers leave it open, and the method—in particular, why a conceptual rather than purely empirical project is the right tool, given that the empirical facts are largely not in dispute. Indicate what a useful deliverable would look like for someone actually deciding about animal research, farming, or captive care, and state honestly what would count as failure within six months. No bibliography is needed; I will add the references myself.
