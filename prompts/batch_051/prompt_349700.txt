Leopold von Ranke (1795–1886) is usually read as the historian who tried to efface himself from his own account, letting sifted and authenticated sources display the past on its own terms rather than as a vehicle for present concerns or present judgments. Later theorists pushed back: E. H. Carr argued that documents do not select themselves, since the historian always decides which facts get into the story, and Hayden White argued that even sober academic history is shaped by the same narrative choices as literary plotting.

Now suppose Ranke could be shown the following contemporary development. A historical-research group has built an automated reconstruction engine. It ingests millions of digitized early-modern sources — diplomatic dispatches, chancery minutes, account books, parish registers — transcribes them, links persons and places, flags likely forgeries and interpolations, and runs statistical models over the resulting network. The engine then writes a continuous narrative of, say, the crisis leading up to a particular war, making no distinction between important and trivial documents except what the quantitative patterns assign. Its creators claim that this finally achieves Ranke's own goal better than any human historian could: the narrative is derived from the sources alone, without a situated author selecting which documents matter or imposing a plot.

Your task is to predict, as plausibly and discriminatively as you can, what Ranke would say about this engine. Work from your understanding of his actual commitments and be willing to draw distinctions: which elements of the project would he welcome as a natural extension of his source-critical method, and which would he regard as a betrayal of it? In particular, address these questions.

1. Would Ranke accept the engine's output as history in his sense, or only as a preparatory instrument — an index, a cleaned archive, a digest — that still requires a historian's subsequent work? On what feature of the output does the answer turn: completeness, impartiality, narrative coherence, or something else?

2. Ranke is often understood to hold that the historian must first understand the particular document and the situation of the person who produced it, and only then assemble the whole. Would he see a model that never reads an author's situated perspective but only measures co-occurrences across documents as doing the same thing more reliably, or as doing something else entirely?

3. How would he reply, in light of the engine, to the later sceptical arguments of Carr and White? Would he concede to Carr that selection remains unavoidable even here — because someone decided what to digitize and which variables to count — or would he treat the machine as a neutral arbiter that escapes White's charge of emplotment? Or would he split the question in some other way?

4. Are there commitments of Ranke's — for instance, about the state, the role of individuals, or the distinctness of each epoch — that the engine would systematically obscure and that he would insist a human historian must add back in?

Your answer should be a structured prediction with reasons, not merely a summary of Ranke's known positions, and it should not rely on citing particular pages or editions. Where you think Ranke would face an internal tension, say what the tension is and how you expect him to resolve it.
