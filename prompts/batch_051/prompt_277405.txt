I keep coming back to The Prestige, the Christopher Nolan movie about two rival magicians, and I want to read it through the idea that discoveries happen twice at once — Newton and Leibniz both getting calculus, Darwin and Wallace both getting natural selection. The usual line is that simultaneous discoveries show the idea was just ready, so the priority fight is pure ego. The movie looks like a perfect fit: Borden and Angier both invent a trick called the Transported Man at roughly the same time and wreck each other over who had it first. But their versions work through totally different means — one's a hidden twin, the other's whatever the Tesla machine actually does. So does the film actually endorse the discoveries-are-inevitable message, or does it think being first still matters? Does the fact that the two tricks run on such different machinery undercut the analogy with calculus — if the same visible result comes from two totally different routes, is it even the same discovery, and what does that do to the someone-would-have-found-it-anyway argument? And if the movie does say priority matters, what does it think it matters for — naming it, owning it, getting paid, or just being the one who paid the price? I want a reading that respects both the film and the philosophical question, including the places where the movie pushes back on the tidy inevitability line. In American English please.
