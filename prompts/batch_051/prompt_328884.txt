The paradox of blackmail is normally stated as follows. One person holds an embarrassing but true piece of information about another. In the standard setup, disclosing the information would itself be lawful, and selling one's silence would itself be lawful. Each component, taken separately, seems permissible: we may reveal what is true, and we may be paid to keep a confidence. Yet joining the two into a conditional proposal—silence in exchange for payment, with disclosure as the alternative—turns the transaction into blackmail, a serious offense. A familiar line of thought, which I will call the composition argument, takes this to be incoherent: if each component is permissible and the whole is nothing over and above the two components, then the combination should be permissible as well, and the criminalization of blackmail must be unprincipled.

Now consider a change in the surrounding information economy. Increasingly, sensitive personal information is held by data brokers and platforms whose standing practice is disclosure: they publish, sell, or circulate the information, and the person concerned can obtain silence only by paying a recurring fee for deletion, suppression, or non-inclusion. Here there is no prior individualized threat; silence is retailed as a service, and disclosure is simply what happens to those who do not pay. Does the composition argument survive this development? If the argument succeeds in the classic dyadic case, does it commit us to saying that this pay-for-silence economy is likewise permissible—and if it does, is that a reason to accept the economy or a reason to suspect the argument? If the argument does not extend to the new case, identify the exact premise the development undermines (for example, the assumption that the two components can be evaluated independently, or the assumption about the baseline against which a proposal counts as coercive, or some other premise), and then state the strongest repaired version of the argument you can and say what it establishes. I am asking about the fate of the argument, not for a policy proposal.
