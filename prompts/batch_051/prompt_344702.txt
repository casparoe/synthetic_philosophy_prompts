I am taking a course on Confucian role ethics this term, and I have begun to suspect that a habit I have long practiced is either a genuine philosophical position or a bias that has acquired a more respectable vocabulary. The habit is this: when someone argues that I have a moral obligation that is not attached to any particular relationship or social role, I set the argument aside almost entirely. Last week, a classmate claimed that my purchasing decisions are morally significant in the same way my treatment of my brother is, since they affect workers I will never meet. I found myself unwilling to engage with the argument — not because I thought it was false, but because I thought it was addressed to the wrong person. By contrast, when an obligation comes attached to a role I actually occupy — daughter, student, friend, neighbor — I take it seriously even when it is costly or uncomfortable. I have come to call this my role test for ethical claims. Reading the primary texts for the course, and some secondary literature on role ethics, has made the test seem principled rather than lazy: if the self just is the sum of its roles, then perhaps a demand that is owed by no role-bearer to any role-relevant other is not merely weak but ill-formed. And yet the test is doing a great deal of work in the background, screening out most impartial demands before I have really examined them, and it rather conveniently flatters my preference for obligations that are concrete and bounded. I would like an honest assessment. Is the role test a defensible epistemic shortcut, or a bias disguised as a method? Is there a corrected version — say, heavily discounting role-free demands rather than ignoring them, or treating role-based duties as defaults rather than as the whole of obligation — that would retain whatever is good in the habit without sealing me off from moral claims that ought to reach me?

In your reply, please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
