Several public health systems decide which treatments to fund using cost-effectiveness analysis built around the quality-adjusted life year, or QALY. A QALY is calculated by taking each additional year of life that a treatment produces and multiplying it by a health-related quality weight: a year in full health receives a weight of 1, a year judged half as good as full health receives 0.5, and a year in a state considered worse than death receives a negative weight. The resulting products are summed across all the patients the treatment would affect, and the treatment is compared with its alternatives on a cost-per-QALY basis; in the most explicit versions of the practice, there is a rough cutoff above which a treatment is normally denied funding.

Many of the same systems, however, behave differently at the margins. When a single identifiable patient is dying now, clinical practice and public sentiment often push toward spending very large sums on that patient, even when the same money would produce far more QALYs if spent on less visible patients or on prevention. Some systems also maintain separate funds for treatments for rare diseases whose cost per QALY would sit far above the ordinary cutoff. There is, then, an apparent inconsistency: the formal rule treats the value of a health outcome as something that can be multiplied and then summed across lives, while the exceptional cases seem to treat certain patients, or certain kinds of loss, as having a claim that simply outweighs the summed total.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of value as precisely as you can. Distinguish what the QALY rule strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Is the framework committed to an additive, multiplicative, or lexically ordered structure of value, or is it underdetermined among several rival structures? Do the rescue-the-identifiable-patient exceptions and separate rare-disease funds presuppose a different or inconsistent theory, or can they be reconciled with the QALY framework within one coherent view? And what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed theory of value were replaced, for example, by a view on which no amount of moderate benefit to many may outweigh a severe loss to one, or by a view on which a year lived at low quality cannot be traded at a constant ratio against a year lived at high quality? Finally, say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: where more than one defensible theory would license the same funding pattern, and what further information would distinguish those theories.
