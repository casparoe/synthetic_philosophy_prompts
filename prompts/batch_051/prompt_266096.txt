This fall I'm in a medieval philosophy seminar and we've been picking through Abelard's claim that the sin is entirely in the consent, not in the act. As I understand him, the temptation or inclination isn't yet sin, and the outward deed is basically indifferent; what makes a person guilty is a free, conscious consent of the will to do something they take to be wrong, and the deed that follows adds nothing to the guilt itself (it may change what penalty others think is owed, but not the state of the soul). I find the view weirdly attractive, but a friend pressed me with a modern problem and I can't shake it. Social psychology over the last few decades has made a lot of cases where people act harmfully—discriminatory behavior, biased judgments, the usual microaggression-type stuff—through implicit biases: fast, automatic dispositions that operate without any conscious intent, often in direct tension with the person's explicitly endorsed values. There's no moment of temptation, no inner yes to a bad act, sometimes no awareness even afterwards, and the behavior still comes out. If Abelard is right that the sin is in the consent not the act, where does all this go? Is it a vice but not a sin? Does that amount to letting the person off the hook? Or does the framework have to loosen what "consent" means until it stops doing the work Abelard wanted it to do?

I'm not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or an empirical review of the implicit association test; I'm asking weather the conceptual structure of his position holds up against this specific development. Act as a professional philosopher who's read Abelard sympathetically but is willing to say the argument is dead. Before committing, brainstorm two or three candidate repairs or reinterpretations and sort out how much work the distinction between sin (guilt before God / in the soul) and ordinary blameworthiness is doing.
