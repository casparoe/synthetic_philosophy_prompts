I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on animal minds, and I keep getting stuck on the scale argument for insect welfare. The rough idea is that even if the chance any given insect is sentient is pretty low, there are something like a quintilion insects alive at any moment, so the expected number of sentient individuals is enormous—and that's supposed to make insect welfare a dominant moral priority. But the whole thing runs on expected value reasoning, and lately I've been reading the decision-theory stuff on Pascal's mugging and fanaticism: cases where you multiply a tiny probability by a huge value and the EV calculation just runs away with you. The standard fixes—bounded utility functions, penalizing low-probability hypotheses, skeptical priors over radical scenarios—all seem aimed exactly at arguments with this shape. So my question is whether the scale argument actually survives those fixes. Structurally it looks like a Pascal's mugging: tiny probability, huge number, done. But maybe it's different because the probability of insect sentience isn't as tiny as the mugging's, or because the value per individual is finite and moderate, or because a theory of consciousness constrains the probability instead of leaving it as a free parameter. Which premise does the anti-fanaticism move bite on, and can the argument be restated in a form that survives it? Or is its real force just an artifact of unbounded expected-value reasoning?
