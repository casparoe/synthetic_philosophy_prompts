I'm drafting a statement of principles for a working group on continuity-of-government planning and want a consistency check on my own commitments before circulating it. I hold all of the following:

(a) In a decapitation scenario, the designated survivor's authority is legitimate only insofar as it is exercised to restore and preserve the constitutional order, not to advance any political program.
(b) Unpostponable decisions about the use of force, public finances, and external relations may nevertheless be unavoidable, and the judgment of what counts as necessary cannot be settled in advance by fixed rules.
(c) Democratic legitimacy requires that such authority be temporary and revocable, with the restoration of ordinary electoral politics as the fixed endpoint.
(d) Yet no fixed timetable for that restoration can be written down, because the emergency's duration is unknowable and elections can only follow the state's survival.
(e) The general rules of succession should be publicly established in advance, since only public pre-authorization can make emergency power legitimate rather than a form of usurpation.
(f) The operational details, including the identity of the designated survivor, must remain secret, since disclosure would make the arrangement a target and invite pre-emption.
(g) I hold that the familiar worry about such plans — that commitments made in calm times will not bind people in a crisis — gets things backwards, since binding when it is hardest to keep is exactly what the plan is for.

Which of these commitments are in genuine tension with one another, which merely appear to be, and how should the tensions be ordered by severity? For each genuine tension, identify the least costly revision and what it sacrifices. I want to know where the real fault line lies rather than receive a list that treats every pair as equally problematic.
