Two of my cousins got into an argument at dinner about a proposal in their county. The county can either approve a big industrial plant that will bring jobs now but leave the groundwater messed up forty years from now, or turn that land into a park. Here's the wrinkle, though: the plant would also change who lives in the county over the next few decades — different workers move in, different families form — so the people alive in forty years under the plant plan would mostly not exist under the park plan. That's where the argument went off the rails.

Dan argued that if the people stuck with the contaminated water wouldn't have existed under the park plan, then the plant doesn't make anyone worse off than they otherwise would've been, because their alternative is never existing and they still have lives worth living. So there's no victim, and the park plan isn't actually better for anyone. Maya said that can't be right, because it would make any decision that shifts who gets born immune to criticism no matter how lousy the result, and at some point you have to compare futures rather than just individual complaints. Dan came back that "worse" has to be worse for somebody, and morality is about what you do to actual people, not about scoring possible worlds in a spreadsheet. Maya said so a couple who could have a healthy child but knowingly has one with a painful genetic condition gets a pass, since the child can't complain compared to not existing? That seems wrong. Dan said it might be a sad choice, but it's not a harm to anyone who exists, and a county owes actual residents, not merely possible ones. Maya admitted the no-victim point has real force against saying the plant harms its own future people, but she said all that proves is that we need a way to evaluate choices where the identities shift, not that such choices are fine: the county still picked a future full of kids with health problems over a future full of healthy ones. Dan said calling one future "nicer" from the outside is exactly the spreadsheet mistake, because if he harms no person, he's done nothing wrong.

I couldn't sort it out. Walk me through their points one by one: who's right about which specific move, where are they talking past each other, what if anything are they both missing, and in the end is the fact that different people would exist a genuine reason to think the plant is less wrong, or just a distraction? Is Dan right that a choice which harms no individual can't be wrong?
