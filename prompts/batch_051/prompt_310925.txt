A textbook I was sent for possible adoption summarizes the Gould and Lewontin spandrels critique roughly like this, and I want to know how much I can trust before I assign it:

"Gould and Lewontin showed that the adaptationist programme is a Panglossian mistake. They argued that most traits of organisms are not adaptations at all but are more like the spandrels of San Marco — architectural leftovers that simply have to be where they are because of how the dome is built. They concluded that natural selection is only one relatively minor force among many, that explanations appealing to developmental constraint or random drift are usually to be preferred, and that adaptationist just-so stories should be rejected rather than tested."

My instinct is that this is stronger than what I remember of the paper, but I'd rather not rely on a fuzzy memory. Which parts would a working philosopher of biology accept, which would make them wince, and what is the corrected version of each? I'd especially like to know whether the final claim — that just-so stories should be rejected rather than tested — actually follows from the accurate parts of the summary, and what the authors did and did not claim. And please separate, where you can, what is now settled in the field from what is still genuinely constested, so I don't present a live debate to my students as consensus.
