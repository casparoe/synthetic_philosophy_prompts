My ethics professor summarized Scanlon's contractualism like this: an act is wrong if it would be forbidden by any principle that no one could reasonably reject, where "reasonably" just means the principle has to be justifiable to each person on the basis of their own reasons. The payoff, he said, is that you can't aggregate: you can't add up lots of small benefits to different people to outweigh one big harm to someone else. So for Scanlon you can't justify torturing one prisoner to prevent a thousand migraines in other people, however good the sum looks. But then my professor said the view is also stuck with numbers never mattering anywhere, because each person's rejection is a veto, so a lifeguard who can save one drowning swimmer or five has no more reason to save the five than the one. He called this the point where contractualism stops being anti-utilitarian and becomes just obviously false. I have a nagging feeling the last step is too quick — I thought the reasons involved in reasonable rejection had to be something like generic reasons, not just each person's private stake, and I also thought Scanlon had tried to say something about why the five can be saved in rescue cases where the claims are the same kind. Is the professor's summary fair? Please seperate what's accurate, what's a common simplification, and what Scanlon would explicitly deny, and then say whether the concluding criticism — the lifeguard case as a reductio — survives the corrections.
