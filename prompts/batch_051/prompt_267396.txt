Here are the criteria I was taught for an act to count as a Christian baptism: the candidate is washed or immersed in water; the minister uses the prescribed Trinitarian words; the minister intends to do what the church does in baptizing; and the candidate is either a professing believer or, if below the age of reason, is presented by sponsors who profess the faith on the candidate's behalf.

Now consider this case. A nurse attends a dying premature infant whose devout parents fear the child will die unbaptized. The nurse is a convinced atheist: she does not believe the rite does anything to the child, but she knows the parents will regard the child as baptized if water is applied and the words are said. To comfort them, she pours water on the infant and says the prescribed words with deliberate exactness. Asked afterward what she intended, she says she intended the outward action the church prescribes, and intended the parents to believe their child had been baptized, but did not intend any supernatural effect, any change in the child, or any real membership in a church.

Did she baptize the child? Go through the criteria one by one, saying where each is satisfied, unsatisfied, or indeterminate; where the criteria leave the question open, say exactly what is left open. Then assess whether the case shows that the criteria themselves need revision, and whether the verdict shifts if baptism is understood as a symbol, as an act valid by the rite itself, or as a cause of grace. In particular, say whether the verdict would change in a tradition that rejects infant baptism and requires the candidate's own conscious profession, and whether the fourth criterion does real work or merely restates a prior theological commitment.
