At a recent workshop on distributive justice, a colleague ended a debate with a one-liner, and I would like to have it taken apart rather than left to do that work. We were discussing how a fixed health budget should be allocated; the proposal on the table was broadly egalitarian. My colleague replied, in substance: "Harsanyi's theorem already settles the formal part — the social welfare function is a weighted sum of individual utilities; after that it is only a question of choosing the weights." I want to set the policy dispute aside and ask the more general question. The theorem, as it is usually glossed, says roughly this: if each individual's preferences and society's preferences each satisfy the expected-utility axioms, and society is indifferent between two options whenever every individual is indifferent between them, then the social welfare function can be written as a weighted sum of the individual utility functions, up to a constant. For present purposes, treat that glossed statement as the content under discussion, not Harsanyi's papers. Take the sentence "the social welfare function is a weighted sum of individual utilities; after that it is only a question of choosing the weights" and disassemble it. What distinct precise claims could it be making, from the most modest to the most ambitious? For each reading, say whether it is a purely formal remark about a representation theorem, a substantive normative claim, or something in between; which readings actually follow from the theorem as glossed and which require further assumptions — about the comparability and scaling of utility, the sign and source of the weights, whose preferences are counted, or what it is for a group to have preferences at all; and which readings, if any, would actually license dismissing an egalitarian allocation without further argument. I want a menu of readings with verdicts, not a summary of Harsanyi.
