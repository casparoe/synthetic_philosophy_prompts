I work as a senior analyst on the staff of a U.S. senator who sits on the Foreign Relations Committee. Two or three times a month, a draft memo or floor statement lands on my desk that moves from a historical analogy to a concrete decision: "This is Munich again," against negotiating after an adversary has seized territory; "This is Vietnam," against a limited deployment; "This is a Thucydides trap," for the claim that a rising power and an established power are nearly bound to end up at war, so we should deter rather than accommodate. My job is to screen that analogy before it reaches the senator, who will often repeat it in public; my chief of staff signs off on my recommendation. I have about an hour per memo, no research staff, and the product is a one-page note that clears the analogy, demotes it to an illustration, or kills it. I am not looking for generic critical-thinking advice about correlation and causation; I need something tied to the specific ways these historical arguments go wrong.

Please write me a reusable procedure for deciding when a historical analogy in a policy memo is doing legitimate inferential work and when it is misleading. The procedure should require the user to state exactly what decision the analogy is being used to justify; to lay out the causal chain in the historical case, link by link; to check which links are actually present in the current case; to strip loaded labels such as "appeasement" out of the claim and see whether the inference survives in neutral language; and to separate two jobs an analogy can do — serving as evidence that a mechanism is at work now, versus serving as a standing decision rule, such as the rule that one should never negotiate under threat. It should also force the user to notice when the episode is doing rhetorical work rather than evidential work, borrowing emotional force from a familiar name. Where a step calls for a judgment the procedure cannot make, say so and hand the question to a person rather than pretending the step is mechanical.

Please structure the response as follows. Section 1, "The procedure": a numbered checklist I could run in an hour, with each step ending in a yes/no/flag outcome, explicit instructions on where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands off to human judgment; roughly a page. Section 2, "What each step catches": one or two sentences per step identifying the specific failure mode of Munich, Vietnam, or Thucydides-trap reasoning that the step is aimed at; up to a page. Section 3, "Worked applications": run the full procedure on two memos — one arguing from Munich against negotiating after a seizure of territory, and one arguing from the Thucydides trap that conflict with a rising power is likely and that accommodation rather than deterrence would be the mistake — about half a page each, and make at least one of them come out with a verdict the senator will not want to hear. Section 4, "Limits": the cases the procedure is likely to mishandle, the steps that are disguised historical judgment, and the conditions under which I should stop trusting it; half a page.
