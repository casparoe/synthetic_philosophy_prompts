In my populism and democracy course we keep talking about how populist politicians claim to speak for 'the people' against some corrupt elite, and basicaly the phrase 'the will of the people' gets thrown around like it settles things. Last week in seminar a classmate was defending the 2016 EU referendum result and said, basically, that 'the will of the people must be respected,' and that anyone still criticizing the outcome is being anti-democratic. The professor raised an eyebrow and said 'the will of the people' is doing a lot of work in that sentence, but we never actually unpacked it, and now I cant stop thinking about it.

I want to bring a clear list to the next meeting. What are the different precise claims that slogan could be making? Like, is it a claim about aggregating everyone's actual preferences, and if so does it run into Arrow-type problems or the awkwardness that millions of eligible people didn't vote and lots of the majority had different priorities? Or is it something more like Rousseau's general will, the common good people would choose if they voted as citizens rather than as private individuals? Or is it not really a claim about what anyone wants at all—more like a story that constructs 'the people' as one body with one voice, so there's something to point at afterward? Could you go through the main readings, say for each whether it's trivially true, plausible but contested, or just false, and then say which one actually supports my classmate using it to shut down criticism of the referendum?
