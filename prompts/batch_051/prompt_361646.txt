I joined my hospital's ethics and AI review committee a few months ago, and we are now evaluating a vendor's machine-learning tool that assigns discharge priority and recommends follow-up intensity. Our discussions keep derailing in the same way: someone tests the system against a case it mishandles, someone else proposes a principle the system should honor, and a third person objects that the principle is just a litigator's preference dressed up as ethics. Before we vote, I want the full specification of what an adequate theory of ethical reasoning in a machine would have to get right for a role like this. Please write the desiderata, not a survey of positions.

In particular, give me:

- the types of cases the theory must handle correctly, chosen so that they pull apart naive decision rules — for example, a case where maximizing expected benefit and treating patients as equals diverge, and a case where the correct response is for the machine to refuse to decide and hand the matter to a human;
- the distinctions a theory must not collapse, with the reason each matters: doing the right thing versus being a responsible agent versus being blameworthy; ethical reasoning versus mere compliance with a stated policy; treating a value as a side constraint versus as something to be promoted; uncertainty about the facts versus uncertainty about the values; and any others you would insist on;
- the empirical and design constraints the theory cannot contradict — for instance, what is known about automation bias and operator complacency, and the requirement that the system's decisions be explainable to the clinicians who remain accountable for them;
- for each requirement, the case or consideration that makes it a genuine requirement rather than an engineering convenience, a regulatory artifact, or one camp's preference;
- a short marking of which requirements are non-negotiable for any adequate theory and which are defensible only from within a particular ethical outlook;
- and, briefly, which single requirement does the most to exclude the usual approaches, and how rule-based, straightforwardly consequentialist, and human-approval-trained systems fare against it.

Finally, because I expect genuine disagreement on the committee, tell me what would change your mind: which argument, case, or empirical finding would lead you to add, drop, or re-rank a requirement, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail. I am not asking for a reading list or for citations; I want the reasoning worked out from the inside.
