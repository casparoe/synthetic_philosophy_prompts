Something's been bugging me. The way countries handle statehood looks inconsistent. Kosovo declared independence in 2008, most Western countries recognize it, Serbia and Russia don't, and because of that it can't get into the UN. Taiwan has its own government, army, currency, and passports, but almost no one formally recognizes it and it's been like that for decades. Somaliland has controlled its territory, run elections, and issued its own currency since the early 90s, but no UN member recognizes it, so on most maps it doesn't exist. Palestine is a non-member observer state at the UN and a big chunk of countries recognize it, but it's not a full member.

I'm a software engineer, so I keep reaching for a distributed-systems way of thinking about it: a node counts as part of the network when enough peers accept it, and without that acceptance it's not really in the network even if it responds to pings. But that doesn't quite fit either, because some of these places act like states irregardless of what the rest of the system says. So I can't tell whether recognition is doing the work of creating statehood or just reporting that statehood is already there.

The actual practice seems to presuppose some theory of what a state is, but I can't figure out which one, and it looks like the system wants it both ways depending on the case. What theory of statehood does this recognition practice presuppose? Is that theory coherent, or is the practice underdetermined and just running on precedent and politics? And what would actually have to change in how countries, the UN, and courts treat these cases if you swapped in the other theory?
