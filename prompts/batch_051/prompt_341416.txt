I am a retired research biologist, and since leaving the laboratory I have been reading my way through the philosophy of biology. A conversation with a former colleague who spent his career as a lichenologist unsettled a piece of vocabulary I had used without thinking for decades. He insisted that a lichen is not one organism but a small ecosystem, and I found I could not produce a criterion of organism that settled the argument without, somewhere along the way, dismissing a case I wanted to keep. In my former life I would have reached for genetic uniformity, physical contiguity, reproductive unity, or physiological integration, but each of these now seems to pull in a different direction as soon as one moves away from ordinary animals and plants.

I would like a working definition of 'organism' — or, if you think the better concept is 'biological individual,' say so and defend the substitution — that can be applied to the following cases without being adjusted case by case. I take these to be clear organisms: a single gray wolf; a single Escherichia coli; an adult human being. I take these to be clearly not organisms: a quartz crystal; a detached human heart; a flock of starlings. The cases I genuinely cannot adjudicate are a lichen; a Portuguese man o' war, which field guides treat as one animal even though it is composed of specialized polyps; an ant colony; an aspen grove in which thousands of trunks share one root system and are genetically identical; an adult human together with its resident gut microbes, the so-called holobiont; and the aggregated slug stage of a cellular slime mold, when formerly free-living amoebae merge into a single coordinated body.

Please propose a definition, run it through all twelve cases, and tell me where it yields a classification you would defend against a skeptical colleague and where it yields no determinate answer. Say honestly whether you would need to revise the definition after seeing the cases, or whether some of the clear cases should themselves be reclassified. Finally, address whether a single definition of 'organism' can do the work the word is asked to do across physiology, development, ecology, and evolutionary theory, or whether the honest conclusion is that the word covers several related senses that ought to be distinguished.
