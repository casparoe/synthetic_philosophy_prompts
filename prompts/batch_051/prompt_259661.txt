After a long research career in the physical sciences, I have been spending my retirement on analytic philosophy, and one of the puzzles I keep returning to is Fitch's paradox of knowability. As I understand it, the paradox starts from the verificationist principle that every truth is knowable: if a proposition p is true, then it is possible that p is known. Fitch's argument then takes any unknown truth, a proposition of the form p and no one knows that p, and asks us to consider the possible situation in which that very conjunction is known. Since knowledge distributes over conjunction, knowing the conjunction would require knowing each of its conjuncts: knowing that p, and knowing that no one knows that p. But knowledge is factive, so knowing that no one knows that p entails that no one knows that p, contradicting the first conjunct. The principle that every truth is knowable therefore appears to collapse into the claim that every truth is actually known.

I would like to work through a variant in which the relevant attitude is weakened from knowledge to rational belief. Let B(p) mean that p is rationally believed by an ideally coherent agent, or, if you prefer, that p is rationally permissible to believe, and retain the principle that every truth is rationally believable: if p is true, then it is possible that B(p). Grant B the same conjunction-distribution property the original argument requires, so that B(p and q) entails both B(p) and B(q). However, do not grant factivity, since even rational beliefs can be false. Does the Fitch argument still go through when B is substituted for K throughout? If it breaks down, locate the exact inference that fails. Then consider what added constraints on the belief operator would be needed to restore a Fitch-style collapse: for instance, a consistency condition forbidding an agent from believing both a proposition and its negation, or some form of positive or negative introspection. What I most want to know is whether the contrast is deep or merely technical: does a verificationist who retreats from knowability to rational believability genuinely escape the paradox, or only sidestep one line of the proof?
