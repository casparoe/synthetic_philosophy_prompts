I've come to accept the view that statements about undetermined future events — the stock example being tomorrow's sea battle — are neither true nor false at the time they are made. On this picture the future is genuinely open, bivalence fails for such statements, and they acquire a truth value only when the relevant events settle the matter. Set aside the familiar fatalist worry that assigning truth in advance would make the future inevitable; I am not moved by it. What I want is a systematic account of what would actually count against this position. In particular: (a) the retrospective practice, once the time has come, of saying that an earlier prediction was true or that the person who made it was right — does this require the earlier statement to have had a truth value all along? (b) The smooth embedding of future-tense statements under negation, conjunction, and quantification, and their role in practical deliberation, where we seem to rely on their behaving classically. (c) The asymmetry the view appears to require between a fixed past and an open future — what would count against that asymmetry itself? For each candidate defeater you consider, say what the position commits me to that the rival view (future-tense statements are already true or false) does not commit me to, and rank the defeater as decisive, merely awkward, or easily absorbed by a refinement of the view, noting which a committed defender should concede counts against them. Finally, assess whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a defect for a metaphysical position of this kind or simply the price of stating it precisely.
