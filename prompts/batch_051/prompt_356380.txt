I am writing a novel in which the protagonist is warmly approved by the society she lives in and yet is slowly overtaken by a private conviction that her actions have been inexcusable. I have been reading Adam Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments for the moral psychology, and two strands in his account appear to conflict; I would like to know whether they can be reconciled, and at what cost.

The first strand appears in Smith's treatment of the judgment of propriety. He explains our judgment of another person's feelings as a process in which an actual observer imaginatively places himself in that person's situation and notices what feeling arises in himself; when this sympathetic feeling corresponds to the feeling the other person displays, the observer approves of that displayed feeling as proper, and when it does not correspond, he disapproves. On this account, moral judgment about the sentiments of others looks like a comparison between two actual feelings: the spectator's imagined feeling and the agent's displayed one.

The second strand appears later, in Smith's treatment of conscience. There he argues that the standard by which a person judges her own conduct is not the actual approval or disapproval of those around her, because real spectators can be ignorant, partial, or dazzled by wealth and eminence. The relevant tribunal is an impartial spectator: an imagined judge who is fully informed, free of distorting passion, and able to view the person's conduct from a consistent point of view. This internal tribunal can condemn conduct that every actual spectator applauds and can acquit conduct that every actual spectator condemns.

My question is this. If approval of others' sentiments is grounded in the felt correspondence between an actual spectator's sympathy and an actual agent's emotion, and if conscience can in principle contradict every actual spectator's verdict, then what exactly is Smith's impartial spectator, and where does its authority come from? I would like the response to lay out the strongest available reconciliations — for example, a distinction between the causal origin of conscience and the standard of correctness it applies, or a distinction between being approved and being worthy of approval — and then say which reading does the least violence to Smith's text and which readings amount to special pleading. I am less interested in how to resolve the character than in whether Smith can consistently hold both positions.
