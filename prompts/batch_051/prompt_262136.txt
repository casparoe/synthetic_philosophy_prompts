Since my last piano student moved away and I began playing only for myself, I have been unable to settle a question about the value of unobserved accomplishment. Last month I spent weeks learning a difficult étude; no one has heard it, no one has been taught through it, and it will most likely never be performed for another person. I nonetheless find it hard to accept that the achievement was worthless. I tried to formulate that conviction as an argument, and I want to know whether the argument survives scrutiny.

(1) An achievement is excellent to the degree that it is a difficult, skillful performance of an activity worth doing. (2) What makes an excellent performance good is the exercise of skill and effort itself, not any further benefits the performance brings about. (3) If something is good in virtue of intrinsic features of that performance, then its goodness does not disappear when the performance is unobserved and benefits no one else. (4) Therefore, a difficult and skillful performance that no one else sees and that causes no further benefits is still good.

I keep worrying that step (2) trades on an ambiguity between being good for the performer and being good, period, but I am not sure that is the only weak point, or whether the argument is valid if that ambiguity is removed. Could you work through it step by step? Is the argument valid? If not, which step or steps fail, and what is the strongest corrected version of the relevant step; does the conclusion then follow? Please write your answer in American English.
