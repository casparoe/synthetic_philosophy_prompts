After forty years in physics I retired and started reading philosophy. Last month I went back to Wigner's old 1960 paper The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences, the one adapted from his Courant lecture. There's a passage I keep coming back to, in the part where he runs through examples, where he points to the role of complex numbers in quantum mechanics. The wave function is complex, and he treats the fact that the mathematics uses complex rather than real numbers as something that calls for explanation—a kind of miracle, on a par with the existence of laws of nature at all. Im paraphrasing from memory so dont hold me to exact wording. A philosopher friend keeps using the phrase "unreasonable effectiveness" to push back on the idea that mathematics is just a human invention or a modeling tool. My question: what are the distinct precise claims that slogan could actually be making? I want a menu, from the trivial (mathematics is useful, which nobody denies) to the radical (the universe is mathematical, or the math we have was somehow bound to fit the world). For each reading: state it precisely, say whether it's a platitude, substantive but defensible, or false, and then tell me which reading the complex-number passage actually needs if Wigner's miracle is to be a real explanatory problem rather than just an expression of awe. Also say which reading a working physicist would have to accept to call the effectiveness of mathematics unreasonable in a scientifically interesting sense.
