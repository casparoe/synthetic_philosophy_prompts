I'm a PhD student fillling in for the regular instructor in a second-year undergrad epistemology class, and the session is on pragmatist theories of truth — Peirce and then James, right at the spot where they actually start pulling apart. The students are used to thinking of truth as correspondence to reality (their words) and will turn up assuming the pragmatists are the relativists who think a belief is true if it makes you feel good or helps you get what you want. I don't want to spend the whole hour doing damage control, so I need a plan for the order of ideas: what has to be in place before the Peirce/James disagreement even becomes visible — the pragmatic maxim as a way of making ideas clear, belief as a habit of action, the long-run community of inquirers — and what can wait until later. I'd especially like help with a single running example that keeps Peirce's picture of truth as the opinion inquiry would settle on in the long run from collapsing into James's picture of truth as something a belief earns through the difference it makes in experience.

And I want a misconception map: which wrong readings will show up in what order, and at what point in the sequence should I head each one off — for instance the cheap 'true means useful' reading that often appears right after they read James, versus the even cruder 'true means useful to me' reading that's probably already in the room. What should I check for at the end to know they've got the actual distinction rather than a bumper-sticker version of it, and what should I deliberately leave out for a first pass and tell them I'm leaving out? If I'm honest I still wobble on whether Peirce would have treated James's view as a friendly amendment or a genuine mistake, so help me teach it in a way that doesn't rush them into a false reconciliation.
