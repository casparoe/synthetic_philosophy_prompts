I cover the courts for an ideas-and-science outlet, and I've been follwing a judge who keeps getting defamation suits about star ratings, reviews, and analyst forecasts. She's now got a new one on her docket, and I want to figure out how she'll rule before the hearing by reverse-engineering the rule she's actually been applying.

Her recent track record, as best I can reconstruct it:

1. A wine critic called a sommelier's list "a scam" and called him "incompetent." She ruled it protected. Her line was that no reasonable reader of a wine review takes "scam" as a factual allegation of fraud.

2. A little research shop rated a biotech "underperform," said it expected the phase 3 trial to fail, and called the CEO "more of a hype man than an operator." The CEO sued over the hype-man line. She ruled it protected: an unverifiable judgment about style, with the rest being forward-looking investment opinion.

3. A safety nonprofit gave a car seat 2/5 and wrote that "in our testing, the harness failed to stay latched in a 25-mph frontal collision." The manufacturer showed the harness never failed in the nonprofit's own test logs. She ruled it actionable: a rating isn't a shield for a false claim about a concrete, checkable test result.

4. A home-services platform gave a contractor 1.5 stars and said the score rested on "14 customer complaints alleging shoddy work." The contractor showed the complaints didn't exist or belonged to a different firm. She ruled it actionable: "14 complaints" is a precise, checkable factual assertion.

5. A patient wrote on a doctor-review site that "Dr. M is the most careless physician I've ever met," with one star. She ruled it protected, as classic subjective hyperbole.

You can read that as "opinions are fine, specific false facts are not," but I keep wondering where an unstated factual basis falls.

The new case: a nursing-home rating nonprofit gives a facility one star for "staffing quality." There's no narrative and no explanation of methodology; the score is computed from a private database of reported staffing hours. The facility sues and shows the database badly understates its staffing, and it actually exceeds state minimums. The nonprofit says the star rating is an editorial judgment, not a factual assertion, and argues there's no particular sentence in the publication that the facility can point to as false.

So predict her ruling if she's being consistent with herself. Which earlier case is the closest analog: the car-seat case, the contractor case, or maybe the wine critic? What competing rules about ratings and facts fit her five rulings equally well, where exactly do they come apart on this one, and what future case would cleanly seperate them? I'm more interested in her reasoning than the outcome, and don't bother with case law citations.
