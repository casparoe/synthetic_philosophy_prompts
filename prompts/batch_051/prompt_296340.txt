'The basic move is this. In the natural attitude, we go around taking the world to be just there, mind-independently, without raising any questions about it: tables, other people, causes, the whole lot. The epoché is Husserl's way of putting that taken-for-granted world out of play — you suspend your ordinary commitment to the world's existence, you put it in brackets. It's not exactly Cartesian doubt, and Husserl is careful to say you are not denying anything, but the practical upshot is similar: you treat the world as if it were a claim you have simply stopped endorsing. Once you do, the world is still there for you, only now it's there as something you experience and mean rather than something you're asserting. What the phenomenological reduction then examines is that leftover, and the leftover turns out to be your own stream of experience along with the ways that stream is directed at its objects. So the method is, in effect, a disciplined introspection of a private mental sphere, and the whole point is that Husserl shows the natural attitude to be a prejudice that philosophy should leave behind.'
