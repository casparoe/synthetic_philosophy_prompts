I've been trying to pin down exactly what the problem of logical omniscience does to one of the standard epistemic logic puzzles, and I want you to work it through with me. Take the muddy children puzzle: n children, some number k of them have muddy foreheads, each can see everyone else's forehead but not their own, and the parent announces that at least one child is muddy. In the usual telling, if exactly k children are muddy, they all raise their hands on the k-th round of the parent asking "do you know whether you're muddy?", and the proof quietly assumes the kids are perfect reasoners — their knowledge is closed under arbitrarily nested "she thinks that I think that she thinks..." chains.

Here's the variation. Suppose each child is a finite reasoner in one very specific way: every child can nest beliefs only up to depth d, meaning it can run thoughts of the form "they know that I know that..." with at most d levels, and if it tries to go one level deeper it just bottoms out at "I can't represent what they'd conclude from that." Everyone knows this about everyone (so d is common knowledge), and the parent's announcement still works the same as before.

Work out what actually happens as a function of d and k. My guess is the classic k-th-round result survives iff d ≥ k−1, because a muddy child who sees k−1 muddy foreheads needs a chain of "if I were clean, then..." nested k−1 deep to pull the trigger. But check whether the failure below that threshold is total (nobody ever raises a hand), whether the clean children could ever wrongly conclude they're muddy, and what the first round of nobody answering tells us about how much of the puzzle is doing the logical work and how much is just the public announcment. If you want a concrete case to run first, use n=5, k=3, d=1, and then generalize.
