Mill's Subjection of Women runs on a familiar move: when someone says women are by nature unfit for public life, he points out that we have no way to know that, because every woman we have ever observed has been raised under customs that push her away from public life. Then he adds that even a proved natural difference wouldn't by itself tell us what the law should be. So suppose we got the thing he said we could never have: solid twin and cross-cultural evidence that some average psychological differences between the sexes show up very early, across quite different settings, and have a substantial heritable component. Does Mill's argument survive, or does the epistemic half just collapse and leave only the is-ought point? I want to know which specific premise is under pressure, wether a repaired version can still block the inference from an average natural difference to the conclusion that a subordinate position is fine, and what Mill would have to give up if the evidence came out that way.
