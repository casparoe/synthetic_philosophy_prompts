In a seminar on determinism and scientific explanation, my students and I have repeatedly tripped over the word "unpredictable." We agree on familiar cases — a coin toss is unpredictable, an eclipse is predictable — but our agreement dissolves as soon as we ask what the word adds to the bare fact that we do not happen to know the outcome. Chaos theory makes the problem acute rather than merely verbal: the butterfly effect and the geometry of strange attractors have made it natural to say that a fully deterministic system can be unpredictable, and a double pendulum can amplify initial differences smaller than any feasible measurement so quickly that no finite precision fixes its behavior beyond a certain horizon. I do not want to hand my students a slogan; I want an account of what is being attributed when something is called unpredictable, and I want it to survive contact with awkward cases. My own suspicion is that the word is covertly relational — unpredictability for a particular predictor, given particular information and resources — but I would rather have that suspicion tested than assumed.

I would therefore like a working definition of "unpredictable" — or a small family of definitions, if you conclude the word does more than one job — that classifies the following cases well, and I ask that you run the definition through each case rather than treat the list as decoration. The cases are: (1) the result of a fair coin toss before it lands; (2) the path of totality of a total solar eclipse at a designated city fifty years from now; (3) the position of the second arm of a double pendulum thirty seconds after it is released, given a measurement of its initial state accurate to one part in a million; (4) the position of that same pendulum three seconds after release, under the same measurement precision; (5) the local weather at a specified hour three weeks from now, given the best current observations and models; (6) the next output of a well-designed pseudorandom number generator, for someone who knows the algorithm and the seed; (7) the next output of that same generator, for someone who sees only its previous outputs; (8) whether a particular unstable atomic nucleus will decay within the next hour; (9) the next digit of the decimal expansion of pi at a specified but astronomically large position, for someone with an ordinary computer and unlimited time; and (10) the contents of a message encrypted with a one-time pad, for an interceptor who has the ciphertext but not the pad.

For the first several cases I have firm intuitive verdicts; for the later ones I do not, and I expect the definition to surprise me somewhere. After running the definition through the list, please say explicitly where it yields a classification you would not want to defend, and then either revise the definition or argue that the surprising verdict should be accepted. Conclude by telling me whether "unpredictable" should remain a single concept or be split, and which of your ten classifications is the least secure. I would be grateful if you would answer in American English.
