I have been developing a principle that I think sharply narrows what should count as weakness of will, and I would like a candid assessment of it. I call it the Synchronic Judgment Principle: an action is genuinely akratic only if the agent acts against an all-things-considered judgment that she holds at the very moment of acting. If, by the time she acts, her evaluation has already shifted so that the chosen action now seems best to her on the whole, then the choice may be foolish, imprudent, or later regretted, but it is not weakness of will.

The main payoff of the principle, as I see it, is that it shows hyperbolic discounting to be the wrong place to look for akrasia. In the familiar one-marshmallow-now-versus-two-later case, the child who takes the single marshmallow is not, at the moment of choice, judging that two later is the better option. Under a present-biased discount function, one marshmallow now genuinely ranks higher for her at that moment. So the child acts in line with her current all-things-considered judgment. What shifts between the earlier time and the moment of choice is the ranking itself. That is preference reversal across time, not a self failing to do what it judges best. Genuine akrasia, on my account, is both rarer and stranger: the smoker who, while lighting a cigarette, judges that refraining would be better all things considered, and lights it anyway without revising that judgment, so that the judged-better and the chosen action come apart in the same moment and the same self.

My stronger claim is therefore that a large share of what gets called weakness of will—procrastination, overeating, breaking one's resolutions—is better described as intrapersonal preference change across successive selves, and should be treated as a problem about how a person manages future preference reversals rather than as akrasia proper. I suspect something close to this already exists under a standard name, but I have not seen the point stated this bluntly.

Give me the three strongest objections to the Synchronic Judgment Principle as a criterion for akrasia, and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. I especially want to know whether there is a defensible diachronic sense of weakness of will—one where the failure consists in an earlier judgment losing control to a later self—and whether my principle defines that phenomenon away. In answering, also say whether the principle is just a version of a standard view under another name, and if so, exactly where the stronger claim about excising hyperbolic discounting from the theory of akrasia goes beyond that view or stops being distinctive.
