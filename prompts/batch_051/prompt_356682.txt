I'm in a reading group on markets and information, and one member keeps pushing a argument that sounds airtight but lands somewhere none of us wants to live. His folk version of the no-trade theorem goes roughly like this.

(1) Suppose someone offers to buy an asset from me at a price they set. Their willingness to buy at that price is evidence they think the asset is worth more than that price. (2) By symmetry, my willingness to sell at that price is evidence to them that I think it's worth less. (3) We're both competent, and neither of us has any reason to think we're systematically better informed than the other. (4) So when I see them willing to buy, I should revise my estimate of the asset upward, and when they see me willing to sell, they should revise their estimate downward. (5) If we both revise rationally like this, at least one of us should end up unwilling to trade at that price, and since each of us knows the other is revising too, the trade should unravel before it happens. (6) Therefore a rational person should never accept a purely speculative trade — the other side being willing is itself evidence that you're the sucker. (7) But people do accept speculative trades all the time, in stocks, currencies, and prediction markets, and many of those trades seem perfectly sensible rather than foolish.

He thinks the flaw is (3): in real markets someone usually does know more, and the less-informed party should indeed back out. Others in the group think the real issue is that (1) ignores the way a trade can help both sides even when they have identical beliefs — say, different endowments, risk exposure, or liquidity needs. Another subgroup says (5) quietly assumes everyone has the same prior, and that giving up common priors is the cheapest way out.

Where does the argument actually go wrong? I'm less interested in a survey of fixes than in a committed diagnosis: pick a step (or steps), explain why it looked innocent, and say what rejecting it commits you to about markets, information, and rational trade. If you think the conclusion is basically right and we should bite that bullet, defend that instead.
