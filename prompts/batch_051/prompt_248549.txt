Analyze NASA's decision to proceed with the launch of the space shuttle Challenger in 1986 through the lens of the ethics of belief — in particular, the Cliffordian view that it is morally wrong to believe on insufficient evidence, so that where the evidence is weak the appropriate state is doubt rather than belief. Take the central facts as given: on the night before the launch, engineers from Morton Thiokol recommended against launching because of concerns about the O-ring seals at the forecast low temperatures; that recommendation was reversed under managerial pressure; the launch went ahead; the O-rings failed. I want a disciplined mapping rather than a recounting of events. Who held which beliefs, on what evidence, and where would a duty to doubt have cut? Then say where this doxastic lens genuinely explains the failure and where it misdescribes it — for example, whether what mattered was faulty belief formation, or a decision under risk with asymmetric stakes, a contest over burden of proof, and an organizational procedure that no account of individual intellectual virtue fully covers. Finally, say what the episode should teach us about treating doubt as a moral duty: does the case confirm that duty, expose its limits, or show that it must be supplemented by something institutional?
