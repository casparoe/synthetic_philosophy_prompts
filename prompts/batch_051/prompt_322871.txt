I am a retired geochemist who has lately been reading the philosophical literature on natural kinds, and I find that my instinctive classifications from four decades in the field are surer than my ability to state the rule behind them. I would like you to extract that rule.

Let me lay out my verdicts.

1. Ordinary hydrogen and deuterium are both hydrogen. They differ in nuclear mass, and heavy water behaves measurably differently from ordinary water, but I would never say deuterium is a different element. Proton number fixes the element.

2. Diamond and graphite are both carbon, yet I want to say they are different material kinds. They differ in bonding arrangement and in nearly every property that mattered in my work. So I split carbon by structure while joining hydrogen across a difference in nuclear mass; I cannot yet say why that is not inconsistent.

3. Ordinary water, ice, steam, and heavy water are all water. Phase changes do not make a new kind, and the substitution of deuterium for ordinary hydrogen does not either.

4. 'Jade' is not a natural kind. It covers jadeite and nephrite, two compositionally distinct minerals that happen to look alike and serve the same commercial and aesthetic role. Yet I would treat jadeite itself as a kind. On my own lights, then, shared appearance and shared use do not make a kind.

5. 'Tree' is not a kind; it is a growth form that has arisen independently in many unrelated lineages, whereas I treat a particular lineage, such as the oaks, as a kind. But a ring of interbreeding populations, in which each link interbreeds with its neighbours while the two terminal links do not, leaves me wanting to say that the terminals are distinct species even though there is no sharp boundary anywhere along the chain. So I seem to allow real kinds with blurred edges.

6. The divide between stars and planets, drawn at whether a body sustains core fusion, marks a genuine joint. The planet/dwarf-planet boundary that demoted Pluto strikes me as a convention.

7. 'Acid' in the Arrhenius and Brønsted–Lowry senses tracks the real phenomenon of proton donation. The Lewis sense, electron-pair acceptance, includes substances such as boron trifluoride that are not proton donors. I therefore doubt that a single natural kind answers to the term 'acid' as I learned it.

These verdicts pull in opposite directions. The hydrogen and water cases suggest that what matters is a deep composition; the carbon case suggests that what matters is structure and the properties it gives rise to; the jade and tree cases say that shared looks and shared use count for nothing; the ring case says a kind can be real without a sharp boundary; the planet and acid cases suggest that some of our categories mark joints while others are theory-relative or conventional.

Give me a committed answer, not a survey of options. State the principle—or, if you must, a small set of precisely stated candidate principles with the one you would defend identified—that best captures these verdicts. Go through the cases and tell me which verdicts it keeps and which it forces me to abandon, and explain why those are the ones to abandon. Then apply your principle to a case I have not settled. Stratigraphers fix the boundary between geological periods by driving a physical 'golden spike' into a specific bed of rock in a real outcrop, a decision made by a commission even though the boundary is meant to mark a real transition in the Earth's history. Is the resulting period boundary—say, wherever the Cambrian ends and the Ordovician begins—a natural joint, a convention, or something in between, and what, by your principle, would settle the matter?
