Consider the following dispute. In classic demonstrations, subjects randomly assigned a good, such as a mug, demand more to part with it than others, or than they themselves, would pay to acquire it. The standard explanation is loss aversion: once ownership sets a reference point, giving the good up is coded as a loss, and losses weigh more heavily than equal-sized gains. But for goods that people have used, modified, and woven into their routines, a gap between willingness to accept and willingness to pay might instead record real transaction costs, uncertainty, or legitimate attachment. A theory of the endowment effect needs a principled way to separate these.

Take the following criteria as a working test. A valuation tendency counts as a cognitive bias, rather than a legitimate preference, to the extent that: (i) it tracks the reference point, what the person happens to own or expect, more than it tracks the good's expected contribution to the person's welfare, plans, or projects; (ii) the resulting gap between willingness to accept and willingness to pay is not explained by income effects, transaction costs, information, search, or the strategic situation; and (iii) it lacks reflective endorsement—presented with the pattern in a calm setting, the person cannot fit her valuation into a coherent set of aims and commitments and would instead treat it as a mistake she would avoid in the future.

Now judge this case against those criteria. In 2015, M was assigned a 99-year occupancy right to a community garden plot through a lottery she had not entered; she had no interest in gardening and nearly declined because of the upkeep. She accepted. Over the years she cleared the plot, built a pergola with her son, made friends with two neighboring plot holders, and came to use the plot on most Saturday mornings from April through October. The produce is negligible in value, and a similar private plot could be rented for $300 a year, which she says she would never pay. The city permits transfers, and comparable plots now sell for about $3,000. When a new resident offered her $4,300, she declined; when asked how much she would demand, she eventually said $6,500. Pressed to explain, she cited her son, the neighbors, and the fact that the plot is the only place she can be outdoors without a goal. She acknowledged that a nearby park would cover much of the same need and that she had seen the market price for her plot, but said she simply did not want to sell.

Does M's refusal reflect the endowment effect as loss-aversion bias, or a legitimate preference? Go criterion by criterion; identify the places where the criteria underdetermine the verdict; and say whether the case itself shows that some criterion needs to be revised if the bias/preference distinction is to do useful work. Conclude with your overall finding—bias, legitimate preference, or irreducibly mixed—and identify the single further fact about M that would do most to settle it.
