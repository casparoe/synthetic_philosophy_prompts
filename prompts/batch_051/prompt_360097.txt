I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I am preparing a feature on the old Daoist ideal of wu-wei: roughly, action that is unforced, uncontrived, and successful precisely because the agent is not monitoring or straining to produce it. The sources present what looks like a standing paradox. Skilled figures are described as cutting, swimming, or ruling effortlessly, without deliberate intention, yet the tradition also prescribes years of ritual, study, and self-cultivation aimed at reaching exactly that state, which sounds a great deal like trying. The same shape of puzzle turns up, stripped of its metaphysics, in modern work on choking under pressure, where performers deteriorate once they attend to movements they have automated, and in familiar advice to stop trying so hard, which tends to fail precisely at the moment one tries to follow it.

Before I commit the piece to a thesis, I want to assign a probability to a specific claim: that the paradox of trying not to try picks out a single real feature of skilled human action, namely that there is a coherent mode of performance, distinct from effortful self-monitoring execution, that is reliably damaged by the very effort to produce or monitor it, and that the Daoist discussions of wu-wei and the modern choking-under-pressure findings are both tracking this same phenomenon. Please give me your credence in that claim and the reasoning that produces it. Decompose the question into the sub-claims your number depends on (for example: that a coherent spontaneous or effortless mode of performance exists; that self-monitoring effort inhibits it; that indirect methods such as habituation, absorption in the task, or imagistic rehearsal can reliably bring it about; and that the Daoist and modern motor-control cases are instances of one underlying phenomenon), assign each a rough probability, show how they combine into your final number, and then tell me which term your credence is most sensitive to and what sort of evidence would shift it most.
