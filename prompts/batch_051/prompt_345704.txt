Draft the joint statement that a committed Cartesian materialist — someone who holds that conscious experience consists in the presentation of a unified, temporally ordered representation at a single privileged stage or site in the nervous system, a kind of theater where distributed processing results are assembled for a consumer — and a committed defender of the multiple drafts model — someone who holds that there is no such stage, that the brain continuously maintains many parallel, distributed, and revisable content-fixations, and that questions about the exact moment at which a content "becomes conscious" are often ill-posed — could both sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Write the deliverable itself, not a commentary on the two positions, and make every part self-contained.

The document should contain four parts.

Part 1: A numbered list of agreed claims. These should be neither vacuous nor watered down. Cover at least: the phenomenology any account must respect, including reports of a unified, serial, temporally ordered stream of experience and the contrast between contents that guide behavior and contents available for report; the fact that much perceptual and motor processing is parallel, distributed, and unconscious; the fact that report and probe paradigms can yield different "timing" results for the same stimulus; and the shared commitment to a materialist account that rejects an immaterial observer.

Part 2: A disagreements table. Each row should state one precise question in neutral terms; the Cartesian materialist's answer in wording a careful Cartesian materialist would accept as an accurate statement of their own view; the multiple drafts answer in wording a careful multiple drafts theorist would accept as accurate; and a verdict on whether that row's disagreement is substantive or merely verbal. Include rows on at least these questions: whether there is a determinate instant at which a given content becomes conscious; whether perceptual binding requires convergence at a common site rather than distributed coherence; what makes a content "in" consciousness rather than merely influential on downstream processing; and whether the content of experience at time t is well-defined independently of what is later probed.

Part 3: A list of cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a test, argument, or case that both sides agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, including at least one specified outcome that each side would count as evidence against its own view. Be concrete. Address at least: the probe-dependent timing of briefly masked stimuli, apparent-motion or color-phi displays, and cases in which a later stimulus appears to alter the report of an earlier one. For any display or experiment you invoke, describe it in one or two sentences so a reader can follow without external material.

Part 4: A closing paragraph both sides could sign, stating what would count as genuine progress on their disagreement and identifying any portion of the original dispute that turned out to be about vocabulary or framing rather than fact.
