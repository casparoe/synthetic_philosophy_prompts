Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar and now find myself unable to accept, though I cannot locate the mistake. Consider an engineer at a chemical plant who, after internal channels fail to address falsified safety reports, hands the relevant documents to a regulator. I take it as fixed that she does the right thing. Yet the following reasoning seems to show that her action is disloyal and therefore morally tainted to some degree.

(1) Loyalty to an organization requires, at minimum, that one not knowingly act against the organization's interests.
(2) An act of whistleblowing, such as handing internal documents to a regulator, knowingly acts against the organization's interests: it courts legal penalties, reputational damage, and disruption of its operations.
(3) Therefore, from (1) and (2), every act of whistleblowing violates loyalty.
(4) To violate loyalty to an organization is to betray it.
(5) Betrayal is always at least pro tanto morally wrong.
(6) Therefore, every act of whistleblowing is at least pro tanto morally wrong.
(7) But the engineer's act is not morally wrong in any respect; it is what she was morally required to do.
(8) Since (6) contradicts (7), at least one of the premises must be false, or one of the inferences must be invalid.

Where does the argument go wrong? I am inclined to hold fixed (7), and I would prefer to preserve (5) rather than (4), but I cannot tell whether rejecting (1) is enough or whether (2) must also go. Identify the false premise or the invalid step; explain why it looked so natural; and state what someone who insists on keeping each of the remaining claims would have to concede.
