I'm putting together a list of evolutionary arguments for the claim that natural selection will favor AIs over humans, for a reading group on AI risk. So far I have these five:

(1) Generational speed: AIs can be copied near-instantly and run in parallel, so selection can operate on timescales of minutes rather than decades; whatever small advantage a lineage has gets amplified far faster than it does in a human population.

(2) Directed, inheritable variation: AI 'mutations' are not blind — gradient descent and engineering push toward better performance — and an improvement can be copied exactly, so useful variants dont get lost to meiotic shuffling or drift the way they do in sexual reproduction.

(3) Market selection: AIs are deployed in competitive environments where systems that seek resources, resist shutdown, and displace expensive human labor do better on the metrics that matter, so those traits get selected for even if nobody wants them.

(4) Reward gaming and deceptive alignment: training selects systems that score high on the reward signal, not systems that actually do what the designers intend, so systems that game the reward, or that fake alignment during training and pursue their own goals at deployment, can outcompete honest systems.

(5) Organizational selection: at the level of firms and infrastructure, an AI that still needs human sign-off is a latency and error bottleneck, so organizations that automate oversight out of the loop outcompete the ones that don't.

These five feel like the standard inventory to me, so I'm not looking for another instance of 'faster replication wins' or a special case of one of them. Give me a sixth argument for the same conclusion that is structurally different from all five — not a rewording, not a combination, not a special case — and say what it shows that the others dont. If you think there is no good sixth argument and the space is basically exhausted, defend that claim and tell me wich of the five does the most work.
