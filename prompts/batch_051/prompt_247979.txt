My uncle has got himself into a weird legal tangle and I keep going round in circles about it. Our state's Determination of Death Act, passed in 1983, says: "A person is dead when the whole of the person's brain, including the brainstem, has irreversibly lost all function." That's the entire definition; there's no clause about artificial support, uploading, or anything like that.

He joined a trial for gradual uploading. Over about a year, nanomachines replaced his neurons one by one with silicon units that mimic the input-output behaviour of the neuron they replace exactly, so the surrounding brain carries on as normal throughout. He stayed conscious and responsive the whole time and kept passing memory and cognition tests at every stage. The process has now finished: his original biological brain has been completely dismantled, and everything that used to happen in it now happens in the silicon substrate. To everyone around him he seems like the same man.

The question is whether he is dead under the Act. The drafters obvioulsy weren't thinking about substrate replacement. I'd like you to work through what the plain words, the purpose of the Act, and what the drafters would have said if asked each give, and then say which approach should win. Also, should the legal answer track the deeper question of whether he survived as a person, or is the law better off just making its own call? Please write your answer in British English.
