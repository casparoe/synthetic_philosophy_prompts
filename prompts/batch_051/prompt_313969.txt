During the past year I found myself in circumstances where several of the certainties that had quietly organized my practical life—about what may be assumed, what counts as a reason, whose word is authoritative—ceased to be shared by the people I had to coordinate with. The experience was unlike ordinary uncertainty, and it led me to Ludwig Wittgenstein’s *On Certainty*. The passage that keeps occupying me is the one in which Wittgenstein says, if I may paraphrase, that a picture of the world is not acquired by satisfying oneself of its correctness, nor held because one is satisfied of it; rather, it is the inherited background against which the distinction between true and false first becomes possible. I want to use this passage to compare two readings of what such a background could be. On the first reading, it is composed of hinge propositions: propositional certainties with a distinctive epistemic status, exempt from ordinary doubt and confirmation. On the second reading, it is non-propositional and normative, more like ways of acting, judging, and using language than like claims that could be true or false. Please compare these two readings. Which better captures the passage and the later sections of *On Certainty* about doubt, justification, and the limits of inquiry? What difference would it make in practice—or in epistemology—whether the background is propositional or non-propositional? I am less interested in a survey of the secondary literature than in a defended comparison that treats the passage as a test case.
