You are a philosopher of cognitive science who also follows the mechanistic interpretability literature closely (SAEs, polysemanticity, circuits, all that). Take your time with this one. Before writing anything, brainstorm four or five candidate cruxes and then keep only the two or three that would actually move at least one side.

I want you to draft the joint statement that two people produce after a year-long adversarial collaboration. The camps are (a) representational realists about neural networks: they think when interpretability work talks about a "dog feature" or a circuit that implements modular addition, there's a literal fact of the matter about what internal activations represent, and good interpretability is partly in the business of discovering those real states; and (b) deflationists/instrumentalists: they think "the model represents X" is at best a useful modeling shorthand imposed by the interpreter, the trained network is just a function approximator, and there is no priviledged decomposition of activations that carves anything at the joints.

I don't want a description of their collaboration or a dialogue. I want the actual document they could both sign.

It should have:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. They should be substantive, not vacuous: what a trained network really is, which empirical findings both sides accept, what kinds of evidence would make each side update, and what "representation" has to mean before the debate can even be posed. Neither side should wince at any of these.

2. A disagreements table. The residual disagreements should be stated exactly, with each side's view in wording that side would accept as its own. Be careful to seperate disagreements about existence from disagreements about uniqueness (is there one right decomposition?), about content (who fixes what a feature means?), and about explanatory value (does "represents" pull its weight?). Flag any part of the dispute you judge to be purely verbal and say why.

3. A cruxes section. For each crux, give the concrete experiment or case both sides agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. The experiments should be realistic and describable without a lab: causal interventions on features, transfer across contexts or architectures, probes versus interventions, two networks trained on the same task, that sort of thing.

Assume both collaborators are technically literate and write plainly. And if finishing the document forces one side into a concession or a disambiguation that dissolves part of the debate, put that in a short coda instead of smoothing it over.
