I've been arguing with a friend who works on causal discovery, and I can't tell whether a standard line in that field is dead or just needs repair. The line, as I understand it: you observe a bunch of conditional independencies among variables, you fit a directed acyclic graph whose missing arrows explain those independencies (Markov), and among all the graphs that fit you prefer the most economical one, the minimal graph. The philosophical claim is that this minimal graph tracks true causal structure. The usual guarantee is faithfulness: in the population there are no conditional independencies except the ones the graph's structure forces. No accidental screening-off; no two causal paths that exactly cancel. And the standard defense of faithfulness is a parameter-counting argument — the unfaithful parameter values form a measure-zero set in a continuous space of parameterizations, so unless Nature is aiming at a knife-edge you're basically safe.

Now the development that keeps bugging me. Suppose the systems we actually want causal maps of — gene regulatory networks, homeostatic physiology, economic equilibria, engineered feedback controllers — are exactly the systems whose parameters have been tuned, by selection or by design, to hold some variable near a target. Then they live in the non-generic region where one path cancels another; its their whole point. The measure-zero defense now looks like it excludes precisely the cases we care about. So my question is whether the minimal-Bayes-nets-track-causation argument survives this. If it does, show which premise does the work and why the tuning argument doesn't touch it. If it doesn't, name the premise that breaks and say what a repaired version should claim — maybe something weaker about interventions, or about sparsity doing useful work even when faithfulness fails.
