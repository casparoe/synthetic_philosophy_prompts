In my philosophy of science reading group, the verisimilitude discussion has stayed in the same place for months, and I want to understand the structure of the impasse rather than re-argue the usual positions. Everyone shares the motivating picture: Popper needed a notion of one false theory being closer to the whole truth than another, because the history of science looks like a sequence of strictly false theories and we still want to call that sequence progressive. Everyone also agrees that Popper's first attempt at defining closeness to truth — comparing a theory's true consequences with its false consequences — collapsed, since on that definition no false theory could come out closer to the truth than another false theory. The field then split into two camps that, in our group, systematically talk past each other. One camp treats truthlikeness as likeness: how similar the possible worlds a theory permits are to the actual world, measured by something like distance over worlds. The other treats it as content: how a theory's truth-content and falsity-content trade off, perhaps with restrictions on which consequences count. My working hypothesis is that this is not really a technical dispute about formal definitions but a disagreement about what closeness to the truth is supposed to do — a semantic relation between a theory and the world, or a normative standard for selecting theories that will serve as guides to further inquiry. Is that diagnosis right, or is a substantive formal disagreement hiding underneath it? And on your diagnosis, what would count as progress, as distinct from merely proposing another definition?
