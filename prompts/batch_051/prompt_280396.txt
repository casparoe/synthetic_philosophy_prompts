I’ve been reading Lucretius, De Rerum Natura, and I keep coming back to this bit from Book I:

This ultimate stock we have devised to name
Procreant atoms, matter, seeds of things,
Or primal bodies, as primal to the world.

The way he just lists “matter” alongside “atoms” and “seeds” makes me feel like the whole atomist position is baked into a certain definition of matter as solid, impenetrable bodies, with void as whatever’s left that lets them be distinct and move. My question is: what’s your actual credence that the ancient atomist account of matter—atoms as solid, impenetrable bits moving in void—is not just a historically important guess that modern physics happens to vaguely resemble, but is still doing real philosophical work today as an account of what material bodies fundamentally are? I want a number, and I want the reasoning decomposed: how much should ride on whether there are genuinely fundamental particles as opposed to fields or quantum states; how much on whether impenetrability and solidity are still central to what matter is; how much on whether empty space (void) is physically real in something like Lucretius’s sense; and how much on whether “matter” even has a determinate sense at the fundamental level. Then tell me which of those sub-questions your final number is most sensitive to, and what evidence would shift it most. Also say how confident you are in that number overall, and single out the point you’re least sure of.
