I'm a TA for an intro ethics course, and I'm trying to calibrate my grading on a short-answer question about Augustine's On Lying. The exam gave students a passage from the text and asked them to explain it and apply it to two cases. I keep going back and fourth on one student's answer — the general shape looks fine, but I suspect some of the subtle stuff is wrong, and I don't want to over-credit or under-credit it. Here is the question as it appeared, then the answer. Could you grade it like you were marking the exam?

Question (as set):

Read the following passage from Augustine's On Lying (chapter 3), then answer both parts. (a) Explain, in your own words, how Augustine distinguishes lying from merely saying something false. (b) Apply the distinction to the two cases below, and for each say whether Augustine would call the person a liar and why.

"Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind. Whence also the heart of him who lies is said to be double; that is, there is a double thought: the one, of that thing which he either knows or thinks to be true and does not produce; the other, of that thing which he produces instead thereof, knowing or thinking it to be false. Whence it comes to pass, that he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie. Therefore he who utters a false thing for a true, which however he opines to be true, may be called erring and rash: but he is not rightly said to lie; because he has not a double heart when he utters it, neither does he wish to deceive, but is deceived."

Cases: (i) Jonah tells his sister that the capital of Australia is Sydney, which he firmly believes; it is actually Canberra. (ii) Marina tells her frightened patient that a drug will help her, even though she is convinced it won't; the drug in fact works.

Student answer:

"Augustine's basic point is that lying is about what's in your mind, not just whether your statement lines up with the facts. So you lie when you willfully assert something false in order to lead someone else into error. Jonah is the easy case: he says something objectively false, but he believes it, so there's no duplicity in his mind and no intention to deceive. Augustine says this person is erring and rash, not lying; he is the one who is deceived, not the deceiver. Marina is the mirror image. Her statement turns out to be true, and on Augustine's view the truth or falsity of what is actually said is what settles the question, judged by how things turn out. So she doesn't lie, even though her intention was bad. It's a failed lie, maybe a sin of intention, but not strictly a lie, because the words matched the world."

Grade this as if you were marking a real exam answer. Give it a mark out of 10, then structure your response as follows:

1. Overall grade and justification — 100–150 words, giving the mark and the main reason for it in a paragraph.
2. Point-by-point comments — 250–350 words, going through the answer claim by claim and tying each to the passage, saying what gets credit and what doesn't.
3. What the answer reveals about the student's understanding — 50–100 words.
4. Confidence and least sure point — 50–100 words, saying how confident you are in the grade and singling out the specific call you are least sure about.
