I am taking an upper-level seminar on the philosophy and science of deep learning, and last week we discussed the debate over whether neural networks have genuine internal representations. A visiting lecturer, who works in machine learning but not directly in interpretability, gave what she called a "deflationary" summary of the field. As I wrote it down, she argued roughly this: asking whether a network's hidden layers contain representations is like asking whether a cloud has a shape, because the structure we report is something we project onto the model rather than something the model uses. The activations of a hidden layer, she said, are just points in a high-dimensional vector space, and any direction we label as encoding a property—gender in a language model, say, or the presence of parentheses in a code model—is a convenient simplification of that vector arithmetic, not evidence that the network itself represents the property. She added that superposition, the phenomenon in which a network seems to store more features than it has neurons by spreading them across overlapping vector directions, further undermines claims about individual neurons having stable meanings; but she slid from this to the conclusion that the network represents no features at all. She also claimed that because different research groups can train different probes to recover different, even contradictory, features from the same network, there is no fact of the matter about which concepts the network uses. Her final conclusion was that interpretability is best treated as "model folklore": a useful source of hypotheses about what a network might be doing, but not evidence about its internal representations.

I found myself uneasy with this summary, but I do not know enough to identify exactly where the confusions are. I am especially suspicious of the move from "individual neurons are not cleanly aligned with single features" to "the network represents nothing," and of the use of probes as evidence that there is no fact of the matter about representations. Sort this out for me: go through the summary claim by claim and say what an interpretability researcher or a philosopher of cognitive science would accept, what they would reject or qualify, and where the lecturer has conflated two different issues. Then say whether the deflationary conclusion could be restated in a way that would survive these corrections, and whether the argument's more modest claims actually support it.
