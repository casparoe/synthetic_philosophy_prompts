I spent my career in physics modelling coupled dynamical systems — feedback loops, controllers and plants, state estimation, where you choose to draw the boundary of the system you are analysing. I have recently started reading philosophy of mind with no formal background in it, and the extended mind debate keeps nagging at me.

As I understand it, the claim is that cognition need not stop at the skull. Take Otto, who has a memory impairment and writes everything down in a notebook he always carries; Inga just remembers things biologically. The extended mind people say Otto's notebook is part of his memory because it is reliably present, he trusts it, and he reaches for it exactly when the job calls for memory. The usual objections are that this confuses coupling with constitution (the notebook merely causes him to remember, it does not do the remembering), and that there must be some mark of the cognitive that separates a notebook from a hippocampus.

Restate that whole debate — the parity claim, the coupled-system argument, and those objections — in the vocabulary I actually think in: state variables, feedback loops, controllers and plants, observability, modelling choices about system boundaries. Be as faithful as the translation allows.

Then give me the residue report: which parts map cleanly onto coupled systems, which parts you had to fudge or distort, and which parts have no counterpart in the dynamical-systems vocabulary at all. The question I most want answered is whether my instinct is right that the whole dispute is just a modelling choice about where to draw the box, or whether the philosophers are tracking something my toolkit is structurally unable to express. I do not mind which way the answer goes; I want to know what is lost in the translation.
