In my reporting on artificial intelligence, one thing I keep noticing is how much of contemporary machine learning is held together by a single evaluative shortcut: we judge a system by what it produces. A model is reinforced when its final answer is right, when a human prefers it, when a benchmark goes up, or when a user stays engaged. A number of researchers I interview argue that this outcome-based approach is the root of much that worries them—reward hacking, sycophancy, benchmark gaming, and the pressure toward models that sound persuasive rather than reason honestly. Their proposed alternative is process-based supervision: instead of scoring the result, human evaluators or trained critic models score each step of the model's reasoning or action as it happens, checking whether the step follows, whether uncertainty is stated, whether the salient considerations are being addressed, and whether the model is being straightforward with the user.

Let me pose a counterfactual to think through that proposal. Suppose that beginning in 2030, an enforceable international agreement bars frontier AI developers from using any training signal derived from outcomes. That means no reward models trained on human preference comparisons between final responses, no reinforcement against human satisfaction scores, no fine-tuning on benchmark performance or downstream task success, and no market-based signals such as revenue, engagement, or user ratings. Developers may train only with process-based supervision: step-level human critique, and over time automated process critics, that evaluates how a model is reasoning rather than where it ends up. Enforcement is imperfect but consequential—the major labs comply, while a smaller black market continues with outcome-based methods.

I want your argued prediction of how the world of frontier AI looks in 2040. Which capabilities genuinely advance under a process-only regime, and which stagnate or regress—long-horizon autonomous agents, open-ended mathematics, persuasion, creative work? Does the alignment picture actually improve, or do the problems migrate: process evaluators become the new thing to game, developers quietly smuggle outcome information into process criteria, and models learn to produce step-by-step reasoning that looks defensible while hiding the real computation? I care less about your yes or no than about the chain of reasoning: mark the links you think are robust, the links that depend on assumptions about what humans can reliably judge step-by-step, and what would have to be true for the whole prediction to flip.
