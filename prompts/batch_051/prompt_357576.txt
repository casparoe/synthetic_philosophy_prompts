I cover science and ideas for a general-audience publication, and I am working on a long piece about how the transition from foraging to farming gets used in contemporary debates over human progress. In my background reading I came across a widely recommended popular book whose chapter on this topic leans heavily on Marshall Sahlins's famous essay The Original Affluent Society, first presented at the 1966 Man the Hunter symposium. The chapter summarizes the position, in substance, like this: Sahlins used Richard Lee's 1960s fieldwork among the Dobe !Kung to argue that hunter-gatherers were the original affluent society, meeting their material needs in about fifteen to twenty hours of work per week and achieving affluence not by producing much but by wanting little, which left them with more leisure than we normally imagine and something close to material plenty. On the chapter's telling, Sahlins held that scarcity is in large part a cultural artifact rather than an inescapable condition, that foragers were generally better off than the early farmers who succeeded them, and that the agricultural revolution was therefore humanity's worst mistake.

I am sympathetic to this deflationary view of progress and would like to use a version of the claim in my own piece, but I know from covering the field that popular summaries of anthropology often trade away distinctions that specialists care about. Please audit the chapter's summary in a committed way. Go claim by claim, mark what tracks Sahlins's actual argument, what is a defensible simplification, and what overstates or attributes to him a thesis he did not hold, and then tell me how a careful journalist should put the point if it has to be compressed into a paragraph. I would rather have your argued verdict than a menu of interpretive possibilities.
