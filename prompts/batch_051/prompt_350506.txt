Lately I have been unable to settle a disagreement I keep having with myself about how pleasure and pain relate to one another. On one side, I am drawn to a view that could be described as lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism. As I understand it, the view says that below a certain level of intensity, suffering and enjoyment are commensurable and may be traded off in the ordinary way, more or less as commonsense would; but once a pain crosses the threshold — the kind of prolonged, dignity-destroying suffering one finds in torture or certain severe illnesses — no finite accumulation of below-threshold pleasure, however large, can morally outweigh even one instance of it. What attracts me is that this view yields a clean decision procedure and permanently blocks the conclusion, which I regard as monstrous, that enough mild enjoyments could justify inflicting extreme suffering. The underlying asymmetry, that suffering is morally more urgent than pleasure, also still feels right to me. Yet I also have a firm intuition I cannot make myself abandon. Pain and pleasure introspectively present themselves as opposite directions along a single continuum of felt valence, not as two different currencies that abruptly stop being exchangeable. I accept small pains — the burn of a hard run, the distress of an honest but painful conversation — in exchange for larger goods, and when I imagine the pain gradually intensifying, the exchange does not strike me as changing in kind; the pain simply becomes more unpleasant and calls for proportionally more on the other side. On this intuition, the badness of pain and the goodness of pleasure are commensurable throughout, and any sharp threshold is a pragmatic line drawn for caution or for public policy, not a real discontinuity in the nature of the states themselves.
