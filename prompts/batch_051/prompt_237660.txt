I'm leading a seminar discussion of Hamlet, and I want to frame it around the question of when self-examination is harmful rather than a virtue. A familiar reading says Hamlet fails because he thinks too much: he delays, rehearses, and turns inward instead of committing himself to action. A more precise way of drawing that charge is the distinction between productive self-examination and pathological rumination, where rumination is repetitive, past- or self-focused, and disconnected from any decision the person could actually make. Read the play through that distinction. Which scenes and character choices does it illuminate, and where does the play resist it? In particular, does the play treat the prince's introspection as the cause of his failure, or as a rational response to a court where appearances cannot be trusted and action carries enormous risk? End by saying what Hamlet draws out about the harm of self-examination that the philosophical and psychological literature tends to flatten.
