I have been trying to sort out the distinct ways in which an artefact can be political in virtue of its design and operating requirements, rather than in virtue of the intentions of its users — the sense made familiar by Langdon Winner's question whether artefacts have politics. My working list so far is:

(1) Exclusion by physical affordance: the Long Island parkway overpasses, built low enough that buses could not pass under them, which in practice kept the poorer, mostly black beachgoers who depended on buses away from Jones Beach.

(2) The encoding of interests in operating requirements: the mechanical tomato harvester, which was economical only for large, well-capitalised farms and thereby favoured them over small growers.

(3) The scripting of behaviour: benches with armrests or sloped surfaces that make it impossible to lie down.

(4) The distribution of visibility: the Panopticon, whose geometry allows a single watcher to observe every cell while the inmates never know when they are being watched.

I want one further kind of artefact politics that is not on this list and not a special case of any of these four. It should be the sort of thing Winner himself would recognise as a distinct way in which an artefact can be political. Give me that kind, a concrete example, and say what it adds to the taxonomy — that is, why it cannot be absorbed into (1)–(4).
