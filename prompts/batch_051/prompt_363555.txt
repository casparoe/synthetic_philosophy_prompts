In my Holocaust perpetrators seminar the Browning-Goldhagen unit has stalled in a way that's getting frustating. We read Ordinary Men and Hitler's Willing Executioners back to back, and we went over the same Reserve Police Battalion 101 episodes: middle-aged Hamburg men, mostly not hardcore Nazis, not specially selected, given an opt-out by Major Trapp before the first massacre at Jozefow, most of them stayed and killed, and it went on for months. Then some of those same men were gratuitously cruel on the death marches later. Nobodys disputing any of that. What we can't agree on is what the facts show.

The Browning side in the room says the situation did the work: conformity, peer pressure, careerism, not wanting to break with the group, plus the step-by-step brutalization from the first killing onward. Their whole point is that these men really were ordinary, so the lesson is that almost anyone could have done it under those conditions. The Goldhagen side says that just pushes the question back: why did ordinary Germans kill so readily and so cruelly, when the same situation wouldn't have produced the same result everywhere? They think the culture was already saturated with eliminationist antisemitism, so the men didn't need to be forced or gradually broken in; they mostly wanted to do it or at least thought it was right. And they read the same death-march cruelty as evidence of gratuitous, ideological violence, where the Browning people read it as exactly what brutalization plus wartime conditions would produce.

I'm not asking who wins. I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is there a hidden empirical counterfactual doing the work, like what these men would have done in a different situation, or what a non-German battalion would have done? Are the two sides using different standards for what counts as a satisfying explanation of perpetrators, one wanting a mechanism from situation to action, the other wanting a motive deep enough to match the scale and cruelty? Or are they answering different questions, Browning explaining why so many ordinary men killed given the situation, while Goldhagen is explaining why so few refused given the culture? My hunch is the last one, but I also suspect both sides are leaning on counterfactuals neither can test. Given your diagnosis, is there anything that would actually move the debate — a comparison case, a different sort of evidence, a sharper way of stating the question — or is it just not resolvable as framed?
