I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, and I've been going in circles about a proposal a few people in my department have been floating as a practical fix for the problem of old evidence. Quick recap of the problem, because I don't want the mechanism to get detached from it: if a theory T entails a piece of evidence E, and E was already known with probability 1 before anyone formulated T, then standard Bayesianism says E can't confirm T, since conditioning on something you're already certain of shouldn't move your credence. But that feels wrong—old evidence does seem to confirm some theories, the canonical example being general relativity and the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion, which was already known before the theory explained it. So here's the proposal. A few journals in my field are considering a rule: a dataset counts as evidence for a hypothesis only if the author publicly registred the hypothesis and a precise prior probability for it before the dataset became public. If the data was already public at the time of that deposit, the analysis gets stamped "post hoc" and can be published, but the paper isn't allowed to claim the data supports the hypothesis. The idea is to legislate the old-evidence problem out of existence. I'd like to treat this as a mechanism and work out how it would actually behave, not whether it's a good idea in the abstract. What does it incentivize authors, labs, data collectors, and reviewers to do? Where does it get gamed first—invented priors, timestamp tricks, splitting a dataset so the withheld half stays "new," or sitting on data so other people's analyses become post hoc by accident? What would it do to fields that depend on public archives, meta-analysis, or old observational datasets? And does the old-evidence problem actually go away, or does it just get pushed into the choice of prior, or into what counts as "the same hypothesis"? Walk me through the second-order effects and say which of the failure modes, if any, are fixable without scrapping the whole rule.
