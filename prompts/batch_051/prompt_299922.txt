Two friends of mine, Dana and Marcus, got into an argument about Hobbes over dinner at my flat the other night, and afterward they asked me to say who was right. I genuinely couldn't, so I've written out the exchange as faithfully as I can and want you to referee it.

Dana said: "Hobbes's basic point is sound. Any functioning sovereign, even a mediocre one, keeps us out of the state of nature, where everyone is at war with everyone else and life is violent and insecure. So citizens shouldn't act in ways that risk pulling the whole arrangement down."

Marcus said: "But you can't just help yourself to that. We aren't in the state of nature making a fresh choice. The argument shows, at most, that people in that original condition would have found it rational to set up a sovereign. It doesn't show that we, who signed nothing, are obliged to obey the particular state we happen to have."

Dana: "Hobbes doesn't need a literal original signing. The covenant is the idea that each person gives up the right to govern themselves on the condition that everyone else does too. The reason to obey afterward is ongoing rational self-interest, not a historical piece of paper."

Marcus: "Then you've watered the conclusion down to 'it's rational to want there to be some state or other.' That doesn't pick out this state, and it doesn't get you from prudence to obligation. If the sovereign turns abusive, self-interest might tell you to leave, resist, or keep your head down depending on your circumstances — not to obey."

Dana: "But if everyone reserved the right to drop out whenever they personally judged the state not worth having, the state couldn't do its job and everyone would slide back into war. That's the target of Hobbes's Fool objection: the fool denies that justice is a real thing and keeps his covenants only when it benefits him, and Hobbes says such a person can't expect to remain part of the commonwealth. The exit clause defeats the whole arrangement."

Marcus: "The Fool is someone who free-rides while still receiving the protection the commonwealth provides. A subject whose state has stopped protecting them is in a different position. Hobbes explicitly says a subject's obligation to the sovereign ends when the sovereign can no longer protect them. So your position isn't 'obey any state'; it's 'obey while it protects' — which is closer to my test than to yours."

Dana: "I can accept that, but it hurts you. If the real test is protection, then consent was never doing the work. Take a commonwealth by acquisition, where a conqueror assumes power: Hobbes says it's rights are the same as those of a commonwealth by institution, even though there's no founding agreement."

Marcus: "I don't think that example works the way you want. In acquisition Hobbes has the vanquished covenant for their lives, so there is a kind of agreement after all, made from fear. And Hobbes says covenants made from fear are valid, so now you've got half of your contract signed at gunpoint. How does that get you to genuine obligation rather than just a survival instinct? At most Hobbes shows what a rational person would do to stay alive, not that the state has any claim on them."

They turned to me as if one of them had obviously won, and I honestly can't tell. Please go through the exchange and adjudicate it point by point: for each substantive claim, say who's right, who's wrong, and who's half right; flag anything important both of them are missing; and finish by saying who is more right overall, if either.

Oh, and please write your answer in British English (spelling and conventions), not American.
