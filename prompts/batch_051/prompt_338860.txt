I’m a third-year PhD student in political methodology, moving my work toward causal inference, and I keep hitting a conceptual wall in the formal apparatus I’m supposed to be using. Pearl’s three-layer hierarchy—association, intervention, counterfactual—gets presented in the textbooks as a settled way of mapping what structural causal models can do, and the do-calculus gets taught as a kind of neutral identification machinery. But in applied work in my field, I see researchers treating interventions as surgical replacements of structural equations without ever defending the idea that social mechanisms can be carved up in that way, or that the rest of the model remains invariant under the intervention. I’m not looking for a bibliography or a literature review; I want a defended agenda.

Suppose you were advising a quantitatively trained PhD student with three years to spend on the conceptual foundations of this framework. Which unresolved question about what structural causal models assume most deserves careful work, and why? I want you to rank the candidates explicitly. My own list starts with three: (a) the interpretation and justification of modularity or autonomy of causal mechanisms, (b) the adequacy of the local-replacement conception of intervention for human and institutional systems, and (c) the gap between the formalism’s counterfactual layer and a more metaphysically robust notion of counterfactual dependence. You may replace one of these or add something I’m not listing. For each candidate, tell me how upstream it is for other questions, how tractable it is for someone trained in causal inference but not in academic metaphysics, and what kinds of results—conceptual, formal, or empirical—would move it up or down the ranking over the next couple of years. Also, please explicitly separate what is genuinely settled in the mathematical apparatus from what is contested among people who know both the formalism and the philosophy, and be careful not to present a contested reading as if it were the standard one.
