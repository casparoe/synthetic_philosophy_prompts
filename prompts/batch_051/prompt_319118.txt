I am preparing a short seminar presentation for a department of conservation biology and applied ecology, and I want to be ready for the question period rather than surprised by it. My thesis is that conservation practice quietly relies on a welfare assumption — roughly, that protecting or restoring habitat is good for the animals living in it — and that this assumption is much less secure than the field's confidence suggests. I will argue that on any serious attempt to estimate the net welfare of an ecosystem, certain forms of habitat degradation (for instance, the conversion of high-productivity, high-disease, high-predation habitat into sparser, lower-biomass habitat) may reduce total animal suffering even as they reduce biodiversity and biomass. I will explicitly not argue that conservationists should advocate habitat destruction; my claim is narrower: the biodiversity ledger and the welfare ledger can come apart, and a movement that takes the moral high ground on behalf of living things cannot simply refuse to keep welfare accounts.

The audience is a mix of field ecologists, reserve designers, and restoration practitioners, plus two or three environmental philosophers. Their shared commitments are the value of biodiversity, the primacy of measurable ecological variables over hard-to-observe subjective states, and a keen sense that conservation is politically vulnerable to being painted as anti-human sentimentality. Most are suspicious of wild-animal welfare talk, which they associate with pets and zoos rather than with population dynamics; a few are quietly sympathetic but have never seen welfare integrated with quantitative field ecology. The argument I intend to defend runs as follows: conservation's public moral case is at least partly welfarist, since it invokes the flourishing of wild living things; if welfare matters at all, it matters for the vast numbers of short-lived, high-fecundity animals whose lives typically end early in starvation, disease, or predation; and the ecological conditions that drive those early deaths — high primary productivity, high density, abundant predators, heavy parasite loads — are precisely what successful restoration tends to increase. Restoration and protection therefore need a welfare balance sheet as well as a biodiversity balance sheet, and on some realistic parameterizations that welfare sheet will be negative. I regard the link between habitat quality and the prevalence of suffering as my most exposed premise; the audience will know the field data are patchy.

Write me a Q&A preparation sheet: the objections in roughly the order they will actually be raised, phrased as this audience would phrase them, with how serious each is, what I should concede on the spot, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest. Include the question I should most hope nobody asks, with the best answer I can give. Make the substantive work the floor plan of the hostile-but-fair question period, not a list of general doubts, and keep each suggested reply as short as it can be while still doing its job.
