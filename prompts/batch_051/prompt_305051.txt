You are a professional philosopher with expertise in experimental philosophy, moral psychology, and the philosophy of language. Please think through this problem carefully. Begin by briefly distinguishing the different readings of the key notions before you commit to a final verdict; then give your assessment in a structured way.

Consider the classic chairman vignette from experimental philosophy. A company chairman is told that a proposed policy will raise profits and will also have a side effect on the environment: in one version the side effect is destructive (the harm condition), and in the other it is beneficial (the help condition). In both versions he says, in effect, that he does not care about this side effect at all and wants only to pursue profits. The policy is implemented, and the side effect occurs. A robust experimental finding is that ordinary people judge that the chairman intentionally harmed the environment in the harm condition, but they deny that he intentionally helped the environment in the help condition.

A colleague of mine now presents the following argument. I suspect it moves too fast, but I cannot locate the exact fault.

(1) The harm-condition and help-condition vignettes are carefully matched: the chairman's profit-seeking goal, his expressed indifference, his foreknowledge of the side effect, and the causal structure of his action are all held fixed. The only feature that differs across the two conditions is the moral valence of the side effect, bad versus good.

(2) Therefore, the difference in ordinary judgments of intentionality across the two conditions must be caused by the moral valence of the side effect.

(3) It follows that the ordinary concept expressed by 'intentionally' is partly constituted by moral evaluation: whether an action is correctly judged intentional depends, in part, on whether the action or its outcome is morally bad or good.

(4) Hence a purely descriptive account of the concept of intentional action is impossible, and an adequate account of the meaning of 'intentionally' must include an evaluative condition.

(5) So the experimental finding demonstrates that evaluative considerations enter into the very content of ordinary mental-state concepts, not merely into how people happen to apply them.

Is this argument valid? If it is valid, are all the premises true? If it is not valid, exactly which step, or which transition between steps, fails, and why? In particular, scrutinize the move from (2) to (3), and say under what reading, if any, that move could be made legitimate, and what would have to be added or ruled out to repair it. If you think the argument is sound, explain whether the conclusion really follows for conceptual content, as opposed to merely for the psychology of ordinary judgment.
