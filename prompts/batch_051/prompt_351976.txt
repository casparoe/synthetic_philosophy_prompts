Epistemic status: half-baked frame, looking for a model to work the mechanics instead of the standard Thucydides 101 take; dont hold back.

I keep coming back to the Sicilian Expedition as a cleaner stress test for epistemic defenses of democracy than people usually treat it as. Quick reconstruction of the facts I'd want assumed, since I don't want the analysis to depend on anything contested: in 415 BCE the Athenian assembly voted to send a massive armada to Sicily against Nicias' warnings, largely on the strength of Alcibiades' pitch; the decision rested on blatantly false beliefs about Sicilian wealth and about local allies being ready to revolt; Alcibiades then got recalled over the mutilation of the herms and defected to Sparta, leaving Nicias, who had opposed the whole thing, in charge, and the expedition was annihilated. I want this run through the epistemic-democracy toolkit, not one toy model: (a) Condorcet Jury Theorem, (b) information cascades/herding, and (c) correlated errors — shared rhetoric, reputational incentives in a public assembly, the way the crowd's mood systematically distorts information-gathering. Map the case onto each: who are the voters, what were the actual options on the table, what private information did different Athenians plausibly have, and which of the theorem's conditions fails hardest. Then run it in the other direction: what should the episode make an epistemic democrat update? Is it just a clean violation of the independence condition, which the theory can absorb, or does it show that real democratic deliberation systematically manufactures the correlation epistemic defenses need to rule out? Be honest about where the theory goes silent on the things Thucydides cared about most — fear, prestige, the mood of the assembly — and at the end tell me whether this case argues against democracy's truth-tracking pretensions or only against lazy versions of them. Steelman the epistemic democrat before you take the shot.
