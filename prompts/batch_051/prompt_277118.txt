After thirty years in crystallography I retired and joined a philosophy-and-economics reading group, and last week two of the regulars got stuck arguing about bargaining solutions. I may be missstating some of the details, but here's the back-and-forth as I wrote it down.

(1) Diane: Take two parties bargaining over a feasible set of deals with a disagreement point for what happens if they walk away. Nash's solution maximizes the product of their utility gains over the disagreement point, but it violates monotonicity: if the set expands so that player 1's attainable payoff improves at every utility level player 2 might demand, player 1 can still end up with less. Kalai–Smorodinsky fixes this — draw a line from the disagreement point to the "utopia" point where each side gets their individual best payoff, and take the Pareto-best point on that line. A player with better options never ends up worse off.

(2) Alan: But "better options" is doing a lot of work there. Player 1's individual best payoff is usually a deal where player 2 gets almost nothing and would never agree. Why should that jointly unattainable point move the fair share? Nash's independence of irrelevant alternatives is the principle we actually want: if we'd pick A from {A,B}, adding option C that nobody chooses shouldn't flip the agreement to B.

(3) Diane: I don't accept IIA for bargaining, because the menu of alternatives is part of the fairness facts, not a distraction. If C is something you'd take in a heartbeat and I'd hate, its availability changes what a symmetric compromise looks like: you are giving up more by accepting the old agreement, and that can give you a claim to something closer to B. K-S builds that in; Nash's IIA rules it out before the argument even starts.

(4) Alan: And that's exactly the vulnerability. K-S lets me inflate my utopia point with a fanciful alternative that gives me a huge payoff and you next to nothing, and the compromise drifts toward me even though our realistic alternatives haven't changed much. That's option inflation. Nash is more robust because it only looks at trade-offs along the frontier near where the agreement is actually struck, not at pipe dreams.

(5) Diane: More robust isn't the same as principled. Nash's product criterion is just one way to balance the gains; proportional division of the gains is another, older fairness idea, and K-S is that idea made precise. Both solutions have pathologies, but you haven't shown why local trade-offs deserve more say than each side's best possible outcome.

Then they both looked at me and I genuinely couldn't referee it. Please go through move by move and say which claims are right, which are overstated or muddled, and who is more right overall once the real disagreement is isolated.
