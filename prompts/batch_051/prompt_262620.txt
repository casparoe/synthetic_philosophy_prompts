I am not a lawyer and I have never studied legal philosophy, but I have been trying to work through a view about old, unused laws, and I would like you to apply pressure to it rather than defend it. My view is this. When a law has gone unenforced for a long time — say, a city ordinance that has not been enforced at all in decades, while many people openly do the thing it prohibits — the state's inaction does something more than make enforcement surprising or politically costly. It changes the law's standing. The state has, in practice if not in words, told people that the conduct will be tolerated, and people have ordered their lives around that message. On my view, such a law is not merely a bad law or an embarrassing one; it has partially lost its claim to bind. Reviving it is not the same as enforcing a live law, no matter how good the law's original reasons. The long silence itself is a reason against enforcement, and this is a genuine principle — roughly what I have seen called desuetude — not just a prediction about backlash or a rule about how officials happen to behave.

I want to know what would count against this position. I am not asking for a debate speech against it; I want an honest map of where the position is exposed. Which observations, cases, or arguments should genuinely move me, and which would merely be awkward? What would a defender of my view have to concede counts against it, even if they keep the view in the end? I am especially interested in the following lines of attack, and whether they are decisive, serious but survivable, or weak: the rule-of-law worry that a legal system must be able to reassert its own dormant rules, or else every failure of enforcement gradually repeals laws the legislature never agreed to repeal; the difficulty of saying how long, how complete, and how visible the non-enforcement must be before the law loses force; the problem that officials might be unable to enforce a law for reasons that have nothing to do with toleration, such as lack of resources or fear of violence; and the possibility that my view collapses into something less interesting, such as ordinary fair-notice protection or a claim that the law was never really valid to begin with. I would also like to know whether the view is in danger of being insulated — of always being able to explain away counterexamples by saying the non-enforcement was not long enough, not deliberate enough, or not public enough. If it is insulated, is that a vice in a principle of this kind, or is it just what it is for a normative principle to have threshold problems?

Please use schematic or invented examples rather than citing actual statutes or cases, since what I want is the structure of the argument, not the authorities.
