Here's something I can't get straight. If my grandfather helped run a massacre and I stood up decades later and said "I apologise for what he did," nobody would treat that as an apology from the family. It would just be me expressing regret, and a lot of people would find it a bit off — like I was claiming a standing I don't have. But when a state does roughly the same thing — a government today apologises for land grabs, forced removals or a massacre carried out by a governmnent that predates the birth of every current official — the apology is treated as a real act of the state, it gets reported as news, and people expect it to actually change the relationship between the state and the wronged group. So there's an asymmetry in how we think about who can apologise for what: an individual can't apologise across generations, but a state apparently can. I want to know whether anything justifies that asymmetry, or whether it's just a useful fiction we've quietly agreed to live with.

Please answer in four numbered sections, in this order, with these rough lengths.

1. "The asymmetry, sharpened" (about 150 words). Restate the two cases so they differ in as little as possible besides the individual/state line. Be specific about what you take the apology to be doing in each case — expressing an emotion, acknowledging a wrong, changing a relationship, something else.

2. "What could justify the asymmetry" (about 250 words). Give the strongest justification you can find. It might appeal to the continuity of the state as a legal person, the way current members benefit from the wrong, the idea that officials are authorised to speak for the citizenry, or something else. State which feature is doing the real work.

3. "Stress-testing it" (about 250 words). Run that justification against nearby cases: a corporation apologising for a past wrong, a church apologising for abuse, a descendant apologising for an ancestor, a prime minister apologising for something their own government did last year. Say where the justification holds and where it creaks.

4. "Your verdict" (about 100 words). Say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or dropped, and why in a sentence or two.
