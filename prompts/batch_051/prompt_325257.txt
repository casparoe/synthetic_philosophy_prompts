I'm a PhD student working on divine simplicity, and here's a borderline case I've been chewing on for a methods seminar. The standard move to avoid saddling God with parts is to distinguish three grades of difference between attributes: real distinction (the two could exist apart, so you've really got two things), formal distinction (they can't exist apart but the definitions differ, and its not just in our heads), and virtual distinction (one single reality that can produce different effects downstream). So my working criteria are: two attributes F and G of a thing are really distinct iff the thing could have F without G or G without F; formally distinct iff F and G have non-identical definitions but the thing couldn't have one without the other; virtually distinct iff F and G differ only in what they can do or explain, not in the thing itself.

Now the case. Imagine a being whose whole inner life is one unstructured act that is at once a perfect grasp of every truth and a perfect love of every good those truths present. There's no faculty of intellect doing one thing and a faculty of will doing another; there's just the one act, and it is truth-directed under one description and good-directed under another, where those descriptions aren't picking out parts of the act but different ways the act relates to objects. Is that one attribute or two?

Go through the three criteria and say where the case lands under each. Where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, say whether that's because the criteria are bad or because the case was built to have no stable answer. And the thing I actually want to know: does this case show that 'virtual distinction' is doing work the other two grades can't do, or is it just a face-saving label for people who don't want to admit the being has two attributes after all?
