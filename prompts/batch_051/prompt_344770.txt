I teach a small grad seminar in formal epistemology—maybe 14 students, one 100-minute slot—and I want to run a game that makes them feel Aumann's agreement theorem instead of just proving it on the board. The thing I want them to run into bodily is that two Bayesians with the same prior who each have private evidence about the same event can't just shrug and agree to disagree: once each person's probability is written up where everyone can see it, the two numbers have to be the same, and if they aren't, something in the setup has to break—different priors, a hidden assumption, a failure to update on the other person's announcement. Design a structured activity with two volunteers, envelopes or cards for the private signals, and the rest of the room acting as bookies with play money who can post and accept bets at the volunteers' own announced odds. Include the exact common prior and information structure (make it simple but not trivial), the rules for when bets may happen and when the volunteers must re-announce, a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto both the one-shot theorem and the dynamic "keep announcing and revising" result. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—like someone smuggling in a different prior without noticing, or the bookies missing the fact that an honest gap can exist before the announcements become common knowledge—and say which feature of the theorem the game cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply in the wrap-up. Keep it runnable in the room, no computers.
