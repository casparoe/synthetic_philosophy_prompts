I teach an upper-level philosophy of science seminar, and the final unit is on the methodology of long-run social and economic predictions. We ended the unit with a heated exchange about whether Malthus's principle of population can survive its failed timetable by moving the timetable indefinitely, or whether that move quietly dissolves the theory's content. I'm now writing the final exam, and I want an essay question that forces students to resolve that argument in a disciplined way. The question must not be answerable by the two-step story my students have all absorbed — "Malthus failed to foresee technology, but climate change has made him relevant again." It should require them instead to reconstruct the principle as a layered theory, with a central scarcity mechanism surrounded by revisable auxiliary assumptions about land productivity, energy, and fertility, and then to say — with explicit criteria, not slogans — when a long-run prediction has been falsified rather than merely postponed, and when a rescuing revision is responsible science rather than immunization against any possible outcome.

Please draft the exam question (for a fifty-minute closed-book essay), a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks built around five must-hit points. In the marking scheme, state the marks for each point, specify the concrete move a top answer must make, and add a one-line note on the error students typically make at that point. The crux should be the distinction between "the prediction was late" and "the theory has been altered until nothing could refute it," tested against both the post-1800 divergence from Malthus's expectations and the contemporary appeal to planetary limits as a delayed vindication. The question should require students to propose and apply at least two explicit criteria for telling responsible postponement apart from immunization, and the model answer should itself use those criteria rather than merely gesture at them.
