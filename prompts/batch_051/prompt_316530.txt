I'm teaching a unit on collective decision-making in an undergrad social epistemology course, about 18 students, one 75-minute session. I want them to actually feel the discursive dilemma before I name it: three judges can each hold perfectly consistent views on a case, but majority votes on the premises and majority vote on the conclusion point in opposite directions, so the "court" as a group ends up with an inconsistent set of answers -- both elements of liability satisfied, and yet the defendant not liable. No lecture up front; I want the problem to come at them out of the rules of an activity, and the term only comes up in the debrief.

Heres the activity I have in mind. I run them as a three-judge appellate panel on a small contract dispute. Doctrine says the defendant is liable iff there was a valid contract and she breached it. I'd assign three judicial profiles: Judge A holds valid contract=yes, breach=no, so not liable; Judge B holds valid=no, breach=yes, so not liable; Judge C holds valid=yes, breach=yes, so liable. Each judge's own three answers are consistent, but premise-majority gives valid+breach, which licenses liability, while conclusion-majority says not liable. I'm imagining a first round where each judge fills out a worksheet and reads their three answers aloud, then we do a stand-up or colored-card vote issue by issue, and then a second phase where they have to write a unanimous one-paragraph explanation of the court's ruling and discover they can't, or can only do it by one judge caving or by quietly switching aggregation rules. Then a debrief where we map the two phases onto premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation, and onto the more general point that a group can be of two minds even when no member is.

Can you turn this into a runnable plan? Give me a plain-language case handout, the three judge worksheets (and if there's a way to keep the profile pattern from being obvious to the sharp students until after the second vote, tell me), a timeline for the 75 minutes, debrief questions that get them to say the distinction instead of me monologuing, and an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time, plus which feature of the real formal paradox the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself.
