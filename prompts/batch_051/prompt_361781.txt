I'm a software engineer who got dragged into my partner's philosophy of biology reading group, and I've somehow became the grumpy guy who feels like the word "code" is doing too much work whenever someone says "DNA is a code." We're doing a unit on genes and information, and the phrase gets used as if it settles things — last week it was waved in someone's face to dismiss the question of whether "genetic information" is literal or just a metaphor.

The exchange that bugged me: another member said, in so many words, that DNA is "literally a code — a sequence of symbols specifying a product," so genes carry semantic information the way a computer program does, and so a genome is a program for building an organism. I wanted to object, but I realized I couldn't articulate the different things "code" might mean there. As a programmer I know that code in one sense is just instructions something will later interpret; in another it's a cipher; in another it's a message with meaning. I don't know which of these biology is appealing to.

Lay out the distinct precise claims "DNA is a code" could be making, from the relatively uncontroversial claim about a mapping between nucleotide triplets and amino acids up to the strong claim that the genome has semantic content independent of any cell that reads it. For each reading, give a verdict: trivially true, defensible but contested, or just wrong. Say whether the claim requires a sender, a receiver, or an interpreter, and whether the interpreter is itself specified by the code. Finally, tell me which reading would actually license the leap to "genomes are programs," and if none does, where exactly the inference goes wrong.
