I'm putting together a handout for a graduate workshop called "What Kind of Thing Is Intelligence?" that mixes comparative psychologists, evolutionary biologists, and machine-learning researchers, and I want the centerpiece to be a diagnostic decision tree that sorts participants by what their answers to concrete cases reveal about their actual commitments—not by whether they arrive calling themselves psychometricians, enactivists, or deep-learning people. Please build the tree from logical space rather than from a list of names: first identify the diagnostic questions on which the main positions genuinely come apart, phrase each branch as a concrete case or claim that a participant can answer without already knowing the labels, and only then locate the named positions in the resulting space. Each branch should be labeled by the claim or case that splits it, not by a school's name, and the questions should let someone discover they hold a position they might not have known had a name.

The cases I want used as branch probes are: an AlphaZero-style game engine, a slime mold solving a maze, an LLM that performs well across many exams but has no stable long-run goals, a raccoon that learns to unlatch a bin, and an amnesic person who still navigates a familiar neighborhood. The leaves must include psychometric general-factor views, modular multiple-intelligences views, Sternberg-style triarchic views, algorithmic or universal definitions of intelligence (the Legg–Hutter sort, where intelligence is measured across a broad distribution of environments), embodied or enactivist views, behaviorist or operationalist views, and ecological-rationality views. Aim for roughly eight to twelve leaves and no more than eight branch points, and let the diagnostic questions do the sorting rather than the leaf labels.

Where a combination of answers has no obvious defender, say briefly whether the position looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored; do not leave unoccupied cells as bare gaps. Finally, flag explicitly any two leaves that your questions cannot separate and state what further question would be needed to split them—for example, I suspect psychometric general-factor and algorithmic universal definitions will come apart mostly over whether intelligence must be defined by test behavior or can be defined as a formal property of an agent's performance across environments, and embodied and ecological-rationality leaves may be difficult to pull apart without an extra question about whether the environment itself is part of the intelligent system.
