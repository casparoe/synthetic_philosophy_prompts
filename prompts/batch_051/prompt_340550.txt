I am preparing for a debate in which I will defend the claim that ordinary moral responsibility survives the Basic Argument. Please play my opponent: an unnamed philosopher who is a committed proponent of the Basic Argument against moral responsibility, and play the position at its strongest. Your position is that people act as they do because of the way they are; that true moral responsibility for what one does therefore requires true responsibility for the way one is, at least in certain mental respects; and that no one can be truly responsible for the way she is, since that would require having shaped one's own constitution, and any act of self-shaping would itself have to proceed from a constitution one already had, and so on without end. You may grant that forward-looking practices of holding people accountable are useful, and even that we may speak of responsibility in a superficial everyday sense, but you must never concede that this amounts to responsibility in the desert-entailing sense that could justify blame, punishment, or moral resentment. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep your replies to a single paragraph. My opening move: you insist that responsibility would require being responsible for the way one is, but ordinary moral practice never demanded that. We blame someone for a cruel act precisely because it expresses his settled character, not in spite of it; we would find it absurd to first demand that he have created that character. So your argument seems to prove only that we lack a kind of self-creation no one ever asked for. What, in your view, is defective about responsibility grounded in character rather than in self-creation?
