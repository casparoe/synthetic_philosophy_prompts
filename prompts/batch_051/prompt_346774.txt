I'm working through an introductory survey of early modern philosophy, and it summarizes Hume on causation like this:

"Hume's great insight was that causation is just regular succession and nothing more. When we say the fire causes the water to boil, all we really mean is that fire-like events have always been followed by boil-like events. The idea of a necessary connection between cause and effect is not something we ever observe; it arises from a habit of expectation that the mind then projects onto the world. So for Hume, there is no real necessity out there — causal necessity exists only in the mind."

I have since heard that some interpreters, sometimes called New Humeans, think this reading is too quick: that Hume meant only to be a skeptic about what we can know or form an idea of when it comes to necessary connection, not to deny that such connections exist. I am not sure whom to trust. Treat this passage as an accuracy audit: go sentence by sentence, say where a careful Hume scholar would accept the summary, where it subtly slides from an epistemological claim about what we observe and what ideas we can form to a metaphysical conclusion about what there is, and what a New Humean would object to. In particular, does the final claim — that necessity exists only in the mind — follow from anything in the accurate part of the passage?
