A while back I was at a dinner where two friends of mine, Nadia and Marcus, spent most of the evening arguing about a novel the three of us had read in translation. The argument began with the question whether some words are simply untranslatable, but it quickly became about what a translator owes a reader when that seems to be true. The book's closing line depends on a single word in the original language that means both "host" and "guest"; the ending works because the two characters have, in a sense, swapped roles, and the reader is not supposed to be able to settle which one is receiving the other. The published English translation renders the word as "guest," which makes the last line read cleanly but removes the ambiguity.

Nadia said the translator made the wrong choice. Since the word is doing real work in that final line, translating it as if it simply meant "guest" quietly changes what the book says; the translator could have written something like "host and guest," or kept the original word with a note, and the fact that those options are less elegant is exactly the cost of being honest about a meaning English doesn't carry in a single word. Marcus said that is a strange standard for a novel: a translation is supposed to live as a piece of English writing, not as a museum of the original, and a phrase like "host and guest" at the emotional peak of the book would make the reader notice the translator's solution instead of the characters. Nadia replied that noticing the solution may be less comfortable, but the present translation is comfortable only by making the author seem to say something he didn't say; the translator used fluency as a cover. Marcus answered that every language has things that don't line up, so if a translator were required to preserve all of them, translation would turn into annotation; a translator inevitably produces a new book, and the only obligation that isn't impossible is to make that new book good. Nadia said that was precisely where the disagreement lived: a translator's power to make a foreign book sound native is a power to put words in the author's mouth, and she would rather have the loss announced than papered over, while Marcus thinks announcing the loss is giving up on the task. They turned to me to referee, and I found that each time one of them landed a point, the other had a decent answer, so I let it drop.

I'd like you to go through their exchange and adjudicate it point by point: which claims each of them is right about, where each of them overreaches or mischaracterizes the other, what neither of them considered, and then, overall, who is more right about the translator's decision. Please also say how confident you are in your overall verdict, and single out the part of your adjudication you are least sure of. Answer in American English.
