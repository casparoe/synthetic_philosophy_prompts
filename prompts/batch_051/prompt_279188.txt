I'm a novelist, and I've been researching cryonics for a book, and somewhere along the way I actually signed up. Now I can't tell whether the views I ended up with are one position or three. Heres where I am:

(1) Signing up seems clearly rational: if revival has even a 0.01% chance, the expected value in extra years is enormous compared to the annual fee. It's a simple EV calculation.

(2) I also think Pascal-style muggings are bogus. If someone says give me $10,000 and you'll get an astronomically large or infinite reward in some unspecified way, I think you should ignore it, even though expected value says pay. Huge unfalsifiable payoffs shouldn't be allowed to swamp every decision, so the value of an outcome has to be bounded somehow.

(3) In the rest of my life I mostly don't act on tiny probabilities of huge outcomes. I don't play the lottery, I don't prep for low-probability catastrophes, and I think people who rearrange their lives around 0.01% chances are usually making a mistake. Cryonics feels different, but I cant say why it's different except that the payoff is more of my own life.

(4) I believe personal identity is psychological continuity: if the procedure preserves my memories and character through the right causal route, the revived person is me. But then I think about my current stream of awareness stopping, and a later person waking up with my memories, and that person just seems like a copy, not someone I should care about the way I care about my own future.

(5) I also believe that when a probability is genuinely unquantifiable, pretending it's a number and running expected value is self-deception. You should fall back on something like: the cost is small, the harm of being wrong is small, and it might help a lot. Honestly that's closer to my actual reason for signing up.

Does this hang together? If not, I can't see which parts genuinely conflict and which just look like they conflict. Be specific about which commitment has to give, or what the cheapest repair is, and what it costs.
