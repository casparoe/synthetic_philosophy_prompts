One of the RL people in my lab has an argument that seems to show we can never know what any optimizer is actually optimizing—or what space it's searching over—and I cant find the move that escapes it. Here's the version I wrote down from him.

(1) When you observe an agent, all you ever collect is a finite set of history–action pairs: what the agent has seen so far, and what it did.
(2) For any such finite set, there is a reward function that makes the observed actions exactly optimal: reward 1 for taking the observed action in exactly that history, 0 otherwise. Then "the agent is a perfect maximizer of this reward" fits the data perfectly.
(3) Infinitely many other reward functions fit equally well and make different predictions for unseen histories. So the observations can't single out the correct one.
(4) More observations never break the tie, because at any finite stage the same trick applies. So no amount of behavior can justify preferring one reward function to another.
(5) Hence behavior alone can never justify inferring an objective. The same style of argument applies to the search space: "maximizes X over space A" and "maximizes X' over some jerry-rigged space B" can be made to fit the same behavior.
(6) Since humans are just agents you observe, and you've only seen a finite amount of anyone's behavior, you can't justifiably say your flatmate went to the kitchen because she wanted coffee, or that a chess engine is trying to win rather than trying to approximate some weird tablebase-lookalike.
(7) But of course we know those things all the time, and fields like behavioral ecology, economics, and ML safety do exactly this. So either they're systematically unjustified or one of (1)–(5) is false.

Which premise or transition should I break, and what do I pay for it? I'd least like to give up (7), and I dont want a solution that just says "we guess," because then half of applied science gets downgraded to guessing.
