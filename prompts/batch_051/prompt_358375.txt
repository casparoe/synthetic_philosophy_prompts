Day job: I teach a seminar on conceptual engineering, and I keep going back and forth with a colleague about where fixing a concept stops and updating a concept to fit the facts starts. I've settled on four marks of a genuinely ameliorative or engineering project, as opposed to plain descriptive conceptual analysis:

(a) it targets a concept, or the role a term plays in thought and practice, not just a word or label;
(b) the motivation is the concept's poor fit with our purposes or values, not just new empirical findings;
(c) the proposed change is defended in terms of what the concept should do for us — what it ought to track or enable — not just what the thing in fact is;
(d) the point of the change is downstream effects in classification or practice, not just a cleaner theory.

Now the borderline case. A state wildlife agency is revising its regulatory definition of an invasive species. Currently it is, roughly, a non-native species whose introduction causes or is likely to cause harm. The working group proposes to amend it so that a species whose range has shifted on its own because of climate change does not count as invasive when it arrives in a new area, even if it is non-native there and causes harm. Their stated reason: the old definition gets the underlying phenomenon wrong — these species were not 'introduced' by anyone, so calling them invasive is just a scientific mistake. But they also say the old definition is becoming harmful: it lumps climate-displaced species together with human-introduced pests and pushes managers into killing species they would otherwise protect, so the definition should be changed so that classification steers practice toward the outcomes we now want. They claim their merely bringing the definition in line with what 'invasive' really means, while their critics say they are quietly re-engineering the concept to fit new values.

Go through my four marks one at a time and commit to a verdict: is this conceptual engineering in the ameliorative sense, a descriptive correction, both, or neither. Don't just lay out the readings; say which it is and why. Then tell me whether the case exposes a flaw in one of the four marks — say, a mark that can't separate this case from ordinary scientific revision, or a mark that is doing no work. Work the example from the marks themselves; no need for citations or named authors.
