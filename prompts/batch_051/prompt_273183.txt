I have been thinking about how emergency measures are treated, and there is a pattern I cannot get to cohere. In a public health crisis, a government imposes lockdowns, border controls, quarantines, and limits on assembly and movement under a broad emergency statute. The restrictions are announced quickly, with little real scrutiny, and are enforced with fines or arrests. Later, a court sometimes holds that a particular restriction was never legally justified: it exceeded what the statute authorized, or the evidence did not support its specific burdens, or it singled out one group without adequate reason. That much is not strange.

What is strange is what happens after. The officials who issued and enforced the invalid restriction are not punished, provided they believed in good faith that some restriction was necessary. Fines already collected from people who violated it are not returned to them as wrongful seizures. People who obeyed the restriction while it stood are not told they made a mistake; they are usually described as having done the right thing, while people who quietly defied it are described as reckless even when the rule they defied later turns out to have lacked legal authority. And the same courts that strike these measures down once the crisis is over are often much more deferential while the crisis is ongoing, so a measure can be effectively unchallengeable as long as the danger lasts and indefensible once it lifts.

Suppose this is a recognizable, if simplified, account of how emergency powers work. What philosophical commitments does the practice presuppose about the relation between a measure's being legally valid and its being legitimate — that is, between its having been lawfully made and its deserving to be obeyed? Does the practice rest on one coherent view of that relation, or does it quietly treat the same measure as dead for some purposes and alive for others? And if we forced the pieces to be consistent, which would have to be given up — the officials' immunity, the retention of fines, the praise of compliance, or the courts' real-time deference — and what would the changed practice presuppose instead?
