Consider the familiar case of Buridan's ass: a donkey is placed midway between two bales of hay that are identical in every respect relevant to its hunger, so that no reason favors one bale over the other, and the standard moral is that a perfectly rational agent cannot choose without a preference and therefore starves. Now change the case in one precise way. One of the bales in fact contains slightly more hay than the other, but the difference falls below the donkey's just-noticeable difference: from the midpoint, the two bales are perceptually indistinguishable. Moreover, before the donkey looks, a reliable sign tells it that exactly one bale is larger, though not which one. The donkey therefore knows that there is a reason pointing toward one particular bale, while also knowing that it does not know which bale that is, and that it could not find out by looking. Analyze how this variation affects the standard diagnosis of the original case. In particular: does the donkey now have a reason to choose a particular bale, or only a reason to believe that such a reason exists? How should a proponent of the principle of sufficient reason formulate the principle—in terms of objective symmetry between the options, or in terms of the agent's informational state—so that it yields consistent verdicts across the original case and this variation? Does a defender of the view that indifference is compatible with arbitrary choice treat the variation exactly like the original, and what does that continuity show about which feature of the original case was doing the philosophical work? Can a stochastic tie-break, such as going left if a tossed coin lands heads, dissolve the deadlock, or does adopting the tie-break itself require a reason the donkey lacks? Finally, state where you are least confident in your answer and which further fact or theoretical commitment would most change your conclusion.
