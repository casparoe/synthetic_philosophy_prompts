You are a political philosopher retained by the Commission on Political Obligation, a standing body that issues reasoned decisions about when a person is bound to obey the commands of a political authority. The Commission decides new petitions by coherence with its own recorded precedents, not by ad hoc moralizing, and it has asked you to prepare an opinion in a hard new case. Think carefully and do not rush to the answer. Begin by brainstorming the candidate principles that fit the Commission's recorded precedents, note which precedents rule out which principles, identify the closest analogs on each side of the new petition, and only then recommend a decision. If the precedents underdetermine the result, say so explicitly: explain what principle the Commission would be adopting under each possible outcome, and describe the smallest further case that would settle the matter.

Recorded precedents:

1. The Founders' Covenant. About five thousand people in a state of nature, with no common power able to bind them, explicitly covenant with one another to institute a sovereign with authority to make and enforce law. The Commission holds that every signatory is politically obligated to obey that authority.

2. The Ordinary Subject. A person born within a well-functioning commonwealth never signed any founding covenant. Throughout her life she votes, calls on the police, uses public roads and parks, and relies on the courts. The Commission holds that she is politically obligated.

3. The Isolated Hermit. A person lives in an ungoverned border region, has no contact with the commonwealth's institutions, receives no protection from it, and asks nothing from it. The Commission holds that he is not politically obligated.

4. The Short-Term Visitor. A foreign tourist spends three weeks in the commonwealth, uses its roads and emergency services, and benefits from public safety. She has no intention of remaining or becoming a member. The Commission holds that she is not under the commonwealth's political obligations, though she remains bound by the ordinary criminal and traffic laws while present.

5. The Warlord's Peace. After the collapse of a commonwealth, an armed faction ends the street violence, runs summary tribunals, and provides de facto protection to everyone in the territory. The faction's rule is arbitrary in important respects and is not conducted through general, public law. A resident who has not consented, who does not participate, and who receives the faction's protection because it is impossible to avoid is held by the Commission not to be politically obligated to the faction's leader.

New petition: The Reluctant Resident. A new commonwealth has formed and is, by the Commission's own standards, just and well functioning: it governs through general public laws, provides equal legal protection and civil rights, and preserves peace throughout its territory. A woman resides within its borders and receives several goods she cannot practically opt out of — military defense against invasion, public health measures, and a legal order that protects her against private violence. She formally and publicly renounces membership, refuses every optional benefit she is able to refuse, declines to vote, and states that she accepts nothing from the commonwealth. She would emigrate to an ungoverned territory if any existed, but none does, and the commonwealth controls every border. The question before the Commission is whether she has a political obligation to obey the commonwealth's laws.

Draft the opinion. What should the Commission decide if it wants to be consistent with its own precedents, which precedent is the closest analog, and if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the result, what principle would the Commission be adopting either way?
