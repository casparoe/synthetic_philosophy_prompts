I have been reading around in epistemology and keep returning to a classic maneuver known as the bootstrapping problem. The stock presentation runs roughly as follows. A subject reads a fuel gauge and, on that basis, comes to believe the tank is full; she then puts together the gauge's reading and the tank's being full and concludes that the gauge read correctly on this occasion; by repeating this move over many occasions, she accumulates a track record that seems to confirm that the gauge is reliable—all without ever checking the tank in any independent way. The troubling feature is that her only access to the facts that confirm the gauge's reliability is the gauge itself, so the confirmation looks circular.

The classic presentation, however, assumes a source that delivers bare verdicts. I want to know whether the problem survives when the source also reports on itself. Suppose a forecasting system—a weather model, or an app that predicts commute times—delivers each forecast along with a confidence score and a running accuracy figure, all produced by the same underlying mechanism that produces the forecast. A user who has never independently checked any of its forecasts nonetheless becomes convinced that the system is reliable because its accuracy log says so, and because the log even records the occasions on which its confidence was misplaced. Does the bootstrapping problem still arise in its original form? If it does, what exactly does the self-monitoring fail to add to the user's epistemic position? If it does not, what principle distinguishes this case from the fuel gauge? I am interested in the structure of the argument, not in any practical verdict about whether one should trust such apps.
