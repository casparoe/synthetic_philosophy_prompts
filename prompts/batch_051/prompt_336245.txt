I cover science and ideas for a general readership, and I keep noticing that the hardest parts of the stories I report are not empirical but conceptual. Lately I have been following a dispute over a regulatory threshold — when an automated decision system becomes high-risk enough to trigger oversight — and the fight, once you scrape away the lobbying, is the sorites paradox. The rule works perfectly for clear cases at either end, but moving step by step through the grey zone forces the question whether some precise cut-off is already there in the meaning of the term even though no one can find it.

On one side, I find the epistemic treatment of vagueness genuinely compelling. It says vague predicates do have sharp boundaries, but those boundaries are unknowable to us: our words have more precise extensions than our use of them can reveal. The payoff is that ordinary two-valued logic survives intact. We do not have to abandon bivalence or blame modus ponens for the paradox; a long chain of conditionals really does fail at exactly one step, we just cannot tell which. On the other side, I find it almost impossible to believe there is a fact of the matter hiding in the grey zone. When a regulator must choose between adjacent risk scores, the idea that one particular point is, as a matter of the meaning already fixed, the first high-risk case feels to me and to everyone I interview less like an unknowable truth and more like no truth at all: our linguistic and institutional practices simply do not discriminate between neighboring cases, and any line imposed on them is a stipulation rather than a discovery. I am not asking to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the no-hidden-line intuition to be the thing that yields, what it would cost for the epistemic theory to be the thing that yields, and whether there is a reading of there being a fact of the matter — perhaps one sensitive to how social and legal kinds are constituted by actual decisions — on which both the logic-preserving theory and the anti-arbitrariness intuition can survive.
