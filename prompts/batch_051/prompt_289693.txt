I keep going back and forth on whether a reinforcement learning agent's reward signal should count as valence in any ethically significant sense—in particular, whether a sufficiently sophisticated RL agent that receives negative reward can be said to suffer, where suffering means being in an aversive state that matters from the agent's own point of view. I have collected the following arguments for the negative answer: that the reward signal, by itself, cannot ground an attribution of suffering.

1. Scalar error: reward is just a number used in a parameter update. "Lower than expected" is a fact about the learning algorithm, not a felt state for the agent.
2. Wrong kind of origin: in RL the reward is an externally supplied objective given by the designers, whereas valence in a creature is generated by the creature's own homeostatic and evaluative machinery. The two have different kinds of normativity.
3. Behavioral sufficiency: everything negative reward seems to explain—avoidance, caution, risk sensitivity—can be produced by systems we feel no temptation to regard as feeling anything. So reward buys a behavioral prediction, not a phenomenal one.
4. No owner: for a harm to be felt, it must be felt by someone. RL agents need not contain a self-model or any structure that integrates signals into a single first-person perspective; a parameter update has no subject.
5. Reward prediction error is not pain: in biological systems pain and negative reward prediction error dissociate—there can be pain without negative RPE and negative RPE without felt pain—so identifying RL reward with valence imports a claim about brains that RL theory alone does not license.

I want one more argument for the same negative thesis that is not a restatement, special case, or combination of any of these five. Then tell me honestly whether the new argument is any good, and whether it is genuinely independent of the ones I already have or secretly one of them in disguise.
