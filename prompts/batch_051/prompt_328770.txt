There's a move in arguments about whether we should have more people that keeps tripping me up, and I can't tell if it has a name or if I'm watching people smuggle in a premise.

First case. A friend argues that a child allowance shouldn't count the well-being of the children who would be born because of it as a benefit, since those children wouldn't exist under the alternative. It isn't as if the policy makes any actual child better off; without the policy there's no child there to be worse off. So their future happiness, he says, is not a point in favor of the allowance. Another friend says that's obviously wrong: if two policies differ only in that one leads to more people leading good lives, that policy is better, and you don't need to point to some particular person who's better off for it to be true.

Second case. A debate over whether to build a new city on a forest. One side says the city will house thousands of people over the next century, most of whom will be glad to live there, so that's a point for the city. The other side says none of those future people could complain if the forest stayed a forest, because they wouldn't exist to complain, so their would-be happiness counts for nothing either way. The well-being ledger is a wash, they conclude, so the choice has to be settled on other grounds.

What puzzles me is the slide from "this doesn't make any particular person better off" to "this doesn't make the world better." Those aren't the same claim. The second only follows if you assume a rule I don't have a name for: roughly, that one outcome can be better than another only if it's better for some person. And here's the part that really confuses me: the same people who refuse to count happy future people as a benefit seem perfectly willing to count miserable future people as a cost. If a policy would lead to children with awful lives, suddenly the ledger isn't a wash anymore. So the practice is lopsided: possible good lives get treated as irrelevant, possible bad lives as highly relevant.

Is there an established name for this package — the slide, the underlying rule, and the lopsidedness? I know it's somewhere near the non-identity problem, but that one's usually about whether you harm the people you create; here the fight is over whether their happiness is even eligible to matter. If the package doesn't have a single name, what are the names of its pieces, and does the rule actually commit you to the lopsidedness, or have people been combining two views that can come apart?
