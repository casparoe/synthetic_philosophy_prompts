A city's annual budget process has gotten so long and acrimonious that the council decides it can't keep trying to rank every request against every other one. So it adopts this procedure. The mayor's office publishes an aspiration level: a number meant to capture the minimum citywide benefit per dollar that a proposal has to clear to be fundable. Departments submit project proposals, and the council evaluates them in a random order, funding each submission that clears the bar until the general fund runs out. Unspent money rolls into a reserve. If the reserve stays above a fixed level for two years, the aspiration level automatically rises by 10% next year; if a department has every submission fall below the bar for two consecutive years, the bar gets lowered by 5% the following year. The stated rationale is that the city doesn't need the optimal budget, just a bunch of clearly good-enough projects.

Walk through what this actually does over, say, five or ten years. What do departments learn to do with the bundles, timings, and cost estimates in there proposals? Who can game the random review order, and how? What happens to the aspiration level given that departments can also lobby to have the benefit-per-dollar scores revised? Is there a stable equilibrium, or does it cycle? And does the process end up being less cognitively and politically costly than the old fight, or does it just move the fight somewhere else?
