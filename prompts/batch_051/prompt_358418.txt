Over the past few years I have been following the argument about whether rational thinking can be trained the way martial arts are trained: decomposed drills, kata-like routines for particular reasoning moves, and graded sparring with increasingly realistic problems. The debate has reached a strange stalemate. Both camps now cite the same studies and no longer mainly dispute the data: trainees improve on the drills themselves and on near copies of them, but the improvements rarely appear in unprompted, messy, real-world reasoning. People can become fluent at spotting sunk-cost reasoning in written scenarios and still show no measurable change in their own sunk-cost behavior. One side reads the pattern as 'the training works; transfer is merely slow and hard to measure.' The other reads it as 'rationality is not one general skill but many domain-specific skills, and the martial-arts model is the wrong picture.' The debate is no longer about the evidence; it is about what counts as the trained skill showing up outside the dojo. My hypothesis is that the two sides mean different things by 'transfer' — one counts the learned procedure whenever it is invoked with scaffolding or in a recognizable frame, the other only when it is used spontaneously in a genuinely new domain — and that they may differ about whether 'rationality' names a single capacity or a family of practices. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a verbal disagreement, a factual disagreement, a disagreement about standards of success, or two groups answering different questions under one heading? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress — an experimental design, a formal account of transfer, a redefinition of the object of study — or is the impasse built into the way the question is posed?
