I serve on a state tax reform commission charged with deciding how to tax wealth transferred at death. The staff proposal before us would replace our current estate tax — a flat levy on an entire estate before distribution, with a large exemption — with an accessions tax that taxes each recipient on what they receive during their lifetime, cumulatively, at progressive rates, with a per-person lifetime exemption. The staff argue that the proper object of tax is not how much a decedent accumulated but how much unearned advantage each heir collects: a large fortune left intact to one child would be taxed heavily because it entrenches dynastic advantage, while the same fortune divided among many grandchildren would be taxed lightly or not at all, because no one recipient receives a life-changing share. Commissioners on the other side reply that the proposal double-taxes money already taxed when it was earned, penalizes the donor's right to use her savings for her children, and conflates equal opportunity with equal outcomes; they add that inherited wealth is a minor determinant of unequal starts compared with schools, neighborhoods, and labor markets, so the proposal would be mostly symbolic.

The commission must choose among three positions: retain the current estate tax; adopt the accessions tax as proposed; or retain the estate tax and add a surtax on very large individual inheritances while otherwise leaving the regime unchanged.

Draft the commission's policy memo recommending one of these positions. I want the memo to take a definite, actionable position and defend it, not to survey the trade-offs and stop. Give the strongest consideration against your recommendation an honest hearing, and state explicitly which philosophical assumptions about inherited advantage, equal opportunity, and the permissible scope of taxation your recommendation rests on.
