I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and the same problem keeps surfacing in stories about finance, health, and public policy: a claim arrives as an average, and I suspect the average misrepresents what will happen to a single actual person. I have been reading about the difference between ensemble averages and time averages. An ensemble average is taken across many parallel copies of a process at one moment; a time average is what a single trajectory accumulates as the process repeats. When a process is non-ergodic, the two come apart. A gamble that multiplies your wealth by 1.5 on heads and by 0.6 on tails has an expected value of 1.05 per flip, so the ensemble-average wealth rises, but someone who repeatedly stakes everything on it sees wealth shrink over time, because the relevant growth rate is the geometric mean — the square root of 1.5 times 0.6, about 0.949. I am convinced this distinction explains a good deal of misleading coverage, but I do not yet have a way to use it under a deadline.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure — a checklist I can run in about fifteen minutes, using basic numeracy and no stochastic-process theory — for deciding whether an average reported in a study, a press release, or a claim will mislead my readers about what happens over time, and how I should restate it when it will. The procedure should ask its questions in a fixed order; say what counts as a sufficient answer to each; mark where it stops with 'not reportable as stated'; mark where it hands the matter to editorial judgment because the question is one of values rather than facts; and distinguish the cases in which the ensemble average is the right number from those in which the time average, the geometric mean, or the shape of the distribution is the honest summary — for example, a one-off pooled risk, where an arithmetic average may be exactly right, against a repeated individual decision, where it may not be. Then run the procedure on these examples and give me the headline or lede each supports: (a) an investment newsletter recommends the heads-tails gamble above on the strength of its positive expected value; (b) a local news story reports that a neighborhood's average income rose 15 percent over a decade because affluent newcomers moved in, while longtime residents' incomes were flat; (c) a health story reports that a procedure carries a 1 percent risk of a serious complication per year and concludes that over twenty years the risk is 20 percent. Include at least one worked example in which your own procedure reaches a conclusion I would rather not print, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot do.
