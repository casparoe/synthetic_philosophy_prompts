I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've spent the past year reading around Wittgenstein's On Certainty after stumbling onto it through a blog about machine learning and common-sense reasoning. The claim I keep encountering, as I understand it, is that some commitments aren't ordinary evidence-based beliefs. Commitments like — the world has been around for a long time, the future will keep broadly resembling the past, our memory and senses are roughly trustworthy — are not normally tested against evidence because they are the scaffolding that lets evidence count as evidence in the first place. When someone "doubts" them in a seminar, we tend to treat it as a performance rather than a serious empirical possibility. You don't run an experiment on whether the past is real; you'd already be using the past to design the experiment.

I want to treat this as a test-design problem, because that's the language I think in. Is the claim that a commitment is a hinge, rather than merely a strongly held, high-confidence empirical belief, empirically testable at the level of an individual mind? I'd like you to sketch the strongest test battery you can. Probes I've been mulling over: asking people what evidence they would accept as counting against a candidate hinge, and comparing that with their answers for ordinary high-confidence beliefs; presenting them with clever but fake demonstrations that a hinge is false and coding whether they investigate, suspend judgment, debunk, or laugh; offering small real-money bets against the hinge or against an ordinary belief; and looking for systematic mismatches between what people say under skeptical framing and how they actually act and plan. For each probe, say what result would count for the hinge reading, what would count against it, and what confounds would render it ambiguous. Then step back and say what a clean positive result still wouldn't settle: whether we've learned about epistemic structure or just about how people behave when they feel tested, whether a hinge is a belief or an ability or an attitude, and whether our classification is a genuine kind or an artifact of our tests.

Please answer as a professional philosopher who works both in hinge epistemology and in the overlap of philosophy with cognitive science, and think carefully before committing to a test design; begin by pinning down the target claim, because I suspect "hinge" is doing several jobs at once. I'd also like you to separate what is settled among people who know this literature from what remains genuinely contested, and not to present a contested view — for instance, about whether hinges are beliefs at all, or whether they can be rationally evaluated — as the consensus. If the testable version of the claim is a weaker or different claim from the philosophically interesting one, say so explicitly rather than letting the operationalization substitute for the original idea.
