I'm a software engineer on a team that builds flood and traffic simulations for a consultancy, and about once a week someone files a ticket saying a model "gives a different result every time we run it." Probably half of those are real non-determinism bugs — an uninitialised variable, a race condition, iterating over a dict in an order that changes between runs — but a lot of the time the model is just doing what I understand chaotic systems do: a tiny difference in a starting value, or even in floating-point rounding, grows until two runs look nothing alike after a while. The team splits on this every single time, and we burn hours arguing about whether a ticket is a bug or just the butterfly effect. I've read bits and pieces about chaos theory — sensitivity to initial conditions, strange attractors, the idea that deterministic doesn't mean predictable — but I don't know how to turn any of it into something I can use when a ticket lands, and our clients use these models for real decisions about flood defences and road layouts.

I'd like a reusable decision procedure our team can actually run on each ticket in under an hour, without anyone needing the theory. It should lay out the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure should stop and say "this is a bug" versus "this is accepted sensitivity to initial conditions" versus "hand this to a person because it depends on what we need the model for, and that person has to sign off." It should also give us an operational sense of what "unpredictable" should and shouldn't mean for a model we're shipping. The hard cases we keep hitting are: bit-identical reruns that still differ, runs that only diverge after some threshold, runs where the inputs were never meant to be exact in the first place, and runs where the difference is within what we'd otherwise call acceptable noise. Then run the procedure on three tickets: (1) a flood model that diverges after hour 6 of a 24-hour run when we nudge one parameter by one part in a billion, (2) a traffic model that gives different totals on two runs with identical inputs but on different machines, and (3) a flood model where two runs differ by about 4 percent in the predicted peak level but agree on which areas flood. Include at least one case where the procedure tells us something we won't like, and be honest about what the procedure can't settle.

When you set out the theory underneath the procedure, could you seperate the parts that are settled from the parts that are genuinely contested among people who actually work on this stuff, and not present a contested bit as if it were the consensus? I'm not a philosopher or a mathematician — I just need something an engineer can trust.
