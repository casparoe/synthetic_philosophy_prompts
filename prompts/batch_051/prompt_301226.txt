I have been working on a project that requires classifying mechanistic anomalies: cases in which a system that normally produces some outcome O instead produces an unexpected outcome O′—a cell line that dies under a treatment that usually leaves it untouched, a trained neural network that produces a bizarre failure mode, a metabolic pathway that starts emitting a harmful intermediate. In each case the practical question is whether we are looking at the same mechanism misbehaving within its normal operating range, or at a different mechanism that has taken over or been assembled on the fly. That practical question seems to force a conceptual one: what individuates a mechanism in the first place?

The view I have been defending is this. A mechanism is individuated by its organized parts and operations—the entities, activities, and their spatial and temporal organization at whatever level of description explains the phenomenon of interest—rather than by the phenomenon it happens to produce, by its normal function, or by its coarse-grained input–output profile. On this view, two systems can share a function while containing different mechanisms, and a single mechanism can underlie different phenomena when its context, inputs, or regulatory conditions change. The hard cases are precisely the ones that matter for anomaly detection. If I knock out one component and the rest of the network reorganizes so that O is still produced, I want to say the mechanism has changed while the phenomenon has not. If the same organized parts act in the same way but a downstream shift means they now produce O′, I want to say the mechanism is the same while the phenomenon has changed. I also suspect that sameness-of-mechanism is always relative to a chosen level of decomposition, and that two descriptions at different grains can both be correct without being competing accounts of the very same mechanism.

I would like an honest map of what would count against this view. Which cases, arguments, or results from scientific practice—about degeneracy and robustness in biology, multiple realizability, networks that reorganize to preserve function, or the way the phenomenon itself seems to fix the boundaries of the parts—should make me abandon the parts-and-organization criterion, or at least revise it substantially? Please distinguish considerations that a committed defender of the view would concede as genuine counter-pressure from considerations it would dismiss as changing the subject or conflating levels. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Also say what my criterion predicts about the hard cases that its main rivals—individuation by function, by the phenomenon produced, or by input–output profile—do not, and whether any of those differential predictions have in effect already been tested by scientific practice. Finally, address whether the view, as I have stated it, has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that would be a problem for a criterion of this kind or merely what one should expect of an interest-relative notion. I suspect I would find considerations from degeneracy most difficult to absorb, but I want the full map rather than only my suspected weak point.
