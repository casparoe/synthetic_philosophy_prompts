In my government class, we have been debating whether it is ever legitimate for unelected judges to strike down laws passed by an elected legislature, and our teacher gave us four working criteria for when such a decision is justified. A court may invalidate a statute only when (1) the statute conflicts with a reasonably specific provision of a written constitution, not merely with the judges' preferred general principles; (2) the resulting constitutional violation is serious enough that leaving the statute in place while the legislature considers a fix would cause ongoing, irreparable harm; (3) the court's remedy is narrow, in the sense that it invalidates no more of the statute than is needed to stop the violation and does not replace the invalidated provisions with a regulatory scheme of its own; and (4) the constitutional norm being enforced enjoys broad and stable public support, rather than simply reflecting values the judges happen to hold.

I have been trying to apply these criteria to a case I invented after reading about several recent disputes. A country's constitution contains a single terse clause guaranteeing freedom of expression, and nothing more. After a wave of deaths linked to medical misinformation, the legislature passes a statute making it an offense for a social-media platform to keep up any post that asserts a factual claim about a medical treatment which the national public-health agency has placed on its list of false or misleading claims. The statute has no exception for satire, for quoting a listed claim in order to criticize it, or for disagreement with the agency's list, and it applies even when the claim is posted by a licensed physician offering a sincerely held but unorthodox medical opinion. The agency's list is updated without any public hearing, and several independent researchers have shown that it includes some claims over which qualified experts genuinely disagree. The country's courts have traditionally protected political and artistic speech most strongly while allowing much broader regulation of advertising and professional advice. A constitutional court rules that the statute chills legitimate medical debate and gives an administrative body the power to decide which ideas may be expressed. The judges rely heavily on the familiar idea that open debate is the best way to expose falsehood, a rationale that appears nowhere in the constitution's text. The court formally invalidates only the statute's enforcement sections, leaving the reporting requirements standing, but without those enforcement sections the statute has no practical effect. Public opinion is divided: most citizens want tighter controls on medical misinformation, but many also worry about giving the government power to define acceptable opinion.

According to the four criteria, is the court's decision a legitimate exercise of judicial review? Work through the criteria one by one, and point out where they fail to settle the answer. Does this case reveal a flaw in any of the four criteria — for instance, that a seriously harmful law could satisfy all four, or that the criteria allow more judicial power than is safe? If so, propose the smallest revision you would make and explain why it fixes the problem without creating a worse one.
