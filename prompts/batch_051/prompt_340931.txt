An epistemology textbook I’m reading gives this summary of Nozick’s tracking theory, and something about it feels a little off to me but I can’t pin down what.

The book says: Nozick analyzes knowledge as true belief that tracks the truth. The extra ingredient is sensitivity—if the proposition were false, you wouldn’t believe it. That is why you can know you have hands, since if you lacked them you would presumably notice, but you cannot know you are not a handless brain in a vat, because if you were envatted you would still believe you weren’t. From this Nozick concludes that knowledge is not closed under known entailment. Critics treat that as a reductio, since you could knowingly accept both that you have hands and that you don’t know you’re not a handless brain in a vat, while admitting the first entails the second. But defenders, the book says, reply that closure was never really an intuitive requirement—it’s just a leftover Cartesian assumption.

As an expert epistemologist, could you help me audit this? I especially want to know whether sensitivity is the whole story, whether Nozick rejected closure outright or only in certain cases, and whether the critics’ reductio objection is actually as decisive as the book makes it sound.
