I'm designing a coding manual for a classroom study and I keep getting stuck on the same prior question. We're going to watch high-schoolers solve circuit problems over several weeks, and for each wrong answer we want to code it as one of three things: (a) a coherent alternative theory the student actually holds (the classic "the battery runs out of current" stuff), (b) a context-sensitive rule of thumb — or p-prim, if I have the term right — that they apply opportunistically and inconsistently, or (c) a plain missing fact, like not knowing what a resistor does. Before I write the manual I want to know what any theory of systematic student errors has to get right for that three-way classification to be principled rather than vibes.

So what would a theory of student misconceptions actually have to get right? Give me the requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each. It needs to cover the phenomena, like the same kid giving a textbook "current gets used up" answer on one problem and a correct answer on an isomorphic problem ten minutes later, or an error that survives two rounds of instruction and then pops back up three weeks after the test. It also needs to respect distinctions that keep getting collapsed: systematic isn't the same as theory-like, and a productive error (a wrong rule that works fine on every example the student has seen) is a different thing from a misconception they're actually committed to. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of particular camps. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to knock out the most popular theories — my money's on the consistency one, since half the time people treat inconsistency as a decisive objection to the "coherent theory" view and half the time they treat it as exactly what you'd expect if errors are just context-sensitive fragments, but honestly I go back and forth. If some subset of the requirements can't be met at the same time, say so, because that's the part I actually need. Dont bother scoring named researchers.
