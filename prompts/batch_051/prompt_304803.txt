I started a new job last spring and a conversation there has been nagging at me. The company pays its senior engineers around six times what it pays the cleaners, and when I asked about it everyone gave the same line: if we flattened pay, the best engineers would leave for a competitor, the place would shrink, and the cleaners would end up worse of than they are now. So the inequality is supposed to make the worst-off people better off — which, as I remember it from a college course, is basically Rawls's difference principle applied to a firm.

But then I thought about my siblings and our small family business. If one of us said, "I should take the biggest share because otherwise I'll start my own thing and you'll all make less," the rest of us would call it a shakedown, not a fair split. Same basic justification: the inequality leaves the worst-off absolutely better off than the alternative. Yet in a big company, or a whole economy, we think that kind of incentive inequality is normal, even fair, and in a family or among friends it sounds indecent.

So here's the asymmetry I want you to take apart: does the difference principle's plausibility depend on scale and anonymity, and if so should it? I want the strongest case for saying the asymmetry is defensible — that big institutions really are a different kind of thing — and the strongest case for saying it's just trickle-down reasoning we'd never accept face-to-face. If the real work is done by some specific feature (exit options, consent, how close to subsistence the worst-off are, whether the inequality started from a fair starting point, or something else), identify it and test it against a case where that feature flips and little else changes. Also, if you're tempted to say Rawls only meant the principle for the basic structure of a whole society, tell me what exact feature of a society's basic structure licenses the inequality there but not in a firm or family. And answer in American English please.
