I want to test a distinction I teach in legal ontology, between a doctrine that is a legal fiction and a legal kind that is real in the law. I will call something a legal fiction only if all four of these hold: (1) the law treats the item as the kind of thing it is not, or cannot be, rather than finding or constituting it as that kind; (2) the ascription is introduced for a limited doctrinal purpose and not as a general status; (3) the law either avows the ascription as untrue or declines to assert its truth; and (4) the doctrine is dispensable in principle, so that the law could achieve the same purposes through rules addressed only to entities it already recognizes.

Now consider a hypothetical device in a model environmental statute. A settlor conveys land to a perpetual ecosystem trust that has no human beneficiary and whose sole permitted activity is preserving the site's ecological functions. The trust is recorded as the owner of the land and is the named party in any claim to protect the site; an autonomous monitoring system, not a human trustee, is authorized to act on the trust's behalf. The trust may also be sued for its own breaches. The enabling statute says the trust is to be treated as a legal person for purposes of property, standing, and liability, and adds that nothing in the statute asserts that the trust is in fact a person.

Is the ecosystem trust a legal fiction under my four criteria, or does the statute constitute it as a real, if novel, legal person despite the disclaimer? Work through the criteria in order; where a criterion does not determine an answer, say so and identify whether the difficulty is in the case, the criterion, or the underlying fiction/reality distinction. End with a table whose rows are the four criteria plus a final row for the overall classification. Use these columns: Criterion; Assessment (satisfied, not satisfied, or indeterminate); Controlling feature of the case; Whether the criterion itself needs revision (with a brief note).
