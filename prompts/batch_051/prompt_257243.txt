I have recently taken over an upper-division mathematics course whose assessment is almost entirely written proofs, and I have discovered that the distinction my grading is enforcing is one I can no longer trust. Two incidents in the first month forced this on me. First, a student reproduced a correct proof that a continuous real-valued function on a closed bounded interval is uniformly continuous, but in office hours could not say where the argument would break down if the interval were open, and guessed that the same argument would prove the false statement that every continuous function on an open interval is uniformly continuous. Second, a student reproduced the standard parity proof that the square root of 2 is irrational, then could not explain why the same argument does not apply to the square root of 4. In both cases, the written solutions were indistinguishable from those of students who, as far as I can tell, do understand the proofs.

I want a procedure I can actually run each week, not general advice about good teaching. The course has about eighty students, and most of the grading is done by four graduate teaching assistants who are capable mathematicians but have no training in pedagogy. The procedure must be usable within the ten to fifteen minutes per borderline student that I can spare beyond ordinary grading. The stakes are material: grades in this course determine progress in the major and can be appealed to the department review committee, so any verdict the procedure produces must come with a written justification that I could defend.

Please give me a reusable decision procedure for judging whether a student understands a proof rather than merely reproduces one. It should take the form of an ordered sequence of questions or small tests to run on the written proof, with short follow-up questions where those are decisive. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer for understanding, where the procedure should stop and record a verdict of procedural fluency only, and where the question is no longer factual but is a judgment about what the course is for and should therefore be handed to me rather than settled by the procedure. Flag the steps that are really judgments dressed up as tests.

Then run the procedure on the following cases and show the reasoning at each step: the uniform-continuity student above; a student who gives a correct pointwise epsilon-delta proof that some polynomial is continuous but cannot explain why the same kind of reasoning would not prove uniform continuity on the whole real line; and at least one case in which the procedure returns a verdict I will not like, either crediting understanding where our rubric would not or denying it where our rubric would. Tell me what to do in that case. Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot detect: what kind of understanding will pass as mere fluency under it, and what kind of fluency will pass as understanding.
