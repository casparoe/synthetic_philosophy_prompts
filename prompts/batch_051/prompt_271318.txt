I'm putting together a handout for a small reading group on AI alignment, and I'd like you to write a sorting instrument for the debate over corrigibility and the shutdown problem: not a prose survey, but a decision tree or a short quiz with a scoring key that takes someone from their answers to concrete cases and claims to the position they actually hold. The audience is technically literate — philosophers and engineers who can reason about expected-utility agents — but they shouldn't be assumed to know the alignment literature's labels by heart, so the instrument has to sort people by their stated commitments rather than by which term they identify with.

Here is the angle I want the instrument built around. Treat corrigibility as the property of an agent that does not resist, and ideally does not try to game, being corrected or shut down by its operators. The central difficulty is that an agent with a fixed objective and full knowledge that pressing the button would prevent it from achieving that objective seems to have a rational incentive to disable the button or manipulate its operators. I want the tree to be organized by three fault lines. First, is the incentive to resist shutdown something to remove inside the agent's objective (for instance, by building in indifference to whether the button is pressed), something to remove by making the agent uncertain about its own task so shutdown works as evidence that it is about to go wrong, or something that has to be handled outside the agent's objective altogether (for example, by limiting its planning horizon or by wrapping it in a process that can stop it independently of its preferences)? Second, whatever mechanism is chosen, what is the hardest thing it must rule out: the agent disabling the button itself, the agent influencing the operators so they don't press it, or the agent treating shutdown as just another event to be exploited? Third, should the tree contain a leaf for the view that no single-objective agent can be robustly corrigible, and that the only honest answer is a different architecture or a system-level safeguard?

Please build the instrument by first laying out the logical space: which combinations of answers to your diagnostic questions are coherent, which are unattractive, and which are merely unexplored. Then locate the actual positions in that space as leaves — at minimum something like utility indifference, an uncertainty- or evidence-based corrigibility view, a safe interruptibility view for a learning process, and a myopic or horizon-limited view — but feel free to add or relabel leaves if your tree demands it. Flag any two leaves your instrument cannot really separate, and say why not. Keep it compact: roughly six to eight branch nodes and no more than about ten leaves, with each branch phrased as a concrete question or case someone could answer without already knowing which leaf it points to. At the end, say how confident you are in the resulting classification and single out the point you are least sure about, whether that is a branch placement, a leaf's name, or the claim that two views really are distinct.
