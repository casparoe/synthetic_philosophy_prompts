I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and a recurring question in my reporting is what an egalitarian society should actually equalize: income and wealth, subjective well-being, or something else, such as the capabilities people have to live the lives they value. Over the years I have cobbled together a position I would like stress-tested before I ever impose it on a reader. I call it equality of revision. The claim is that what people are owed, as a matter of egalitarian justice, is not a level of welfare and not a stock of resources; it is the effective ability to revise their lives — to leave a job, a marriage, a religion, a region, a career, or a set of inherited expectations, and to take up a credible alternative life without being ruined for having tried. The metric on this view is a person's exit set: the quality, range, and accessibility of the alternative lives they could plausibly reach from where they now stand. Two people are unequal, I think, when one can rewrite their life several times at modest cost and the other is locked into whatever trajectory they happened to enter, even if the two have the same income, the same capabilities on any standard list, and are equally content right now.

A case that drives the intuition: a well-credentialed professional and a shift worker with the same savings, health, and current happiness look equal on many metrics, but the professional could retrain, move cities, end a bad relationship, or change careers without catastrophe, while the shift worker's only feasible moves are all downward. Equality of revision says the gap between those two — the gap in feasible second chances — is itself the injustice, prior to any difference in eventual welfare or current resources. I am aware this sounds somewhere near capability, opportunity, or real-freedom views, and I am not sure whether I have found something new, rediscovered something under a new name, or simply built a theory on a metaphor.

I would like an honest assessment rather than encouragement. Please reconstruct the strongest version of equality of revision, name the nearest existing positions and say how it differs from each, and then push back: where does it misidentify the wrong, where does it demand something absurd, and where does it fall apart on inspection? Tell me which objection is most likely to be fatal rather than merely uncomfortable, and say explicitly what argument or consideration would most change your verdict — what would have to be true for you to reverse it, and where your own reasoning is least secure. End with a single line in the form \answer{...} containing a short verdict phrase, such as \answer{capabilities under a new name} or \answer{genuinely distinct but incomplete}.
