I sit on the statutory advisory committee of a commission charged with revising our state's business entity law, and we must decide whether to recommend recognizing a new class of "single-purpose autonomous operator" as a limited legal person. The question reaches us through a petition from an autonomous shipping company that operates an unmanned coastal cargo vessel directed in real time by a single AI system: the AI chooses routes, contracts with ports and suppliers through electronic agents, buys fuel, maintains insurance, and handles cargo claims. Under current law these acts must be performed by a legal person, and today the corporate owner performs them all, which means the owner's other assets are exposed to claims arising from the vessel. The company proposes that the AI, together with the vessel and its operating assets, be recognized as a segregated legal person in its own right: it would hold the ship and insurance in its own name, sue and be sued, collect freight revenue, and limit liability for the vessel's debts and torts to the vessel's own estate. The company would retain a residual claim to profits but no authority to direct operational decisions, much as shareholders own a corporation without operating it. The company's argument rests on the familiar corporate-person analogy: corporations are persons, though they have no minds of their own, precisely because they are stable decision procedures attached to pools of assets, and the company says its shipping AI is just such a procedure. Opponents on our committee reply that the analogy breaks at the one place that matters: a corporation always has human decision-makers beneath it — directors and officers — who can be identified, removed, and made answerable, whereas the AI is itself the decision-maker, so recognizing the AI as the person would leave no human in the chain whose judgment or accountability makes personality earn its keep.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before next Thursday's vote. It should recommend one concrete course of action — recommend the amendment, reject it, or recommend a narrower variant such as recognizing the entity only as a segregated fund without full personality, or allowing it only on condition that a human overseer be designated — with the reasons for that choice and a realistic account of what the committee would be committing us to. It must engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the side you reject rather than caricaturing it, and it should press on a distinction our committee keeps blurring: the difference between personality as a way of marking a bearer of rights and duties, and personality as a device for partitioning assets and channeling liability. I also want an honest judgment about how much of the corporate-person analogy actually survives once that distinction is drawn, and whether whatever function remains needs a human being anywhere in the loop.

Write it as continuous prose only, like an email or letter rather than a formatted report, so no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Act as an expert legal theorist who works on legal personality and responsibility: start by carefully thinking through the strongest arguments on each side, including arguments that cut against the recommendation you ultimately make, and consider the alternatives you reject before you settle on the bottom line.
