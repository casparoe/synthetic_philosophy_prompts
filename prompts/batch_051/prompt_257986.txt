During a seminar on corporate personality, and again in a long argument about whether business corporations should enjoy constitutional free-speech protections, I repeatedly watched the discussion get closed off with the phrase "a corporation is just a legal fiction." Pressed on what followed, different people said different things: that corporations don't exist in the way human beings do, that corporate rights are really just the rights of the individuals behind the firm, that attributing beliefs or intentions to a corporation is loose talk, or that the law's treatment of firms as persons is a convenience it could revoke tomorrow. I'm fairly sure this sentence is doing real argumentative work, but I can't get anyone to state exactly which claim they are making.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses "a corporation is a legal fiction" can be used to assert? For each reading, I would like a verdict: trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false. In particular, I want to know how each reading bears on the further claims that corporate rights exist only in a diminished or derivative sense, that statements about what a corporation intends or is guilty of are merely metaphorical, and that corporate personality is somehow less than fully real. Then tell me which reading, if any, actually licenses the inference my interlocutors wanted — that because a corporation is a legal fiction, its rights and liabilities need not be taken at face value.
