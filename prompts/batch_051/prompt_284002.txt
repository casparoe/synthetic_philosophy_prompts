The exam question was:

"Explain and assess the following strategy in the debate over the burden of proof. Some atheists argue that atheism should be understood negatively — as the absence of belief in God, rather than as the belief that God does not exist — and that the burden of proof therefore rests entirely on the theist, who asserts a positive existential claim, while the negative atheist asserts nothing and has nothing to defend. Is the courtroom analogy, with its presumption of innocence and its distinction between a verdict of not guilty and a finding of factual innocence, a good model for this position? Does a negative atheist incur a burden of proof in at least some dialectical circumstances — for example, when adding that theistic belief is unjustified? Does the distinction between atheism and agnosticism survive, or collapse, once the negative atheist begins drawing conclusions about what it is rational to believe? Defend a view."

The student's answer was:

"The negative-atheist move is important and largely successful. Its central claim, which I accept, is that there is an asymmetry between the person who asserts that God exists and the person who merely does not accept that assertion: the former makes a positive existential claim and must carry the burden of proof, while the latter asserts nothing and so has nothing to defend. The courtroom analogy captures exactly this. Under the presumption of innocence, the defendant is treated as not guilty unless the prosecution proves guilt beyond reasonable doubt, and a verdict of not guilty does not assert factual innocence. In the same way, the negative atheist is not asserting that God does not exist but only registering that the theist's case has not been made. So the burden rests on the theist.

The position also clarifies agnosticism. Once one understands that atheism is only the absence of belief, agnosticism becomes a confused halfway house. Either one believes the theistic claim or one does not; there is no third doxastic state. Someone who says she neither believes that God exists nor believes that God does not exist is really just a negative atheist who has been misled by the older, positive definition of atheism. Since the theist has failed to discharge the burden of proof, the rational default is to take the proposition that God exists to be false — just as a jury, when the prosecution fails, must proceed as though the defendant were innocent. Where the evidence has genuinely been searched for, its absence is itself evidence of absence, and after centuries of natural theology we still have no reliable evidence of the relevant kind, so the default state should be disbelief. The negative atheist therefore has a two-stage position: first, she asserts nothing in her capacity as a non-believer; but second, on the basis of the theist's failure alone, she is warranted in asserting that God does not exist. The burden never shifts to her, because all the positive work is done by the theist's failure to make a case."
