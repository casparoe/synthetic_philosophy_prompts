I'm a PhD student in ancient philosophy, working through Aristotle's *Politics* and getting stuck on a specific sentance in Book I, Part II. Here's the passage, in Jowett's translation:

"For that which can foresee by the exercise of mind is by nature intended to be lord and master, and that which can with its body give effect to such foresight is a subject, and by nature a slave; hence master and slave have the same interest."

What I'm trying to figure out is whether the final "interest" claim is doing any real argumentative work, or whether it's just there to make the whole thing sound benign. Specifically: when Aristotle says the natural slave "can with its body give effect to such foresight," is he saying the slave is more or less a tool that the master's mind directs, or does the slave have some secondhand share in the master's foresight? My problem is that the sentence seems to run together a descriptive claim about what certain people are naturally suited for with a normative claim about what nature intends, and I can't tell whether "hence master and slave have the same interest" is supposed to follow from the body–soul relation or from the fact that both parties are preserved by the arrangement. If it follows from the body–soul relation, then why should sharing an interest follow from one party being the thinking part and the other being the bodily part? If it follows from mutual preservation, then isn't Aristotle just assuming what he later tries to prove about natural slaves being naturally slavish? I'd love an interpretation that separates the teleological claim from the instrumental claim, because I keep finding myself paraphrasing him into either a tautology or something vaguely circular. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the question.
