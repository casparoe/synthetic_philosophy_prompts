I am an undergraduate in a course on bounded rationality, and we have been reading the debate over whether satisficing is a genuinely distinct account of rational choice or simply optimization in disguise once the costs of thinking and searching are themselves priced in. I am building a one-page study handout for my study group, and I would like it to be a decision tree (or a short sorting quiz with a scoring key, whichever fits the material better) that starts from concrete choice scenarios and sorts someone into a position in this debate on the basis of what they say about the cases, not on the basis of which label they recognize.

Please build the tree from the logical space of commitments first, and only afterward locate the named positions within it. The branch points I care most about, phrased so a student can answer them without already knowing the literature, are these: Must the agent have a complete and consistent utility function over all possible outcomes? Must the costs of deliberation and search themselves be subject to optimization? May a rational stopping rule be fixed by an aspiration level rather than derived from maximizing expected utility? May that aspiration level adapt as the agent observes what options are available? And should a choice procedure be evaluated by its internal coherence or by its long-run performance in a structured environment?

The leaves should include at least these positions: the optimizing-under-search-costs view (which treats satisficing as reducible to optimization), Simonian satisficing proper, the ecological-rationality or fast-and-frugal-heuristics view, and the view that treats maximizing versus satisficing as an individual difference in decision style. Please model the scenarios on ordinary search problems, such as how many more apartments to inspect before committing, whether to accept a job offer that expires, or whether to return to a known restaurant rather than try a new one. For each leaf, include one sentence on its most awkward commitment. For any combination of the branch commitments that no defensible position occupies, say briefly whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any positions your tree cannot separate. Around eight to twelve leaves or questions would be the right size, and please do not include citations or a reading list; this is only for my own revision.
