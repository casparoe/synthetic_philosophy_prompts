I work as a quantitative risk modeller at an insurance firm, and I think in a particular idiom: expected losses, tail risk, discount curves, model risk, Bayesian model averaging, and the difference between uncertainty I can attach probabilities to and uncertainty I cannot. A philosopher I know has been pressing me on something he calls strong longtermism — roughly, the claim that the dominant determinant of how good or bad our present actions are is their expected influence on the very far future, so that reducing risks of human extinction or permanent collapse eclipses ordinary near-term concerns such as poverty, disease or political reform. I found myself translating his case into my own terms as he made it: very long time horizons, fat-tailed distributions over the number and quality of future lives, subjective probabilities of ruin-level events, a discount function that declines only slowly, and a large helping of model uncertainty. The translation kept catching at the same places, and I could not tell whether that was a limitation of my framework or a sign that the view was doing something my framework is built to rule out.

What I want is a systematic restatement rather than another defence or attack. Take strong longtermism and render it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of a risk modeller — expected value over long horizons, tail risk and ruin, discounting, Knightian uncertainty, scenario sets, model averaging. Then give me a residue report, claim by claim: what translates cleanly; what can be made to fit only if you quietly distort something; what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Finally, say whether the residue is best explained by my framework's lacking resources that a full picture would need, or by the longtermist claims being less precise than they present themselves as being. My working hypothesis is that strong longtermism just is tail-risk management with a much longer time horizon and a moral discount rate set near zero, and I want to know whether that hypothesis captures the view or misses the point.
