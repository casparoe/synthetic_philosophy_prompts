I'm in a reading group that's been fighting for months about whether quantum indeterminacy actually does anything for free will, and we've decided to run a proper adversarial collaboration between two members. One holds an event-causal libertarian line, roughly Kane-style: indeterminism in the brain, especially in torn, self-forming choices, isn't just noise; its part of what lets the agent settle something that their prior reasons hadn't already settled, and the agent can be genuinely responsible for which way that settling goes. The other holds the luck-objection line, roughly in the spirit of Mele and Strawson: if the neural event that finally settles a choice is objectively undetermined, then whether one intention forms rather than another is a matter of luck in exactly the sense that undermines the control needed for basic moral responsibility, so quantum randomness can't buy you anything here.

They've agreed to anchor the whole collaboration to one shared toy case, because otherwise they keep talking past each other. The case: a person is genuinely torn between keeping a difficult promise to a friend and accepting a career opportunity that would mean breaking it. They deliberate until their reasons are, by their own lights, balanced — no further deliberation is going to tip the scales. On the event-causal-libertarian picture under test, the neural event that actually settles the choice is a quantum-indeterminate event somewhere in the decision circuit that does the final integrating. In a contrast condition, the same kind of event is fixed by the prior total state of the brain plus environment. Neither condition involves an outside manipulator or an implanted randomizer. The two sides have also agreed to keep track of a parameter that kept coming up: the timing or "location" of the indeterminism — whether the quantum event occurs early, in the process that surfaces the candidate considerations before the person has evaluated them, or late, at the final settling between two options that are already endorsed but exactly tied.

Write the document this collaboration should have produced. I want:

(1) The claims both can sign about what the quantum indeterminism does and doesn't contribute in this case, what determinism would or wouldn't have secured, and why the phenomenology of deciding — the introspective sense of having settled it oneself — doesn't itself settle anything.

(2) The precise residual disagreement, in two phrasings: the wording the event-causal libertarian would accept for his own view, and the wording the luck objector would accept for his.

(3) The cruxes — the sub-questions where, if one side conceded just that, the rest of the disagreement would mostly collapse. I suspect one is whether agential control can be partly constituted by an undetermined event that is not itself a free action but is integrated into a reasons-responsive process. Another might be whether the ability to do otherwise in the exact same prior circumstances adds anything to desert once the agent has already formed and endorsed his judgment.

(4) For each crux, at least one test or argument both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance — in particular, how the early-vs-late location of the quantum event should matter if the event-causal libertarian is right, and how it should matter if the luck objector is right.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section and then a disagreements table.
