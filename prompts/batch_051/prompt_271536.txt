I am in a seminar on mysticism and the limits of language, and my term paper is meant to reconstruct a debate as an adversarial collaboration rather than as an argument for one side. The two positions I want to bring together are these. Position A, the apophaticist, holds that the ultimate is not merely something we have not yet described but something about which no literal assertion can be true, and yet it can still be known: by acquaintance, by union, or by what the tradition calls unknowing. On this view, ineffability marks a real split between cognition and assertion — there is something to know, but no truth-evaluable sentence can capture it. Position B, the epistemologist, holds that knowledge is a propositional attitude, or at least that any state worth calling knowledge must have a truth-evaluable content to which the knower stands in an appropriate justificatory relation. On this view, if nothing is literally true of the ultimate, there is no content for a state of knowledge to be about, and the phrase ineffable knowledge is either a contradiction or an honorific term for a feeling.

Please write the joint statement these two parties could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, followed by a disagreements table. The agreed claims should be substantial rather than vacuous, and should include: (a) what both sides concede about the phenomenology and the cross-cultural recurrence of ineffability reports; (b) what they can jointly say about the difference between something's being ineffable and something's merely not having been expressed yet; and (c) a jointly agreed account of the status of the statement that the ultimate is ineffable — whether it states a fact about the ultimate, prescribes a rule for talking about it, or does something else. The disagreements table should have a column for the question, a column for the apophaticist's wording of her own view, a column for the epistemologist's wording of his own view, and a column for the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side. After the table, take each crux in turn and give the case, argument, or thought experiment both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. The statement should aim throughout to separate disagreements that are substantive from disagreements that are merely verbal, and it should not weaken either side's position so that it becomes easier to sign.
