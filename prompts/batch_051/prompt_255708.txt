I'm taking an upper-level course on probabilistic reasoning, and I've been developing a distinction I want checked before I use it in a paper. I call the two quantities "local surprise" and "global surprise." Local surprise is the probability of a specific coincidence: for example, the probability that a particular friend calls within the same hour you happen to be thinking of them. Global surprise is the probability that, among all the coincidences you could have noticed over some stretch of time, at least one coincidence striking enough to report would occur. The birthday paradox is the clean model: any particular pair sharing a birthday is improbable, but some pair sharing a birthday in a modestly sized room is not.

My thesis is that when people treat an anecdote as evidence—for a hidden connection, a supernatural cause, or just that something is "going on"—they instinctively evaluate it by local surprise, but the anecdote's evidential force should be bounded by global surprise. If a coincidence is no more than what should have been expected to happen somewhere in the relevant pool of possible coincidences, a single report of it should move our beliefs very little. I've been calling the principle the "coincidence tax."

Is the distinction already in the literature under another name, or is the wording just capturing a familiar point? Is the principle actually right, or does it overcorrect—are there cases where a high local surprise carries evidential weight even when global surprise is high? And what breaks first if I try to formalize the tax, for instance in deciding what counts as the relevant pool of possible coincidences?

In those five points, cover: (1) the closest existing idea or ideas, (2) the strongest reconstruction of the distinction and the principle, (3) what the principle gets right, (4) a case it handles badly or a place where it stops being useful, and (5) whether the term "coincidence tax" earns its keep. Use American English.
