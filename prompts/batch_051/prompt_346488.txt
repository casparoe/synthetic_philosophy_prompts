I teach a third-year undergrad seminar on writing and mind (14 students, 80 minutes), and I want to run something that makes them feel, not just hear, that an alphabet is a theory of a language rather than a neutral transcription of speech. They've read about the differnce between a phoneme and a letter, but I don't trust that they've internalized it. No lecture up front; I want them to bump into the problem themselves and only have it named in the debrief. By the end I want them to be able to say for themselves that the choice of what counts as the same sound is not just given by the speech signal.

I made a small invented language for it. Kavit's consonants are p b t d k g m n l r s h, vowels i e a o u, syllable shape (C)V(C), stress always word-initial. The facts (which you will know but the students won't start with) are:

- /p t k/ are aspirated [pʰ tʰ kʰ] word-initially and unaspirated elsewhere.
- /b d g/ become [p t k] word-finally.
- /n/ becomes [ŋ] before k or g, and [m] before b or p. The [m] before b/p is deliberately ambiguous, since Kavit also has a distinct /m/ phoneme.
- Plural suffix is -u after consonants and -nu after vowels.

Now design the session. Small groups each get one of three design briefs: build a writing system for Kavit that (a) makes it easiest for an outsider to pronounce forms correctly from spelling, (b) keeps one spelling per root even when the root is pronounced differently, or (c) uses as few symbols as possible while still distinguishing words. You can adjust the briefs if you see a better conflict, but keep the core idea that the goals genuinely pull against each other. After that, the groups should mix and have to turn their systems into one official village-school spelling with a hard cap of 15 symbols plus maybe one accent mark. That negotiation is where I want the trouble to happen.
