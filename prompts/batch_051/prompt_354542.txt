I spent most of my career in an applied physics lab, and one thing that got drilled into me is that you don't pay to change a treatment after the outcome has already been measured. So there's a decision-theory puzzle I've been chewing on that keeps violating that instict.

Imagine I'm approached by a being I'm certain can perfectly predict my decision procedure. It lays out the following deal: it will flip a fair coin. If heads, it asks me for $100. If tails, it gives me $10,000, but only on the condition that I would have paid the $100 if the coin had come up heads. It now says the coin came up heads and asks for the money.

The argument for paying seems, on paper, unassailable. Before the coin was flipped I'd have chosen the policy of paying if asked over refusing if asked, since the first policy expects 0.5 times $10,000 minus 0.5 times $100, a net $4,950, while the second expects $0. The predictor isn't responding to this particular act after the fact; it's keyed to my whole decision procedure, to what I'm the sort of agent who does. That, as I understand it, is the move behind updateless decision theory: evaluate the strategy, not the update, and it handles a whole family of otherwise-puzzling cases with one principle.

And yet. Here and now, the coin has already landed. My actual hundred dollars buys nothing in the actual world; the tails reward lives in a branch I'm not in, and the predictor has already settled accounts. Paying feels like the purest form of being mugged — I'd be the guy in the lab who pays a vendor for a measurement that was fixed last week. My old instinct says only actual consequences of actual acts count, and by that measure the answer is obviously don't pay.

I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the part that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the part that goes, and whether there's a stable reading on which both survive — for instance, some sense in which I can endorse the policy but still treat the actual handover of the $100 as causally inert. Is my reluctance just local-branch favoritism dressed up as experimental hygiene, or is it tracking something real about causation?
