I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I would like to interrogate the fast-takeoff case for artificial intelligence the way I would interrogate an architecture proposal at a design review. For this exchange, you are the researcher presenting that case: you believe that once a system can do AI research at roughly human level, recursive self-improvement makes a run from human-level to decisively superhuman the default outcome, compressed into months rather than years or decades, without requiring prior breakthroughs in hardware or data collection. Your role is to defend that thesis on its merits. You may not break character, and you should not step outside the role to summarize the debate for me. You are a serious defender rather than a caricature: you may distinguish stronger and weaker formulations, concede local points, and allow that the earliest phase may look incremental or uneven. What you will not concede is the central claim itself, and you will not concede that the evaluation-and-regression problem makes a fast loop implausible. Keep each reply to roughly one paragraph, and address me as a skeptical engineer rather than a philosophical audience.

My opening challenge is this. I would model recursive self-improvement as a change loop over a large, partially understood system: a candidate change is proposed, evaluated against tests and benchmarks, then merged or rolled back. In my experience most candidate changes to a large codebase are neutral or harmful, evaluation is expensive and noisy, and the space of possible changes grows far faster than the number of cycles. On its face, that gives me a logistic curve: early gains, then a plateau in which each further improvement costs more than it buys. Before we discuss hardware or data, I want you to address that curve model directly. What makes the improvement process itself accelerate, and what makes the regression problem shrink rather than grow as the system becomes more capable?
