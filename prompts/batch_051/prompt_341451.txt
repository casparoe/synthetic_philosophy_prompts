You are a moral philosopher who specializes in collective action and the ethics of individual contributions to large-scale harms. Think carefully about the following argument. Before settling on a diagnosis, brainstorm the plausible ways each premise or inferential step could be rejected, then give your final assessment of where the argument goes wrong and what rejecting that step commits us to.

My study group has been unable to resolve an argument that seems to let individuals off the hook for all the collective harms of modern life, and we would like a careful diagnosis. We reconstructed the argument as follows:

(1) An individual action can be morally wrong on account of some harm only if the action makes a difference to that harm: had the agent done otherwise, less of the harm would have occurred, or the harm would have been less likely to occur.

(2) A single purchase of factory-farmed animal products makes no such difference. Producers decide how many animals to breed and kill on the basis of aggregate demand over a production cycle, not on the basis of any particular sale, and one customer's purchase is neither necessary nor sufficient for any animal to be raised, kept, or killed that would not otherwise have been, nor does it appreciably alter the probability of future production decisions.

(3) Therefore, a single purchase of factory-farmed animal products is not morally wrong on account of the suffering involved in producing it.

(4) The same pattern applies to every case in which an individual contribution disappears into aggregate demand: one optional flight, one purchase of clothing made under exploitative conditions, one marginal use of fossil-fueled electricity, one act of compliance with a polluting supply chain.

(5) So none of these individual actions is morally wrong on account of the harms associated with them.

(6) But that conclusion seems to show that an ordinary consumer cannot properly be criticized for any climate-related or supply-chain-related choice, no matter how avoidable, so long as each choice is only a drop in a large aggregate bucket. We find this conclusion unacceptable, but we cannot see where the argument slips.

Identify the premise or step we should reject, explain why it initially looked plausible, and work out what we are forced to give up or accept once we reject it. We would also like to know whether the correct diagnosis treats the cases in step (4) together or whether they come apart in ways that matter.
