In A Theory of Justice, Rawls claims that the principles of justice are whatever principles rational, mutually disinterested parties would choose from behind a veil of ignorance concealing their class, natural talents, sex, and conception of the good, where parties evaluate principles by how well they secure social primary goods for themselves. I reconstructed an argument from the secondary literature, and it seems to prove too much, but I cannot find the mistaken step.

(1) To choose rationally under ignorance of who one is, a party must treat each social position as equally likely to be her own.
(2) So behind the veil each party should assign equal probability to being any member of society.
(3) Rational choice under a known probability distribution maximizes expected utility.
(4) With equal probabilities across all social positions, maximizing expected utility is equivalent to maximizing the society's average utility.
(5) Therefore the original position, correctly interpreted, selects average utilitarianism.
(6) Since Rawls says the original position selects the two principles, and the difference principle is not average utilitarianism, Rawls's own device either selects a rival theory or selects nothing.

Which step should a defender of Rawls reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am least willing to surrender (3), since it seems simply to state what decision-theoretic rationality amounts to; but I also cannot see how the veil remains a model of fairness if (1) fails.
