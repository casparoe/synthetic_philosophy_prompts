I keep seeing a parallel between the problem of the criterion and the way a computer boots, and I can't tell whether it's deep or just the word "bootstrapping" doing all the work. In epistemology you need a criterion of truth to sort good beliefs from bad, but you need at least some good beliefs already to justify the criterion, so there's a regress before you even start. A computer's boot looks like the same structure: it can't load the operating system until some code is already running, and that code either gets loaded by an earlier, smaller loader or is just hardwired in firmware — so the regress only stops at something you trust without loading it. Descartes, on this picture, is trying to shrink the trusted seed to almost nothing: one indubitable point, then the clear-and-distinct rule, then everything else. Hume then says there's no boot at all; we're always already running on habit and custom, and justification isn't the kind of thing beliefs get started from. My question: is this a real identity of structure or just a loose analogy? If it's real, does anything transfer from computing to epistemology — say, the idea that the seed should be small enough to inspect, or the distinction between a bootloader and hardwired firmware (roughly, a foundationalist stop versus a naturalist one)? And where does the parallel break? Name something one side does that the other never does. Keep the reply under 250 words.
