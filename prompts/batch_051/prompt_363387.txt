I work as a software engineer on risk models, and I recently found myself in a disagreement that someone shut down with a slogan. A colleague and I were estimating the probability that a particular loan segment would breach a loss threshold next quarter. We had the same historical data, the same feature definitions, and after a long exchange we had each examined the other's full analysis. Our numbers still differed — roughly 0.3 and 0.55 — and neither of us had moved. At that point a third colleague said: "Rational agents can't agree to disagree." His point was that since we had shared all relevant evidence and still disagreed, at least one of us must be reasoning badly — or, in our terms, that at least one of the models or analysts was buggy. I gather the slogan descends from a formal result in economics about common priors and common knowledge, but I only know it as a catchphrase.

I doubt one clean claim could do everything that sentence is being asked to do. Among the things it might be taken to assert are: two reasoners who ingest identical inputs must output identical probabilities; two reasoners who have seen each other's estimates and still do not converge thereby fail some obligation of rationality; all sound reasoning must begin from the same prior; and any remaining disagreement is just a difference of priors that no amount of evidence can adjudicate. Those are very different claims, and they would justify very different engineering responses — retraining a model, accepting the disagreement as legitimate, or treating one side as simply wrong.

Please lay out, as precisely as you can, the distinct claims the sentence "Rational agents can't agree to disagree" could be used to express. For each reading: state it in a form precise enough to evaluate, give a verdict — near-trivial, substantive and defensible, or false or overstated — and say what the slogan would have to mean for the third colleague's conclusion to follow: that when two systems or people with the same evidence disagree, at least one of them must be wrong. Pay particular attention to hidden assumptions such as the common prior assumption, and describe what happens to each reading when those assumptions are dropped or hold only approximately, as they always do in a real system.
