I'm enrolled in an upper-division seminar on the self and consciousness, and last week we discussed the view, associated with Hofstadter, that the self is an abstract self-referential pattern: a strange loop in which a symbol, the I, points back at the very system that deploys it. After class, two students carried the argument into the hallway, and now I have been asked to referee it. I honestly cannot untangle where each of them is right. Could you adjudicate their exchange?

(1) Marcus began: The strange-loop account is supposed to explain how a physical brain gives rise to a self. But a loop by itself does no explanatory work. A thermostat has a feedback loop, a sentence can refer to itself, and a camera pointed at its own monitor produces a loop, yet none of those has an I. So the mere presence of self-referential structure in the brain cannot be what makes a self, because self-reference is everywhere in places we all agree have no self. The view owes us a criterion that excludes these cases.

(2) Lena replied: That criterion is built in from the start. The strange-loop view is not that every loop is a self. It is that a self appears when a representational system develops a symbol for itself, an I, that is richly connected to its whole network of concepts, so that the system can model its own modelling, attribute experiences and intentions to itself, and revise its self-model. A thermostat's feedback does none of that. You are attacking a simplified version of the position.

(3) Marcus came back: But phrases like "richly connected to its whole network" and "model its own modelling" are doing all the work, and they are just placeholders. Nobody has specified how much richness flips the switch, so the view can always retreat by declaring any counterexample not rich enough. And even if someone did specify it, a rich self-representation would still just be a structure. It would explain the content of a self-model, not what it is like to be the system. You would have mapped the broadcast without explaining the listener.

(4) Lena answered: That last move is exactly the question-begging one. You are assuming in advance that there is a listener over and above the process, something for which the structure exists. On the strange-loop view, the self just is that self-referential structure; there is no further thing. So demanding that the account also produce the listener is not a fair demand. It asks the view to explain an extra entity the view denies exists.

(5) Marcus responded: Then the view is eliminating the thing it was supposed to explain. If the self just is an abstract pattern, calling it a strange loop has not made it concrete. Abstract patterns, like the number seven or a triangle, do not initiate action. But my self is exactly what does initiate action; it raised my hand earlier in class. If all the view gives me is an abstraction, it has left the self causally inert, which cannot be right.

(6) Lena finished: You are confusing a pattern with what it is instantiated in. An algorithm is an abstract pattern, but when it is implemented in a computer it is exactly what causes the outputs. The same program running on different hardware produces the same behaviour, so its identity and its causal powers sit at the pattern level, not at the level of the chips. Likewise, the strange loop exists as a pattern in neural activity, and when instantiated, it has effects. Saying the self is a pattern is not saying it is inert; it is saying the pattern level is the right level for describing it.

Then give an overall assessment: is either of them more right, or do they mostly talk past each other? If the argument bottoms out in a deeper unresolved question about what an explanation of the self must accomplish, say what that question is and what would have to be settled before the dispute could be resolved.
