In a reading group on why promises bind, one participant argued that a promise is best understood on the model of a debt: promising creates a claim the promisee holds against the promisor, much as borrowing creates a claim the lender holds against the debtor's future conduct. The attraction of the picture is that it explains how promissory obligation can arise even when the promisee never relies on the promise and would be better off if it were broken. I would like an audit of this analogy. Give the correspondence table in as much detail as you can — what corresponds to the borrower, the lender, the principal, the interest, the discharge, the default — and then locate the points where the mapping breaks. Test in particular whether promises resemble monetary debts in being releasable but non-transferable, whether promissory content is open-ended in a way a fixed sum is not, whether the promisor–promisee relation is more fiduciary than that of creditor and debtor, and whether the analogy quietly imports third-party enforceability that promising lacks. The decisive question is whether the debt model does genuine work against the expectation and social-practice accounts of promissory obligation, or merely restates the conclusion in borrowed vocabulary. If it fails, offer a better analogy and say what that replacement would actually license about why promises bind.
