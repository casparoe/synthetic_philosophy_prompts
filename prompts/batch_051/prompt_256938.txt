I spent most of my career in the earth sciences, and I retired with an evaluative habit that I am now applying far outside its home turf. In my old field, a model earned its keep partly by what it ruled out: an explanation that could be bent after the fact to accommodate almost any observation was not admired for its scope so much as suspected of being empty. Since retiring, I have been reading broad synthetic histories of the human species — the sort of book that traces nearly everything, from the adoption of farming to the structure of modern institutions, back to a single engine such as geography, a cognitive shift, or the plasticity of human social arrangements. Without quite intending to, I began subjecting these books to the same test. After reading one, I ask whether I can state, in advance of being handed the conclusion, what kind of evidence would have weakened the central thesis, and whether the thesis rules out any plausible pattern the evidence might have taken. If I cannot supply that after a genuine effort, I set the book aside and decline to assent to its central claim, however erudite and elegantly argued it is. My reading group tells me this is the wrong instrument applied to the wrong material: history, they say, is an interpretive discipline, and a narrative can be rigorously constrained by evidence without making the risky predictions my old science demanded.

I find their objection plausible, which is precisely why I want a committed judgment rather than a survey of the options. Is my rule a sound export of scientific standards to historical explanation, or does it impose a criterion that such explanations cannot and need not meet? If the rule can be saved, I would like the precise restatement that keeps the demand for disconfirmation without treating every retrospective narrative as empty; if it cannot be saved, I want to know exactly where it breaks down. And because I am aware that a person can mistake his temperament for his epistemology, I would also ask you to say how someone in my position could tell, from the inside, whether he is enforcing intellectual discipline or merely dressing up a prior antipathy to large, elegant stories as method.
