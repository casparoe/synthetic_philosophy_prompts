A couple of my friends got into it over whether a specific finite string of digits can be random, and I honestly could not tell who was winning at each step. Priya wrote 000000000000 on a napkin and said "this obviously isnt random." Sam said that depends: if a fair coin produced it, then it is random, because every 12-flip sequence has exactly the same probability, 1 in 4096. She said the probability point misses what we were actually asking—the string itself has a pattern: you can describe it in two words, "twelve zeros," whereas something like 011010110001 basically has to be listed digit by digit. Whatever is harder to compress is more random than whatever is easy. He said compressibility is relative to a description language; with a perverse code, 000000000000 could get a long description and some messy string a short one, so she had not found a fact about the string itself. She said the choice of language only shifts description lengths by a constant, and for a long string the gap between "twelve zeros" and an unstructured string is so large that no reasonable language erases it. He said fine, but then she had given up random-as-a-fact-about-the-process: a deterministic program can output a string that looks incompressible, and she would not want to call that string random just because nobody can find a shorter description. She said why not—if the string passes every statistical test we can actually run, calling it random is just admitting our model cannot do better than chance, and randomness lives in the failure of any pattern to pay off, not in some unobservable causal history. Then he said she had turned randomness into a property of our ignorance. Around there it fizzled out.
