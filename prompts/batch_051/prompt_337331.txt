I'm a novelist working on a historical novel set in a philosophical community in ancient India, and my protagonist is a defender of the Mīmāṃsā school. In the chapter I'm drafting, he must hold his own in a public debate against a skeptic who keeps asking why anyone should accept the Veda's authority when every other text has a human author and is therefore fallible. I've written my protagonist's reply as a numbered chain of moves, because the rhythm of debate helps me see whether the character is actually reasoning or just asserting. But I'm no longer sure whether the reasoning is sound, and I don't want the scene to be convincing on the page while being intellectually hollow. The argument I've given him is this: (1) Whatever we know from a person's words depends on that person's being a reliable conduit for the truth, so if a speaker could be mistaken or lying, we need independent evidence of reliability before we accept the testimony. (2) Every human author is capable of both mistake and deception, so any humanly composed text can always be met with the question why this author should be trusted. (3) The Veda, however, has no human author: it is beginningless, and the ancient sages are held simply to have perceived its already existing words, not to have composed them. (4) If a text has no author at all, then the two standard defects of testimonial knowledge—error and insincerity—cannot attach to it, because there is no mind whose error or insincerity could be transmitted through the words. (5) Therefore the Veda's testimony does not need the kind of independent reliability check that human testimony needs; its trustworthiness is intrinsic rather than borrowed from a person. (6) Thus, unless a particular Vedic statement is positively contradicted by another source of knowledge, it should be accepted as authoritative. My question is whether this argument actually goes through. Please give me a committed verdict: is the argument valid as written? If not, identify the exact step that fails and explain what would have to be added or revised to make it work—or whether the whole thing collapses under pressure. Don't just lay out possible objections; tell me which diagnosis you would defend and why.
