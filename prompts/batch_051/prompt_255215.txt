Below are notes for a short essay I am planning, tentatively titled "Continuity and the Content of 'Physical': A Way Out of Hempel's Dilemma." I would like your comments on the idea, and in particular on the four questions listed after the notes. Please engage with the argument on its own terms; no citations are expected.

Notes. Hempel's dilemma is usually presented as a choice between two ways of defining "physical." On the first definition, "physical" means "posited by current physics." This makes physicalism hostage to the known incompleteness of current physics, and almost certainly false. On the second, "physical" means "posited by the true, completed physics of the ideal limit of inquiry." This saves the letter of physicalism but drains it of content: since we do not know what such a physics will say, we do not know what we are asserting when we assert that everything, including the mental, is physical. The dilemma is meant to show that "physical" has no definition that is both determinate enough to give physicalism content and tolerant enough to survive changes in physics.

I want to suggest that the dilemma depends on a particular metasemantic assumption: that the content of "physical" must be inherited from the catalogue of some body of theory, whether current or ideal. Against this, I propose that "physical" works more like a natural-kind term whose reference is fixed by a continuity relation rather than by a theory's current or final inventory. By analogy: "gold" did not mean "whatever occupies the role gold was believed to occupy at the moment of baptism," and it also does not mean "whatever an ideal chemistry would call gold"; it tracks a kind that survives revision because later claims stand in the right explanatory-continuity relation to earlier claims. Similarly, a property is physical, I want to say, just in case it is posited by a theory that stands in the relevant continuity relation to the theories of current fundamental physics — one that preserves their core empirical successes as limiting cases while correcting and extending them. This yields a definition that is not hostage to current physics, since a successor theory still counts as physical if it preserves current physics as a limiting case, and not vacuous, since continuity with current physics is a substantive constraint that some candidate future theories might fail.

The obvious objections are: (1) continuity is massively multiply realizable, so the definition does not determine a unique ontology; (2) a panpsychist or otherwise mentality-friendly physics might count as physical by this criterion, leaving the definition too permissive for physicalism's anti-dualist purposes; (3) "continuity" itself can be read narrowly, by current standards, or leniently, by ideal standards, which may simply reproduce Hempel's dilemma one level up; and (4) the analogy with natural-kind terms is imperfect, because "physical" is not a term introduced by ostension to a kind but a term of philosophical systematization.

Questions for your comments. (a) Is the diagnosis of the dilemma as resting on a metasemantic assumption about deference accurate? (b) Does the continuity proposal provide anything beyond rephrasing the standard two horns? (c) Which of the four objections is most damaging, and could the proposal be amended to answer it? (d) Is this idea interesting enough to develop into a full essay, or does it rehearse a move that would strike a seminar audience as already obvious?
