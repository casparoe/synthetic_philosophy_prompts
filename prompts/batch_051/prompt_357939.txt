Here's the list, typed quickly:

1. I'm a textbook expected utility maximizer. I accept the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, and I'm happy to extend expected utility to countably infinite lotteries in the obvious way, as a sum of probabilities times utilities.

2. The paradox itself doesn't seem deep to me. Sure, dollar expected value diverges, but utility of money is concave: with log utility the expected utility of the original St. Petersburg game is finite, and the rational price comes out modest. Diminishing marginal utility is basically all the paradox is trying to teach.

3. But I don't want to be pushed to bounded utility. I think utility is unbounded above — there's no finite cap on how good an outcome can be, and for any outcome there's another one I'd value more. Bounded utility has always felt like an ad hoc fix.

4. I also have a brute intuition I'm inclined to treat as a fixed point: a rational person may always refuse to stake everything they have on a single gamble with a tiny probability of a huge payoff, no matter how the expectation works out. If a decision theory says there's a bet whose fair price is more than any finite amount, I'd sooner say we applied the theory outside its domain than say I should take it.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are in tension and how badly; for any pair that only looks like it's in tension, say why it isn't; and then tell me the cheapest revision that would clean up the most.
