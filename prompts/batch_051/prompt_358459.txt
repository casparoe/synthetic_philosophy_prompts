For as long as clickwrap and browsewrap have existed, lawyers, judges, and philosophers have argued about whether clicking "I agree" — or, in the browsewrap case, merely continuing to use a site — constitutes real agreement to the terms of a contract. One side treats the click as an ordinary instance of contractual assent: the user is on notice of the terms, has an opportunity to read them, is free to decline, and the objective manifestation of assent through clicking is exactly the kind of conduct contract law has always taken as binding. The other side treats this as a fiction: almost no one reads the terms; the drafting party has every incentive to make them unreadable and no incentive to negotiate; the "choice" is often a choice among identical one-sided terms; and calling the click "agreement" converts a unilateral imposition into a supposedly voluntary obligation. Strikingly, the underlying facts — very low readership, one-sided boilerplate, the absence of genuine negotiation — are not seriously contested, and yet the debate about whether the click is agreement has barely moved in decades. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a verbal dispute about the word "agreement"; a hidden empirical dispute about notice, comprehension, or the practical availability of alternatives; a normative dispute about what contract law is for, cheaply coordinating transactions versus protecting the conditions of meaningful consent; a bedrock-intuition dispute about autonomy, in which each side starts from a different picture of when a person binds herself; or is the question itself ill-posed? Engage with what each side actually says — including the roles played by the duty to read, by objective as opposed to subjective assent, and by the contrast between a click as a performative act and consent as a mental state — and say what would actually count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding, a doctrinal reformulation, or an honest conclusion that no progress should be expected. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading — one about the doctrinal test for an enforceable obligation and one about the moral conditions under which consent binds — and that the word "agreement" lets them talk past each other. Is that right, or is the impasse doing something else?
