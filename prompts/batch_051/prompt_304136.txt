Heres a passage from David Hume's An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, Section 7 ('Of the Idea of Necessary Connexion'), Part 2, with the original spelling kept:

'These ultimate springs and principles are totally shut up from human curiosity and enquiry. Elasticity, gravity, cohesion of parts, communication of motion by impulse; these are probably the ultimate causes and principles which we shall ever discover in nature; and we may esteem ourselves sufficiently happy, if, by accurate enquiry and reasoning, we have traced up the particular phænomena to, or near to, these general principles. The most perfect philosophy of the natural kind only staves off our ignorance a little longer: as perhaps the most perfect philosophy of the moral or metaphysical kind serves only to discover larger portions of it. Thus the observation of human blindness and weakness is the result of all philosophy, and meets us, at every turn, in spite of our endeavours to elude or avoid it.'

Please argue for the following position: this passage is mainly a problem for the New Hume reading, not for the regularity reading. Specifically, when Hume lists elasticity, gravity, cohesion, and impulse as 'ultimate causes and principles', he is listing general regularities, not hidden powers; and the way he says philosophy only 'staves off our ignorance a little longer' suggests the endpoint is not an unknown something behind the phenomena but simply the most general level of observed uniformity. Make the case that the New Hume reader cannot take comfort in the phrase 'shut up from human curiosity and enquiry' because what is shut up is not a veil over a real necessary connection but the very idea of a necessary connection, for which we lack any impression. Along the way, be fair to the New Hume reading: treat the wording about nature not as mere rhetorical flourish, but show that the regularity reading can absorb it. Write in continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
