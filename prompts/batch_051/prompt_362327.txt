An economist I know says that statements like "the benefit Ann gets from the last ICU bed is greater than the harm Bob suffers from being denied it" aren't just hard to verify — they aren't the kind of thing that can be true or false at all, because interpersonal comparisons of utility are meaningless. I can't tell whether that's a substantive discovery about people or just a convention economists adopted because they can't measure utility the way they can measure temperature. Acting as a philosopher of economics, start by brainstorming two or three candidate tests (forced-choice tradeoff experiments, willingness-to-pay surveys, brain imaging, whatever), then pick the strongest one and spell out exactly what a positive result would show, what a negative result would show, and — this is what I actually care about — what part of that claim no test could settle even in principle. Don't let it drift into the much weaker point that such comparisons are hard; I want the line between hard-to-meaure and no-fact-of-the-matter.
