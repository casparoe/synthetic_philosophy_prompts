I keep coming back to karma, but only in its strong form, as a complete theory of moral desert: every significant harm and benefit a person recieves is one they have earned, if not in this life then in an earlier one, so the cosmic ledger always balances and there is no brute moral luck. It has real systematic virtues. It dissolves the problem of undeserved suffering without needing a god, it gives a non-arbitrary answer to 'why them and not me', and it explains exactly why the world's distribution of goods and harms is not a scandal. I genuinely find the axioms plausible on reflection.

And yet when my niece's three-year-old was diagnosed with leukaemia, I found I could not believe, not for a second, that she had earned it. And I don't think this is just sentimentality or scope-insensitivity on my part; I think the intuition is tracking something like the thought that desert cannot outrun memory — that no-one can deserve punishment for an offence they cannot so much as recognise as theirs. The past-life move seems to make the theory's verdicts not just harsh but incoherent, beacuse if the child is not psychologically continuous with the earlier wrongdoer, with no memory of the deed and no way of recognising it as hers, in what sense is the desert hers?

Give me the strongest version of each side. On one side: why a defender of karma-as-desert should say the intuition is a failure of nerve, or a category error, and that the theory should be kept. On the other: why the intuition should force us to either abandon the desert reading or reinterpret karma so thoroughly (as mere causal conditioning, say) that it stops being a theory of desert at all. Then tell me what you'd actually give up, and whether there is a reading on which the theory and the intuition are compatible without gutting one of them.
