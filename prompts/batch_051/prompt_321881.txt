For the past term I have been defending, in a historiography seminar, the view that certain non-human processes — epidemic diseases, climatic shifts, and major volcanic events — should be treated as historical actors in their own right rather than relegated to "context" or "background conditions." The case I have been making runs through three claims. First, such phenomena are bounded and individuable enough to be tracked across time and space: a pandemic is not "nature" in general but a particular pathogenic event with a particular trajectory. Second, their effects are selective in the way an actor's effects are: the impact of a crop-failing cool year or the introduction of smallpox into central Mexico is mediated by, but not reducible to, pre-existing social arrangements — which is the same reason we are comfortable calling a human ruler an agent even though their actions only take effect through institutions. Third, counterfactual isolation seems to work for these phenomena roughly as it does for human actors: we can ask how a region's political development would have differed without the eruption of Tambora, and the question feels tractable rather than metaphysical.

I want to know how exposed this position actually is. What would have to be true for treating volcanoes, climates, and pathogens as historical actors to be a mistake rather than a useful correction to anthropocentric historiography? Give me a map of the position's vulnerability rather than a balanced survey: distinguish evidence or arguments that would be decisive against it from those that would merely be awkward, and state what a defender of my position should themselves concede counts against it. Please address three pressure points explicitly. (1) The boundary problem: is there a principled distinction between an "actor" and an exogenous shock or an enabling condition, or does the view risk collapsing into the claim that every large cause with uneven effects is an actor? (2) The determinism objection: does attributing agency to geophysics commit me to treating human decisions as epiphenomenal, or can that implication be blocked only by adding caveats that empty the position of its content? (3) The verbal-dispute worry: does "historical actor" carry any explanatory weight beyond "cause that historians ought not ignore," and has the position been insulated from refutation by definitional flexibility? For each, say what a decisive counterexample or finding would look like, and what the position's defenders would themselves treat as a genuine cost.

Finally, be explicit about what the position predicts or licenses as an explanation that its rivals do not — that is, what explanatory difference it makes to treat a drought as an actor rather than as a condition, and where that difference would show up. And tell me frankly whether the view, as I have formulated it, risks anything at all, and whether that insulation should count as a defect for a thesis of this kind or as the appropriate modesty for a historiographic category rather than an empirical hypothesis. The objection I would personally find most telling is the one that argues the language of "actor" smuggles in intentionality while the theory disavows it; tell me whether that is where the view is genuinely weakest or whether it is a rhetorical distraction from sturdier problems.
