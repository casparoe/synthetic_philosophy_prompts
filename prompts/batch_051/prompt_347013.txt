I keep running into the claim that it's rational to discount the future: other things equal, a benefit now is worth more than the same benefit later. But whenever I push on the claim, the other things are never actually equal. The later benefit is less certain, I might not be around to receive it, the future person who gets it may be only loosely me, and money now can be invested and turn into more money later. So I can't tell whether there's any pure time preference left over — a preference for earlier benefits merely because they come earlier, over and above those confounds. I want to know whether that leftover is real, whether it can be isolated, and whether isolating it would even matter. Design the strongest test you can, ideally seperating (a) an individual's own future consumption, (b) benefits to different future strangers, and (c) society's choice about benefits to people alive in 200 years. For each, say what result would count for or against the existence of pure time preference, and then say what even a clean positive result would leave unsettled — in particular, whether showing people discount the future in a laboratory would license a government discounting the wellbeing of people centuries from now.
