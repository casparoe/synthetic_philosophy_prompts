Two friends of mine got into a long argument over a thought experiment one of them brought up, and I got stuck as the unwilling referee. The setup is this. There's a machine with a dial numbered 0 to 1000. At 0 you're in no pain and you get no money. Each time you turn the dial up one notch you get $100, and the pain increases by an amount so small you genuinely can't tell the difference between one setting and the next. At 1000 the pain is severe, and if you were offered a choice between staying at 0 and being moved straight to 1000, you'd refuse even for the full $100,000 the thousand increments would pay you. So far they agreed.

Dan said the case shows a rational person could prefer 1 to 0, 2 to 1, and so on at every single step, while also preferring 0 to 1000. So rational preferences don't have to be transitive, and there's nothing incoherent about ending up somewhere you'd have refused.

Maya said that's incoherent. If you prefer 0 to 1000, at least one of the thousand steps has to be a step down; otherwise your preferences say 1000 is better than 0 and 0 is better than 1000 at the same time.

Dan: I can, because better-than just doesn't chain across a thousand imperceptible differences. Each step is an improvement — the hundred bucks is worth more to me than an unnoticeable worsening. The whole jump is not an improvement — the total worsening is enormous. There's no magic notch where the pain first becomes too much, so there's no single step to reject.

Maya: That's the giveaway. If there's no notch where it flips, then "too much" isn't doing any work, and your pairwise verdicts aren't real preferences — they're just noise from a difference you can't detect. A real preference has to be stable enough to flip somewhere along the dial. If you can't find the flip, you don't prefer each step; your indifferent, and indifference isn't a reason to turn the dial.

Dan: Fine, take indifference. If you're indifferent between every adjacent pair, then transitivity of indifference would force you to be indifferent between 0 and 1000 — but you're not. So some transitivity principle has to go either way. That's the point.

Maya: No, the point is your end-to-end preference is the one that reflects what the states are actually like. The rational move is to pick a stopping point in advance — I'd stop at 50, say — and treat anything after that as off limits. Then each step you take is rational relative to your plan, and you never arrive at 1000.

Dan: That's arbitrary. The step from 49 to 50 is rational because the pain is imperceptible and the money's real, but the step from 50 to 51 is supposed to be irrational for the exact same reason? Your just drawing a line to save transitivity, and it could've been 51 or 49.

Maya: It's not arbitrary, it's justified by the fact that you already know 1000 is worse than 0. Since isolated choices are unreliable, you need a rule, and any sensible stopping point beats none. Rationality is about whole lives, not one dial turn at a time.

They asked me to settle it and I honestly couldn't. Please go through the exchange and say who's right about which specific point, and what both of them are missing.
