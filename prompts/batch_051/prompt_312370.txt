I am a philosophy professor working on the boundary between moral philosophy and the foundations of causal reasoning, and I am preparing an argument on which I would like your judgment. I have been turning over a parallel between Pearl's causal hierarchy and the doing/allowing distinction, and I cannot yet tell whether it is a genuine structural identity, a loose analogy, or a mistake.

Here is the mapping as I see it. The first two rungs of Pearl's hierarchy are association and intervention. Association concerns P(y | x): the probability of an outcome conditional on a passively observed value, under the world's existing causal regime. Intervention concerns P(y | do(x)): the probability of the outcome when x is set by a manipulation that cuts x off from its ordinary causes while leaving the rest of the structural equations intact. The third rung concerns counterfactuals, such as P(y_x | x', y'), where a hypothetical intervention is evaluated in light of what actually occurred. The moral material I want to compare with this is familiar: doing harm, in particular killing, versus allowing harm, in particular letting die. The parallel would be that doing harm is the agent's do-operation on some node in the causal graph, while allowing harm is the agent's abstention from that operation, so that the pre-existing mechanisms run forward as they would have done. Killing and letting die, on this account, differ in precisely the way rung 2 differs from rung 1.

If the parallel is genuine, it has teeth. Pearl's hierarchy holds that the rungs are not mutually reducible: interventional facts are underdetermined by purely observational facts, absent further structural assumptions. The moral analogue would be that the difference between killing and letting die cannot be read off a purely observational description of outcomes; one needs to know which mechanisms would remain invariant under the agent's interference or abstention. That would explain why two cases with identical outcomes and identical correlations can nonetheless differ morally, because they have different structural equations. Conversely, if the parallel is genuine, it licenses importing technical results: one could ask what the moral equivalent of identifiability is — that is, what it would be for an interventional query to be determined by observational data given a specified graph — or whether anything like the back-door criterion determines when outcome data suffice to settle the doing/allowing status of a harmful result.

But my worry is that the analogy does little more than borrow the words "intervention" and "observation." In Pearl's framework every non-intervention is unambiguously rung 1, whereas in ethics not every failure to prevent is an allowing in the loaded sense; allowings seem to presuppose duties, undertakings, or special relations that have no natural place in a causal graph. More seriously, an agent's deliberate refraining is itself chosen among alternatives and causally efficacious; it can always be modeled as an intervention on an action variable set to the value refrain, in which case the alleged contrast between rung 1 and rung 2 collapses at the level of the agent. And it is unclear how the moral furniture that often does the work in the doing/allowing debate — intention, foresight, whether the agent creates a threat or merely fails to arrest one — is to be represented in a structural causal model at all.

I would like you to do three things. First, state the mapping as precisely as you can and mark the points where the two sides genuinely align and where they come apart. Second, say what would have to be true on both sides for a result from the do-calculus to transfer to the ethics of killing and letting die; in particular, spell out what a moral analogue of identifiability, or of the back-door criterion, would have to look like. Third, identify at least one concrete claim the parallel straightforwardly predicts that turns out false, and tell me whether that failure refutes the parallel or merely restricts the class of cases in which it holds.
