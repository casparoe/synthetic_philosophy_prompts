I am a novelist, and I am writing a novel whose protagonist works in video-game quality assurance. Over the course of the book she becomes unable to make her avatar kill non-player characters, and the story turns on whether this is a genuine moral development or a confusion dressed in moral language. I want to portray both readings fairly, so I need the underlying conceptual issue rather than the usual talking points.

Here is the claim I need to examine: a player's reluctance to harm a non-player character can amount to genuine moral concern for that character, and is not merely aesthetic distaste, parasocial attachment, protectiveness toward a world one has built, or concern about what the choice does to one's own character. Please act as a moral philosopher with a working knowledge of experimental design. Think carefully, and before committing to a single design, sketch the different ways the claim could be operationalized and say which you choose and why.

Then give me a concrete design: the manipulation or observation, the comparison conditions, and the outcome measure. Tell me what result would favor the moral-concern reading and what result would favor the proxy reading. Finally, and most importantly, say exactly what a positive result would and would not establish, and whether the remaining gap between the two readings shows a limitation of the tests or a fault — or perhaps a feature — in the very notion of a video-game character mattering morally.
