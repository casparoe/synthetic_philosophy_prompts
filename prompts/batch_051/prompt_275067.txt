so im the head of fact-checking for the online newspaper at my high school, which sounds a lot more impressive than it is — its me, two section editors, and an advisor who mostly checks commas. we keep having the same fight about when we can write something as a flat fact vs when we have to hedge it or attribute it to somebody. our advisors rule is only print what you know, but nobody can say what counts as knowing, and then opinion pieces just assert stuff like the district will cut the music program as if its already true. i tried reading about the norm of assertion debate — the idea that theres a rule about what you need in order to assert something, and the main candidates are knowledge, truth, belief, and justified belief — but i cant figure out how to turn that into something we actually do before deadline.

i need a reusable checklist for approving a sentence before it goes in an article. it has to be runnable in under 20 minutes per piece by a 16-year-old whos never taken philosophy, on a school chromebook with no legal databases or anything. we publish weekly, maybe 12 pieces an issue, and the principal reviews everything before it goes up. weve had two retractions this year and hes basically told us one more kills the paper, so the stakes are real even if nobodys getting sued. the checklist should do at least these things: (1) classify each sentence as us asserting it, us reporting what someone else said, us quoting, or us predicting; (2) say what bar each classification has to clear, and which of the four norms (knowledge, truth, belief, justified belief) should be the default for news vs for opinion; (3) flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as checkboxes, because our advisor loves boxes and i dont want to pretend the philosophy does more than it does; and (4) say when the right move is to kill the sentence instead of hedging it. i also want you to be honest about what the checklist cant do and where it will probably misfire.

then run it on three cases, and make one of them a case where the checklist tells me not to run something i really want to run. case a: an anonymous student source tells us a teacher is being investigated, but we cant reach the teacher or the district and our only support is that one person, even though im pretty sure its true. case b: an op-ed wants to flatly say the district will cut the music program next year, based on nothing but a leaked draft budget. case c: our editors want to write so-and-so said X at a school board meeting even though the recording cuts out right before X and the kid denies saying it — they say its fine because were reporting what was said, not saying it ourselves, and i have a feeling that a sentence like according to a source, [totally false thing] would still be our problem. for each case say what the checklist outputs, whether the answer changes if we switch the default norm, and what the weakest step in the procedure is for that case.
