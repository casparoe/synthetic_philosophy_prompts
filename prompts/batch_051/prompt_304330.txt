I have been teaching a graduate seminar on the epistemology of computer-dependent proofs. In our last session I defended the following claim: a proof whose verification is partly delegated to a computer, such as the four-colour theorem's exhaustive case analysis or the formal certification of the Kepler conjecture, functions less like a proof in the traditional sense and more like a piece of calibrated laboratory equipment, something whose output we trust because we have checked the instrument rather than because we have followed an argument. One of my students replied that this is a distinction in our psychology, not in the mathematics, and that no observation could settle it. I think the disagreement is at least partly testable. Design the strongest experiment you can to test whether unsurveyable proofs are epistemically different from surveyable proofs rather than merely longer. The experiment might involve mathematicians attempting to extract a lemma, explain why a theorem holds, or transfer a method to a neighboring conjecture. Specify what result would support my instrument view, what result would support the student's just-a-long-proof view, and what the experiment would still leave open about whether a community can genuinely know a theorem on the basis of a proof no one has read in full.
