For the past several months I have been reporting a long piece on letterforms — how type designers invent them, how readers and machines recognize them, and where those two processes come apart. One problem keeps surfacing and has become the conceptual center of the piece. A reader unhesitatingly accepts as the same letter a geometric sans-serif 'a', a double-storey 'a' in a book face, a brush-painted storefront 'a', and a child's unsteady schoolroom 'a'. Yet not every mark counts: alter the relations among bowl, stem, and aperture in the wrong way, and at some point the mark ceases to be an 'a' at all. The cleanest available account of this is a role-and-convention view. On that view, a letter is a grapheme — an abstract unit whose identity is fixed entirely by the contrasts and combinations of the writing system in which it functions — and any particular mark is an 'a' only because a community of readers assigns it that role. Shape is a costume: it carries the role but contributes nothing to the essence. The view has real virtues. It explains why strikingly dissimilar forms can do the same job across hands, typefaces, and even scripts; why we can read badly degraded handwriting; and why a script can be simplified without its letters ceasing to be themselves.

But the letter-drawers I have spent time with resist this. Their working conviction is that there is genuine 'a'-ness — a shape-internal logic or spirit, not merely a cluster of remembered examples — which makes some forms more canonical, more legible, or more deeply 'a' than others. They say they can see it in an isolated nonsense mark that is not functioning in any word, long before context or meaning is available; that it guides and constrains their design work; and that the boundary between 'a' and 'not-a' is not just a statistical contour drawn around the shapes people happen to have seen. I do not want a verdict that simply picks one side. I want to know what it would take for the role-and-convention theory to be amended so that it can house this stubborn sense of shape-internal normativity — or what it would take for the designers' intuition to be debunked as an instructive illusion, a case in which a mark's system-role presents itself falsely as a property of the shape. And if both can survive, what would the negotiated position claim: that the 'spirit' of a letter is neither pure geometry nor pure convention, but a norm-governed style belonging irreducibly to a community of reading practice?
