Design an in-class structured role-play for an upper-level high-school philosophy elective (16–20 students, one 80-minute block) that makes the Ship of Theseus stop feeling like a bar-trivia puzzle and start feeling like a live dispute about practical continuity, authority, and material integrity. The activity should force participants to argue about which of two candidates is “the ship” before the vocabulary of identity is introduced, and it should make them feel why the case survives simple answers.

The scenario should involve two candidate vessels: one kept in service through continuous plank-by-plank replacement at sea, and one rebuilt in a museum out of all the original discarded planks. The dispute should arise naturally from incentives rather than from a teacher’s question. For example, there may be an insurance claim, a retirement ceremony, a naval commission, a burial at sea, a sacred blessing, or a legal registry entry that can belong to only one of the two. Assign roles with conflicting practical interests: the crew, the dockmaster, the museum curator, the insurer, the heirs of Theseus, the harbour priest, the shipwright, the city magistrate. Each role should have a clear reason to treat one candidate as the proper object of some obligation or entitlement, and those reasons should cut in different directions.

During the main phase, participants may not use the words “same,” “identical,” “identity,” “is,” or “really.” They may only argue about the entity to which specific promises, rights, debts, rituals, and responsibilities attach. The activity should end with a forced decision under time pressure: a vote or ruling on which candidate gets registered for the contested purpose, with any abstentions counting as a loss for both sides. That decision must have an agreed consequence within the fiction, not just a summary of opinions.

Then run a debrief. The debrief should first collect the arguments used during the game and sort them into distinct identity criteria: continuity of matter, continuity of form or function, spatiotemporal continuity, causal-history relations, and conventional or role-based continuity. It should show that the practical dispute forced participants to precisify what the original puzzle leaves open, and that different roles picked different precisifications because their interests differed. It should end by mapping the two candidate ships onto at least two standard solutions to the Ship of Theseus, and by asking students to say whether the vote resolved anything or merely exposed the original question as underdetermined.

Include a full operational plan: materials, room setup, role cards, a timeline for the 80-minute block, and a debrief guide. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—especially the risk that students treat the ban on identity vocabulary as a word game rather than a genuine constraint, the risk that one side becomes obviously sympathetic, and the risk that the vote produces a runaway majority before the debrief can draw out the minority’s best case. Be frank about which feature of the philosophical problem the role-play cannot reproduce: the exercise can make students feel that identity is interest-relative, but it cannot by itself show whether that interest-relativity is a discovery about identity or merely an artifact of the artificial dispute you built into the game.
