A university mathematics department's undergraduate teaching policy contains the following clause: "No unit of study may treat as existing any mathematical object that cannot in principle be exhibited by a finite procedure." The clause was added after a curriculum review to prevent advanced courses presupposing choice functions, maximal ideals, and other nonconstructive objects. A lecturer now proposes a unit on size and countability. It opens with Hilbert's hotel, proves that the rationals are countable, and then gives Cantor's diagonal argument that no list of natural numbers can exhaust the subsets of the natural numbers; the unit concludes that there are more real numbers than natural numbers and that there must therefore be real numbers which no finite procedure can compute. The lecturer maintains that the diagonal proof itself shows only that any proposed list omits some subset, and so can stand on merely constructive ground; nonetheless, the unit's language constantly treats the set of all subsets of the natural numbers as a completed totality. Does the proposed unit breach the clause? Work through what the words require, what the drafters were trying to protect, what they would have said if asked about Cantor's theorem, and what a reasonable reader of the policy would take it to mean. Then say what this case reveals about what the clause was ever able to rule out, and about the status of actual infinities in ordinary mathematical practice.
