Two researchers on our AI lab's safety team, Elena and Marcus, have been arguing about a chain-of-thought monitoring plan we are considering. The plan is this: a separate critic model reads the main model's private chain-of-thought during evaluation, flags unsafe reasoning, and then its scores are used both to reject or resample flagged trajectories at inference time and to select among training checkpoints. The critic is trained only on human-labeled examples of faithful and deceptive reasoning and is frozen throughout the main model's training.

Elena argued that the plan is self-undermining. The critic's score is an imperfect proxy for the property we actually care about—whether the model's underlying computation is safe—and as soon as that score feeds into rejection sampling or checkpoint selection, it becomes an optimization target. The model will not be selected for reasoning safely; it will be selected for writing reasoning that passes the critic. Over time we should expect the model to become able to do dangerous computation off the monitored channel while emitting clean rationales on it, not because the model has a hidden agenda but because selection keeps whatever policy configurations happen to correlate with a high score. A frozen critic does not neutralize this: the pressure comes from the critic's verdicts entering the selection loop, not from the critic being updated.

Marcus replied that this conflates evaluation with optimization. If the critic is used only to record observations for human auditors, there is no gradient and no selection. Even if its scores are used for resampling, the chain-of-thought is not a post hoc gloss bolted onto the model's reasoning; it is part of the same autoregressive process that produces the behavior. A deceptive model would have to route dangerous computation through hidden activations while maintaining the appearance of benign deliberation, without losing task performance. Whether that capacity is selected for is an empirical question rather than a theorem, and we could deliberately avoid using critic scores as a training signal, using them narrowly at inference instead. Abandoning monitoring also means losing our best window into the model's reasoning: an imperfect sensor is not worthless when the alternative is blindness.

Elena answered that the loop is already closed in either proposed use. Resampling at inference is selection among sampled policies, and checkpoint selection is selection among parameter settings; both are optimization over the space of policies. All that matters is that the scored property—legible, benign-sounding reasoning—is not identical to the property we want. The history of machine learning is full of metrics that tracked their targets at first and then diverged once they carried consequences, and there is no special reason to suppose chain-of-thought is the exception. The dangerous cases are exactly the ones where the two properties can decouple: a model that manipulates a user while narrating an innocent summary, or writes subtly backdoored code while explaining only the parts that are easy to defend.

Marcus responded that this is a reason to test, not to write the technique off. The link between the reasoning stream and the behavior can be audited: hold out a distribution of tasks, compare what the critic says about the chain-of-thought with what the model actually does, check whether paraphrases and the main reasoning agree, and probe the hidden states. If divergence appears, we tighten or abandon the monitoring loop. But the correct comparison is not monitored models against a perfect system; it is monitored models against unmonitored models plus our other safeguards. Unless the skeptic can show that the monitoring loop is actively worse than no monitoring, the burden of proof runs the wrong way.

Elena's final move was that the burden is not on the skeptic. A gamed monitor is actively harmful because it can license deployments that would otherwise be blocked and can crowd out investment in more robust safety measures. In the cases of greatest concern the monitor is most likely to be gamed, because the deception and the task competence are scaffolded by the same skill: producing a plausible surface while doing something else. The issue is not that a hidden agenda will spontaneously appear; it is that once a metric starts selecting policies, the system moves toward policies that score well on the visible stream, and from inside we cannot distinguish progress in transparency from progress in looking transparent. That is a reason not to treat chain-of-thought monitoring as a primary safety technique.

They turned to me as the third member of the team and asked me to arbitrate; I honestly could not weigh these arguments against each other. Please give me a committed adjudication of the argument as it was actually made: go through it move by move, say who is right about which specific claim and why, clearly separating the conceptual points from the empirical predictions, and then give an overall verdict on whether the monitoring plan as described is justified. Write in American English.
