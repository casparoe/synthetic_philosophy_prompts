My friend, fresh off a lecture on causation in the law, summarized the debate about causation by omission to me like this: "If a lifeguard watches a child drown and does nothing, the omission is just as much a cause of the death as a push into the water would be. Omissions are just negative events, so once you let them in as causes you are committed to saying that everyone who failed to save a child on another continent caused that child's death. Since that is absurd, there are only two defensible positions: either omissions count as causes only when there was a pre-existing legal duty to act, or they are never really causes and the law merely pretends they are for reasons of policy." I suspect this is misleading, but I cannot say exactly where it goes wrong. Please go through it claim by claim and say which parts a careful philosopher working on causation would accept, which parts they would call a slide or a conflation, and whether the supposedly absurd conclusion actually follows once the accurate parts are stated more precisely.
