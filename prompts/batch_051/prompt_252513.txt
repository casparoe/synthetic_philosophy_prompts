Consider two people, one of whom is about to receive a mug and the other of whom is about to lose one, where a coin flip decides which is which. If you ask the would-be receiver how much she would pay to keep the mug and the would-be loser how much he would accept to give it up, the answers diverge sharply: the price to give it up is reliably higher than the price to obtain it, even though the mug is the same object and the ownership that separates the two positions was assigned minutes earlier by chance. The same person, moved from the buyer's position to the seller's position, gives both answers. The asymmetry I want to examine is this: a loss counts for more than an equal gain, and what a person happens to have at the moment — the reference point — seems to do the counting.

The same asymmetry shows up in judgments people take much more seriously. A $100 fine feels worse than a $100 bonus that never arrives. A person who would never pay $20,000 to secure a view from her window may refuse $20,000 to give that view up. People grieve the loss of an object far more than they would celebrate acquiring an identical one. The gap appears to shrink when people routinely buy and sell the same kind of item — dealers in secondhand goods show little of it — and to widen for objects tied to memory or identity. Legal systems often encode the distinction as well: the state must generally compensate a person when it takes away a right or an asset, but not when it merely declines to confer a benefit of equal value.

Is there anything that justifies treating losses as systematically weightier than foregone gains, or is this only a psychological bias that we should try to unlearn? Please give me the strongest principle you can find for why a loss might legitimately matter more than a missed gain of the same size, and then test it against the cases above — especially the coin-flip mug, where the difference between "mine" and "not mine" seems to have no substance at all. If the principle covers the mug case, say whether it also commits us to the more serious personal and legal asymmetries. If it does not, say where the line lies, and whether the mug case is a mistake we should correct while the grief case is a legitimate preference. End with a clear verdict on whether the loss/gain asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
