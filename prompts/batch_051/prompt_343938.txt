I am trying to work out what I actually mean by weakness of will, and specifically whether it is anything more than the present-biased discounting that behavioral economists call hyperbolic discounting. Here are my verdicts on a set of cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them.

1. The snoozed alarm. I set my alarm for six because I have decided to run before work. When it rings, I am awake enough to know that I will feel better all day if I get up, and that I will be annoyed with myself if I don't. I hit snooze anyway, several times, while the thought is still in my head that I should get up. I call this akrasia.

2. The menu switch. I plan to order the fish because I am trying to eat carefully. When the waiter describes the braised short rib, I order the rib, and my thought is not that I shouldn't but that it is worth it today. I do not call this akrasia. I call it a last-minute change of mind, maybe self-indulgent, but not weakness of will.

3. The afternoon cigarette. A friend has resolved every morning for months not to smoke that day. Every afternoon she lights one while thinking that she shouldn't and that she will later wish she hadn't, and smokes it anyway. She says afterward that she did the very thing she had set herself not to do. I call this akrasia.

4. The switched lab choice. In an experiment, a participant commits to a larger reward at a later date when both rewards are distant, but chooses the smaller reward when it becomes available immediately. I do not call this akrasia. It is a preference reversal of the sort hyperbolic discounting describes, but I see no point at which the person acts against their own concurrent judgment.

5. The lapsed gym membership. A friend signs up every January intending to train three times a week, and by February has stopped. Asked why, she says she has simply lost interest and doesn't want to go. I am torn. I want to say she failed to follow through on a plan, but I do not want to call it akrasia, because when she skips the gym there is no better judgment she is overriding at that moment.

The difficulty is that a theorist I respect says my distinction between the first pair and the second pair is illusory. On their view, all five cases have one underlying structure: at the moment of temptation, the immediate option is weighted so heavily that the person sincerely values it more. What I call a contrary judgment is just the faint voice of the earlier or later perspective. But the line between cases 1 and 3 on one side and cases 2 and 4 on the other feels real to me.

Please give me a committed answer, not a survey of options. Is there a principled account of what akrasia requires that draws the line where I draw it, and that explains the difference between acting against a concurrent judgment and merely changing one's mind? State the principle precisely and say what motivates each clause. If no non-gerrymandered principle captures all my verdicts, tell me which verdicts I should give up, and defend the simplest principle that remains. Then apply your principle to a case I cannot settle: someone installs a website blocker to stop late-night browsing, disables it at midnight, and browses for hours, never once forming the thought that they shouldn't until afterward.
