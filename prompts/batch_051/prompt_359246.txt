I'm running a small discussion group on the Letter Spirit question — what makes every 'a' an 'a' across typefaces, handwriting, and historical scripts — and I'm assembling a list of candidate answers for a handout. So far I've got four, and I want a fifth that is genuinely different rather than a restatement or recombination:

(1) The skeleton account: an 'a' is whatever realizes a specific abstract stroke-structure — roughly, a closed lower bowl plus a stem or arch on the right — so the one-storey and two-storey forms are the same letter because they share that skeleton and differ only in inessential elaboration. (2) The distinctive-feature account, borrowed from phonology: an 'a' is a bundle of contrastive features (a closed lower counter, an x-height profile, an opening or arch on the upper right that marks it off from o, d, b, g, and u) whose job is to keep the mark distinct from its neighbors in the alphabet. (3) The reader-recognition account: a mark is an 'a' just when competent readers of the script read it as one under normal conditions, and that recognitional practice is all the essence there is. (4) The genealogical account: a mark is an 'a' because it descends, via the actual transmission of writing systems, from the relevant ancestral letterform (aleph, alpha, and it's later descendants); identity across styles is lineage, not shared shape.

Can you supply a fifth account that foregrounds something the four above don't — perhaps the motor/perceptual side, the dynamics of producing the mark, or the role the glyph plays in a larger visual system — without collapsing into (3) or a blend of two others? Say briefly how your account relates to the four, whether you think it's any good, and how confident you are in it, singling out the point you're least sure of. Keep the response under 300 words.
