I have been trying to get clear on the distinction between a person's genuine moral preferences and the biases that distort them, and I keep coming back to an analogy with a standard idea in statistics and machine learning, which I cannot tell is deep or merely seductive. In moral psychology, people often speak as if a person's authentic preferences could in principle be recovered by de-biasing: correcting for implicit bias, disgust reactions, scope insensitivity, motivated reasoning, and so on, to reveal what the person really values. But in statistics and machine learning there is a familiar point that the distinction between signal and noise is not intrinsic to a dataset. Noise is just what a fitted model leaves unexplained; different model classes, loss functions, and prediction tasks split the same data into signal and noise in incompatible ways. The signal is supplied by the modeling choices, not found in the data alone. This strikes me as the same structure as the moral-psychological problem: a disposition gets classified as a bias rather than as a preference only relative to some prior picture of which of the agent's attitudes should be explained away, and there seems to be no independent access to that picture. The mapping I have in mind is this: observed judgments correspond to data; authentic preferences correspond to the true signal; biases correspond to noise or residuals; de-biasing corresponds to model fitting; and the normative assumptions used to decide what counts as a bias correspond to the choice of model class and loss function.

My question is whether this is a real structural identity or a loose analogy. I know the machine-learning point is technical and my moral-psychological version is looser, and I want to know how much that looseness is doing. If the analogy is deep, does anything transfer from machine learning — for instance, the point that denoising cannot be evaluated without a specified task, which would predict that de-biasing cannot be evaluated without a specified normative purpose? If it breaks, what exactly supplies the ground truth on the ethics side that is missing on the data side? One disanalogy I can already see is that agents sometimes feel an attitude as alien from the inside, whereas a datapoint has no say in how it gets classified, but I am not sure that felt alienation is any more than another observation that itself needs interpreting.
