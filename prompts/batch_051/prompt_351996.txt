Consider the way many legal systems and informal social norms treat desecration. It is widely treated as a serious wrong — and in many jurisdictions a crime — to burn a national flag in public as a protest, to deface a war memorial, to desecrate a copy of a scripture, or to mistreat a human corpse, even when the object is the offender's own property and no identifiable person is physically harmed or threatened. Yet in many of the same systems, the identical physical treatment is prescribed or permitted when it carries a different social meaning: worn-out flags are ceremonially burned by the very institutions that prosecute flag-burners, damaged scriptures are buried or otherwise disposed of by their own communities, corpses are dissected in medical schools or cremated, and sacred images are routinely reproduced, handled, and eventually discarded. There is also an asymmetry in whose sacred things count: the revered objects of minority or foreign traditions often receive less protection than those of the majority, and objects valued intensely for merely personal reasons usually receive none.

What view of the sacred — or of value, harm, or offense — does this pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory or theories that would make the pattern coherent. Does the practice strictly presuppose that certain objects have a special, non-instrumental status, that they are in some sense sacred in themselves, or can it be fully explained by a person-regarding account, for example through expressive harm to observers, the denigration of a group, or the protection of feelings of reverence and identity? Distinguish what the practice presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and note where it is philosophically underdetermined — for instance, a Durkheimian view on which the sacred object stands for the group itself, a theistic view on which desecration offends a deity, and a sentimentalist view on which the wrong is ultimately the distress of the reverent might each license much of the practice.
