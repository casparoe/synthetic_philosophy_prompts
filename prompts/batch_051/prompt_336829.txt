A recent inheritance has forced me to confront something I used to treat as a merely theoretical puzzle. I inherited my late uncle’s effects: a box of photographic prints, a few letters, and one small painting he bought at auction years ago and could never securely attribute. Trying to decide what to keep, restore, copy, or discard made me realize that my reactions to cases do not line up behind any single idea I can state. My verdicts are these. A perfect, molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a beloved painting, indistinguishable to every expert and every instrument, would not be something I could treat as the painting itself, yet I cannot say what the original has that the duplicate lacks. A forgery painted in the style of a known seventeenth-century master and sold as genuine, then exposed: I believe its value genuinely drops when the attribution is corrected, even though no property of the canvas changed. I still unhesitatingly call a master’s painting the original even when restorers have repainted eighty percent of its surface.

The original negative of a photograph of my grandmother matters to me more than any print made from it, and a print made by the photographer from that negative matters more than a modern scan-and-reprint from that print, though I could not tell the two prints apart without labels. For a digital file, by contrast, a bit-for-bit copy leaves no residue of “the original”; every copy is exactly as good as every other, and yet I would be far more anxious about the loss of the only drive holding an old recording than about erasing one of a thousand identical copies. A ship whose planks are replaced one by one over decades remains, to me, the same ship, while the discarded original planks, gathered and reassembled in a shed, do not amount to “the” original ship — and I am not sure both of those verdicts can be true. A Stradivarius and an acoustically indistinguishable modern violin: I would rather own the Strad, I do not think this is mere snobbery, and I cannot say why. A signed first edition matters to me only a little against an identical unsigned copy, far less than the forgery case. A live performance has something a recording of that same performance lacks, but a recording of that particular evening has something a later re-performance would lack.

The nearest principle I can articulate is that the value of an object tracks its causal and institutional history rather than its appearance. But that principle seems to say the wrong thing about the digital file, to remain silent about performance, to deliver the opposite of my ship verdict by favoring the reassembled original planks over the continuously maintained vessel, and to leave unexplained why the signed first edition matters far less to me than the forgery, even though both involve provenance. I would like the principle — or a small set of candidates — that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely with the case that earns each clause, an honest list of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and its ruling on the case I keep going back and forth on: a meticulously documented replica of my uncle’s painting, made by a conservator and kept by me as a remembrance while the original hangs in a public collection.
