I've been trying to pin down a friend's views about whether novelty and variety are part of what makes a life go well. Shes pretty decisive about cases but hasn't stated a theory. Heres her verdicts so far:

- A homebody who has never wanted to leave her town hasn't missed out on anything; the good life is not a checklist of experiences.
- Someone who craves novelty but is stuck in a pleasant routine is worse off than someone equally pleasant who gets the novelty, even if the stuck person eventually stops noticing the craving.
- A year spent having one pleasant, familiar week on repeat would be slightly worse than a year of varied experiences at the exact same level of pleasantness.
- A pill that made you sincerely content with an unchanging routine forever would still cost you something, though she can't say exactly what.

Next week I want to ask her about three new cases. A traveler with a memory impairment has rich novel experiences each day but forgets them by morning; a person spends a year trying new things only because she's bored, hates most of it, but ends the year genuinely glad she did it; and a painter's own life is extremely routinized but her paintings are wildly inventive.

What would she probably say about each new case? What principle seems to be doing the work, which rival principles also fit her old verdicts just as well, and which of these new cases — or which further case I should make up — would best settle what rule she's actually running?
