I have been reading about the handicap principle in evolutionary biology, and I keep finding that it touches on things I remember from earlier reading of David Hume — an unlikely pairing, but one I keep returning to. The principle, in the version that interests me, holds that some animal signals are honest precisely because they are costly to produce. A peacock's tail or a gazelle's stotting display does not function like a conventional sign; it is reliable because a low-quality animal cannot afford the cost while a high-quality one can. The apparent wastefulness is not a flaw in the signal but the very thing that makes it trustworthy. On the standard account, this can be modelled as an evolutionarily stable strategy: a population in which receivers act on such signals, and in which signallers of different quality send them at different rates, cannot be invaded by cheap bluffers.

Hume died nearly two centuries before any of this was formulated, and he had no theory of natural selection, so I am not asking what he actually wrote about peacocks. But he did write at length about the minds of animals and about how we attribute reason and passion to them. His settled view, if I have it right, was that humans and other animals differ only in degree, that both learn by experience and custom, and that appeals to innate ideas or a priori principles are usually an evasion rather than an explanation. He was also suspicious of elaborate systems that claim more certainty than experience can supply. I am therefore trying to work out what the most genuinely Humean attitude to the handicap principle would have been, if we grant him the Darwinian framework he could not have had.

The difficulty is that the principle seems to cut both ways relative to Hume's commitments. On the one hand, it is a resolutely naturalistic account of something that looks like design or wisdom in nature, and it turns mere ornament into a causal structure built from ordinary advantage and disadvantage; I can imagine him finding that congenial. On the other hand, the explanation is usually given as a mathematical equilibrium argument, with receivers treated as if they were weighing probabilities and updating on the honesty of signals, and I suspect Hume would have wanted either to translate all of that into habit, custom and association or to dismiss the equilibrium language as a useful fiction. There is also a deeper difficulty: the peahen does not seem to be making a learned inference from past experience of the sort Hume's account of animal reason emphasises, yet the capacity to read the signal appears to be, in some sense, built in. Would the handicap principle therefore force a more nativist picture of animal cognition than Hume's custom-based account can comfortably absorb, or can his framework accommodate it without strain? And if one had to divide the modern principle into the parts Hume would have accepted, the parts he would have reinterpreted, and the parts he would have rejected, where would the lines fall?
