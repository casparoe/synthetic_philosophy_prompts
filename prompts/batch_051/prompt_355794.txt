Context: I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science but I sit in on the ML interpretability reading group, and I keep going back and forth on a case that came up in our lab. My advisor gave me a working list of criteria for saying that something inside a neural network is a genuine representation: (1) the structure carries information about some variable in the input or task; (2) it's stable—its not an artifact of my probe, but a direction or subspace you can recover across seeds and checkpoints; (3) downstream computation actually reads it out, so intervening on it changes behavior; (4) it's at least somewhat abstract, invariant across surface differences in the input.

Now the case. We have a mid-level vision model (a vision transformer) trained on animal classification. You can train a linear probe on an early layer and recover a clean head/neck orientation direction: it tracks pose across species, generalizes to held-out images, and shows up across seeds. But when we ablate the direction or zero it out, final accuracy barely moves, and if you train a student on the layer's outputs with that direction removed, the student just reconstructs the same information one layer later, as if the network recomputes it on the fly. So the information is there and stable, but the causal-readout criterion seems to fail.

Is that direction a representation or not? Go criterion by criterion, say which ones it meets and which it fails, and then tell me whether the case shows that the criteria themselves need fixing—especially whether causal readout should be required, or should be required in a weaker form. Lead with your bottom-line verdict, then go through the criteria, then say what you'd change. Keep it under 400 words.
