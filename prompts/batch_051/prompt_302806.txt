I was recently served with a fixed-penalty notice under a local byelaw that makes it an offence to "behave in a public place in a manner likely to cause annoyance to other persons." The wording struck me as so open-ended that I started reading about the void-for-vagueness doctrine, and I now understand the courts to say that a criminal law is void if a person of ordinary intelligence cannot tell from its language what conduct is prohibited and if it leaves enforcement officials without any standard to guide their decisions. What I cannot reconcile is why almost any law survives that standard, taken seriously. The statute book is full of phrases such as "reasonable", "without good cause", "in the public interest", and "with intent to annoy"; nobody can list in advance all the cases that fall under them, and a police officer cannot simply read off an answer in a hard case any more than I can. I have heard the practical explanation that judges uphold these phrases because a modern legal system could not function otherwise, but that sounds like an excuse, not an application of the test.

I keep coming back to a sharper version of the worry. When a court says a law contains "no standard of conduct", I would have thought a law against "annoying behaviour" actually does contain a standard — just a very loose one. So is the real rule that every criminal provision must be a precise list or a bright-line rule, and that any genuinely flexible standard is automatically void? If so, the doctrine would seem to sweep away nearly everything; if not, I cannot see what separates a permissible open-ended standard from an impermissibly vague one. I have no legal training and have been piecing this together on my own, so I would be grateful for an explanation from the ground up. Could you also say whether a byelaw worded like the one I was given would be likely to fail or survive the doctrine, and why?
