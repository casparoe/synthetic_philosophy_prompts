I cover the "science of history" beat at a general-interest magazine — mostly social science, big data, that sort of thing — and I keep getting pitched papers that are basically: history is quantifiable, the model says a crisis is coming, and look, Peter Turchin said America would get unstable around 2020 and it did. I've read enough of the cliodynamics stuff to know the rough shape of structural-demographic theory (population pressure, elite overproduction, popular immiseration, the state's finances falling apart, then a disintegrative phase, with these secular cycles repeating over centuries), but I'm not a modeler, and I cant tell anymore which of these pitches are real forecasting and which are just curve-fitting with nice charts.

I want a reusable decision procedure I can run every time one of these lands in my inbox. The constraints: I usually have about fifteen minutes before I have to tell my editor whether its worth a story, I have decent stats but no time for code, and I can get one expert on the phone maybe once before filing if the procedure says its worth it. The stakes are asymmetric in both directions: if we write up a curve-fit as a real prediction we look credulous and burn trust, and if we spike something that turns out to matter we missed a genuinely big story about history becoming predictive. My editor signs off on anything that uses the word prediction in the headline, so the procedure needs to produce labels I can defend to her — something like cover straight, cover-with-heavy-caveats, spike, or call an expert for.

Ground it in what cliodynamics and macrohistory can actually support, not in generic skepticism about modeling humans. The distinctions I need kept alive: forecasting a broad class of outcomes versus a dated event; out-of-sample testing versus retrodiction (the model nails the past because it was fit to the past); overfitting when you've got maybe dozens of cycles and lots of parameters; whether a model that says societal pressure is high is falsifiable in any way that matters; and the difference between a model forecasting instability and a model explaining it after the fact. Tell me which of those distinctions survive the simplification, and where the procedure stops being mechanical and a person (me or my editor) has to decide.

Then run the procedure on three worked examples: (a) a paper that says a rich northwest-European country is in the late disintegrative phase of its current secular cycle and faces serious political breakdown sometime in the next ten to twenty years, based on rising inequality, elite overproduction, and public debt; (b) a hyped model that claims to forecast the exact year of civil-war onset or state collapse, with a backtest that correctly predicts a bunch of famous historical breakdowns; and (c) one where the procedure gives me an answer I dont want — say, a Turchin-adjacent paper arguing the current decade is another pressure peak, where I'd love to run the big thinkpiece but the procedure says the claim is too vague to qualify as a forecast. And say plainly what the procedure cannot settle — where cliodynamics itself is doing the obscuring rather than the clarifying.
