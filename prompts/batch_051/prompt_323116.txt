Recently I stayed with my brother and he spent a whole evening trying to convince me to leave my job — I'm a public-health statistician, pretty settled — and retrain into something around AI or synthetic biology, because he says we're living in a tiny window where everything about the long-term future gets decided. He shows these graphs: population flat for hundreds of thousands of years, then basically a wall; energy use the same; computing the same. His line is that not all centuries are the same size. This century is the kink in the curve, so a person who could work on steering the transition has something like a thousand times the ordinary amount of leverage, and therefore not using that leverage is itself a big moral decision. So I should quit and give a big share of my income at the problem now, not later.

I tried to push back by saying that when things are moving fastest is exactly when one person has the least control — in a flood you don't steer the river, you get swept — but he said no, during a phase transition small nudges can pick out the whole final state, like nucleating a crystal. The graphs do look true to me, and I don't want to reject an argument just because the conclusion is inconvenient. But I also don't want to blow up a life I like over a curve with one bend in it. Can you actually work through the argument? I want to know what has to be smuggled in to go from 'the trajectory bent sharply upward' to 'I personally should switch careers and give more now,' and which of those hidden steps is doing the most work — because I honestly cant tell if this is the most important thing I've been told or just a really motivated reading of a log-scale chart.
