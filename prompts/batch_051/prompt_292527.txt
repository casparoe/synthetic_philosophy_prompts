As someone who teaches and publishes in metaethics, I have become less interested in adjudicating the dispute between moral realists and anti-realists than in diagnosing why it so reliably deadlocks among intelligent, well-informed philosophers. The striking feature of the exchange is that the two camps often accept the same lower-order data: they agree that some actions are wrong, that moral disagreement is deep and persistent, that evolutionary and social pressures have shaped human moral psychology, and that ordinary moral thought and talk at least appear truth-evaluable. Yet from this shared material, realists conclude that there are stance-independent moral facts, while anti-realists of various stripes—expressivists, constructivists, and error theorists—conclude either that there are no such facts or that moral truth is somehow constituted by our attitudes rather than discovered by them.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a disagreement about which propositions are true, but about what a theory of morality must explain and what should count as a satisfying explanation of moral thought and talk. Is that a fair diagnosis? Or is there a genuine, potentially decidable question hiding underneath—about the semantics of moral language, the epistemology of moral intuition, or whether the realist’s metaphysical postulates actually do explanatory work? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the impasse rather than a survey of the positions: what exactly the two sides are disagreeing about, whether the disagreement is verbal or substantive, what would settle or dissolve it, and whether we should expect philosophical progress on this issue. Please write the answer in American English.
