I've been tinkering with what I call the two-curve account of weakness of will, and I want an honest assessment before I get more attached to it. The core move: everyone actually has two discount curves, not one. There's the usual hedonic one, which discounts future payoffs as they recede from the present — hyperbolic, whatever. And there's a second one I'm calling the verdictive curve, which discounts other-time judgments: how much pull a verdict you made at t1 has on you at t2. My claim is that weakness of will is a mismatch between these two curves. When the hedonic curve is steeper than the verdictive curve, future payoffs outvote your earlier verdicts and you get akrasia. When the verdictive curve is steeper, earlier verdicts outvote future payoffs and you get whatever people call self-control. Either way there's no acting against your better judgment in the present. There's only a present balance between two dated forces, each discounted by its own curve.

Example: the dieter who at breakfast decides dessert is off the table and at dinner eats it anyway. The standard story has a synchronic conflict at dinner — she judges it bad and does it anyway. My story: the breakfast judgment has to survive from morning to evening, and by dinner it's been discounted below the cake. She isn't eating against her judgment; she's eating at a discount to it.

So: is this already in the literature? It sounds a little like Ainslie's picoeconomics but I can't tell if thats more then a vibe. Is the verdictive curve doing real work, or does it just collapse into the hedonic curve, since a future judgment is only one more future thing whose influence on you needs discounting? And give me the strongest case of akrasia my account can't handle — maybe a case where the judgment and the temptation are exactly simultaneous, so there's no gap for the verdictive curve to discount across.
