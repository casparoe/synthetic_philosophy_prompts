I just spent an evening arguing with a friend about whether anyone may be punished without having actually chosen or foreseen the harm. My friend kept pressing a line from Oliver Wendell Holmes, Jr.'s The Common Law, Lecture II, where Holmes contends that the early criminal law did not look to the defendant's actual state of mind but measured liability by an external standard—what a person of ordinary prudence would have foreseen from the act under the circumstances—so that the law asked whether a reasonable man would have avoided the act, not whether this particular accused intended it. I want to turn this into a sorting quiz for an undergraduate philosophy of law seminar. The quiz should be eight to ten concrete cases—things like a pharmacist dispensing a mislabeled drug after checking the label, a hunter shooting at movement without identifying it, a driver who loses control during a stroke, an arsonist trying only to scare someone but causing death—each with two or three answer options about liability and blame. The scoring key should map answers onto positions along a spectrum from pure strict liability to pure specific intent, including the external-standard negligence view Holmes describes, and it should flag combinations that are logically available but rarely occupied, such as punishing the blameless but not the reckless. The diagnostic questions must sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they endorse, and none should simply ask whether they accept Holmes's view. The instrument should also leave room for a position I argued: that the external standard is not a genuine exception to the fault principle, because failing to meet the standard is itself the fault.
