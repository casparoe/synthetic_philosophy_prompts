One standard argument associated with the Copenhagen interpretation runs as follows. Quantum mechanics is a calculus for the probabilities of measurement outcomes. For a measurement to occur at all, the apparatus must be described in classical terms: without a classical boundary between the measured system and the measuring device, the formalism supplies no definite events and no rule for applying the Born probabilities. The quantum/classical cut, wherever it is placed, is therefore in principle inescapable.

Decoherence theory later showed that this classical appearance can arise without postulating a fundamental boundary. When a system interacts with a large environment, the off-diagonal terms of its reduced density matrix decay rapidly, and interference between alternatives becomes unobservable in the basis selected by that interaction. The cut then looks like a practical, movable choice rather than a requirement of the theory itself.

Does the Copenhagen argument for the ineliminability of the quantum/classical cut survive this development? Answer by stating whether the original argument is dead, in need of repair, or largely intact; identify the specific premise that decoherence bears on; and explain what decoherence does and does not supply — in particular, whether showing that interference becomes unobservable is enough to yield a single definite outcome, or whether a remnant of the cut, or something playing its role, still has to be assumed.
