I just had a long argument with a mate about a tiny bit of Plato's *Crito*, and I still can't tell whether he's got me cornered.

At 43a, Socrates says: "I am surprised that the watchman of the prison was willing to let you in." And Crito replies: "He is used to me by this time, Socrates, because I come here so often, and besides I have done something for him."

My friend seized on this and built an argument that seems to prove way too much:

(1) The watchman is using his office to give Crito special access, because he knows him and because Crito has done him favours.
(2) If that's what's happening, then by any ordinary definition the watchman's behaviour is clientelistic: he's treating a public responsibility as a personal relationship.
(3) But it would be absurd to call the watchman corrupt. He's just being decent to an old man whose friend visits every day.
(4) So the usual Western distinction between impartial public duty and corrupt personal favour must be too rigid.
(5) And if that distinction is too rigid, then a lot of practises that NGOs and anti-corruption campaigners call "clientelism" or "gift-giving" are just ordinary human decency, not corruption.
(6) Therefore the standard anti-corruption framework is just applying parochial Western standards, and we should stop treating much of what it calls corruption as corrupt.

I feel like something slips between (3) and (5), but I can't name it. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? If you have to reject a premise, say which one and what rejecting it costs. And if the argument is valid, which premise is the least costly to give up?
