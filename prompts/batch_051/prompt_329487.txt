I am a retired population biologist, and in the last few years I have been working through the philosophy of biology, where one dispute continually draws my attention. The propensity interpretation of fitness holds that the fitness doing explanatory work in evolutionary theory is not an organism's or a genotype's actual reproductive output but a probabilistic disposition to leave offspring in a specified environment, much as a coin's bias is something over and above any particular run of heads. The standard motivation is that this reading blocks an old charge of tautology: if fitness were simply realized reproductive success, the principle that fitter organisms leave more descendants becomes the vacuous claim that organisms that leave more descendants leave more descendants. On the propensity reading, by contrast, fitness can be ascribed on the strength of one body of evidence, such as physiological capacities or environmental conditions, and then fail to be realized, which is what gives the theory empirical content and exposes it to error.

I would like you to help me design the best test you can of the claim that fitness is a propensity of this sort rather than a summary of actual reproductive outcomes. Treat the question as partly empirical and partly conceptual. Be concrete about what you would measure, compare, or intervene on, and about what observations would favor each reading. In particular, assess the idea I find most promising: that propensity ascriptions earn their keep only if fitness can be estimated from organism-environment properties independently of the outcomes it is meant to explain, and then used to make risky predictions. Say whether you would pursue that idea or replace it with something better.

I am after a committed judgment rather than a survey of options. Tell me which design you would actually run, which outcome would lead you to abandon the propensity reading, which outcome would lead you to defend it, and exactly what even a perfectly favorable result would leave unsettled.
