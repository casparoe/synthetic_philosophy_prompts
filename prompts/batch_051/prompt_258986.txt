I'm putting together a teaching handout for an upper-level undergraduate course in Indian epistemology, and I'd like you to write a classification instrument: a decision tree (or a short quiz with a scoring key) that takes a student from concrete cases to one of the classical positions on how many instruments of knowledge, pramāṇas, there are. The leaves I want included are: Cārvāka (perception only); Buddhist epistemologists and early Vaiśeṣika (perception and inference, with testimony treated as a special case of inference); Sāṃkhya (perception, inference, and testimony); Nyāya (perception, inference, testimony, and analogy or comparison); Prabhākara Mīmāṃsā (Nyāya's four plus postulation, arthāpatti); and Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā (Prabhākara's five plus non-apprehension, anupalabdhi, an inventory Advaita Vedānta also accepts). The students have no Sanskrit and are meeting these schools for the first time, so build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from the labels: first the diagnostic situations, then locate the schools in the resulting cells, and flag any cell no historical school occupies with a one-line note on whether that is because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

For the diagnostic questions, please work through something like the following cases, phrased so a student can answer without knowing which school each answer tracks. (1) Seeing smoke beyond a ridge without seeing fire — does this yield knowledge, or only a well-grounded suspicion until the fire is seen? (2) Learning something about a region you have never visited from a highly reliable guide — is this ultimately an inference from the guide's reliability, or a way of knowing that perception and inference do not exhaust? (3) Being told by a forester that a gavaya, a kind of wild ox, is much like a cow, then later seeing an unfamiliar animal and recognizing it as the thing called gavaya — does this require a distinct instrument, or is it fully accounted for by perception and testimony? (4) A friend is fat and eats nothing during the day, so you take it he must eat at night — is the move to the unseen night-eating a case of inference, or a distinct kind of postulation needed to make the observations cohere? (5) Looking around a room and knowing there is no jar in it — does knowing an absence require an instrument distinct from perception and inference? Aim for no more than six decision points and about six or seven leaves; give each leaf the school's name and one sentence on its most characteristic commitment. If the tree cannot separate Buddhist and early Vaiśeṣika, or cannot separate Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā from Advaita, say so plainly instead of inventing a distinction.

One thing I want to invite explicitly: if you think the framing of how many pramāṇas there are itself misrepresents what these schools were doing — for instance, because their real disagreements concern how a source's reliability is established, or whether absence and postulation are categories at all rather than distinct instruments — tell me that rather than dutifully producing a tree that flattens the debate. If you do produce the instrument, begin with the tree or quiz itself, with no preface and no restating of my request; put the rationale, scoring key, and any caveats about unoccupied or unresolvable cells after it.
