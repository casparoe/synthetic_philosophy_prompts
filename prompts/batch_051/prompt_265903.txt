I work on the policy planning staff of a middle power's foreign ministry, and I have been asked to draft the analytical annex for a decision on whether we should extend a security guarantee to a smaller neighbor that a much larger regional power has been intimidating through border exercises, trade restrictions, and support for separatists in the neighbor's east. The internal debate has split three ways. The first camp treats this as the 1930s again: the larger power is probing whether we will resist, each retreat emboldens it, and if we do not draw a line now we will face a general war later; they describe any hesitation as appeasement. The second camp treats it as a replay of Vietnam or Afghanistan: we would be pulled into an open-ended commitment to a weak local government, in terrain and a political culture we understand poorly, with no credible exit, and the greater danger to us is overreach rather than the regional power itself. The third camp insists that the lesson of history is structural rather than moral: when a rising power meets an established one, the Thucydides trap makes conflict more likely than not, so the task is crisis management and measured accommodation rather than deterrence or retreat.

I need an audit of these three historical analogies, because the memo's conclusion will largely be decided by whichever frame is allowed to carry the argument. For each analogy, give the correspondence table explicitly: what is being mapped to the appeaser, the sacrificed neighbor, and the later general war; what is being mapped to the quagmire, the weak ally, and the domestic turning point; what is being mapped to the rising power, the established power, and the mechanism that supposedly produces war. Then identify where each mapping breaks once you look at the present case directly rather than through the lens of the source case. I especially want to know what each analogy quietly imports from its source that may be absent here — assumptions about the other side's motives, about the reliability of the local ally, about the counterfactual path not taken, or about how tightly the structural forces bind — and whether those imported features are actually load-bearing for the conclusion each camp draws. Finally, tell me which policy conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, whether any of the three analogies deserves to frame the memo, and what a better framing would look like if none of them does.
