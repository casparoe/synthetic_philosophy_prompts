I'm preparing to teach Robert Bolt's A Man for All Seasons, alongside the 1966 film version, in a course on the moral psychology of integrity, and I want to use the play to work through a specific question: what is it to be uncorruptible, and is that always a good thing? The stock reading treats Thomas More as the saint of conscience—the man who will not take the Oath of Supremacy, will not sell his convictions for office, safety, or even his family's peace, and therefore goes to the block rather than bend. I'm suspicious of that reading, or at least I think it has to be earned rather than assumed. The More the play actually gives us is also legalistic, guarded, silent in ways that can look like evasiveness, and protective of his own self in a manner that can look like pride; his unbendingness costs Alice and Margaret terribly, and the play leaves us to ask whether More's way of handling Richard Rich—warning him away rather than taking him in—has any share in Rich's slide toward perjury.

I'd like a philosophical reading of the play through the lens of integrity and incorruptibility, one that does not decide too early whether More's constancy is a virtue or an instance of something less admirable—an unbreakable person who is also self-enclosed and relationally destructive. I'm especially interested in the difference between being the kind of person who cannot be bought and being the kind of person who cannot be moved: between integrity as a stance toward corrupting goods and integrity as simple immovability. Let the concept do real work in your reading; it can explain why Rich functions as More's dark double, why the King's need for universal compliance exerts its particular pressure, and why the compromises of Wolsey, Cromwell, and the other courtiers look like failures of a distinct kind rather than mere differences of interest. But let the play talk back too: attend to the scenes in which incorruptibility starts to look like obstinacy, or like a refusal to take responsibility for the damage one's purity causes, or like pride wearing a better name. Be faithful in both directions—do not reduce the play to an illustration of a philosophical theorem, and do not let its dramatic force quietly settle the question of whether More's way of being uncorruptible is, on the whole, good.

Structure the response as follows:
1. The reading (~550 words): a synoptic statement of how the play stages the problem of incorruptibility and where it seems to come down.
2. Where the lens illuminates (~500 words): the scenes, contrasts, and choices that the concept of incorruptibility makes legible, and that a looser talk of conscience alone would miss.
3. Where the work resists (~500 words): the moments where the play undercuts or complicates the thought that More's unbendingness is simply admirable, and what alternative the play is gesturing at, if any.
4. What the play contributes (~350 words): what the dramaturgy—the silence, the Common Man, the staging of the trial—adds to the philosophical question that a bare argument about integrity would not.
