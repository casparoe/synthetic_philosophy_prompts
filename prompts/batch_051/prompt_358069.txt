I'm running a philosophy of science reading group with a mix of grad students and engineer types, and we've been going around in circles on the pessimistic meta-induction. You probably know the argument: lots of once-successful theories turned out to be radically false about their unobservable posits (phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether), so by induction we should expect our current successful theories to be false too, and full-bore scientific realism starts to look naive. I want a handout that sorts people into positions on this debate by what they actually say about cases, not by which label they like.

So: write a decision tree (or a short quiz with a scoring key, whichever works better) that takes someone from their answer to a root question—roughly, whether a theory's empirical success is any reason at all to believe its claims about unobservables—and branches through concrete diagnostic questions until they land in a leaf. The leaves should include at least: standard scientific realism (successful theories are approximately true), structural realism (only the mathematical structure survives theory change), entity realism (manipulating entities gives confidence in them even when the surrounding theory changes), selective realism (only the parts of a theory that actually did the predictive work are preserved), constructive empiricism (theories aim at empirical adequacy, not truth), and instrumentalism (theories are just tools; truth-talk about unobservables is beside the point). Please build the tree from the logical space of answers first and then locate these six positions in it, rather than writing one stock question per label. Note any answer combinations nobody seems to defend and say briefly whether the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored; also flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate. Questions should be answerable by someone who doesn't already know the jargon, so no "are you a structural realist?" items. The group can handle a little technicality, but write the whole classification instrument in American English.
