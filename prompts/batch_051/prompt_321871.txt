I retired from physics a few years ago and, having time at last, have been working through the free-will problem. I find that I hold individual cases quite firmly but cannot state the principle that holds them together, and I would like you to try.

Let me fix my terms as I would have in my old discipline. By determinism I mean only this: the state of the world at one time, together with the laws and boundary conditions, fixes every later state. By indeterminism I mean only that some transitions are governed by objective chance, so that a single prior state can issue in more than one outcome. My trouble is that determinism is constantly treated as the thing that threatens free will, while I cannot see why putting an objective chance into the causal chain should remove whatever the threat was. My reactions to cases push me one way, but I cannot assemble them.

Here are the verdicts, as honestly as I can report them.

1. The deterministic deliberator. A woman decides to return a lost wallet full of cash. Suppose that this decision is the deterministic outcome of her beliefs, her values, and the particular course of her reflection. I cannot bring myself to say the act was not free; it issued from her in the only sense I understand, even though, under those suppositions, she could not have done otherwise.

2. The coin toss. A man cannot decide between two deserving charities, flips a coin, and gives according to how it lands. I count the giving as his act, but I would not say the selection of the charity was his choice. The random event did not add freedom to his decision; it replaced a judgment, not an obstacle to one.

3. The quantum neuron. Now suppose that whether a certain neuron fires during deliberation is fixed only probabilistically, so that the same prior mental state can with certain probabilities issue in either of two actions. I see no gain for freedom here. If the chancy firing moved the person toward an action at odds with her settled values, I would call that noise interfering with her freedom, not a condition of it.

4. The built character. Someone who has spent years shaping his habits so that certain temptations no longer arise seems to me more free in the relevant sense, not less, even though his later conduct is now more fully determined by his earlier choices. Determined by himself, but determined all the same.

5. The chancy machine. Consider an artificial agent whose action selection includes a hardware randomizer, so that, in the strict sense, the agent could have acted otherwise under identical circumstances. I do not recognize this as whatever it was I was worried about losing under determinism. I would sooner call the woman in case 1 free than call this machine free.

My working principle, such as it is: an act is free to the extent that it issues from a process that tracks the person's own beliefs, values, and considered judgments, and not free to the extent that it is moved by chance or coercion. But the principle is too permissive. A deterministic automaton programmed with the right values and a scoring rule would satisfy it, and I would not call the automaton free; and I cannot spell out 'the person's own' without assuming the very self I am trying to locate.

So I have three questions. First, is there a coherent principle that delivers my verdicts, or most of them, and what is it? Second, which of my verdicts does that principle force me to revise — and if it spares the human cases but revises the machine cases, what principle tells me why that is not mere species chauvinism on my part? Third, what, if anything, is gained for free will by replacing some deterministic transition in the chain of a decision with a quantum-indeterministic one? Please also run your principle through the case I keep avoiding because it seems the cleanest test: a person whose entire character, values, and deliberative habits were deliberately installed in childhood by another person, and who then acts from them. Is the act free on your principle, and do you stand by the result? And correct the cases where you think they are badly framed; I would rather lose a verdict than keep a principle that only flatters my examples.
