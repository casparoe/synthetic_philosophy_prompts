Two colleagues in my office (both engineers, neither does philosophy professionally) got into a long arguement over whether causation is something real in the world or just a habit of ours. Dan kicked it off by saying he'd been reading somewhere that fundamental physics never mentions causes: the laws are written as symmetric, time-reversible equations, and nowhere in them does one event "produce" another. His line was that causation is a folk concept, a bit like saying the sun rises — fine for everyday chat, but not part of how the world actually is, and the more science advances the less work it does.

Mira pushed back: she said that's the same move people used to make about heat or life, saying once you have the underlying physics the concept must be empty, and it's too quick. She said that even if the equations don't use the word 'cause', the practice of science is soaked in causal thinking — an experiment just is a case of manipulating something and watching what changes. If causation were only a projection, she said, then the difference between a controlled experiment and an uncontrolled anecdote would also be only a projection, which is absurd.

Dan replied that experiments are just situations where background conditions are held fixed, and calling the manipulated variable "the cause" is a decision about what we find salient, not a discovery. He added that the rooster doesn't cause the sunrise just because it reliably precedes it.

Mira came back with something like: fine, but then why do processes have a direction in time? You can't rerun a broken glass backwards, interventions can't be undone by running the same laws in reverse, so there is a real gradient he was ignoring. She also said that if Dan eliminated causation he'd still have to explain control — her thermostat changes the temperature, and denying that the thermostat causes the heating is just changing the subject, because nobody means anything spookier than that.

Dan's answer was that 'control' is just another folk concept, and it smuggles in an agent doing something, which is exactly the anthropomorphism we should be dropping. The honest picture, he said, is events and patterns; "the thermostat caused the heating" is just useful bookkeeping.

Mira's last move was that this doesn't explain why counterfactuals work so well: if you'd removed the thermostat, the room would have stayed cold, and we build bridges, drugs and policies on such counterfactuals. That kind of dependence, she said, doesn't look like mere bookkeeping — it looks like something in the world.

Then someone changed the subject to the office coffee machine, so it never got settled.
