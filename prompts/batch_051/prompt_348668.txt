I've been trying to pin down what I actually mean when I call someone free, and my gut calls on cases won't cooperate. Someone living under a benevolent dictator who never interferes but could: I'd still say they're not really free, but I can't point to a single thing they're stopped from doing. A worker whose boss has the legal right to fire her for any reason but happens to like her: she goes about her life fine, but I wouldn't call her free at work. A slave with a kind master who lets her come and go: obviously not free. Someone with agoraphobia so severe they can't leave the house, even though no one is stopping them: I'd call that unfree too, but in a different register. A city imposes an 11pm curfew after a riot, applied to everyone: I'd say people are less free at night, but it doesn't feel like the same thing as the slave. A monk who has taken a vow of obedience and follows it by choice: I don't want to say he's made himself unfree; he seems self-governing, even though his actual condition might match the slave's on a given day. A recovering addict who knows the compulsion is still there: free-ish, but somehow less free than someone who never had it.

My first pass at a principle was: freedom means other people aren't interfering with what you want to do. That handles the nice dictator and the curfew, but it says the kind master's slave and the agoraphobic are free, which feels wrong. The domination view fixes the slave and the dictator but seems to declare the agoraphobic fully free and to treat the curfew as no loss of freedom. The self-mastery view gets the agoraphobic and the addict but sits weirdly with the monk and the dictator.

What I want: test the candidate principles against these cases, be explicit about which of my verdicts each one forces me to abandon, and tell me whether the cleanest single principle is worth losing those verdicts, or whether I should accept that 'freedom' splits into two or three related notions. Then give your own preferred account and apply it to the case I keep flip-flopping on: a worker with strong transferable skills and savings, whose boss has arbitrary power over her but never exercises it. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then reasoning, no preamble, no restating the question.
