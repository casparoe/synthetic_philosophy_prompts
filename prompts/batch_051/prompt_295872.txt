I'm putting together a decision tree — like one of those "which philosopher are you" quizzes, but serious — for a community college ethics module. The question: when is it okay to kill insects for human benefit? The students have done the usual animal-welfare stuff (Singer/Regan type arguments) but nothing on bugs, so the tree should sort them by what they actually endorse in cases, not by which label they recognize.

Each branch point should rest on a concrete case or claim, e.g.: a fly drowning in your soup that you can't fish out without killing it; spraying a hectare for mosquitoes to maybe prevent two dengue cases; aphids on a rosebush; bedbugs in a cheap apartment; a locust swarm heading for someones crop. Answer options should be about doing or endorsing something, never "do you think insects are sentient?" straight up.

Leaves that must show up: (1) insects don't matter, (2) only indirect duties (it's wrong only when it coarsens you, not because the bug matters), (3) they matter but so little that ordinary pest control is fine, (4) they matter enough that only health/real necessity justifies it, (5) industrial insecticide use is a moral catastrophe because numbers aggregate into something huge, (6) the ecological view where individual bugs don't count but biodiversity and ecosystems do.

Work out the logical space first: lay out the questions and combinations of answers, locate those six leaves, and mark empty cells with a quick note on whether they're empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored — especially "moral status but no aggregation at all" and "individuals matter and ecosystems matter but you'd still pick the ecosystem." Flag any positions the tree can't separate rather than pretending a question splits them. Keep it to around 5–7 branch questions; if a question would only isolate one weird leaf, fold it into that leaf's one-line description instead of making it a branch.
