Six months ago I joined a county heritage commission, and I now have to vote on whether an interpretive sign at one of our sites should be rewritten to follow the archival record or left to follow the local community's transmitted account where the two conflict. The group supporting the transmitted account says, in effect, that collective memory is a different way of knowing the past rather than a worse approximation of the archive, and that it is therefore a category mistake to judge it by how well it corresponds to the documents. I can feel the pull of that claim, but I also suspect it may just be a dignified way of saying that the community prefers a story that holds it together, and I do not want to let that premise decide my vote without knowing whether it can be tested.

Take that claim seriously as a testable hypothesis. State what the claim would have to mean for evidence to bear on it, then design the best concrete test or study you can—real or realistically specified—that could distinguish genuine epistemic achievement by collective memory from the simpler hypothesis that communities defend divergent memories because those memories sustain identity and solidarity rather than because they track something the archive misses. Specify what you would measure, what comparison or condition you would use, and what pattern of results would count for each interpretation. Then say exactly what a strong positive result would and would not settle about the question I actually have to vote on, namely whether memory or the record should give way when they conflict. I want your committed assessment, not a menu of options: on the best available considerations, is the claim testable enough that it should move a public decision, or does the substantive part of it begin where the testable part ends? And should we keep saying that community memory is a way of knowing, or retire the phrase and say something leaner about identity and continuity?
