In most jurisdictions, death can be legally declared in one of two ways. Outside the intensive care unit, death is determined by the irreversible cessation of circulation and respiration. Inside the ICU, however, a patient on a ventilator can be declared dead while the heart is still beating and the body is still warm, provided there has been irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem; once this declaration is made, the patient's vital organs may be removed for transplantation. The same legal systems that speak of death as a single biological event allow either criterion to be used, with the choice depending on the clinical setting. On its face, the practice looks inconsistent: a patient with a beating heart and no brain function is dead, while a patient with a beating heart and an irreversibly destroyed cortex but a functioning brainstem is alive; and outside the hospital, death is almost always determined by the heart and lungs rather than the brain.

Which rival accounts of death — for example, a purely circulatory definition, or a higher-brain definition tied to the permanent loss of consciousness and personhood — does the practice quietly rule out, and where is the practice philosophically underdetermined such that more than one account of death could license it?
