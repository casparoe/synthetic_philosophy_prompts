Here is a passage from Meditation II of Descartes' Meditations on First Philosophy, in an older English translation:

"But I had the persuasion that there was absolutely nothing in the world, that there was no sky and no earth, neither minds nor bodies; was I not, therefore, at the same time, persuaded that I did not exist? Far from it; I assuredly existed, since I was persuaded. But there is I know not what being, who is possessed at once of the highest power and the deepest cunning, who is constantly employing all his ingenuity in deceiving me. Doubtless, then, I exist, since I am deceived; and, let him deceive me as he may, he can never bring it about that I am nothing, so long as I shall be conscious that I am something. So that it must, in fine, be maintained, all things being maturely and carefully considered, that this proposition (pronunciatum) I am, I exist, is necessarily true each time it is expressed by me, or conceived in my mind."

I want help separating what this passage actually proves from what it is often taken to prove. Read literally, the conclusion is that this proposition, "I am, I exist," is necessarily true each time it is expressed by me or conceived in my mind. But I can see at least four different things someone might mean by that:

(a) that there is a continuing self, a thinking substance, which exists through the whole period of doubt and thinking;
(b) that there is a subject of thought at each moment, whatever minimal thing that might be;
(c) that the act of doubting or of thinking is itself a kind of existence, so that the thought's occurrence proves itself;
(d) that the sentence "I exist" is guaranteed true on each occasion it is thought, though nothing is established from one occasion to the next.

Which of these, if any, does the text license, and which are being read into it? In particular, what work is the deceiver hypothesis doing in this passage, given that Descartes has already said he existed "since I was persuaded"? Would the argument be just as strong without the deceiver, and what does that show about the kind of certainty the cogito delivers? If you think the question pursues a distinction the text cannot settle, or that framing it as a list of alternatives already distorts what Descartes is doing, say so plainly; I would rather learn where the passage's resources stop than get a forced schematic answer. Keep the response to no more than 400 words.
