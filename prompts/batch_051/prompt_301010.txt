I've agreed to run a session for a small philosophy reading group I've been attending since I packed in the lab — I was a research chemist, so I'm comfortable with the formal bits but still new to the philosophy side. There'll be about a dozen of us, an hour all in, and we've been reading around decision theory. I want to run an activity on the absent-minded driver problem, which I find completely maddening. The puzzle is this: a driver is on a motorway and needs to take the second of two consecutive exits. The exits look identical, and because he's absent-minded, whenever he reaches one he cannot tell whether it is the first or the second — he has no memory of passing one already. If he exits at the first he gets a payoff of 0. If he exits at the second he gets 4 (points, sweets, whatever). If he carries on past both he gets 1. Before setting off he can adopt a rule — always exit, always carry on — or he can commit to randomising. If I've done the sums right, the best rule is to carry on with probability 2/3 at each exit, which gives 4/3 in expectation, better than always carrying on (1) or always exiting (0). But the interesting bit is the tension between the plan made beforehand and the reasoning that seems natural once you find yourself at an exit: at that point you're not sure which one you're at and you have to decide all over again, and the on-the-spot reasoning seems to pull in a different direction from the plan.

So what I'd like is a classroom game, for maybe 12 people and 50 minutes, that gets them to feel that tension themselves before I put labels on it. The thing I'm stuck on is how to stage the indistinguishability honestly: in a classroom people can count doors or remember their own previous choices, which quietly gives away whether they're at the first or second exit. I half-wondered about running it in teams where different members stand in for the same driver at different exits and aren't allowed to communicate, but I'm not sure that doesn't change the problem. What I need from you is a proper runnable design: rules, materials, a payoff scheme that doesn't need actual money (points or sweets are fine), and a debrief that maps what happens in the game onto the formal bits — the information set, the mixed strategy, the difference between planning and choosing on the spot. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about wich feature of the absent-minded driver idea the game can't capture and that I'll have to supply myself in discussion.
