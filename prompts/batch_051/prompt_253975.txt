I have no academic background in philosophy or biology; I work as a programmer and train small neural networks as a hobby, so what I know comes from blog posts, code, and arguments rather than from any course of study. Lately I have been trying to work out whether the claim that evolution is a stupid optimizer has real content, and whether natural selection is fairly described as a worse or blinder version of gradient descent. I have constructed an argument that seems to drive me to a conclusion I cannot accept, and I need an expert diagnosis of where it goes wrong.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) Natural selection and backpropagation are both search procedures over a space of possibilities: gene combinations in one case, weight assignments in the other.

(2) Both guide the search by a single scalar score. Selection scores a lineage by reproductive success in its current environment; gradient descent scores a network by its loss on training examples.

(3) Neither process has the score written out in advance across the whole space. Each reads the score only locally and noisily: evolution through whichever individuals happen to survive and reproduce, gradient descent through whichever batch of examples happens to be sampled.

(4) A search that moves through a space using only noisy local readings of one scalar score is stochastic hill-climbing. And two instances of stochastic hill-climbing differ only in how fast they step and in the labels they use.

(5) Stochastic hill-climbing has no lookahead. It cannot tell an uphill stretch on a broad, promising basin from an uphill stretch on the side of a local hump; it cannot leave a plateau in any principled way; and it cannot respect structure anywhere except along the path it already occupies.

(6) So natural selection and gradient descent share every limitation that follows from being a stochastic hill-climber. In particular, if natural selection is a stupid optimizer—one that lodges itself in frozen accidents and fits only the environment in front of it—then gradient descent is stupid in exactly the same way, and whatever generalization a trained network shows must be a gift of the loss landscape rather than a mark of the procedure.

(7) But that conclusion is one I find impossible to accept. Backpropagation uses the derivative of the loss to tell each weight which way to move and by how much. Natural selection merely accepts or rejects whole organisms after the fact, with no mechanism for assigning credit among the many genes that came packaged together. A procedure that assigns credit parameter by parameter and a procedure that culls whole finished variants are different in kind, not in notation or speed. Since they are different in kind, the conclusion in (6) cannot be true, so one of the earlier steps fails.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I would most like to keep (7). Each of the earlier steps looks innocent on its own, yet the conclusion looks false, and I cannot see how to hold both attitudes at once. If the argument is valid, say so, and tell me exactly what I would be committed to if I kept (7) anyway. Either way, give me the price of the move you recommend rather than just naming a step.
