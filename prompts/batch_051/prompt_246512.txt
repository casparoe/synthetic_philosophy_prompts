I'm a software engineer who reads game-theory stuff for fun, not someone with philosophy training, and there's a move I keep tripping over that I dont have a word for. I want to know if the move itself has an established name — but more than that, I want to know exactly what it is, what it's not, and where its edges are, because I can't tell if it's a real third thing or just one of the usual ideas dressed up.

Here's the clearest instance I've seen. Twenty people are gathered and told that if exactly one of them sends a telegram by noon the next day, that one person gets a billion dollars. If nobody sends one, or if more than one person sends one, nobody gets anything. No coordination allowed. The normal advice is: send the telegram, because if you don't send it you definitely can't win, and if you do send it there's at least some chance you'll be the only one. But there's this other way of thinking that says: all twenty people are in the same situation and are just as rational as you are, so whatever conclusion you reach, they'll reach too — so you should ask what happens if everyone acts on your reasoning. If everyone sends, nobody wins. If everyone holds back, nobody wins. So the other way of thinking sort of dissolves the 'obviously send it' answer, but it doesn't obviously tell you what to do instead, except maybe that you should randomize and send with probability 1 in 20, which is weird because that's a policy, not a choice.

Another instance: a magazine once ran a 'luring lottery' where the prize was a million dollars divided by the total number of entries, with the winner drawn randomly among entries, and you could submit as many entries as you wanted on one postcard. Individually, submitting more entries looks smart if other people don't pile in, but if everyone reasons 'submit a big number to raise my chance', the entry count explodes and the prize per entry goes to basically nothing. The same mirror-your-own-reasoning move shows up, and the supposedly right answer is to treat the number of likely responders as the sides of a die and only enter if you roll a 1.

So my question: is there an established name for this move — treating the other players as copies of your own reasoning process, or at least as symmetric agents who will land on the same policy you do, and then optimizing under that assumption? And how does it differ from (a) the everyday 'well, what if everybody did that?' argument, (b) the standard game-theory way of taking the other players' choices as given and doing the best you can against them, and (c) assuming all the players are secretly on the same team? Also, in the first puzzle, does 'everyone reasons the same way' force them all to pick literally the same action, or does it just force them to adopt the same probabilistic rule — and does that distinction matter for whether the move is coherent? I'd rather get 'it's not a clean thing, here are the three nearest named concepts and where each one misses' than a one-word answer.
