I'm giving a departmental colloquium defending a version of the Dutch book argument for probabilism. My thesis is that the best formulation of the argument — the one that strips out the money and locates the defect in the agent's own accepted valuations — establishes a genuinely epistemic failure, not merely a practical vulnerability to being exploited by a bookie. The audience is mostly formal epistemologists and decision theorists who already grant probabilism but are impatient with betting talk: several work in accuracy-first epistemology and think dominance arguments about accuracy do all the legitimate work, and one is a subjectivist who holds that coherence is the only rational constraint and is unmoved by any appeal to guaranteed loss. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. Give exactly five objections, ordered from most to least damaging, each phrased the way a member of this audience would actually say it. In each entry use at most two sentences: the first states the objection, and the second gives my best reply together with the concession I should make or the spot where the reply is softest. Make the fifth entry the question I should be hoping nobody asks. Start with objection one; no introduction or conclusion.
