Last night a friend and I spent a long evening arguing about Nomic, the game in which changing the rules is itself a move, and we ended up stuck on a variant of the problem the game is built to dramatize: how a system of rules can govern its own amendment without letting any rule entrench itself permanently. In the version of Nomic we were considering, every rule is marked either mutable or immutable. A mutable rule can be amended by the ordinary voting threshold. An immutable rule cannot be amended directly, but it can be transmuted into a mutable rule by a special procedure; once it has been transmuted, it can be amended like any other rule. The special procedure requires the unanimous consent of the players. The point of this device is that no rule is absolutely frozen, because the players acting unanimously can always make any rule changeable.

We then imagined a variant. The initial ruleset includes an immutable rule, R, which specifies the transmutation procedure for immutable rules, including itself. R says that any immutable rule may be transmuted to mutable with the unanimous consent of the players, and that unanimity is to be assessed at the close of the vote, not when the vote is cast. R adds that a player who votes against a proposed transmutation ceases to be a player at the close of the vote and therefore does not count as a player for the purpose of assessing unanimity. So if one player votes no, that player is removed from the electorate at exactly the moment the count is made, and the remaining players can count as unanimous even without changing a single dissenting mind.

My friend argued that the standard mutable/immutable analysis handles this without difficulty: R says the vote is assessed at the close of the vote, the dissenter is no longer a player by then, the remaining players are unanimous, so R is validly transmuted and becomes amendable like any mutable rule. I resisted. It seemed to me that a transmutation procedure is supposed to stand apart from the rule being changed and to constrain how that rule changes; if the rule being changed can redefine who must consent to the change, the safeguard has not been made stricter or looser but has simply been redescribed away. I also suspected that the standard account quietly assumes that the set of players whose unanimity is required is fixed independently of the proposal being voted on.

Work through the variant as carefully as you can. What does the standard mutable/immutable account actually say about R: is it effectively mutable, still meaningfully immutable, or something the account cannot classify? What should happen when the one dissenting vote is cast, and does the standard account have a single determinate answer? Does this variant show that the unanimity safeguard depends on a hidden premise about a stable demos, or does it simply show that R was drafted self-servingly and that the standard analysis would set its clause aside?
