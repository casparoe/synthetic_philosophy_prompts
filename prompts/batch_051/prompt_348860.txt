For a reading group I attend, I have been asked to write out my considered views on the moral structure of rights, so that the others can pressure-test them before I turn them into an essay. I find myself holding all five of the following positions, and I would prefer not to abandon any of them without knowing exactly what I am paying for it.

(1) Rights act as side-constraints on action. It is normally impermissible to violate a person's right even when the violation would produce a greater sum of welfare, and this is not simply a caution about our fallible measurement of welfare. (2) Rights are not brute moral primitives; I think of them as instruments or conventions, adopted because they protect certain individual interests that would otherwise be too easy to override or neglect. (3) Nevertheless I do not think the concept of a right is eliminable. Attempts to paraphrase every rights-claim into claims about interests, beneficence, or aggregate welfare lose something that public justification and the marking of what is owed to each person seem to require. (4) I accept a threshold view of overrides: in a sufficiently severe emergency — suspending someone's freedom of movement to slow a lethal epidemic, for example — a right may be overridden, though not by marginal or merely aggregative gains. (5) I think consent marks the difference between a permissible transaction and a rights-violation in a deep way: an act that would clearly be a violation if imposed may be unobjectionable if it is freely agreed to.

I cannot see whether this package hangs together, and I do not have a reliable sense of where the fault lines run. Please audit it. Go through the commitments pairwise and identify which are genuinely in tension, which merely appear to be, and why. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equal. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give — which commitment or sub-claim — and what the cheapest revision would be. Finally, tell me which single revision would leave the soundest overall position, and whether any of the five commitments can be kept in good conscience as a pragmatic simplification rather than as the literal truth.
