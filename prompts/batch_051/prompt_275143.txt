I'm writing a short statement of my views on promising for a reading group — we've been arguing about whether promising is a genuine moral relation or just a useful social technology — and I'd like a consistency check before I circulate it, because I have a feeling I've absorbed pieces from different authors without checking whether they actually fit together. Here's roughly where I am:

(1) I don't think promissory obligation is just a proxy for the balance of consequences. If I've promised something, I can't dump it merely because I now judge that breaking it would lead to a somewhat better outcome overall.

(2) I think the obligation is directed: breaking a promise to you isn't just a free-floating wrong act, it wrongs you specifically. That's also why you, the promisee, are the one who releases me — not a impartial third party and not the community in general.

(3) I think promises are convention-dependent. For the words to count as a promise at all, rather than a private resolution I happen to say out loud, there has to be a standing social practice of promising in the background. Outside such a practice the utterance wouldn't do the promissory work.

(4) I think the promise binds even when the promisee never actually forms an expectation. If I borrow money from a forgetful friend, promise to pay her back, and then learn she's completely forgotten the loan and will never notice whether I pay, I still owe her the money. Same with promising to meet someone who ends up not showing up: it was still a real promise, not a void one.

(5) At the same time, the positive story I keep being drawn to is the Scanlon-style expectation account — roughly, that promissory obligation is grounded in the assurance I intentionally lead another person to form, and in my responsibility for not disappointing the expectation I invited them to trust.

(6) Deathbed promises bind. If I promise a dying friend that I'll look after her kid once a week, the fact that she dies and there's no one left to release me doesn't dissolve the obligation. I still owe it.

So here's what I want: run through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension, which only look as if they might be, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. For each real tension, say what the cheapest fix would be and what it costs. Then tell me which single revision of the set would do the most to make the whole thing cohere. And one request about the writeup: keep clear which of your criticisms are just my own confusion or an unstable combination of mine, versus what's genuinely contested among people who work on promising — and where it's a live dispute, say so instead of presenting your preferred side as the settled view.
