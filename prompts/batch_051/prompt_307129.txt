I've been chewing on the problem of universals for a month and I think I've built a position that avoids universals without going full nominalist, though I'm not sure if I've actually built anything or just rebadged operationalism in metaphysics drag. I'm calling it attractor nominalism, and I want you to be mean about it.

Here's the view. Start from the standard problem: a strawberry and a fire engine are both red. The realist says there is one thing, redness, wholly present in both. The trope theorist says two numerically distinct red-tropes that exactly resemble each other. I want to say neither. A property, on my view, is not an ingredient in an object at all; it's an attractor of a comparison practice. By that I mean: give competent observers, or instruments, a bunch of objects and the question "same or different in colour?" and, across people, lighting conditions, and repeated runs, they converge on a stable partition of the objects into red and not-red. Redness just is that stable partition. Two things "share" redness in the same way a heap of beads "share" being ones a magnet picks up: a procedure reliably puts them on the same side of the cut.

What makes the partition objective is not that the world contains a red-making ingredient, but that the sorting doesn't destabilise when you stretch it—new observers, new objects, worse conditions up to a point, independent instruments. The stability, not a shared entity, is doing the work.

I like the view because it dissolves the one-over-many problem (nothing is wholly present in two places), it grades properties by how real they are (charge produces convergence across cloud chambers and electrometers; afor tastiness you mostly get drift), and it extends cleanly to unobservables: when we say an electron has negative charge we're not pointing at a universal, we're pointing at the fact that scattered instruments keep returning the same verdict.

So, honestly: is this coherent? What is the actual closest existing view—have I just reinvented operationalism, or response-dependence, or something from measurement theory that I should already know? What are the three strongest objections to the whole attractor idea, and which of them are fatal rather than just awkward? And at the end, say plainly whether the view is distinct enough to keep, or whether it collapses into something older the moment you stop waving your hands.
