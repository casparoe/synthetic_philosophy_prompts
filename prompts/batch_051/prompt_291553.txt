I keep coming back to Candide when I teach early modern theodicy, because the students' first move is always to treat Pangloss as though he just is Leibniz, and then the seminar turns into an hour of mocking the strawman. I'd like you to give me a reading of the novel against the actual best-of-all-possible-worlds view: not the slogan, but the machinery in the Theodicy — God surveying possible worlds, compossibility, the distinction between antecedent and consequent will, physical and moral evil being permitted because the best total system requires them. Which episodes of the novel genuinely put pressure on that machinery, and which ones only land against the refrain that all is for the best? The obvious set pieces are the Lisbon earthquake and the drowning of Jacques while Pangloss lectures Candide about it being for the best. But I'd rather have a scene-by-scene judgment of which pieces of Leibniz survive the book and which it never actually touches. And I want your honest read on the ending: does the garden amount to a new doctrine — something like meliorism, tend your own plot, make no claims about the whole — or is it just Voltaire changing the subject when he can't refute Leibniz in the abstract? I've been argueing with myself about this for years. If the novel does refute something real, tell me what exactly it refutes, and whether a Leibnizian has a reply that stays faithful to the Theodicy.
