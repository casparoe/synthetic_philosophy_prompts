You're a professional philosopher who works on what we owe each other, so you'll know the territory. Think carefully before you answer: start by sketching the strongest versions of the two positions I'll describe, then commit to one — I'm not after a neutral survey.

I keep being pulled between a tidy view and a stubborn feeling. The tidy view is that what we owe each other is entirely about what we do: the help we give, the harm we avoid, the respect we show in our words and actions. On this view, feelings are the wrong sort of thing to owe, because you can't directly control them, and demanding that someone feel a particular way seems authoritarian and impossible anyway. If someone looks after you properly and treats you with courtesy, they've done everything they owe you, whatever is going on in their head.

But I have an intuition I can't shake. Imagine a nurse who cares for elderly patients by the book — never rude, never negligent — but is visibly disgusted by them the whole time, in small glances and sighs. Or someone who saves your life but does it with contempt, the way you'd pull a stray dog out of the road. In both cases I want to say the person has still failed to give the other something important, even though every action was fine. Yet when I try to say what that something is, it sounds like I'm saying we are owed feelings, and that seems to demand the impossible.

So here's my question: should I give up the intuition as a disguised demand to control other people's inner lives, should I amend the tidy view so that at least some attitudes really are owed, or is there a reading on which both survive? If you amend the view, tell me how that doesn't collapse into policing people's thoughts. I want a committed answer — wich way would you break it, and what exactly would it take for you to flip the other way?
