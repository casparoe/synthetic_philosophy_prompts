i've been going down a rabbit hole with the modern Stoicism stuff — the productivity-adjacent Marcus Aurelius revival — and I suspect a book I read has quietly slipped a different distinction into the place of the old dichotomy of control, but I cant tell whether I'm being pedantic or whether the difference actually matters. The book gives roughly this summary (I'm reconstructing it from my notes, so the wording is close but not verbatim):

"The Stoic dichotomy of control is a sort of two-bucket system. Some things are up to us and some aren't. What's up to us are our actions, our effort, and the way we choose to respond to events; what isn't up to us are outcomes, other people's reactions, and the big background facts like markets, pandemics, and death. The Stoic practice is to sort every worry into one bucket or the other: pour your energy into the first, and refuse to waste it on the second. If you're sick, your response and your treatment decisions are in the first bucket, but whether you recover isn't, so a Stoic wouldn't stress over the recovery itself. The things outside your control are 'indifferents' — health, money, reputation — which in Stoicism means they don't matter, so you're supposed to be okay with losing them. This is the core of the ancient view, and it's basically what modern CBT inherited."

Then I went back to Epictetus and hit this passage, which stopped me. It's from the George Long translation of the Discourses, Book I, chapter 1, where Zeus is made to say:

"Epictetus, if it were possible, I would have made both your little body and your little property free and not exposed to hindrance. But now be not ignorant of this: this body is not yours, but it is clay finely tempered. And since I was not able to do for you what I have mentioned, I have given you a small portion of us, this faculty of pursuing an object and avoiding it, and the faculty of desire and aversion, and, in a word, the faculty of using the appearances of things; and if you will take care of this faculty and consider it your only possession, you will never be hindered, never meet with impediments; you will not lament, you will not blame, you will not flatter any person."

This sounds to me like Epictetus is saying something noticeably sharper than the book: he doesn't put "my actions and efforts" in the controlled bucket, he puts what looks like the faculty of desire, aversion, and judgment there, and he explicitly says my body is "not mine." But maybe the book is just modernizing the distinction for a practical audience and the differences are cosmetic.
