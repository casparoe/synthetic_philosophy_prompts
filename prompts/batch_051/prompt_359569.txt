I’m a high schooler trying to teach myself programming more seriously, and I’ve been working through Abelson and Sussman’s *Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs*. Up through chapter 1, the substitution model was the one part of programming that felt genuinely like math: an expression evaluates to a value, a variable is just a name for a value, and applying a procedure is just a matter of replacing things carefully. I liked that there was no time, no hidden state, no “what is x right now?” But then chapter 3 introduces assignment and says this: “Once we admit assignment into our programming language, such a definition is no longer adequate. In particular, section 3.1.3 argued that, in the presence of assignment, a variable can no longer be considered to be merely a name for a value. Rather, a variable must somehow designate a ‘place’ in which values can be stored.” That hit me hard, because it seems to say the box/place picture I had before wasn’t just a beginner’s crutch—it’s the correct picture once mutation enters the language.

So here’s the clash I cant get past. On one hand, the substitution model is simple, compositional, and lets me reason locally about code without keeping a history in my head. It feels like the only thing standing between me and total chaos when programs get big. On the other hand, my firm intuition when I write something like `x = x + 1` is that `x` is a persisting location that gets updated, and that this is what real programs—bank accounts, games, anything with changing state—actually need. I don’t want someone to just say “use functional programming” or “mutation is bad.” I want to know: is the persisting-place intuition something I should give up as a habit from Python, or is the substitution model something that only works for a small, safe subset of programs and should be demoted to a special case? Or is there a reading where both are right for different purposes? Walk me through what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether the passage itself has already chosen a side.
