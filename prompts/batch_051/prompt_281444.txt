I work in the grants department of a mid-sized foundation that funds health and education organizations in regions where most capable partners are compromised in some way. Several times a year I have to recommend whether to renew a grant after learning that a partner, or a subcontractor it depends on, is involved in wrongdoing that our grant neither pays for nor causes — a contractor paying its cleaners below the legal minimum, a partner’s director taking kickbacks, a government co-funder with a record of abuses. I want a reusable decision procedure that my staff, who have no legal or philosophical training, can run in an afternoon before each review, not an essay on complicity. The procedure should be a sequence of questions with clear stopping rules, each carrying a standard of evidence sufficient to answer it: which facts, if shown, establish that we are merely causally adjacent to the wrongdoing; which facts establish that we are complicit even though we caused none of it; and which questions are value judgments to escalate to the board rather than settle by checklist. Make the procedure keep the distinctions that actually do the moral work — causing versus enabling versus endorsing versus benefiting from versus normalizing wrongdoing, and the special case in which the harm would occur whether or not we stay. Then run the procedure on two renewal decisions: a highly effective clinic whose building manager underpays its cleaners, and a literacy program delivered through a partner that shares staff with a political faction guilty of voter intimidation. Make sure at least one application returns an answer our board will resist, and state plainly what the procedure cannot decide for us.
