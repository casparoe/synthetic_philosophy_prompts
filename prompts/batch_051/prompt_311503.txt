I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing a longform piece on why precise-sounding rules — content moderation policies, clinical thresholds, statutory definitions — keep producing arbitrary-looking outcomes at the edges. The philosophical material I want to lean on is the theory of vagueness and the sorites paradox, but I need my criteria sorted before I start interviewing philosophers. Please write me the specification: what must a theory of vagueness actually do to count as adequate, not just for seminar purposes but for making sense of the practical cases I cover. I don't need sources or citations; I want the argumentative reconstruction.

Please structure your response with the following five sections, in this order, and keep to roughly these lengths.

1. The phenomena (≈250 words). List the canonical phenomena a theory of vagueness must be answerable to: the sorites argument itself, with a clearly heap-y pile losing grains one by one until it is clearly not a heap and each single-grain step feeling valid; borderline cases; the absence of any discernible sharp boundary even between the clear and the borderline cases; higher-order vagueness; penumbral connections, such as a surface's being red excluding its being green even near the edges; and context-sensitivity, such as tall for a ten-year-old versus tall for a professional basketball player. Give a sentence on why each is something the theory must handle rather than dismiss.

2. Non-negotiable requirements (≈600 words). State each adequacy condition together with the case or consideration that forces it. At minimum, address these: locating the failing premise or rule in the sorites argument while leaving ordinary reasoning intact; giving an account of borderline cases that does not simply ignore them or treat them as incompetence; explaining why, if there is a sharp boundary, no one seems able in principle to find it; handling higher-order vagueness without introducing a new sharp line one level up; preserving penumbral inferences; and explaining why vague predicates remain useful in communication and rule-making despite all this. Mark each as non-negotiable and say why it cannot merely be traded away.

3. Negotiable commitments (≈300 words). Which demands that people commonly attach to theories of vagueness are really the preferences of particular camps rather than adequacy conditions — for example, preserving classical bivalence and excluded middle, assigning degrees of truth, treating context as the whole story, or treating vagueness as purely a feature of language rather than of the world or of our knowledge. Say what each commitment buys and what it costs.

4. Joint satisfiability (≈300 words). This is the part I most want to see: which subsets of the requirements, including the negotiable ones, cannot be jointly satisfied, and why. In particular, I suspect that no theory can simultaneously keep bivalence, deny sharp boundaries, and treat the tolerant step of the sorites argument as valid; test that suspicion explicitly and say where a theory must give ground.

5. The exclusionary requirement (≈150 words). Identify the single requirement on your list that, if enforced strictly, does the most to rule out the familiar families of views — epistemic, supervaluationist, degree-based, contextualist — and say in one or two sentences how each family fails or survives it. Do not turn this into a full scorecard; the list is the deliverable.
