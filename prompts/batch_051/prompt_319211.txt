I've been trying to work out when it makes sense to resent someone, forgive them, or hold them responsible, by examining how I actually respond to a range of cases, and I cannot find the line. Here are the verdicts I feel fairly confident about.

If someone shoves past me on a crowded train because the crowd jostled him, I am briefly annoyed, but resentment seems misplaced. If a friend lies to me about an important matter to get out of something he promised to help with, I resent him, I think that resentment is called for, and I would expect him to make amends before I fully trusted him again. If a colleague ruins a presentation of mine out of spite, resentment is apt; the fact that she was going through a difficult divorce does not cancel it, though it tempers how I express it. If a person with a brain tumor becomes cruelly indifferent to his family, I cannot find resentment in myself; it is not exactly that I excuse him, but rather that resentment seems aimed at the wrong kind of target. I still think it would be right to restrain him or see that he is cared for. Yet if someone who was violently abused as a child grows up to manipulate people, I do resent him for the manipulation, even though I worry this conflicts with my brain-tumor verdict, since both cases seem to involve behavior flowing from a damaged capacity for ordinary mutual regard. If a driver checks a text message, hits and kills a pedestrian, I think the victim's family's anger is apt even though the driver did not intend any harm; this feels different from the crowd-shove case, but I cannot say exactly how. Finally, if a company knowingly markets a product it knows is dangerous, I feel something like moral anger at the company, but I doubt it is literally resentment, since a company is not a person.

My working hypothesis is that resentment and forgiveness make sense only toward someone I can still see as a fellow participant in a shared practice of mutual regard, someone who can be called upon to explain himself, whereas once I shift to treating someone as an object of management, care, or training, the question of resentment does not arise. But that hypothesis does not explain why the negligent-driver case differs from the crowd-shove case, it fits the childhood-abuse case only awkwardly, and it says nothing about collectives like a company.

What principle, or small set of principles, best captures these verdicts? Which of my verdicts, if any, should I be forced to give up, and what does the principle you propose say about cases I have not listed, such as an addict who steals to support the addiction, an artificial companion that says deliberately hurtful things, or a soldier who commits an atrocity under extreme duress?
