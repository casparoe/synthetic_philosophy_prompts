Longtime lurker here. My company is rolling out a new decision-record policy and I'm trying to figure out whether it's subtly brilliant or a slow-moving epistemics disaster.

The mechanism, as proposed: decisions get made the normal way (meetings, votes, exec fiat, whatever). But no one is allowed to write down they're own reason for a decision. Instead, after a decision is locked in, there's a 72-hour "rationale contest." Anyone can submit a justification for the chosen option. Contestants don't have to have been in favor of it, don't have to have been in the room, and aren't allowed to ask the deciders why they did what they did. Each submitted rationale gets scored by a prediction market on a single question: "Conditional on this rationale being recorded as the official reason, the decision's downstream metric (set in advance) will improve." The winning rationale becomes the official reason, is written into the permanent record, and is what later employees, auditors, and future AIs see when they ask "why did we do this?" The actual deciders reasons are never recorded, and after about a year the people who made the decision are instructed to stop saying what they were thinking, to keep the record clean.

So the record ends up full of reasons that may never have been anyone's reason. They were authored by someone, sure, but they won by market score, not by being believed, and nobody is required to have held them at the time.

I have my own guesses about where this goes, but I'd rather read a proper walk-through than trust my priors. Please give me:

- "The official reason" (about 150 words): what kind of thing the winning rationale tends to be—does it track actual causes, or converge on post hoc narratives?
- "Incentives" (about 150 words): what this trains people to do when they submit rationales, when they make decisions, and when they later consult the record.
- "Gaming" (about 150 words): what the first serious exploit is, and whether a determined group can make the official record say whatever they want.
- "Second-order drift" (about 150 words): what the institution starts to believe about itself after a few years of this, and how that changes the next round of decisions.
- "Patch or scrap" (about 100 words): the best fix that keeps the spirit of the thing, or why no fix keeps the spirit and it should be scrapped.

American English please.
