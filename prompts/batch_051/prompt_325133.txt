I've been rereading Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go for a seminar, and I keep coming back to the nonidentity problem. The rough idea: if the donation programme is the reason Kathy, Ruth and Tommy exist at all, then no particular clone is worse off than they would have been without it, because without it they would never have existed; and their lives, for all the horror, do contain love, art and friendship, moments that are plainly worth having. So the usual "someone was harmed" objection seems to loose its grip. Yet the novel is full of moral horror and clearly wants us to feel the students have a complaint. I'd like a reading of the novel through that lens: which scenes make the nonidentity problem vivid, which ones quietly block or complicate it, and does Ishiguro end up showing something the philosophers' version misses? I want to teach the book alongside the problem without distorting either. Please write in British English.
