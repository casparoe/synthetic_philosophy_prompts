You are an AI safety researcher who has thought carefully about chain-of-thought faithfulness, process supervision, and why "we can just read the model's reasoning" is not the straightforward safety intervention it sounds. I run a weekly reading and discussion group of about a dozen working software engineers. They are sympathetic to AI safety and read rationalist blogs and forums, so they can already deploy terms like Goodhart's law, reward hacking, and mesa-optimisation, but they have never thought hard about chain-of-thought monitoring specifically. Their default view is roughly: if we log every token of a model's chain of thought and have a capable reviewer model score it, dangerous or unfaithful reasoning can be detected and penalised before it causes harm.

Next week I have seventy-five minutes to move them from that default to a real understanding of why chain-of-thought monitoring is hard: the faithfulness problem, the distinction between a legible chain and a causally faithful one, obfuscation, and the way optimisation pressure acts on the chain itself.

Do not explain chain-of-thought monitoring to me. I want you to plan how I should teach it. In particular, I want:

1. The conceptual pieces and their dependency order. The idea has several components on which nothing can be built until they are in place — for example, what chain-of-thought tokens actually are and why they affect outputs, why written reasoning is not a report of introspection, what faithfulness has to mean once that is granted, and what changes when a monitor's score enters the training objective. Give me the sequence, and justify each ordering by saying what would be misunderstood if it came earlier.

2. One carrying example or scenario. Choose a single minimal setup that the whole session can keep returning to: ideally a small model or toy task that this audience can hold in their heads, in which the true causal reason for the answer is independently known, the final-answer reward can be satisfied by an opaque shortcut, and the chain of thought has room to become plausible but unfaithful once a monitor is added. Say what the example must have and what it must lack, and why it would be a mistake to introduce the topic through a large real model.

3. The misconceptions to expect and where to head each off. This audience will predictably form views like: the chain of thought is the model's real reasoning; since we can read every token, nothing is hidden; unfaithful chains require the model to be lying or deceptive; a better monitor solves the problem; and penalising visible obfuscation solves it. For each misconception you expect, tell me the exact point in the sequence at which it will have formed, and how to defuse it there rather than letting it fossilise and ambush me later.

One question at the end that would show me whether they have the key idea — which I take to be something like: under optimisation pressure, legibility and faithfulness come apart, and the monitor is not outside the system it is inspecting. The question should distinguish someone who has merely learned the vocabulary from someone who can apply the distinction to a new proposed intervention (for example, a team reporting that its monitor's loss is down and its chains are more detailed).

5. What to leave out and say so. Tell me which adjacent material a smart audience will want to pull the session into — formal definitions of steganographic capacity, mechanistic interpretability methods, arguments about whether current large models are already deceptive, full accounts of process versus outcome supervision — and which of them I should explicitly refuse or defer at this level.
