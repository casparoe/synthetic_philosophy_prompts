I’ve been going round in circles on the many-worlds thing and I think I’ve talked myself into believing a version of quantum immortality. I’m not a physicist or anything, just been reading bits and pieces. In Max Tegmark’s paper The Interpretation of Quantum Mechanics: Many Worlds or Many Words? he sketches a thought experiment where a person sits in front of a gun connected to a quantum measurement. The gun either fires or doesn’t depending on, say, a spin measurement. He says that in the standard collapse view the person ends up dead in some runs and alive in others, but in many-worlds the experimenter should expect to find themselves alive every time, because the dead branches contain no one left to have a next experience. That thought has really got its hooks in me. So here’s the position I’ve ended up with: if you genuinely accept many-worlds, then for any such life-or-death quantum event, your own future experience can’t be in a branch where you’re dead, so you should predict that you’ll keep finding yourself alive, even in setups that look extraordinarily dangerous. I’m not saying you should jump off a building to test it, but as a point about what you should expect if the interpretation is true, it seems hard to escape.

What I want is for someone to lay out what would actually count against this position. Not just the standard objection that many-worlds has trouble making sense of probability — I’ve seen that. I mean: which specific assumptions does quantum immortality silently rely on, and what would have to be true about personal identity, about the measure of branches, about decoherence, or about what counts as a person for the conclusion to come apart? Are there modifications of many-worlds where the conclusion doesn’t go through but the interpretation stays recognisably many-worlds? What would a defender of the position themselves admit would be evidence against it, if such evidence could ever come in, and is that lack of possible evidence itself a problem? I’d like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I’d like to know where the line is between a real test and a rhetorical restating of the position. Also, please say how confident you are in the specific claims you make and single out the point you’re least sure of.
