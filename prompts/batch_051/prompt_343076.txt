Keynes argued that a lot of long-run economic decision-making rests on uncertainty that can't be reduced to numerical probabilities — not becuase we lack data, but because matters like the state of the world in ten years aren't the kind of thing a probability can attach to. For him this was doing real work: it's why he thought long-term investment is intrinsically fragile, and why markets fall back on convention, habit, and animal spirits rather than calculation.

Now suppose you hand me a mature prediction market or a stable of well-calibrated superforecasters, and they assign numbers to precisely the matters Keynes said were out of reach: the chance of a war in a region over the next decade, the chance a new energy technology becomes cheaper than the incumbent, the chance a tax regime survives the next three parliaments. Some of those numbers even track, over the long run, on matters where everyone used to say no rational probability exists.

Does Keynes's argument survive that development? I want to know what happens to the argument itself, not whether the forecasts are useful for particular decisions. If it survives, say exactly which premise the forecasters fail to affect and why. If it needs repair or is dead, give the repaired version and say whether it can still do the original work — the claim that long-term investment has a fragility that no amount of better data removes. Or, if you think Keynes was really making a point about what an individual can rationally base a decision on, as opposed to what a market can price, say so and draw the line.
