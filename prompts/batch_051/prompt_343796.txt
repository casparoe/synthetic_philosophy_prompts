I'm a PhD student in philosophy of perception, and I've gone down a bit of a rabbit hole on photography while drafting a chapter about whether perception can be extended through instruments like telescopes and cameras. A lecturer in my department gave a seminar on the aesthetics of photography last term and summarised Walton's transparency thesis from memory. I scribbled it down at the time, and theres a good chance I'm overthinking it, but something felt off. As I remember, he said:

"Walton's transparency thesis is the claim that photographs are transparent in something like the way windows and mirrors are: when you look at a photograph of your late grandmother, you literally see her — not merely a depiction of her. The argument is that photographs, unlike paintings, are produced mechanically by the scene itself. Light reflected off the objects imprints itself on the film or sensor, with no human's beliefs about how things look getting in the way. That mechanical, belief-independent causation is what makes photographs transparent. And it's also why photographs can't lie in the way paintings can: a painting of a smiling grandmother might be the painter's flattering invention, but a photograph had to be caused by what was actually in front of the lens. So on this view photographs put us in genuine perceptual contact with the past — looking at a Civil War photograph is literally seeing the 1860s — and that's why we rightly admit photographs, but not sketches, as evidence in court. Seeing a photo is, in principle, no different from seeing the event through a window, just delayed. The standard objection is that you can't see something that no longer exists, since vision requires a direct causal link to a present object — and anyway a blurry photo shows you less than a good painting does. Both, I think, refute the view rather quickly."

I can buy that Walton says photos are like mirrors and telescopes, and that mechanical causation is doing the real work. But I got suspicious at the slide from "we see through photos" to "photos can't lie" and then to "that's why they're courtroom evidence" — that feels like a jump from a metaphysical point about seeing to an epistemic point about reliability, and I thought Walton explicitly allowed that you can see badly or be misled by what you see. I also don't know whether the past-seeing bit is a fair statement of his view or an overstatement, and whether the objections at the end are really the knock-down arguments this lecturer made them sound like.

Could you act as a specialist in aesthetics and the philosophy of perception and help me audit this summary? Please think carefully, maybe start by laying out what the transparency thesis actually says and doesn't say, then go through the summary sentence by sentence and flag what's accurate, what's a common simplification, what's wrong, and what a defender of Walton would say in response to the two "refutations" at the end. I'm also trying to work out whether the courtroom-evidence upshot follows from anything true in the summary, or whether it only seems to follow once transparency has been conflated with accuracy.
