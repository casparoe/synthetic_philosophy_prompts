I've been going back and forth with someone on a forum about whether the alignment problem is even well-posed, and it sent me back to Leibniz, who I hadn't read properly since undergrad. The bit that's stuck with me this time is how much of his ethics rests on the idea that rational minds form a moral community — he calls it a kingdom of grace, a moral world inside the natural world — and that this moral order is already harmonised with the physical order, not because of anything we engineer, but because God actualised the best of all possible worlds and every substance expresses the whole universe from its own point of view. He also treats justice as a kind of wise love aimed at the happiness and perfection of rational beings. And then there's the mill argument: even if you could walk into a machine like a mill and inspect every part, you'd never find a perception, so a purely mechanical thing can't actually be a mind.

So here's the question I keep flip-floping on. Suppose Leibniz somehow got a briefing on modern attempts to build aligned AGI. Would he think alignment is a sensible goal at all, given that on his metaphysics a mechanical artefact can't have genuine perceptions or desires, so in the important sense there's nothing there to align? Or would he say that if you did manage to bring a genuine rational mind into being, its place in the moral world would be secured by the same pre-established harmony that coordinates minds and nature, so the alignment problem mostly dissolves and the real question is just whether we can create a new substance, which he'd probably think we can't? Or would he diagnose the whole framing as confused because it smuggles in a modern idea that values are just a reward signal you can design independently of wisdom? I want a definite reconstruction, not a hedge, even though obviously it's counterfactual. And please say which step in your argument is the weakest, and what consideration or textual evidence would most likely make you reverse your overall verdict.
