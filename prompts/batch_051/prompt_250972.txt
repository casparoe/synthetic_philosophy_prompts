I've been asked to moderate a term-long fight between two friends from law school: one is a pretty committed textualist about statutory interpretation, and the other is a purposivist who thinks the whole plain-meaning thing is mostly a cover story for whatever outcome the judge wants. Instead of letting them do another point–counterpoint, I want to hand them a co-authored document — the kind of thing an adversarial collabortion would produce if they actually had to write one together. Could you draft that for me?

The document should have three parts. First, an "agreed claims" section: substantive things both can sign about statutes, meaning, and the judicial role, but nothing vacuous — so not just "words matter" or "judges shouldn't legislate," but the genuine overlap, like what a text can and can't settle by itself, or what's wrong with leaning on one senator's floor speech. Second, a disagreements section, maybe as a table or numbered list, where each point of difference is phrased so the textualist would accept the wording of his own side and the purposivist would accept hers — I don't want one side having to sign a strawman. Third, a short cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one of them. For each crux, give a concrete statuory example or kind of case they'd both agree bears on it, and say what each of them would predict or find intuitive about that case. I suspect two of the cruxes will be (a) whether unenacted legislative history can justify departing from the clear text, and (b) whether "ordinary meaning at the time of enactment" is a discoverable fact or just a re-labeling of the judge's own preference; but don't force these if you think the real disagreements lie elsewhere.

One thing I'd like from you: please start with the document itself — heading and first section — with no preface, no commentary, and nothing after it. And keep it usable as a handout, so title the sections clearly.
