Two friends of mine got into it the other night over a promise one of them had broken. Marcus had promised to help his neighbor move on Saturday, then bailed when he got a last-minute ticket to a game. Elena said he'd wronged the neighbor. Marcus said the neighbor had already lined someone else up and didn't care, so no harm, no foul. What started as a scolding turned into a long argument about what actually makes promises bind, and they asked me to settle it. I couldn't. I'll lay out the moves as best I remember them.

(1) Elena: what makes a promise bind is the assurance. When you promise, you deliberately get the other person to form expectations about what you'll do, and they then arrange their life around those expectations. Breaking the promise is wrong because it betrays the very expectation you invited. That's why a bare prediction doesn't bind but a promise does.

(2) Marcus: but then a promise that fails to produce any expectation wouldn't bind. Suppose I promise to pay you back, and you genuinely don't believe me — you've heard that one before. You make no plans, no reliance. On your view I've done nothing wrong by not paying, because there was no expectation to betray. But I still owe you the money. So the obligation must come from the act of promising itself, not from what it happens to do inside your head.

(3) Elena: fine, but "the act itself" is just some words. Words only have that kind of power if there's a social practice behind them — promising is a rule-governed institution, like a game, and when you say "I promise" you're making a move in that game. The problem is that then the obligation comes from the institution, and the institution is justified by its general usefulness. So why can't I break a promise when breaking it won't damage the institution — a promise made in private, say, where no one else will ever find out? The practice view seems to make the wrong impersonal and too easy to escape.

(4) Marcus: the practice isn't just about the institution's overall health. The rules of the practice exist to protect promisees by giving them a claim on the promisor — that's what makes the wrong directed at you rather than at society. And your earlier view has a worse problem: release. If the wrong is the betrayed expectation, then when you release me from a promise, what are you doing — changing your expectations? But you can fully expect me to show up and still release me. Release doesn't change your mind; it changes what I'm permitted to do. So the obligation can't be identical with your expectation.

(5) Elena: release is a fair point, but it doesn't force me to your view. In promising, the promisee aquires a standing to insist or not insist — the assurance isn't just a prediction in their head, it's an entitlement. Releasing is waiving that entitlement, which is a normative act, not a change of mind. So the assurance view can handle release. But notice you've now given up the pure practice view too: you're talking about claims and directed wrongs, which is closer to saying promising is just a basic moral power — that the past act of promising gives me a reason, full stop, like a debt.

(6) Marcus: and what's wrong with that? Some obligations are basic. I don't need to reduce the duty to keep promises to expectations or institutions any more than I need to reduce the duty of gratitude to the social utility of thanking people. Promising is the exercise of a normative power: by saying the words, I put myself under an obligation to you. That's a primitive moral fact.

(7) Elena: calling it primitive just names the thing. Gratitude has a story — you benefited me, a relationship is at stake — but promising is me placing myself under an obligation by declaring that I do. That's a power, and powers need a source. Why does this speech act have that power while "I intend to" doesn't? And why do some promises fail — coerced ones, ones to someone who couldn't understand what I was saying — unless there are practice rules or expectation facts doing the real work? I don't think you can have the power without the practice or the assurance, and those views each had the problems we already saw.

They were still going when I left. Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of the seven moves, say whether it's basically right, basically wrong, or a mix, and why. Then say what you think both of them are missing, and who's more right overall.
