Stuck on this one and could use a precise diagnosis. I've been going back and forth on whether we owe each other respect as an attitude, not just as a set of behaviors, and I built the following argument to show it can't be about attitudes:

(1) If we can owe each other an attitude, then attitudes have to be the kind of thing we can voluntarily adopt, since ought implies can.
(2) But attitudes aren't under direct voluntary control. I can't make myself respect someone I find beneath me just by deciding to, any more then I can decide to be hungry.
(3) What I can control directly is my actions: whether I listen, interrupt, sneer, give reasons, or just order them around.
(4) So a moral requirement can only ever attach to actions, never to attitudes.
(5) Therefore 'we owe each other recognition' can only mean 'we owe each other certain behavior,' and any demand that we actually feel respect, or hold someone in regard, is confused.

I half-suspect the trick is in step (2), but attacking it just seems to help the argument—pointing out that I can cultivate respect over the years doesn't show I can adopt it right now, which is what ought-implies-can would seem to need. So: is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, or what missing premise is silently doing the work, and what is the sharpest correction of it? If the real problem is an equivocation on 'respect,' name the two senses and say whether the argument trades on them at the step that matters.
