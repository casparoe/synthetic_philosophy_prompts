In debates about judicial review, defenders of a restrained judiciary often argue from an analogy: a judge is like a baseball umpire, who calls balls and strikes but does not write the rulebook, and therefore a judge's task is to apply the law the legislature enacted rather than substitute her own view of what the law ought to be. The analogy is deployed to support the conclusion that unelected judges should overrule legislatures only in the narrowest of circumstances, and that anything more ambitious is rule-making from the bench. I want an audit of how far this analogy actually carries. Give the correspondence table explicitly: what does the strike zone correspond to, and what about the rulebook, the players, the league officials, the blown call, the instant replay? Then identify the points where the mapping breaks. Separate the disanalogies that are merely superficial from those that matter for the normative conclusion. Finally, say whether the restrained conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the argumentative work all along, and offer a better analogy if one exists.
