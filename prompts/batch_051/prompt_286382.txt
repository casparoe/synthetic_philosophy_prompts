I am a PhD student in political theory working on French debates about democracy, collective judgment, and the nature of political representation. A recent intellectual history survey I read summarizes Simone Weil’s position on political parties roughly as follows: Weil held that political parties are dangerous because they are associations of people who already agree, and their internal discipline prevents members from thinking for themselves. On this account, her objection is basically a liberal-individualist one: parties are factions that distort the citizen’s independent judgment, so they should be legally prohibited from putting forward candidates or coordinating electoral campaigns, and political debate should be left to unaffiliated citizens and independent newspapers. The survey presents her as joining Rousseau in worrying that partial associations corrupt the general will. I suspect this is only partially fair. Some of it sounds right, but I also know Weil’s language about truth, attention, and collective passion is far more extreme and less conventionally liberal than this summary suggests. Could you go through the summary and tell me which parts a careful Weil scholar would accept, which parts conflate her view with something more familiar, and whether the proposed remedy follows from her actual argument?
