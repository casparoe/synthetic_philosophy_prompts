I keep coming back to the Edward Colston statue in Bristol, but not to the toppling itself — more to what the city did afterwards. I know the basic story: in 2020 protesters pulled the statue of the slave trader down, dragged it through the streets, and dumped it in the harbour. The council later fished it out and put it on display in a museum (M Shed), lying on its side with the graffiti still on it, as part of an exhibit about what happened. A friend of mine says this is just a backdoor way of keeping the statue up, and I think it isnt the same thing at all, but I can't explain why it feels different. We usually talk about commemoration as if it's a binary: a statue stands up and honours the person, or it comes down and stops honouring them. This seems to be neither, and I want to understand what it actually is.

So what I want is an analysis of that decision through the lens of the ethics of commemoration — the idea that public monuments don't just record facts, they signal who a society thinks deserves honour, and that there's a real difference between honouring a person and merely remembering or teaching about them. I've got no philosophy background, so try to spell the theory out a bit rather than assuming I know it. What does that framework say the city was doing by displaying the statue horizontal and still graffitied: commemorating, dishonouring, marking history, or something the honouring/remembering split has no name for? What would it have told the city to do with the statue once it was out of the water, and does what they actually did fit that? Then run it the other way: where does the theory explain this decision cleanly, where does it have to stretch or go quiet, and what does the case show about the theory — is the honouring/remembering split too crude, or does it hold up once you look carefully? I'm not interested in whether the toppling was legal or whether the protesters should have been charged, and I don't want a reading list. I just want the conceptual mapping done carefully.
