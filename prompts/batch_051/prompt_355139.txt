I am a novelist working on a book about an intelligence that is not seated in any single skull—a city, a swarm, a company—and I keep returning to a thought experiment in which an ant colony is described as a single thinking thing. The version I remember runs roughly as follows. The individual ant is not the thinker, any more than a single neuron is; the colony as a whole is the analogue of the brain; the workers, castes, and queen are the analogues of neurons, circuits, and specialized regions; and the pheromone trails along which ants communicate are the analogues of signals in a nervous system. On the strength of this mapping, its defenders conclude that the colony has something worthy of the name "mind": it can remember, through its trails, nest architecture, and the shared scent of its members; it can decide among nest sites; and it can respond to events as a coordinated whole, even though no ant knows what the colony knows.

A neuroscientist friend of mine says the analogy is quietly doing all the argumentative work. Ants are autonomous agents with agendas of their own, while neurons are not; the colony's "signals" are slow chemical deposits laid down in the open world rather than fast synaptic events inside a skull; and the observer is painting one "mind" over what is really many thousands of separate animals coordinating locally. I would like you to audit the ant-colony-as-mind analogy the way one would audit an argument. Give the mapping explicitly—ant to neuron, colony to brain, pheromone to signal, and the rest—and say what maps cleanly, what maps only under strain, and what has no counterpart at all. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that do not. Then tell me whether the conclusion that an ant colony has a mind in anything like the human sense survives once the disanalogies are on the table, what the analogy can still legitimately do, and whether there is a better analogy for the kind of distributed intelligence I am trying to imagine.
