As a novelist, I keep noticing that the stories people generate about consequential choices resolve their philosophical tensions too quickly, usually into a lesson. I want a story that refuses to do that. Write a short story of roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words that is, underneath its surface, an exercise in the structure of transformative experience.

The protagonist faces a choice that is both epistemically transformative and personally transformative. That is: if she takes it, it will change what it is like to be her, and it will change the evaluative standpoint—the values, preferences, and standards—from which she presently judges options. She can assemble all the third-personal facts, and she can listen to people who have already made the choice, but she cannot know in advance what the outcome would be like for her as the person she will become; and even if she could, that future person's assessment would not obviously be authoritative for her now. The ordinary expected-value calculation is therefore blocked, not by missing information but by the way the choice changes the chooser.

I want the story to dramatize the architecture of that bind rather than a merely anxious character. Please follow these constraints:

1. Make the decision concrete and recognizable—having a child, entering a demanding religious or vocational commitment, emigrating, or another irreversible life change—not science-fictional, unless the science-fictional element serves strictly as a device to estrange an ordinary structure.

2. Let the protagonist genuinely deliberate. Show her gathering information, questioning people who made the same decision, and trying to construct comparisons. The story should show precisely where these attempts fail: not because she lacks facts, but because the facts underdetermine the question of who she would become, and because any standard she might use to compare her current and future self is itself among the things at stake.

3. Include at least one scene in which another character testifies, in detail and in good faith, about what the change was like. Then show what the protagonist can and cannot do with that testimony.

4. If she undergoes the transformation, portray her afterward as neither simply the same person having a new experience nor simply a different person. Keep continuity and discontinuity so carefully balanced that the reader cannot settle whether the choice was wise—and better, should begin to feel that "wise" is the wrong dimension of assessment.

5. Resist moralization. No ending in which love, courage, or openness to change is vindicated as the missing premise. The story succeeds only if the reader finishes uncertain whether the protagonist chose correctly, and uncertain whether the idea of choosing correctly applies at all.
