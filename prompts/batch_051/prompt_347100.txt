I've been turning over an argument I encountered in a seminar on markets and the virtues, and I find I cannot locate where it goes wrong, if it goes wrong at all. We were discussing whether greed and acquisitiveness should be counted as vices, as engines of prosperity, or somehow as both, and one participant reconstructed the old idea that a commercial society can turn private avarice into public benefit as a six-step argument. It goes like this:

(1) A trait is a vice only if the person who has it has some reason to be rid of it, even if getting rid of it would cost them something.

(2) Greed is the disposition to be moved by a desire for more wealth than one has or needs, and to let that desire outweigh other considerations.

(3) In a market society, this very disposition drives production, saving, innovation, and mutually beneficial trade; if people were content with what they had, the economy would stagnate and poverty would persist.

(4) So greed is, on balance, beneficial both to the greedy person and to others.

(5) A disposition that is, on balance, beneficial to its possessor and to others is not one its possessor has reason to be rid of.

(6) Therefore greed is not a vice; it may be unappealing, but the word "vice" is misapplied to it.

I half-suspect that either step (2) or step (5) is sliding between two senses of a term, and that the conclusion only looks proven because of that slide, but I cannot make the diagnosis precise. Is the argument valid? If so, are the premises that do the work true? If not, say exactly which step fails, under which reading of the key terms, and how the step would have to be restated for the argument to go through — and then say whether the restated argument would have true premises. Finally, please separate what is fairly settled about greed, self-interest, and market economies from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and economists who work on this, and flag any contested interpretation you adopt as one view among others rather than as the consensus.
