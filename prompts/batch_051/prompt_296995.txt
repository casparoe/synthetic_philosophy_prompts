I'm a novelist, and something's been going around my corner of the publishing world that I'd like you to stress-test rather than moralise about. A consortium of small and mid-size presses is floating a scheme for the old problem of whether you can still read someone's books once you know what the author did. The proposal: when an author is credibly found, by some independent panel, to have done something seriously abusive — sexual assault, defrauding vulnerable people, that sort of thing — their backlist isn't withdrawn and isn't simply left alone either. It goes into what they're calling a work trust. The books stay in print, but reissues appear under a pseudonym chosen by the trust, with no biographical note or author photo. Royalties are diverted to a victim-support fund. The trust, rather than the author or the estate, handles placement in bookshops, review copies, and prize eligibility. And the kicker: if the living author ever publicly claims the work, the arrangement is void and the money to victims stops, so they're under pressure to keep quiet about their own writing. I want to know how this machine would actually behave. Who has incentives to do what — a publisher holding a profitable backlist, an author's estate, a victims' group, a prize jury, a critic who loathes the author but loves the books? Where does it get gamed? I can already imagine an author pre-emptively confessing to something middling in the hope of getting the clean pseudonymous reissue and a sort of laundered reputation; allegations being inflated because there's now a revenue stream attached; a publisher quietly rooting for a scandal so a backlist gets 'trusted' and acquires a new marketing hook. Does the pseudonym read as a scarlet letter, or as something weirdly attractive? What does it do to debut authors, to how much biography matters in criticism, to what gets acquired in the first place? And can any of the failure points be fixed — a better panel, an appeals process, a sunset clause on the trust — without turning the panel into a moral star chamber? I'm not really asking whether separating the work from the artist is right in the abstract; I want the institutional mechanics, wether it breaks, where it breaks, and which failures are fixable. And could you write the answer in British English, please.
