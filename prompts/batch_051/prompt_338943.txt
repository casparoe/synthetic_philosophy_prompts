Last week I had a long argument with a friend about a rule I have come to rely on for making estimates, and I still do not know which of us was right. The rule concerns the conflict between the inside view and the outside view. Whenever I need to estimate how long a project will take, or how likely it is to succeed, I first reason through the details: the tasks, the people, the obstacles, the dependencies. But if the number I arrive at this way differs from the base rate for projects that look broadly similar from the outside, I throw away my detailed analysis and adopt the base rate outright. I do not average the two, and I do not adjust the base rate for special features of my situation, even when I can articulate those features clearly. The argument began over a concrete instance: I have a project that is, by my own detailed analysis, likely to finish faster than the historical average for comparable projects, because the team is unusually experienced and the technical approach is simpler than the reference class. My friend said it was absurd to ignore that. He argued that the detailed analysis contains real information, that the special features are sometimes genuine rather than self-serving, and that the rational response to two imperfect estimates is to combine them in proportion to their reliability, not to let one win by default. I replied that the lesson of the planning fallacy is that people can always generate a plausible story about why their case is the exception, and that such stories are usually rationalization; I would rather be crudely modest than sophisticatedly self-deceived. Is my rule defensible, or is it a bias against my own first-order reasoning dressed up as epistemic modesty? Under what conditions would combining the two estimates be better than choosing between them, and how could I tell from the inside whether the special features of my case are information that should move me off the base rate or noise that I should ignore?
