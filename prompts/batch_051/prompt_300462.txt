I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been watching the current wave of large-language-model-based automation of programming from the inside. In my circles the standard account runs roughly as follows: tools that complete, generate, test, and refactor code have made senior engineers substantially more productive; an engineer directing a model can now do work that until recently would have required a small team; employers are therefore freezing or shrinking entry-level hiring, reorganizing jobs around reviewing and correcting model output, and carrying out layoffs while still reporting healthy profits. There is also open friction over the fact that a large share of the training data is code written by humans who were paid for some earlier job and had no say in that reuse, and that the resulting tools are then licensed back, in effect, to the same community of engineers.

I keep returning to what I have absorbed from popular discussions of Marx on technology and automation, and I want the present moment analyzed through that lens rather than through the industry's usual productivity and headcount vocabulary. As I understand Marx's view: a firm introduces machinery not primarily to lighten toil but to cheapen production relative to its rivals; the machine raises the productivity of the workers who remain, so that a given output embodies less necessary labor-time and the value of labor-power itself falls; the workers displaced form a reserve army whose availability disciplines the wages of those still employed; and the gains from higher productivity appear as profit and as longer or more intense work rather than as leisure, unless workers organize to force a different distribution. He also seems to hold that machinery does not itself create new value but only gradually transfers its own value to the product, while living labor remains the source of surplus value, so as machinery accumulates relative to labor there is downward pressure on the rate of profit even while the total mass of profit grows.

Analyze the current episode through that Marxian framework, and run the analysis in both directions. First, do the mapping with some discipline: in this episode, what plays the role of machinery, of constant versus variable capital, of the reserve army of labor, of deskilling, of relative surplus value? Which features of the episode does the framework explain straightforwardly, which does it have to strain to fit or actively distort, and which does it leave silent about? Say, too, what the framework implies about how employers, senior engineers, and displaced junior engineers are each likely to respond as the tools improve. Second, let the episode test the framework. If code-producing machines can themselves be improved by capital with only supervisory human labor, is that merely an intensification of the tendencies Marx already described, or does it break the claim that living labor is the sole source of value? Does the open-source origin of much of the training data look like accumulated social knowledge being captured by capital, and does that strengthen or complicate the account of who owns the means of production? I am not looking for a forced application; I want an honest statement of where this lens illuminates the current moment and where it stops helping.
