The psych department at a midsize university is sick of fighting with the IRB about deception studies, so it floats a rule. Researchers can skip case-by-case ethics review only if every participant, before learning anything about the actual study, fills in a deception-lottery form. The form says the true purpose of the study may differ from what they'll be told, and it asks them to check the worst they're willing to undergo: minor (fake questionnaires, a confederate making scripted small talk), moderate (a confederate giving a scripted wrong answer in front of them, a fake quiz with made-up norms), or severe (a staged emergency, false feedback that they failed an intelligence test). After the debriefing, each participant privately rates whether the real deception stayed inside the boxes they checked. If more than 15% say it didn't, the lab automatically gets a public warning attached to it's next three consent forms and extra IRB review. Participants are paid a flat fee no matter how they rate; most are first-years in the research pool who sign up because they need the hours for credit. Treat it as a mechanism, not a moral position. Walk me through how it actually plays out: what researchers are incentivized to do to the study design and the debriefing, what participants are incentivized to do, where the first gaming happens, and whether it ends up protecting anyone who would have been protected under the old case-by-case system.
