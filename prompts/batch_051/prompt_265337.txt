Been arguing with a friend about whether politics is a skill or a civic duty, and I noticed something odd about how the same society gives different answers in different places. On one side, we put the most serious decisions we have -- guilt or innocence, sometimes in cases where the stakes are life and death -- in the hands of twelve randomly picked adults with no legal training. The whole point of a jury is that it's members are ordinary people, not professionals. Some places go further: they fill a citizens' assembly by lottery and let it rework a constitution or an electoral system, on the idea that ordinary people, given time and decent information, can do at least as well as elected officials. On the other side, we act as if running a government is both hard and corrupting: incumbents get re-elected because experience is treated as an asset, politicians are paid as full-time professionals, and yet we impose term limits and complain about career politicians as if doing the job too long makes you worse at it. If governing needs long experience, why trust amateurs in a citizens' assembly? And if ordinary people can be trusted with state power just by being ordinary, why do we need a professional political class at all -- and why are we then so afraid of that class that we kick its members out after a few terms?

What view of political competence, and maybe of human nature, does this whole pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what these practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit well with, say whether the pattern is coherent or whether we're trying to have it both ways, and tell me what would have to change if someone seriously held a rival view (say, politics is entirely an expertise like surgery, or politics is entirely a civic duty like jury service). Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating the question. Write in American English.
