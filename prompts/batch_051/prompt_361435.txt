I'm a PhD student in legal philosophy, and I'm putting together a counterfactual exercise for a graduate seminar on legal ontology. I'd like you to work through it as an expert philosopher of law who specializes in the metaphysics and normativity of legal rules. Please reason carefully, and before offering any predictions, briefly state the distinctions you take to be load-bearing — in particular the difference between a legal fiction and an ordinary legal classification, and the difference between a fiction and adjacent devices such as a presumption, a status, or a definition.

Scenario. A common-law jurisdiction adopts a constitutional amendment, effective immediately, that reads: "No court, legislature, or administrative body shall deem any person, thing, or state of affairs to be what it is not, nor give legal effect to any fact except on proof or stipulation of that fact." Interpret this as an enforceable prohibition on legal fictions in both their explicit form (rules framed with "as if" or "deemed") and their constructive form. The ban reaches, but is not limited to: corporate personality, the adoption rule under which an adopted child is treated as if born to the adoptive parents, constructive notice and constructive possession, and the doctrine of relation back, under which a present act is treated as legally effective at an earlier time. The amendment does not repeal the substantive policies behind these doctrines; it removes only the fictional form in which they were implemented.

Predict, with reasoning, how this jurisdiction's legal system looks after fifty years. I want a visible chain of reasoning rather than a narrative. In particular: which doctrines, if any, are simply reimplemented in non-fictional language with no significant change in outcome, and what would their easy replacement show about whether fictions are merely presentational? Which doctrines, if any, cannot be rebuilt without changing outcomes, and what feature of their function makes the fiction do real work? And where do functional substitutes reappear under names such as presumption, status, or definition, and how could a court tell whether a substitute is a reincarnated fiction? At each step, say how confident you are and identify the assumption whose failure would most alter that part of the prediction.
