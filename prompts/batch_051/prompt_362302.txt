I keep seeing "bad faith" used as the master explanation for why the other side won't agree, especially in politics, where each side is sure the other is just pretending not to get it. But I can't actually say what arguing in good faith requires. Give me a working defintion of good-faith argument that sorts the following cases. My going-in labels: a senator who repeats a statistic his own staff has flagged as misleading is clearly bad faith; a retiree who shows up to town halls with questions built on Facebook facts and genuinely believes every one of them is clearly good faith. The rest I'm stuck on.
(a) a lobbyist paid to oppose a bill she privately supports, who in the hearing uses only true facts but conveniently omits the ones that would sink her case.
(b) a climate activist who cites a paper they've never read and would drop the citation if it turned out flawed, but not the conclusion.
(c) someone who asks for a source for every claim you make, never says whether any source could satisfy them, and offers none of their own.
(d) a debater who announces they'd change their mind given good evidence, but whose standard of evidence is so demanding that nothing that's ever actually been shown to them could count, and they don't notice the bar is that high.
(e) a friend who moves, in one argument, from "the gender pay gap is a myth" to "the gap is explained by choices" to "the choices are shaped by unfair social pressure," without ever acknowledging the claim changed.

Propose the definition, run it through each case, tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts.
