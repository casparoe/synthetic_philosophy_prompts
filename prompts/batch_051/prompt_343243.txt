I'm kicking around an essay idea and I'd like some comments before I commit to writing it up. Its for a blog-ish venue, not an academic journal, so I can be a bit loose.

Working title: The problem isn't the pain: evolution and the goodness of creation.

The basic claim: Most discussions of animal suffering before the Fall spend their energy on whether pre-Fall animals could suffer, whether death existed, and so on. I want to argue that's the wrong place to look. Even if you concede a relatively tame picture — say, pre-Fall death existed but wasn't accompanied by subjective pain, or animals were non-sentient in the relevant sense — there's a harder problem that's about the structure of goodness, not the phenomenology of pain.

Here's the thought. If evolution is true in anything like the standard biological sense, then the history of life long before any plausible human "fall" is a history of competition, extinctions, one lineage's flourishing being causally downstream of another's elimination. Even if none of that hurt, it's still a world where the goodness of a later stage is bought at the price of the elimination of earlier ones. The doctrine of a good creation, as I understand it, isn't just the claim that God made a world that would eventually turn out fine. It's the claim that the created order itself is good — that God could approve of it as it stood, in its own terms.

So I want to draw a distinction between what I'm calling retrospective goodness and constitutive goodness. Retrospective goodness: a state of affairs is good because of what it makes possible or leads to. Constitutive goodness: a state of affairs is good in itself, as the kind of thing it is, independent of what it eventually produces. My suspicion is that the only way to call the prelapsarian evolutionary world good is retrospectively — as a necessary setup for the later creation, or for humans, or whatever. And that's a bigger concession than people realize: it means you can't take the repeated refrain that each stage of creation is good as an approval of the world as it was at that moment. You're stuck saying God saw it as good for what was coming. That makes the goodness of creation instrumental, and I think that's a real theological cost. But maybe I'm overstating it.

Where I'd like comments:

1. Is the retrospective/constitutive distinction doing real work, or am I just relabeling the standard intrinsic-versus-instrumental value distinction and pretending it's new? I suspect it's close to that, but I also want to say it isn't exactly the same, since constitutive goodness is more about divine approval of a thing as the kind of thing it is. Do I have anything here, or should I just say intrinsic/instrumental and move on?

2. Does my argument sneak in a questionable assumption that the pre-Fall world has to be approvable at every moment? Maybe the tradition only needs God to approve of the whole history. Would the strongest defender of the tradition fight me on that, and what would they say? (I don't need a reading list; I mean in your own words.)

3. If you were a sympathetic reader who wants this essay to survive contact with actual theologians, what's the one move I most need to add or weaken?

One more thing: I'm not sure whether the whole argument only bites if you take the Fall and the evolutionary story as both literal-historical. I'd like it to also work for someone who reads the Fall as a mythic/cosmic description rather than a dateable event, but I can't quite see how to make that land.
