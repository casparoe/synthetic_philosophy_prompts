I work in a policy unit inside a small national government, advising a minister on which cross-departmental proposals are worth pursuing. My own background is in public administration rather than economics or philosophy, but I have read the World Happiness Report and followed the public debate about it at a non-specialist level. Over the past two years we have received a steady stream of proposals — from other departments, outside consultants, and members of the public — whose stated objective is to raise our country's position in the World Happiness Report. We have a fixed pot of discretionary money, the minister wants a one-page recommendation on each proposal, and a bad call means some other worthwhile programme is delayed; so we need something better than my current gut reaction.

My rough sense, developed by arguing with colleagues rather than by study, is that a proposal can aim at four quite different things that are easy to blur together: making people's lives genuinely better; improving the underlying conditions the report's model emphasises (income, health, social support, freedom, generosity, and the absence of corruption); improving how people answer the life-evaluation survey question that sits at the heart of the rankings; or improving one of those component scores without improving the life evaluations themselves. I suspect the interesting disagreements are about when these layers come apart, and I want a procedure that forces that question into the open rather than letting it slide past.

Please write a reusable decision procedure my unit could run on any proposal whose stated goal is to raise our position in the World Happiness Report. The procedure should be a sequence of questions in order, with enough detail that a generalist civil servant can apply it in half a day: what counts as a good enough answer to move to the next question, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than information. It should ground itself in the four-layer distinction above, but you should correct or refine that distinction wherever it cannot do the work. It should also allow a proposal to fail where the expected movement in the ranking is smaller than year-to-year sampling noise, where the effect would be unlikely to survive the report's practice of averaging several years of data, or where the proposal trades away something important that the report ignores.

Then run the procedure on these three proposals:

1. A tax credit intended to raise self-reported charitable giving, aiming to lift our score on the report's generosity component.
2. A change to the wording, timing, and seasonal scheduling of the national life-evaluation survey so that more respondents are interviewed on sunny days and after public holidays, on the grounds that internal analysis suggests this would raise reported life satisfaction.
3. A national befriending service for isolated older people, funded by delaying a road-safety programme by two years, argued for on the grounds that it would both improve our social-support factor and genuinely reduce loneliness.

Make sure the worked examples include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I will not like, and say so plainly. Also be honest about: the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, the cases the procedure cannot settle, and the danger that the procedure itself encourages the score-chasing it is meant to catch. Please write the final document in British English.
