I serve as vice-chair of the board of a public history museum that is planning a permanent exhibition on twentieth-century state violence. The curatorial team has proposed an interactive installation called the Comparative Index of Historical Suffering, in which visitors would order roughly a dozen episodes—among them genocides, famines, forced deportations, and campaigns of political terror—on a touchscreen along dimensions such as death toll, duration, the degree of systematic intent, and what the curators term moral gravity. The installation would then display a running visitor consensus ranking in the gallery. The proposal has divided the institution. The education staff argue that an interactive ranking is the clearest way to make scale consequential and to help visitors grasp magnitudes that otherwise blur together. A survivor advisory group has called the installation obscene and morally incoherent, arguing that it invites visitors to treat atrocity as a spectator contest and implies that one community's suffering can be measured against another's as lesser. Several board members share that view, while others insist that refusing comparison altogether is an abdication of the museum's interpretive responsibility, since historians and courts already make comparative judgments about gravity, intent, and scale.

At our next board meeting I need to circulate a written opinion recommending a concrete decision: approve the installation as proposed, reject it outright, or approve it with conditions, such as design changes, framing labels, removal of the consensus ranking, or an alternative device that presents structured comparison without producing an aggregate ordering. Please draft that memo. It should give a clear, actionable bottom line, state the strongest consideration on the losing side in a form its proponents would recognize as fair, and explain what principle about comparing historical evils the museum should adopt going forward rather than merely settling this one exhibit.
