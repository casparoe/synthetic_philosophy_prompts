I've been trying to work out what I actually think about logical omniscience—about when someone counts as knowing something that follows from what they already know. The standard possible-worlds picture says that anyone who knows P thereby knows every logical consequence of P, but my verdicts on concrete cases split in ways I can't easily justify, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, holds them together.

Here are the verdicts I keep coming back to. First, a competent undergraduate has internalized the axioms and rules of a simple deductive system and has just worked through a three-line proof of a small lemma; she can reproduce the proof on demand. I'd say she knows the lemma. Second, a reliable friend tells me the keys are in the kitchen, and I have long believed that if the keys are in the kitchen then they are not in the car. I have never explicitly formed the thought that the keys are not in the car, but I want to say I knew it all along; no new information arrived when I first said it to myself. Third, at a card table I learn within a few seconds, from two independent and reliable sources, that the card is red and that the card is higher than seven. I have known all evening that if the card is red and higher than seven then Kim loses, but I have not yet conjoined the two new facts. There I want to say I do not yet know that Kim loses, and that I come to know it only when I actively put the premises together—yet I cannot see what separates this case from the keys case. Fourth, the same undergraduate has never seen or derived a certain forty-step theorem of the system; before she sits down to work through it, I can't bring myself to say she knew it, even though everything needed for the proof is already in her head. Fifth, a mathematician proved a theorem fifteen years ago and can no longer reconstruct the proof; she still confidently asserts the theorem on the strength of the published record, and I'd say she still knows it.

The case I go back and forth on is this: I learn P and Q at the same moment, I have a standing belief that P-and-Q entails R, and I could answer whether R after a split second if prompted, but I have not yet done the pairing. The card case tells me I don't yet know R before combining the premises; the keys case makes me hesitate, because the move feels no less automatic. I'd like you to work out whether a single principle, or a small set of them, captures the pattern across all five verdicts and settles this sixth case. State the principle in plain terms, show how it handles each of the five cases, say explicitly which of my verdicts it forces me to revise (if any), and apply it to one further case of your own construction that my principle would not have settled.
