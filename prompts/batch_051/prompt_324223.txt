So I have to give a talk at my company's ML reading group next week, and I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, so go easy on the jargon. The room is mostly applied ML folks who train recsys and LLM reward models, plus one economist from the marketplace team who knows revealed-preference theory cold and visibly winces whenever engineers say we "measure" preferences.

My talk argues that inverse RL / RLHF doesn't actually recover human preferences from behavior. The old IRL result is that a given policy can be made optimal under infinitely many reward functions; in RLHF the same thing shows up in disguise, because with finite, noisy comparisons many reward functions explain the training data about equally well. The function our pipeline returns gets picked by the regularizer, the model class, and how we phrase the comparison prompts — none of which comes from the data. So "preferences inferred from behavior" oversells the artifact: we're constructing a preference-like function, and those construction choices are quietly doing value-laden work. My conclusion is we either defend the prior over values openly, or stop saying "inferred."
