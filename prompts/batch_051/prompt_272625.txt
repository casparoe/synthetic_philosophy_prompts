I'm taking a philosophy of language seminar on fiction and reference, and my term paper has to defend a position on empty names and truth in fiction. Before I start writing, I want to check whether the package I've settled on actually hangs together. Here it is. (1) Names are directly referential: the semantic contribution of a proper name is just its referent, and a name adds no descriptive or qualitative material to the proposition expressed. (2) Sherlock Holmes is a proper name in the ordinary sense, not a disguised definite description along the lines of the detective who lives at 221B Baker Street. (3) There is no such person as Holmes; I reject Meinongian nonexistent objects, so in ordinary use the name is empty. (4) Truth in fiction is not literal truth. A sentence like In the stories, Holmes is a detective is true only internal to the fiction, in the sense that the stories prescribe imagining that Holmes is a detective; the fiction operator carries no existential commitment. (5) Still, I think some nonfictional sentences about Holmes are literally true: Holmes does not exist is true, Holmes is a fictional character is true, and so is Holmes is more famous than any real Victorian detective. (6) Empty names can still be meaningful: in using Holmes I manage to express a complete, communicable singular thought even though there is no object for it to be about.

I'd like a consistency audit rather than an exposition of the individual views. Go through the list and tell me which commitments are actually in tension and which pairs only look like they conflict. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each one say what the least expensive fix would be and what it would cost. If one revision would do the most to resolve things at a stroke, identify it and say whether you think it's the one I should make.
