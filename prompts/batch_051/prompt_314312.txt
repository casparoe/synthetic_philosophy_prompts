Here is an argument that honoring sunk costs is always irrational. Let a sunk cost be a cost one has already incurred that no available future action can recover.

(1) For any sunk cost and any available action, the cost cannot be recovered under that action; it remains sunk no matter what one does next.

(2) A consideration can rationally count in favor of one action over another only if it differentiates between them — that is, only if it would hold under one action and not under the other.

(3) Since a sunk cost is lost under every available action, the sunk cost itself does not differentiate between continuing an endeavor and abandoning it.

(4) Therefore, the sunk cost itself cannot rationally count in favor of continuing over abandoning.

(5) But honoring a sunk cost is precisely a matter of letting the sunk cost itself count in favor of continuing over abandoning.

(6) Hence, honoring a sunk cost is always irrational.

I half-suspect that something shifts in step (5), but I cannot say exactly where, and I can also imagine defending the argument as valid while suspecting it only defeats a strawman. Is the argument valid on its strongest reading? If not, exactly which step fails, and under which reading of which term? What is the strongest true version of the conclusion this argument is actually entitled to, and does it vindicate the everyday charge that people who honor sunk costs reason irrationally?
