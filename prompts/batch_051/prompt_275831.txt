Next month I'm giving a paper at a workshop on the ethics of war, and I want to walk into the Q&A knowing what's coming instead of getting carved up. The paper defends a kind of contingent pacifism. The one-line version: the just war tradition's own jus ad bellum criteria are so demanding that, taken seriously as necessary conditions, they almost never all get satisfied, so a state's default should be a very strong presumption against fighting -- not an absolute ban, but close enough in practice that nearly every actual proposal loses. I get there in four steps: first, the standard criteria (just cause, legitimate authority, last resort, probability of success, proportionality) are each genuinely necessary; second, for any war we can realistically assess ahead of time, at least one of last resort, reasonable chance of success, and proportionality is either unknowable in advance or predictably fails; third, "we couldn't know" is not an acceptable basis for mass killing; so fourth, the burden of meeting every criterion to a high evidential standard sits with whoever wants the war, and they basically never clear it.

The audience is the part that scares me. It's mostly just war theorists from philosophy and political theory -- the kind who think supreme emergency and humanitarian intervention are where the theory earns its keep, not embarassing exceptions. Two of them have written on the responsibility to protect. They will not be impressed by invocations of war's horror, and they'll treat contingent pacifism as either moral squeamishness dressed up as rigor or isolationism in a nicer jacket. They talk in terms of deontological versus prudential constraints, the continuum of force, moral tragedy, and the difference between not knowing and not being certain.

Give me the prep sheet I'd actually use. What are the objections this crowd will raise, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased in their idiom? For each one: how much damage can it do, what's my best two-minute reply, and where does that reply get soft? And end with the question I should be praying nobody in the room asks.
