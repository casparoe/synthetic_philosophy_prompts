I'm drafting a short philosophical profile of my views on integrity and incorruptibility for a seminar on the ethics of character, and before I defend it I want an audit of whether the set hangs together. Here are the commitments:

1. Incorruptibility is a trait of the will, not a clean record. It consists in a stable disposition to refuse inducements—bribes, threats, personal enticements—that would lead one to betray a commitment.

2. Because a disposition can be present without ever having been manifested (a plate can be fragile even if it has never been struck), a person who has never been seriously tempted can already be incorruptible. Our uncertainty about such a person is epistemic, not conceptual.

3. There is nevertheless a distinctive admiration owed to the person who has actually withstood a test, and that admiration is not yet owed to the merely never-tested. The honorific force of "incorruptible" attaches to the demonstration, not merely to the underlying disposition.

4. Integrity is a different and richer thing than incorruptibility. It requires wholehearted identification with the commitments that organize one's life; a person whose commitments are held half-heartedly, ambivalently, or through compartmentalization may be highly reliable but lacks integrity.

5. The two can come apart in both directions. A person can be incorruptible without integrity (the rigid, self-alienated official who will not take the bribe), and a person can have integrity with respect to a commitment while remaining capable of wavering under extreme pressure.

6. Incorruptibility has a worth that is not simply conditional on the worth of what it protects: the incorruptible servant of a bad cause is, in one respect, admirable. There is something about refusing to be bought or broken that commands admiration even when the end does not.

7. Integrity, by contrast, is not admirable irrespective of content. The wholehearted Nazi has integrity in the psychological sense, but his integrity itself is not admirable.

Please go through this set and identify which commitments are in tension, rank the tensions from most to least severe, and separate the genuine tensions from the pairs that only appear to be in tension. For each genuine tension, state the cheapest revision and what it costs, and then say which single revision would do the most to make the whole set coherent. In particular, I would like to know whether the asymmetry I draw between 6 and 7—incorruptibility admirable even in a bad cause, integrity not—can be made principled, or whether it is ad hoc.

Please answer in American English.
