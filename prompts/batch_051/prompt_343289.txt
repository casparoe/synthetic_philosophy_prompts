I have a dispute with a colleague about a toy model that serves as a proxy for the question whether higher-level patterns can exert genuine downward causal influence on their lower-level substrate. The model is this: a finite two-dimensional lattice contains identical small particles, which we call simmballs. The only fundamental rules are local collision rules—each simmball moves at a fixed speed and bounces off its neighbors according to a deterministic contact law, with no forces at a distance and no external fields. When the lattice is seeded with certain initial configurations, structures emerge that look like symbols: stable, mobile patterns that move through the lattice, collide, and transform in rule-like ways reminiscent of syntax. The colleague claims that these symbol-like structures are not merely convenient descriptions or epiphenomenal summaries of the underlying simmball dynamics. Rather, the colleague claims, the symbols push the substrate around: one can better predict the future of individual simmballs by tracking the symbol-level pattern than by tracking only local simmball states, and a symbol-level intervention—inserting, deleting, or rearranging a symbol—changes the trajectories of the underlying simmballs in a way that the local collision rules alone would not have generated. I suspect the claim is either trivially true by loose use of cause or untestable. Design the strongest possible empirical test of the claim, whether in a real physical analog or in a computer simulation. Specify the exact manipulation or measurement, state what result would count for the claim and what result would count against it, and be honest about what even a favorable result would still leave unresolved—in particular, whether it would establish genuine downward causation or merely show that symbol-level descriptions are computationally useful compressions.
