Here's a comparision I stumbled into and can't decide whether to take seriously.

Start with the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem in metaethics. Someone offers you a big reward on condition that you admire them. You now have a reason to want to admire them, and a reason to try to make yourself admire them. But it seems you don't thereby have a reason to admire them—the reward is the wrong kind of reason. The usual way of drawing the line is between object-given reasons (features of the thing the attitude is about, like the fact that they're genuinely admirable) and state-given reasons (features of the attitude itself, like the fact that having it would pay). Object-given reasons are meant to be the right kind; state-given ones are the suspicious ones.

Now take Goodhart's law, or at least the usual gloss on it: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. I can't shake the feeling that it maps on. A metric is supposed to track some underlying property—health, creditworthiness, whatever. A fact about that underlying property is like an object-given reason for a high reading, because it bears on what the metric is about. The fact that a high reading will be rewarded, or will look good to whoever's watching, is like a state-given reason, because it bears on the measurement itself rather than the measured thing. Goodhart's law says that once you optimise on the second sort of consideration, the metric decouples from the first—just as trying to admire someone for money tends to produce something that isn't really admiration.

So is this a genuine structural identity, a loose analogy, or am I just confusing two senses of "about"? I'd like to know where the mapping holds and where it breaks, and whether anything on either side predicts something false on the other. One point I'm especially unsure about: in the metaethics case some philosophers want to say the state-given considerations aren't reasons for the attitude at all, only reasons to cause the attitude. Does Goodhart's law need the same move—should we say a target that's been optimised isn't a measure of the property any more, or just that it's a corrupted measure? Or does the analogy fail right there?
