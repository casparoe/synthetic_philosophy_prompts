Parfit's repugnant conclusion is normally derived from an additive conception of value: if the value of a world is the sum of the lifetime welfare of its inhabitants, then for any world containing, say, ten billion people all with very high welfare, there is a much larger world whose inhabitants have lives that are only barely worth living and that still ranks higher. Consider a modification of the puzzle in which value is not a sum but a product. Suppose the value of a world is the product of the total quantity of consciousness it contains and the average hedonic quality of a moment of consciousness. Let total quantity be the total number of conscious moments across all lives, and let average hedonic quality be average welfare per conscious moment, measured on a scale where 0 is the neutral point and negative values represent moments not worth living. World A contains exactly ten billion lives, each with the same fixed number of conscious moments and per-moment welfare 100. World B contains N lives, each with the same fixed number of conscious moments as a life in A but per-moment welfare 1.

Work through the multiplicative calculation and say which world it ranks better. Show that, under this natural definition of average hedonic quality, the multiplicative rule collapses exactly into the additive total view, so the repugnant conclusion goes through unchanged. Then replace the rule with the parameterized version V = C^β · H, where C is the total quantity of consciousness, H is average per-moment welfare, and β is a constant between 0 and 1. Derive the exact threshold on N at which World B beats World A as a function of β and the 100-to-1 welfare ratio, and explain why the repugnant conclusion still follows for every β > 0 even though the required population grows sharply as β approaches 0. What does β = 0 amount to, and which familiar view is it equivalent to? Finally, contrast this with a lexical rule on which no increase in the quantity of consciousness can compensate for average welfare below some specified threshold: does the lexical rule avoid both the repugnant conclusion and the standard difficulties of the average view, or does it only relocate them?
