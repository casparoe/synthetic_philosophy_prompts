I'm a retired pharmacologist—thirty-odd years doing dose-response curves and old-school LD50s, though I've moved on since—and for the past year or so I've been reading philosophy, mostly utilitarianism, and Bentham in particular has gotten his hooks into me. What I like is how operational he is: the good is pleasure and the absence of pain, summed over all the beings affected, and he actually tries to weigh pleasures and pains along dimensions like intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, and how many beings are affected. No appeal to the soul doing the real work; even animals count for him because what matters is whether they can suffer. Its a very bench-scientist-friendly ethics.

Lately I've been trying to square that with a transhumanist current I've seen called the hedonistic imperative. Stripped down, the idea is that evolution set our hedonic baseline for inclusive fitness, not for well-being, and that biotechnology—germline engineering, better pharmacology, neural interfaces—could let us stop only treating suffering and instead abolish it: shift baselines upward, replace the clunky old reward circuitry with a gradient of well-being that never falls below a pleasant baseline, and eventually extend something similar to non-human animals, maybe even redesigning ecosystems so predation and starvation stop. A friend from my old lab calls it Stone Age reward circuitry endlessly tinkered with by natural selection; I think that's a bit flippant but not wrong. Bentham couldn't have imagined CRISPR or a chronically lifted mood baseline, and that gap is exactly what I want you to work in.

So here's the assignment: predict, from the inside, what Bentham would make of the hedonistic imperative if it were described to him in terms he could cash out. Don't give me the textbook Bentham, I've got that. Hold his actual commitments fixed and work out where he'd be an enthusiastic fellow-traveler (I'd guess the extension to animals, and his dislike of treating the natural as sacred, would pull him in), where he'd get genuinely stuck or object, and where he'd have to modify his own hedonic calculus just to state the proposal—does intensity have an upper bound, is a higher baseline a new dimension or just more duration and intensity, does fecundity put a thumb on the scale for pleasures that make further pleasures likelier? I also want one spot where you think he'd flat-out refuse to follow the abolitionists, and your best guess at which of his commitments would force that refusal—maybe something about pain being a useful sanction or teacher, or a suspicion of centrally managed happiness, or something else I haven't noticed. Be concrete about the Bentham you're extending, not the legend.
