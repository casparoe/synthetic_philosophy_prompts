I'm writing up a short position paper on simplicity and induction for a grad seminar on formal epistemology, and I want someone to poke holes in the package before I present it. Here's where I've landed:

(1) Occam's razor is best made precise as a preference for hypotheses with low Kolmogorov complexity—shorter programs, once you've picked a reasonable description language. (2) Solomonoff induction is the right idealization of learning from evidence: its what a perfect reasoner would do, and real learners like us are just doing messy finite approximations of it. (3) The fact that Kolmogorov complexity depends on your choice of universal Turing machine (up to an additive constant) isn't a real problem, because the invariance theorem says that dependence washes out as your data get large. (4) In actual machine learning, the prior and the inductive bias do enormous work even with a lot of data—a badly chosen model class or regularizer isnt fixed by adding more data. (5) Occam's razor is objective; making simplicity relative to the describer would gut the principle.

I've half-convinced myself this is a tidy package, but I keep worrying that (4) and (5) each pull against the Solomonoff picture in different ways, and that (3) is quietly doing more than it can support. I want an audit rather than a lecture: which commitments actually conflict, which only seem to, and how bad each real tension is. Please respond as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion; for each item, name the pair of commitments at issue, say whether the tension is real or apparent, and say what would have to give.
