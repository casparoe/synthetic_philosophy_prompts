Two people in my program got into it after a causation seminar, and I got volunteered as referee even though I don't have a settled view on any of this. Mara works on counterfactual dependence, roughly Lewisian: a token event C causes E just in case, had C not occurred, E wouldn't have occurred. Dev works on interventionism, roughly Woodwardian: causal claims are about variables linked by invariant relations under ideal interventions, and the old event-counterfactual idiom is at best a sloppy shorthand. Their argument went in rounds, like this:

1. Dev said: take early preemption. A hitman shoots the victim; a backup hitman would have shot if the first hadn't. The death doesn't counterfactually depend on the first shot, since it would have happened anyway. But the first shot obviously caused it. So the one-conditional test fails on the paradigm actual-cause case.

2. Mara said: only if you individuate the death coarsely. The actual death—the bullet from the first gun, at that moment, entering at that angle—would not have happened without the first shot. Fine-grain the effect and the dependence returns. Preemption doesn't refute dependence; it tells you the relata have to be fragile.

3. Dev said: that's a gerrymander. You have no principled rule for how fine-grained the events have to be, and by adjusting grain you can get any verdict you like. A causal model does better: represent death as A-or-B, with B firing only if A doesn't. Hold B at its actual value—zero—and switch A off; death goes away. So the actual-cause definition calls A a cause and B not, without fine-graining events, because B is off the A-to-death path and gets held fixed.

4. Mara said: "off the path," "exogenous," "intervention"—those are all causal notions. You're defining causation in terms of causation. My counterfactuals at least don't need primitive causal arrows; they're tracked by similarity across possible worlds. Circularity is fatal if the view is supposed to say what causation is.

5. Dev said: I'm not trying to reduce causation to non-causal terms. Interventionism is non-reductive, so circularity is only fatal on your reductionist hunch. The account earns its keep by fitting experimental practice—what we do when we randomize and manipulate—and yours buys a possible-worlds similarity metric that no experiment ever samples.

6. Mara said: here's one where your practice gives out. A hormone has two pathways: it raises blood pressure through one and lowers it through the other, and at the relevant dose the two effects exactly cancel, so blood pressure is unchanged. Intervene on the hormone dose and the outcome variable doesn't move, so you'd say the hormone doesn't cause the blood pressure. But there are two perfectly good mechanisms there; it causes both an increase and a decrease. You've turned net effect into the whole of causation.

7. Dev said: I'd just use path-specific variables—blood-pressure-via-path-one, blood-pressure-via-path-two—and each shows an effect under an intervention. Choosing variables is part of the view, not a knife in it. And before you object, its no worse than your fragile death.

8. Mara said: wait, that's exactly the move you called gerrymandering when I fine-grained the death. You're rescuing your verdict by redescribing the variables, with no more certainty than you admitted. So either grain-matching is a legitimate move for both of us, or a cheat for both of us.

Then they looped back to whether "holding the backup fixed" in #3 is or isn't the same as my fragile-death move, threw in something about omissions and double prevention, and at that point I had flat lost the thread.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's right about what, which moves are mistakes, and what, if anything, both of them are missing? I don't need a tiebreaker about "who wins the bar argument" as much as I need to know whether their positions actually end up all that far apart.
