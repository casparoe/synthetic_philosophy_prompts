In discussions of the hiddenness argument — the claim that a perfectly loving God would not permit nonresistant nonbelief — I keep meeting a stock analogy: God's hiddenness is like a loving parent who hides during a game of hide-and-seek. The parent is present and caring, but conceals herself so that the child can exercise the search, and so that the eventual finding is more joyful than a plain display would have been. The conclusion drawn is that God's remaining hidden does not count against God's love, and may even be something a loving God would do. I would like an audit of how far this analogy actually holds. Spell out the mapping: what corresponds to the parent, the child, the hiding, the search, the finding, the background knowledge the child has of the parent, and the stakes of failing to find. Then identify the places where the mapping breaks, and distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that do not. Once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion survive?
