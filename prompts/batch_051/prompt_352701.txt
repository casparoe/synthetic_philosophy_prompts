I am a PhD student in the philosophy of language, and I am trying to sharpen a paper before sending it out. The paper concerns whether contemporary machine learning changes the dialectic around Kripke's rule-following challenge. The specific part I care about is Kripke's rejection of the dispositionalist response: the idea that what makes it true that I mean addition rather than quaddition is that I am disposed to answer novel addition problems in a certain way. As I read him, Kripke rejects this for three reasons. First, my actual dispositions are finite: I have answered only finitely many problems, and my actual dispositions include mistakes and gaps. Second, a disposition describes what I would do, not what I ought to do; it cannot explain how a use can be wrong as opposed to merely different. Third, any refinement of "what I would do under ideal conditions" will either be vacuous or silently import the semantic facts it was supposed to explain.

The development I want to bring in is this: systems trained by gradient descent are now a fully realized instance of the dispositionalist picture. Their competence is a stored set of numerical weights with no explicit rule anywhere in the system; the weights determine how the system continues a novel case; and errors are corrected from outside by trainers rather than by the system's own recognition that it has gone wrong. The question for my paper is whether this development revives the dispositionalist horn, or whether Kripke's three objections simply reappear one level down. I am not asking how the systems work in technical detail; I want to know which premise of the critique is genuinely under pressure, which survives, and whether there is a repaired version of the critique that still bites against a trained network. My current worry is that the normativity objection is doing all the real work and the other two have become distractions.

Please give your answer as a table, not an essay, with these columns:

- "Premise": a one-sentence restatement of a specific step in Kripke's rejection of dispositionalism.
- "How the development bears": one or two sentences on exactly what gradient-trained systems change for that step.
- "Status": one of "survives intact", "needs repair", or "undermined".
- "Repaired form": the best repaired version of that step that still applies to trained systems, or "none needed" if it survives.

Include one row for each of the three grounds, plus one row for the background premise that the only candidate meaning-constituting facts are occurrent interpretations, dispositions, or nothing. End with a final "Verdict" row giving the overall assessment: whether Kripke's critique of dispositionalism survives, needs repair, or is dead, and the strongest one-sentence repaired critique of dispositionalism that trained systems still face.
