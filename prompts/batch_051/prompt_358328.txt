I've been going back and forth on a local zoning dispute, and I've noticed that the interpretation I find obvious changes depending on which side of the housing debate I'm currently leaning toward. To be clear up front: I care less about what my city's actual ordinance would do than about the structure of the argument. The clause in question reads:

"In any R-1 single-family district, no dwelling unit shall be occupied by more than three adults not related to one another by blood, marriage, or adoption, and no dwelling unit shall be used as a rooming house, boarding house, fraternity, sorority, or other group living arrangement."

It was drafted in the early 1960s, and the archived council discussion shows the drafters were worried about student boarding houses, overnight lodgers, parking, and the "family character" of residential streets. The case the code now has to reach is this: five unrelated adults have lived in a single large house for four years as one common household. They share meals and finances, hold weekly house meetings, keep a written operating agreement, and one of them holds another's medical power of attorney. No one runs a business from the house. The only visible difference from a large family is five unrelated adults and no children. There have been no noise or parking complaints, and the immediate neighbors have written in support. A newly elected council member, running on "enforce the code," is now moving to cite them.

I can feel myself doing the thing where I read the words one way when I want the household excluded and another way when I want it protected. So I'd like a careful analysis. What does the text actually cover? Does this household serve or frustrate the clause's purpose? What would the drafters have said if this exact arrangement had been described to them, and is that even the right question? Which interpretive approach — plain text, original purpose, original expected applications, or how a reasonable neighbor would understand the rule — should govern here, and why? And if the text reaches them but the purpose doesn't, or the drafters would have said no but the words say yes, what does that show about what the rule was really protecting?

When you answer, please separate what is fairly settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested — for example, whether "neighborhood character" is a legitimate interest and whom zoning is ultimately supposed to serve. Don't present a contested view of zoning's purpose as if it were the consensus.
