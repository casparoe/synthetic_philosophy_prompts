Last month I was cited under a town ordinance from the early 1960s that bans keeping poultry in residential yards. I had assumed the rule was long dead: no one I have spoken to can remember it ever being enforced, and several houses on my street have kept small coops for decades without a word from the town. When I described the citation to a lawyer friend, he waved it off by saying the ordinance has fallen into desuetude. His image was this: a law that no one enforces is like a right-of-way that has grown over. Once everyone stops walking the path, the grass closes in and it ceases to be a path in any sense that matters; likewise, once a community has stopped enforcing a law for long enough, it ceases to be law in any sense that matters, and the town cannot suddenly punish me for cutting across the field. The prosecutor sees it rather differently: the path is still on the map, and it was always my responsibility to consult the map.

I want to understand how much work that analogy can actually carry. Set out the correspondence table first: what in the footpath picture plays the role of the statute, the walkers, the disuse, the overgrowth, the map, the field, and the right-of-way itself. Then identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended use. For instance, a path is a physical regularity produced by use, whereas a law makes a normative claim on people regardless of how they behave; a right-of-way can persist through disuse, and a town can always cut back the brush and reopen the path. Then assess which disanalogies are fatal and which are merely superficial, and say whether the lawyer's conclusion — that long-unenforced ordinances lose their claim on us — survives the audit, or whether the analogy was quietly doing the argumentative work. In reaching your assessment, weigh the rule-of-law objection that a law's validity cannot depend on how faithfully officials have chosen to enforce it. If the footpath analogy fails, propose a better analogy for what actually happens to a law in desuetude, and indicate what work the new analogy can and cannot do.
