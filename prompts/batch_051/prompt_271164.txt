I work in the office of a member of congress who sits on the foreign affairs committee, and a couple times a week I have to get a memo or a short floor speech into shape before it goes out. The recurring problem is historical analogy. Someone always wants to say that a standoff is Munich 1938 and we are about to hand over the Sudetenland, or that a train-and-advise deployment is "another Vietnam," or that two fleets eying each other across a strait is a "Thucydides trap" and war is close to baked in. My boss is not a historian either but he loves a sharp historical line, and I'm convinced these analogies are doing a lot of the persuading before anybody has checked whether they actually fit the case at hand. I have maybe twenty minutes per analogy and no historian on staff.

So here is what I'd like. Take whatever the "lessons of history" critique actually amounts to — the idea that Munich, Vietnam, and Thucydides-trap comparisons mislead more often than they illuminate — and turn it into a reusable screen I can run on any proposed historical analogy before it goes into a memo or a speech. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure says "drop the analogy," where it says "keep it but with this specific caveat," and where it has to stop and hand the decision to a person because the step is a judgment call rather than a checkable fact. Also flag the places where the screen itself is just judgment dressed up as procedure, because I'd rather know that than pretend otherwise.

Then run the procedure on three cases I keep seeing in our office: (1) someone wants to describe a limited territorial concession to a rising regional power as Munich 1938, (2) someone wants to argue that a small train-and-advise mission in a civil war will inevitably become Vietnam, and (3) someone wants to frame a maritime standoff between two large powers as a Thucydides trap. Make one of the three a case where the screen kills an analogy I probably would have been comfortable using, so I can see what the procedure costs. And be explicit about what the screen cannot catch — the places where checking whether a historical analogy actually fits requires real knowledge of the history, not just the structure of the comparison.
