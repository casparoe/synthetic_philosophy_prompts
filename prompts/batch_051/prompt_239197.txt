I've spent years reading rationalist-adjacent discussions in which the endowment effect and loss aversion are treated as canonical debunkings of naive preference. The standing theory is elegant: rational preferences should be invariant under incidental changes in framing or ownership. A person randomly handed a mug should not value it more than an otherwise identical mug she never received; a treatment described in terms of lives lost should not be judged differently from the identical treatment described in terms of lives saved, because the underlying distribution of outcomes is the same. On this view, loss aversion and the endowment effect are reference-dependent glitches. The reference point is arbitrary, so letting it steer valuation makes choices inconsistent, exploitable by way of framing, and in a real sense unprincipled. The theory earns its keep: it supplies one frame-independent standard, a tidy procedure for defusing manipulative framing, and a unified story about why these effects look like mistakes.

And yet I cannot shake the intuition that some of what the theory files under "bias" is doing legitimate normative work. If I have built a life around a baseline — dependents, promises, a mortgage, plans calibrated to my current income — then caring far more about losing a given amount than about gaining it seems less like an error than like taking my actual position seriously. A house or a long-term project may matter to me in a way an otherwise identical one would not, precisely because ownership and history change what there is to protect. The distinction I keep reaching for is between arbitrary anchoring, where the reference point is a trick of presentation, and engaged attachment, where the reference point expresses real commitments. But I am unsure that distinction can be drawn without quietly smuggling in my own values, and I worry I am rationalizing status quo bias because it happens to be my status quo.

I am not asking which side wins. Please answer as a decision theorist and philosopher of economics who can hold both the neoclassical and behavioral traditions in view, and think carefully, beginning by steelmanning each side at its strongest, including the possibility that the notion of engaged attachment either collapses or rescues everything. Then give me a negotiated diagnosis: what would have to be true for the intuition to be debunked and dropped as bias rationalization; what would have to be true for the frame-invariance theory to be amended or abandoned instead; and whether there is a reading on which both survive — for instance, one where "bias" concerns the arbitrariness of the reference point rather than reference-dependence as such, so the real work lies in distinguishing arbitrary from non-arbitrary reference points. Price the moves. What does each option cost, and which is most likely just another excuse for my own irrationality?
