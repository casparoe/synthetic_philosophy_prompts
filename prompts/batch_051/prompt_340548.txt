I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've ended up with a philosophical dataset by accident. For years I've been asking my close friend Maya a running series of questions about when a person deserves moral credit for doing the right thing. By "moral credit" I mean the kind of credit expressed by saying that the person deserves admiration or praise in that moment, not that the action itself was wrong to perform. I've been treating Maya's answers like labeled examples and trying to infer the classifier she's running, and I still can't pin down the decision boundary.

The examples, as close to her wording as I can reconstruct them:

1. Marta finds a wallet full of money and returns it intact because she expects the owner will give her a reward. Marta does the right thing, but Maya says no moral credit.
2. Ben donates a kidney to his sister because he loves her and can't stand watching her suffer; the thought that it is "the right thing to do" never crosses his mind. Maya says yes, full credit.
3. Priya returns a wallet because keeping it would make her feel guilty and she wants to avoid that queasy feeling. She can't name any further reason. Maya says no credit.
4. Tom donates a kidney to a stranger despite the risk, not from affection — he is reserved toward the recipient — but from a firm conviction that this is what his duty requires. He cannot articulate a further reason. Maya says yes, full credit, and considers this the most admirable case of the group.
5. When we were nineteen, a mutual friend lied to her controlling parents to help her younger sister move into an abuse shelter. She told us she believed she was doing something wrong by deceiving them, but she couldn't live with herself if she handed her sister back instead. Maya says the act was right and the friend deserves full credit.
6. Aarav meant to pay a medical bill online, mistyped, and accidentally sent the exact amount to a disaster-relief charity as a donation, where it did real good. The transfer itself would have been the same either way; only the label differed, and he didn't notice until the confirmation email arrived. Maya says no credit, not even a little.
7. Riya volunteers every week as a reading tutor for a blind student, and she genuinely helps, but she started and continues mainly because the service record strengthens her medical school application. Maya says the helping is right, but no moral credit.

I initially guessed the rule was something like "she has to be acting for the right reason." But her verdicts defeat the simple versions of that I try. Cases 2 and 5 suggest you can get full credit without believing you are doing the right thing. Case 4 suggests a bare sense of duty counts. Cases 3 and 7 suggest that some strong personal motives, like avoiding guilt or building a resume, don't count.

The new case I actually need scored: Nisha is a hospital administrator who follows the facility's guidelines and professional standards with complete rigidity, not because she cares about patients, but because she is afraid of being audited and hates having her work questioned. Her compliance is unusually reliable; for years she has been scrupulously honest and fair with patients in all the ways the guidelines require. This morning she gives a family a full and accurate account of a mistake her staff made in a relative's care, exactly as the standards demand. Telling them is the morally right thing to do. Does Nisha deserve moral credit?
