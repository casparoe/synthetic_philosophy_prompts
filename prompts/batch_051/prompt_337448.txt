I head trust and safety policy at a mid-sized platform that hosts user-generated video and text in more than a hundred countries. Several times a week we receive legal demands—court orders, regulator notices, or government takedown requests—asserting that some piece of content is unlawful under the requesting country's rules, even though it may be lawful where the uploader lives or where our company is based. For each demand we must choose among three scopes of action: remove the content everywhere, geo-block it in the requesting country only, or decline to act. The choice is not merely legal; it is a running decision about whose law ought to govern speech on a service without borders. If we always comply with the strictest request, the most repressive state becomes the world's speech legislator by default. If we always geo-block, we quietly participate in censorship merely because a government demands it, and we treat national borders as walls inside the internet. If we always refuse, we substitute our own values for democratically enacted law. I need a defensible standing policy rather than an ad hoc reaction each time.

I am asking you to write a reusable decision procedure my policy staff can run on any incoming demand, grounded in the philosophical considerations at stake rather than in the statutes of any particular country. The considerations I want the procedure to embody are these: the territoriality principle, that a state may regulate conduct within its own territory; the effects or targeting principle, that a state has a stronger claim when content is aimed at its residents or has substantial effects there; comity between legal systems, understood as the respect one legal order owes to another; and the worry that on a borderless platform, the most restrictive jurisdiction becomes the de facto standard for everyone. The procedure should be a sequence of questions asked in order, with guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with refuse, geo-block, or remove globally, and where it must escalate to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. It should also mark the line between what a state may legitimately demand and what we are prepared to do as a matter of our own policy, and it should say plainly what it cannot settle—for example, where genuine legal uncertainty about our exposure remains, or where two principles point in opposite directions. Make explicit which conceptual distinctions are doing the work, such as the difference between where an uploader is located, where the audience is located, and where the harm falls. The procedure must be usable by staff without legal training in under an hour and must be defensible to our general counsel and to a public policy committee. Treat the legal facts in the examples as stipulated; this is not a request for statutory research.

Then run the procedure on these three cases and show the path each takes:

1. A court in a liberal democracy orders us to remove Holocaust denial material globally. The uploader is in that country and the content is unlawful there, but it is lawful in most other countries where we operate. My team's inclination is to comply globally because the material is repugnant.
2. An authoritarian government orders us to remove a video by a dissident journalist in exile that accuses the government of corruption. The video is lawful where the journalist lives and in our home country, and the government cites a law criminalizing insults to state institutions.
3. A court in our home country orders global removal of non-consensual intimate imagery. The conduct is unlawful in nearly every country where we operate, and the depicted person cannot prevent its redistribution by other means.

For each case, state the procedure's recommendation and the reasoning. At least one of these should produce a recommendation you expect me and my team to resist; say why the procedure nonetheless goes that way.
