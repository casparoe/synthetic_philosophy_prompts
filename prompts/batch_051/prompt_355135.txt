I am a student in an upper-level health policy and medical ethics course, and we have been working through the QALY literature and the dispute about how to value a statistical life under scarcity. In a workshop on surge triage, the following proposal came up, and I want to think through how it would actually behave as a mechanism rather than whether it is defensible in the abstract.

Each patient referred for intensive care during a ventilator shortage gets two numbers. The first, p, is the probability of surviving to hospital discharge if they receive intensive treatment, estimated by a triage committee that is blinded to the patient's identity but works from the treating team's documentation. The second, q, is the expected number of quality-adjusted life-years the patient would live if they survived, computed from age, baseline functional status, and comorbidities using a standard instrument. The raw score is p × q, the expected QALYs saved. Patients with p below 0.05 are not entered in the allocation pool at all. Everyone else receives a number of lottery tickets equal to the square root of their raw score, and each time a ventilator becomes available it is assigned to a patient drawn with probability proportional to current ticket holdings. Scores are recomputed every 48 hours for patients still waiting, and families may submit supplementary evidence to contest q, which is then reviewed by the committee.

Treat this as a mechanism and analyze how it would function in practice. What incentives does it create for families, for the treating clinicians who write the documentation, for the triage committee, and for hospital administrators? Where is it gamed first, and how — drawing on the 0.05 cutoff, the supplementary-evidence channel, the timing of recomputation, the incentives to shade p or q upward or downward, and the referral and admission behavior of hospitals? What second-order effects might it have on documentation standards, on public trust, on litigation, and on the way baseline functional status is recorded? Finally, say whether the square-root transformation and the lottery genuinely blunt the standard objections to ranking by expected QALYs saved — that it unfairly discounts older patients and people with disabilities — or merely redistribute those objections into a new shape, and identify which failure modes could be fixed without abandoning the weighted-lottery structure and which could not.
