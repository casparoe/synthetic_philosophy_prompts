Last week, in my school's philosophy club, we were arguing about whether there is a fact of the matter about where ordinary categories stop applying — whether a habit has or has not become an addiction, whether a joke is or is not offensive, whether someone is or is not a good friend. One member kept answering every case with the same one-liner: "One grain doesn't make a heap." His argument was that since no single grain of sand can change a heap into a non-heap, no single action or trait can place something on one side or the other of any rough category; therefore, the lines we draw in our private judgments, our school's discipline code, and even the law are merely arbitrary choices rather than discoveries. He treated the heap as a decisive model for all of these very different cases.

I could tell the slogan was doing too much work, but I could not say exactly what it was claiming or where the leap occurred — for instance, between descriptive categories like heaps and evaluative categories like offensiveness. Could you separate the distinct precise principles that "one grain doesn't make a heap" might be used to express — about whether such categories have sharp cut-offs, about our ability to detect differences, about how vague words get their meanings, about what we can know, about whether vague categories track real differences in the world — and say for each principle whether it is trivially true, plausible but substantive, or probably false? Then identify which of those readings, if any, would actually support his conclusion that every line we draw for a vague category, including the ones in our school's code and the law, is merely arbitrary. I would like the readings set out as a list I can bring back to the club.
