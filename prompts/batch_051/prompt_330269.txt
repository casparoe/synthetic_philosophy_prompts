Ok so here's the deal. I'm a PhD student in phil sci, mostly working on economic modelling, and I'm trying to write a one-pager on where I've landed about idealization before a seminar. But every time I write it down, the commitments rub against each other in ways I can't quite locate. I'd rather have someone go through them cold and tell me which pairs actually pull apart, which ones only look like they do, and what I'd have to give up in each case.

Roughly, where I've landed:

(1) On model choice I'm basically a Friedmanian: models should be ranked by their record of empirical, especially novel, prediction, and the realism of an assumption is by itself no reason to prefer one model over a predictively similar rival. I'm willing to call this the F-twist and bite the bullet on it.

(2) But I'm not an instrumentalist. The best explanation for why some models keep landing novel predictions is that they're latching onto real structure—including unobservable structure—so I want to be a realist about the parts of models that do the heavy lifting.

(3) I also think idealizations are often explanatory virtues rather than vices. Frictionless planes, point masses, infinite populations, zero transaction costs: these shut off confounding factors and let a single causal factor show up clearly. Some of the best explanations in science come from models that are, taken literally, false.

(4) Yet I have this stubborn intuition that not all idealizations are equal. An idealization that distorts the very mechanism being posited—say, modelling the whole macroeconomy as one representative agent, or assuming complete information in a model whose point is that information asymmetries matter—feels importantly worse as an explanation than idealizing away some causally minor detail, even when both models fit the data about equally well.

(5) For a concrete verdict: I'd count the zero-transaction-costs assumption in Coase's theorem as explanatory—it isolates why property rights matter—but I wouldn't count Ptolemaic epicycles as explanatory, even though they were predictively useful, because the epicycle machinery doesn't correspond to anything real in the system.

So: are these jointly tenable? I especially want to know whether (1) and (2) actually conflict, or whether that's just the usual predictivism/realism dance and they can be made to cooperate. And whether my distinction between good and bad idealizations in (4) is doing real work or is just a vibe I'm reading off the case in (5). Please go pairwise, rank the tensions by how severe they are rather than giving me a flat list, say for each one what the cheapest fix is and what it costs, and then tell me wich single revision would leave the most coherent package. Don't tell me what I should believe overall—just audit the set.
