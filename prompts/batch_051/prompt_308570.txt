Heres a real practice that seems philosophically odd to me. Every year at the State of the Union, one member of the cabinet is sent to a secure, undisclosed location — the designated survivor — while the president, vice president, speaker of the House, president pro tempore, and the rest of the cabinet are all sitting in the Capitol. If the Capitol were destroyed and everyone there killed, this person would become president. Usually its a low-profile secretary (Agriculture, Commerce, something like that) who has never stood for national elected office in their life; they know their assignment, the whole government knows it, and the public knows it too, and afterwards they just go back to running, say, the Department of Agriculture.

I'm less interested in whether the scenario is likely than in what this practice presupposes. Specifically, what theory of political legitimacy is built into it? Does it commit us to thinking that legitimacy comes from your place in a legal chain of succession rather than from being chosen, known, or publicly tested? Does it presuppose that keeping some constitutional officer alive matters more than the democratic pedigree of the person who would actually govern? Or is it just prudent continuity insurance, compatible with basically any view of legitimacy? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, say whether its pieces are consistent with one another, and say where the practice could be licensed by several different theories. Then take a definite position: which of these presuppositions do you think is really doing the work, and what would the practice have to change if we instead held that only someone the public has actually chosen — or at least seriously vetted — may hold executive power? I dont want a list of readings: commit to one and argue for it.
