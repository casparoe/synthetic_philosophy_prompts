I chair the ethics committee at a small rehabilitation hospital, and I need a written opinion from you before our Thursday meeting. We've been stuck on a discharge case: Mrs. Alvarez, 74, came to us after a stroke and is now medically ready to leave, but she still needs help with meds and meals and can't safely live alone. Her only adult child, Diane, lives about an hour away. Diane has said she won't take her in and mostly won't return the social worker's calls; when she did pick up she said her mother kept a roof over her head and paid for college but treated parenting like a transaction she was owed repayment on, and added, "I don't owe her anything now." The care team is pushing us to delay discharge and authorize a family meeting, clearly hoping the committee's weight will nudge Diane into stepping up. The committee is split. One side says grown children owe their parents a debt of repayment for being raised — the food, the rides, the tuition — and Diane is just trying to dodge it. The other side keeps going back to the "friendship model" (from that Jane English piece someone circulated): the idea that what adult children owe isn't a debt for past sacrifices but whatever the current relationship actually supports, the way friendship does; a parent who was materially generous but never built a real relationship, they argue, hasn't created the kind of bond that generates caregiving duties later in life.

I'd like the memo to be roughly 500–700 words, something I can circulate without editing. It should recommend one concrete course of action with a time frame — press ahead with discharge on the 20th, hold it for two weeks to attempt mediation, arrange home health visits with no daughter involvement, whatever you think is right — and it should take the strongest version of both positions seriously, especially the daughter's, without strawmanning her. Don't just tell us what the law or our own policy already says; we know that and we still disagree about what the right thing is. The closing line should be something the committee can actually vote on, not a survey of the arguments. Don't assign us reading; my members won't do it.
