I am a high school student writing an extended essay that tries to make progress on the question 'What is intelligence?' rather than just surveying existing answers. I have a rough thesis and a planned argument, and I would like comments on the idea before I draft the full paper. The essay is conceptual, so I do not need literature suggestions; I need help making the argument stronger and anticipating where a skeptical reader will push back.

The governing idea is this. Instead of asking whether a person, animal, or artificial system 'is intelligent' as a yes/no or single-score question, we should ask how quickly it converts relevant experience into reusable competence on problems it has not been directly prepared for. Intelligence, on this view, is a property of a learning trajectory, not of a current state. A chess position a machine evaluates perfectly, or a set of answers a student memorized, is competence; the intelligent thing is the change between 'could not reliably handle this kind of problem' and 'can reliably handle new instances of it.' I will call this the trajectory view.

The essay would develop four claims:

(1) A competence/intelligence distinction dissolves the problem of tests passed by memorization. The memorizer starts competent, so a single score cannot reveal intelligence; only improvement on tasks whose format could not have been pre-stored counts as evidence.

(2) The view offers a unified way to handle very different cases. A pocket calculator or a lookup table is extremely competent but does not improve with exposure, so the view denies them intelligence. A novice who starts behind but improves steeply on novel problems is intelligent even if a rote performer still outscores them at the end. I plan to use a two-students-in-calculus example: one begins ahead but gains little on genuinely new problem types; the other begins behind but improves steeply; by the final test the first still receives the higher score.

(3) The view reframes the IQ debate. The meaningful question is not 'does an IQ score measure intelligence?' but whether the score is mostly a state measurement or a change measurement, and whether the instrument ever includes a phase controlled enough to separate the two.

(4) The view makes certain fixed capacities, such as working memory or reaction speed, count only indirectly: they are resources that may or may not enable steeper learning, but on their own they are not intelligence.

I already see four objections, and I am unsure which are fatal:

(A) Rate of improvement can be manufactured by starting artificially bad. Someone can deliberately underperform at the beginning, or an artificial system can be reset to a blank state, so the measured steepness is partly an artifact of the starting point.

(B) The view seems to deny intelligence to a person with a high fixed ability whose learning is now flat, which may conflict with ordinary usage of the word.

(C) Comparing rates of improvement across different tasks and different ceiling difficulties is not well-defined. A small improvement on a very hard problem may count for more than a large improvement on an easy one, so the view needs a difficulty metric it does not yet have.

(D) There may be a circularity worry: to measure how much competence was gained, one must rank problems by the competence they demand, which already presupposes some notion of intelligence or ability.

I would like comments on the following:

(1) Which of objections (A) through (D) is most damaging, and can the trajectory view be revised to answer it without collapsing back into a state-based view of intelligence?

(2) Is the competence/intelligence distinction doing real conceptual work, or does it merely stipulate a new and narrower use of 'intelligence'? If it is stipulative, what would make the stipulation worth adopting?

(3) Is the two-students calculus example the right carrying example, or should the essay use a non-test, nonhuman, or artificial case as its central illustration? What would the ideal example need to control for?

(4) What is the strongest counterexample I have not listed, and would it break the thesis or merely force a boundary clause?
