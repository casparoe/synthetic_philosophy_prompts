I'm a neuroscience PhD student and I started sitting in on a philosophy of mind seminar this semester because my advisor keeps talking about top-down causation and I wanted to know what philosophers actually think. That turned into a long rabbit hole on the causal exclusion argument, and now I'm pretty convinced the mental causation debate is just stuck. Heres how it looks from the outside. Both sides accept the same three starting points: the physical world is causally closed, mental events are realized by physical events, and mental properties are not identical to physical properties (at least for the nonreductive crowd). Yet one side, call them the exclusionists, says that's already enough to show the mental is epiphenomenal unless it reduces to the physical, because a single physical effect cant have two sufficient causes. The other side says that's a double-counting error or a bad picture of causation: the mental cause and its physical realizer are not competing causes but the same event at two levels, or the mental cause is a difference-maker even though the physical implementation does the actual pushing. Interactionist dualism is ruled out from the start, so thats not what's in play.

My working theory is that the entire dispute is about what cause means. If causation is fundamentally production or generation (something has to do the pushing), then exclusion looks devastating. If causation is fundamentally counterfactual dependence or difference-making, then mental causation is basically fine and the problem mostly evaporates. So I want a diagnosis: is this a verbal dispute about the word cause, or is there a substantive disagreement hiding underneath (for example, about whether high-level difference-makers can be real causes, or whether causal closure was even the right principle to start with)? And given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here, or is the thing just going to stay stuck? Give me the structure of the disagreement, not a reading list.
