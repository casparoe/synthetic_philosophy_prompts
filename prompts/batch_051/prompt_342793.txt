My family has this tradition where every New Years my mom makes a casserole that nobody actually likes. It's bland, everyone eats a little to be polite, and it's fine, but the food itself isn't the point. One year my aunt said we should skip it and my mom got genuinely upset — not because she thought anyone liked the dish but because, as she said, it's tradition. My friend thinks that's basically fake. She says my mom doesn't value the tradition itself, she values what it buys her: an excuse to get everyone in the same house, the feeling that our family is stable over time, not having to make a new decision, being the kind of family that keeps traditions. I don't think that's totally fair, but I can't quite say why.

So can you actually test whether someone values a tradition for its own sake rather than valuing the stuff the tradition gives them? Design a concrete experiment — what you'd vary, who you'd run it on, and exactly what result would convince you it's one rather than the other. Then be honest about what even the best test would leave open. Like, if someone keeps a tradition after you take away all the usual benefits it provides, does that show they value the tradition itself, or does it just show they're bad at noticing which benefits the tradition was really supplying? Does the phrase "for its own sake" survive the best test, or should we just say they value the effects but don't know it? Also say exactly what a positive result would and would not show, because I feel like a experiment can settle part of this but probably not all of it.
