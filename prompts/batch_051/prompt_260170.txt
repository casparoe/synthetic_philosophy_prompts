Hey — I'm a TA for an intro ethics course and I'm grading a batch of short-answer questions on Augustine's On Lying. I want a second opinion on this one before I hand it back. Here's the question as I set it:

In On Lying (De Mendacio), Augustine writes:

"Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind. Whence also the heart of him who lies is said to be double; that is, there is a double thought: the one, of that thing which he either knows or thinks to be true and does not produce; the other, of that thing which he produces instead thereof, knowing or thinking it to be false. Whence it comes to pass, that he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie."

(a) In your own words, what does Augustine say a lie is? (b) Why does he think someone who says a false thing while believing it true does not lie, whereas someone who says a true thing while believing it false does lie? (c) What does the passage imply about whether the actual truth or falsity of what is said matters for whether someone lied?

And here's what the student wrote:

"Augustine's idea is that lying is a mismatch between what's in your mind and what you put out into the world: you have one thing in mind and utter another, and that's what he means by the double heart. So the test is the speaker's state of mind, not the actual truth of the sentence. If I believe p and say p, I haven't lied even if p happens to be false; I'm just mistaken, and since I never had the double heart I'm completely blameless. Augustine also says I lie if I believe p and say not-p, even if not-p turns out to actually be true—that's his 'true lie' case. So on part (c), the objective truth-value of the words does no real work in detecting a lie. The only wrinkle is that true-lie case: I'd call it attempted lying rather than lying proper, because what actually came out of my mouth matched the facts, so anyone who took me at my word wouldn't have been misled about how things stand."
