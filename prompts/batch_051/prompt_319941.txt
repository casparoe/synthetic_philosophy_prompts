I got into an argument with a housemate about the dollar auction game — the one where two people bid for a dollar, and both the winner and the second-highest bidder have to pay their final bid, so bids can spiral past a dollar. My housemate kept saying "you shouldn't throw good money after bad" as if that just dissolves the puzzle. But in the auction, if you're the one sitting at $2.50 with the other person at $3, refusing to raise means you're out $2.50 for certain, while raising to $3.50 gives you a shot at winning the dollar and cutting your overall loss. So it isn't obvious that stopping is better than continuing. I want to know what that proverb could actually be claiming here. Break "don't throw good money after bad" into the distinct precise theses it might be expressing — claims about sunk costs being irrelevant, about whether more spending can actually improve your position, about reputation or looking weak, whatever else seems live — and for each one say whether it's trivial, plausible but disputable, or just false in the dollar auction. Which readings would actually support dropping out of the auction, and which just restate the thing we're confused about? Also, please seperate what's settled (basic sunk-cost reasoning in standard economics, say) from what's genuinely contested among people who study escalation of commitment, and don't present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
