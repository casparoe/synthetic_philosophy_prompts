For a school philosophy club I've been reading Alan Turing's 1950 paper 'Computing Machinery and Intelligence' (the one with the imitation game), and me and my mate got stuck on section 7, where Turing says that building a machine to think like an adult is hard, so instead you should build a child brain and educate it. He compares the child brain to a notebook you buy at the stationers — mostly blank sheets with very little mechanism — and says the hope is that there is so little mechanism in it that something like it can be programmed fairly easily. I know that's a rough paraphrase but it's the gist.

So here's what I want. Give me an actual probability, a number between 0 and 1, that Turing's bet is basically right: that a machine with a fairly small amount of built-in structure could, if given something like a human child's education, reach general human-level intelligence. I don't want 'it depends on your definition of intelligence' — I want a number and the reasoning broken down. How much of your credence rests on whether human babies are actually near-blank slates as opposed to having loads of innate structure? How much on whether 'education' can supply whatever's missing, and how much on whether there's a difference between simulating learning and actually understanding? And which single sub-question would shift your number the most if you got new evidence about it?
