Political systems that make heavy use of direct democracy almost always surround it with institutions that seem designed to distrust the very voters they empower. California lets citizens legislate by ballot initiative, yet courts can and do strike down those initiatives on constitutional grounds. Switzerland's constitution makes popular initiatives central to its political order, but the same order subjects them to limits drawn from fundamental rights and mandatory international law. The United Kingdom put membership of the European Union to a popular vote, but the meaning and implementation of that vote were then filtered through Parliament, courts, and a withdrawal process whose terms no voter had been presented with. The same political systems routinely remove other questions from popular control altogether—monetary policy, core constitutional essentials, and individual rights—and entrust them to insulated agencies, courts, or supermajority rules that no single referendum can undo.

Taken as a whole, what theory of popular political judgment, and of democratic legitimacy, does this mixed practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, while clearly distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. In particular: does the practice presuppose that a popular vote expresses something more than, or other than, an aggregation of individual preferences—for example, a judgment about reasons, a legitimate will that can nevertheless be mistaken, or a standing authorization constrained by deeper commitments? And where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that rival theories of democracy (proceduralist, epistemic, constitutionalist) would each license it? Finally, state what would have to change in institutional design if the presupposed view were replaced by a consistently proceduralist one, in which the outcome of a popular vote simply is the criterion of democratic legitimacy.
