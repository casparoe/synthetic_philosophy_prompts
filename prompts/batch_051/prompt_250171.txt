I'm writing a paper on split-brain patients and the unity of consciousness, and I've found myself stuck on a question that seems prior to the whole debate. Both the one-subject and the two-subjects readings of split-brain data presuppose some criterion for when a set of concurrent experiences belongs to a single subject of experience — or, equivalently, for when two experiences are co-conscious rather than merely simultaneous. I'd like you to propose and defend a working criterion.

Here are the cases I want it to get right, or at least to pronounce on cleanly. (1) An ordinary awake adult watching a film, simultaneously aware of the color of a character's coat, the swelling of the score, and the pressure of the armrest against her elbow. I take this to be a clear case of one subject with several co-conscious experiences. (2) Two strangers in the same cinema, each noticing exactly those three details at the same time, each within a private stream the other cannot enter. I take this to be a clear case of two subjects with no co-consciousness across them. (3) A patient with a complete corpus callosotomy in a standard divided-field experiment: a picture of a spoon is flashed briefly in his left visual field, so that the information reaches the right hemisphere first. Because language is left-lateralized in this patient, his speech is generated by the left hemisphere; asked what he saw, he reports seeing nothing, while his left hand, controlled mainly by the right hemisphere, reliably selects the matching object from a tray of hidden items. This is the case I cannot settle: is the right hemisphere's seeing the spoon co-conscious with the speaking subject's experience of the room, or not? (4) The same patient an hour later, walking down the corridor, passing objects between his two hands, and conversing normally. I incline to say this is one subject in the ordinary sense, but I cannot say what changed between (3) and (4). (5) A patient in deep, unresponsive general anesthesia, stipulating that there are no occurrent experiences at all. I am not sure whether it even makes sense to ask how many subjects of experience are present in a case where there are no experiences, or what your criterion should say there.

It would help if the criterion made explicit whether a mere failure of information transfer across systems is enough to break co-consciousness, or whether dissociation must go deeper — for example, to separate centers of agency and self-ascription — before we should speak of two subjects. So: give me a precise working definition of what it is for experiences at a time to be co-conscious, or for there to be a single subject of those experiences. Run it through all five cases, say which case it handles least well, and tell me whether you would revise the definition in light of that outcome or defend the verdict it yields.
