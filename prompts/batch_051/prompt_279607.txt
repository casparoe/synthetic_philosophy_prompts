In *A Philosophical Essay on Probabilities*, Laplace writes: "All events, even those which on account of their insignificance do not seem to follow the great laws of nature, are a result of it just as necessarily as the revolutions of the sun."

I am trying to pin down what a rational prior could be for an objective Bayesian who takes that passage seriously: chance is not a feature of the world but a measure of the inquirer's partial knowledge of deterministic events. Give me a working definition of "rational prior" and test it against these cases: (a) a symmetrically constructed coin about to be flipped; (b) a coin drawn from a drawer that I know contains either all double-headed coins or all fair coins, with no base rate; (c) a die thrown by a deterministic mechanism under unknown initial conditions; (d) the next digit in a computable but not yet computed decimal expansion of π; (e) a one-off political decision with no clear reference class. I think (a) should be clear and (e) should be clear, though I am less sure about the middle ones. Propose the definition, run it through all five, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. Then say whether the maximum entropy principle is a consequence of your definition or a rival to it. I want a committed answer, not a survey of options.
