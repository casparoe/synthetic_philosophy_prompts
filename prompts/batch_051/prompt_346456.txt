I have been thinking on my own about an idea I once came across, sometimes called sphexishness, and I have worked out an argument from it that I cannot get past. The example, as I understand it, is this. A type of wasp stings a cricket into paralysis, drags it to the threshold of its burrow, leaves it there, goes inside for a quick inspection, comes back out, and then drags the cricket in. If someone shifts the cricket a few inches while the wasp is inside, the wasp comes out, drags the cricket back to the threshold, and goes inside to inspect again. This can be repeated many times, and the wasp seems never to notice that it has already inspected the burrow. It appears to be running a fixed routine whose trigger keeps getting pulled, not acting freely in any meaningful sense.

I have been trying to decide whether this example generalizes in a way that dissolves the difference between the wasp and us. Here is the argument I arrived at.

(1) We withhold freedom from the wasp because its behavior is a fixed response to a cue: the same cue in the same internal state pulls the same routine, even when the routine has become pointless.

(2) A fixed response to a cue does not stop being a fixed response to a cue when the cue is made very specific, when there are many cues, or when the system has memory. Fine-grained triggers and stored memories are just more of the same kind of thing.

(3) One could therefore imagine a system whose triggers include details of the situation, the time since some earlier event, a memory that the same situation has occurred before, and the behavior the system performed last time. With enough triggers and enough memory, its behavior could track circumstances as closely as one likes.

(4) In principle, a large enough set of such if-then rules, together with a large enough memory, could match the outward behavior of a human being over any finite stretch of observation, including hesitation, changing one's mind, explaining a decision, and doing something novel.

(5) But a system made of if-then rules is sphexish in the same sense as step (1). The only difference is the size of the rule book.

(6) So for any observed stretch of human behavior, there is a possible sphexish system that would have produced the same outward behavior.

(7) If two systems produce the same outward behavior over an entire period of observation, no behavioral evidence from that period can tell them apart.

(8) Therefore no behavioral evidence could ever show that a human being is not sphexish. Whatever anyone does, a sphexish system could have done the same. The thing called freedom would then either make no observable difference or be just a name for having a very large rule book.

I know an obvious objection: a person can notice they are in a loop and step out of it. But it seems to me that noticing the loop would just be one more trigger, firing a rule that changes what is done next, so the objection leaves the argument untouched. Am I right? Tell me whether the argument is valid. If it fails, identify the exact step and the reading on which it fails, and explain whether correcting that step would save the conclusion. If it is valid, say why the conclusion is less damaging to the idea of human freedom than it appears. Please write the answer in American English.
