I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, so apologies if this is a mess. I've been running a reading group at my company — six of us, all ML engineers — and we went through Turing's 1950 paper, Computing Machinery and Intelligence, specifically the part where he says don't try to program an adult mind, build a child machine and educate it. One of my coworkers and I turned his idea into a numbered argument, and it seems to prove way too much on it's own terms. We can't find which step is wrong.

Here's the argument, roughly as we wrote it:

(1) For a machine, "being educated" can only mean that its parameters are updated in response to inputs and some sort of feedback signal; there is nowhere else for the change to live.

(2) A human kid is in the same boat, because a kid is a physical system: initial architecture plus a history of inputs and feedback, with no extra ingredient sneaking in.

(3) In principle, a learning machine can be trained to match a human student's behavior on all the normal tests we use to check understanding — answering questions, applying the idea to new cases, walking through the reasoning aloud.

(4) If two learners have the same behavioral dispositions and the same learning history, it would be arbitrary to say one "really understands" the material and the other is merely trained. They are identical on every feature we can point to.

(5) So a trained model that passes the same tests as a student understands the material in the same sense the student does — and if the model doesn't really understand, then the student doesn't either.

(6) So education is just training with a person in the loop. Talk about reasons and understanding is either a shorthand for the trained state or a name for an extra ingredient we have no evidence for.

(7) But that conclusion is absurd. There is a real difference between explaining a proof and shaping behavior with rewards, and between education and indoctrination. Turing himself seems to grant this when he says reward and punishment can only be part of teaching, because the amount of information they carry is tiny, and the rest has to come through other channels like language. But honestly I don't see how that helps: from a machine-learning point of view, language is just more token inputs with a feedback channel behind them.

(8) So one of (1)–(5) must be false. But each one looked obvious when we wrote it down. Which step do you reject, and what's the least bad price for rejecting it? I'd rather keep (3) than give it up, since that's basically Turing's whole hope, and I don't want the answer to be dualism. Please write in American English, and tell me the obvious answer I'm probably missing, including where your own diagnosis is most likely to fail and what specific argument would change your mind about it.
