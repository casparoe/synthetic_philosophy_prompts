I'm on the committee at my university that's revising the campus "statement of community standards." The current draft says university members should express disagreement in ways that "preserve the harmony of the academic community," and a bloc on the committee wants to make this an explicit preamble principle, not just a throwaway line. The resistance is that this is smuggling a Confucian/conservative order-and-harmony value into a document that's supposed to instantiate liberal pluralism, and that "harmony" will become the standard justification for disciplining speech that embarrasses the administration. My co-chair wants to reject it outright; I suspect there's a real principle hiding under the motte, and the bailey is exactly what the critics fear. The passage I keep circling is from the Analects 1.12 (traditionally attributed to the disciple You Ruo; Legge's translation): "In practicing the rules of propriety, a natural ease is to be prized. In the ways prescribed by the ancient kings, this is the excellent quality, and in things small and great we follow them. Yet it is not to be observed in all cases. If one, knowing how such ease should be prized, manifests it, without regulating it by the rules of propriety, this likewise is not to be done." Draft the short memo I'd send to the full committee before our Tuesday vote: adopt the preamble as written, adopt it with a specific revision, or reject it. Recommend one, give concrete wording for any revision you'd make, and engage the strongest arguement on the losing side rather than just citing the Analects to settle it.
