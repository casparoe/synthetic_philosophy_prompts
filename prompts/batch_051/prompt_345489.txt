Here's one I've been turning over. Suppose that, starting next January, the dominant social network (call it a Facebook/X hybrid with about two billion users) introduces the following system. Every account above 100,000 followers that posts a checkable claim—a claim about a future event, or a past event that isn't common knowledge—must attach a probability, any whole percentage from 1 to 99. The platform resolves each tagged claim once it becomes checkable, and scores it with a rule engineered so that the expected-score-maximizing strategy is to state your actual credence; everyone understands the rule is strictly proper, meaning honest probabilities are unbeatable in expectation and confident errors are punished more than hedged ones. It publishes a rolling accuracy score next to your name, and reach, monetization, and the "verified expert" badge all depend on it.

Over the next ten years, what actually happens? I'm not asking whether this is good or bad on net. I want mechanism-by-mechanism predictions on: (1) whether high-reach accounts become more careful and honest, or just more evasive; (2) where the resulting dishonesty goes—vagueness, unfalsifiable claims, refusing to attach numbers, deleting posts after the fact, gish-galloping; (3) who wins and who loses (pundits, scientists, journalists, grifters, PR shops); (4) whether ordinary sub-100k discourse gets pulled in the same direction or does the opposite; (5) second-order effects on polarization, trust, and what counts as an expert.
