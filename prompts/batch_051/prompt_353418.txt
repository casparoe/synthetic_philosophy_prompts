I have repeatedly noticed a pattern in negotiations between groups with incompatible value systems. The parties adopt a package that each ranks above the status quo by its own standards, and the improvement arises because they weight the components differently: each side trades away something it values less for something it values more, while neither side revises its underlying commitments and each continues to regard the other's central commitments as partly mistaken. This is not a concession in the usual sense, not a convergence on shared principles, and not a modus vivendi in which one side simply yields to the stronger. It looks closer to gains from trade carried out in different evaluative currencies, but the analogy may fail precisely because the goods being traded are moral values rather than ordinary commodities. I want to turn this thought into a two-page proposal for a two-year postdoctoral fellowship, and I would like you to draft it. The central question should be whether the exchange model identifies a distinct and defensible kind of moral compromise that standard concession-based and consensus-based accounts miss, or whether it collapses into one of those accounts. Please include: a precise statement of the question; an account of why the standard positions leave it open; a description of what I would actually do month by month over the twenty-four months; a justification of a conceptual rather than empirical approach; and a statement, given in advance, of what would count as failure. I will supply the citations myself, so do not include a bibliography.
