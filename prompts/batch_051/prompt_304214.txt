I chair my city's Memorials and Street Naming Commission. We meet once a month to rule on petitions to remove, recontextualize, relocate, or rename public statues, plaques, streets, and public artworks. The other commissioners are an architect, two neighborhood representatives, a local historian, and a city attorney. None of us is a philosopher, and I keep noticing that we decide each case from scratch: sometimes we act like a monument means whatever its builders intended, sometimes whatever a present-day petitioner feels, and sometimes whatever gives us the least trouble at the next public meeting. I want to replace that improvisation with a procedure.

Please write us a reusable decision procedure for evaluating any proposal about a commemoration in public space: a checklist or ordered set of questions we can actually run in a two-hour meeting, with a public record to defend it. You are a moral and political philosopher specializing in the ethics of memory and public space. Think carefully before writing. Begin by briefly distinguishing the concepts the procedure must keep separate — commemorating a person or deed, endorsing what that person or deed stood for, and merely recording that something happened — and say explicitly which finer philosophical distinctions you are simplifying or dropping, and why. Then give the procedure itself.

The procedure must be usable by non-experts, and it should handle the hard moves: a historical record that is contested or newly documented; a person whose life contains both serious wrongdoing and major public goods; the gap between a street name that people use daily and barely notice and a statue purpose-built to honor; the difference between present-day descendants, direct victims, and the general public who live with the name or monument; options short of removal such as a corrective plaque, relocation to a museum, a counter-monument, or a dual name; and the question of where the default sits when the evidence is mixed. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer, and flag exactly which steps are value judgments the commission must argue about rather than facts a researcher could settle. Mark where the procedure should stop with a recommendation, where it must defer to law or prior obligations such as heritage protection, donor conditions, or a required Indigenous consultation, and where it hands the decision back to the full commission. Do not treat a monument's meaning as fixed by its creators' intentions, and do not treat it as fixed by the most offended observer today; the procedure should say how it navigates between those poles.

Then apply the procedure to these three hypothetical petitions:

1. A statue of a nineteenth-century shipping magnate whose fortune partly came from goods produced by enslaved labor, who later became a prominent abolitionist and endowed the hospital that still bears his name. The petition asks for removal and replacement.
2. A boulevard named for a mid-twentieth-century mayor who maintained segregated neighborhoods through redlining and blocked school desegregation for a decade. The petition asks to rename it after a local civil rights organizer.
3. A proposal to add — not remove — a new memorial to the victims of a forced eviction the city carried out a century ago, placed across the street from an existing monument that celebrates the official who ordered the eviction.

I should be honest about my own leanings: I tend to favor removal and renaming in most contested cases. If the procedure you build returns a "keep" or "contextualize" recommendation on any of these, do not bend it to spare me — say so, and name which application led you somewhere uncomfortable and why. End by stating the procedure's limits: the kind of case it will handle badly, and what we should do when we hit one. Do not rely on being able to search the web, and do not give us citations or a reading list; the procedure has to stand on its own reasoning.

Finish your entire answer with a final line beginning "Summary:" followed by one sentence summarizing the procedure.
