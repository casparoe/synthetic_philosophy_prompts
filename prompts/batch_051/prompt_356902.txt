This started at my job, where I sometimes have to review long chains of technical reasoning, and it has grown into a more general puzzle about how to treat evidence about my own reliability. On the one hand, I keep being drawn to a principle that seems about as fair as a rule can be: information about how likely I am to be mistaken is itself just more information, and it should go into the scale alongside everything else. If I conclude something after working through the details, and I then learn that people in exactly my condition—short on sleep, under time pressure, whatever it happens to be—make a particular kind of error a third of the time, it seems like special pleading to treat my own first-hand examination as the one thing that cannot be undercut by that higher-level fact. On the other hand, I have an intuition I cannot get to budge: once I have actually checked the reasoning itself, once the steps seem right from the inside, a general statistic about what tired people tend to do does not show that these particular steps went wrong. There seems to be something almost cowardly about standing outside my own considered judgment and saying that I will lower my confidence anyway because I am probably compromised—as if I were treating myself as a defective instrument rather than as the person doing the checking. And yet, when I try to articulate that resistance, I seem to be claiming an exemption from ordinary statistical reasoning that I would never grant to anyone else.

I am not looking for a one-line answer about which side wins. I want a negotiated view of what should give way, and on what terms. What would have to be true for my intuition to be rightly dismissed as a self-protective bias, the sort of resistance a reasonable person would explain away and set aside? What would have to be true for the principle instead to be the thing that needs a boundary—for us to say that some evidence about one's own reliability cannot be combined with one's first-order reasoning in the usual way, because doing so would amount to giving up the very perspective from which reasoning can be checked at all? And if neither side should give way entirely, is there a stable way of drawing the distinction so that the statistic and the direct examination answer different questions, and I can respect both without contradiction?
