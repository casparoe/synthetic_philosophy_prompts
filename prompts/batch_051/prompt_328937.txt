You are a philosopher of criminal law with a particular interest in the rule-of-law virtues of precision and fair notice. I teach an undergraduate Philosophy of Law unit on the void-for-vagueness doctrine, and I want an extended case study that forces students to work through the interpretive and constitutional questions rather than settle into a case they already know. The city of Helmsford enacted the following disorderly-conduct ordinance in 1954, and it remains in force:

"Section 14-2. It shall be unlawful for any person in or upon any public street, sidewalk, park, or square to behave in a boisterous, riotous, or disorderly manner; to make any unusual or disturbing noise; to use profane, indecent, or threatening language or gestures; or to do any other act which shall disturb, annoy, or interfere with the quiet, peace, or good order of the city."

Last month a performance collective staged a piece it called The Honest Mirror in Civic Square: a dozen members, in ordinary clothes and without speaking, each spent two hours silently imitating the gait and posture of whichever pedestrian was nearest. No performer touched or blocked anyone, and there was no noise beyond the ordinary hum of the square. The city charged the collective under the final catch-all clause. An officer testified that two residents and a café owner said they were genuinely frightened and "annoyed" by being followed at a short distance, while three other residents testified they found it the most orderly thing to happen in the square all year. The defendants say the clause is void for vagueness as applied to them.

Before I bring this to my students, work through what each of these approaches would say about whether the catch-all clause reaches the performance: (1) the plain words of the clause, read in context; (2) the purpose and legislative history a court might attribute to a 1954 city council; (3) what the drafters would likely have said if asked about silent performance art; and (4) the fair-notice and non-arbitrary-enforcement concerns behind the vagueness doctrine. Then say which approach should control here, whether the clause is unconstitutional as applied, and what precision criminal law owes citizens when a statute must cover conduct its authors could not imagine. Please reason through the alternatives rather than merely announcing a conclusion.
