I have been developing a criterion for comparing possible worlds that each contain infinitely many lives, and would welcome an honest assessment of it. I call it the Bounded-Prefix Criterion. Fix a decision point, and order every life in each candidate world by its causal and genealogical distance from that point: those affected first and most directly come first, those affected through one intermediary come next, and so on. Now consider the finite windows produced by stopping at each successive distance. World A is better than world B, on my criterion, just in case there is some window length N and some positive margin m such that, for every longer window, the aggregate well-being in A exceeds the aggregate well-being in B by at least m. If the difference never becomes positive by a fixed margin for all sufficiently long windows—whether because it oscillates or because any advantage decays towards zero—then the two worlds are, in my terminology, on a par. The intended attraction is that the criterion never sums an infinite number of lives; every comparison is made out of finite aggregates that are guaranteed to exist.

To see it at work, compare a world in which every life has a well-being of 10 with a world in which the first life has a well-being of −50 and every later life has 10. My criterion ranks the first world better, because from the first position onward the finite sums favour it by a constant margin of 60. But compare the first world with a third world in which well-being alternates between 11 and 9. The finite totals now oscillate around those of the first world, and no stable margin ever appears, so the two worlds count as on a par. The main objection I anticipate concerns the ordering: an infinite collection of lives is a set, and a set has no first member, so the criterion appears to depend on a contingent choice of enumeration. My reply is that the causal order radiating from the decision point supplies the needed enumeration, but I suspect this is where the view either stands or falls. I would like the three most serious objections you can find, a verdict on whether the view already exists under another name, and whether the causal-ordering reply is a repair or a fatal weakness.
