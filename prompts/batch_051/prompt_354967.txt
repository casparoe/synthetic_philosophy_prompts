I'm a novelist, about halfway through a historical novel where a character based on Machiavelli spends one evening after his fall from office trying to explain to his daughter why he still thinks men can do anything about fortune. I've got The Prince and the Discourses both open, and I think I've found a snag. In The Prince, in the chapter on fortune, he says roughly that fortune is the arbiter of half our actions, maybe a little less, and the other half is ours; she floods like a river, but prudent men build embankments and breakwaters in advance. But in the Discourses he has a darker passage, about how when Fortune doesn't want her plans resisted she blinds a man's mind and leads him to ruin. The example, if I remember it, is about a ruler who would otherwise have been prudent but takes exactly the wrong turn because fortune has taken his sight away. So the first text says prudence and virtù can prepare you against fortune, while the second says fortune can remove the very prudence you'd use to prepare.

For my scene, Machiavelli can't just say he didn't mean the first one, because his daughter would notice. Is there a reading where the two actually fit together? Maybe one is about what rulers should do, and the other is about what fortune does in exceptional cases? Or is the Discourses line a different register, half-rhetorical, not a claim about how much control we have? I'm trying to figure out whether Machiavelli the character could honestly say both without fudging, or whether I should write him as changing his mind. What are the strongest reconciliations, and which one would a careful reader find honest rather than special pleading?
