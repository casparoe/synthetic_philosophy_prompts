I'm a third-year student in a history and philosophy of mathematics course, and I've been assigned to lead a fifty-minute discussion section for first-years on the opening definitions of Euclid's Book VII: a unit is that in virtue of which each of the things that exist is called one, and a number is a multitude made up of units. The students have only school-level mathematics, so they are confident they already know what a number is and will find these definitions either trivial or baffling. I don't want you to explain the material to me; I want a teaching plan for the section. What needs to be in place before the definition of a unit can mean anything to them, and why does it have to come first? Give me the single example that should carry the whole session, and say what it must have and what it must not have—I default to apples, but I can be talked out of them. Then map the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they will surface: I suspect at least 'a unit is just the numeral 1', 'a unit is a physical object', and 'a unit is whatever you measure with'. For each, tell me exactly where in the sequence to head it off. Say what I should explicitly leave out at this level, and finish with one question to the room whose answer would show whether they have acquired the concept rather than learned to repeat the definition. Commit to a definite order and a definite example, with reasons, rather than offering me a menu of options.
