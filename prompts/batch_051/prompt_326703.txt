I sit on my hospital system's AI oversight and ethics committee. Our emergency department uses a deep-learning model that ingests triage notes, vitals, and labs and posts a disposition recommendation (ward, ICU, or discharge) with a severity score, which physicians may accept or override. After a near-miss in which an attending followed a confident recommendation whose rationale no one could reconstruct, the committee must revise our incident-reporting policy. The form currently classifies every adverse event's origin as human error, device malfunction, or process failure, and the committee is split three ways. One faction wants a new origin category, system judgment, reviewed through the same peer-review channel as clinician error, on the grounds that in practice the model functions as a decision-maker. A second faction says the design stance is the only honest one: it is an engineered artifact with specifications, so its failures are device malfunctions requiring engineering root-cause analysis. A third says both grant too much — the physical stance is the truth, and talk of the model's judgment is metaphor, since all that exists is a trained network of weights and activations. I spent last evening arguing about this with a friend, a philosopher of cognitive science, who kept pressing a line he credited to Daniel Dennett: the intentional stance, which explains and predicts a system by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs, desires, and goals, is licensed whenever it yields real predictive leverage, and the patterns it picks out are real patterns even if the system is not conscious and literally believes nothing. He would not say, though, which stance a hospital's official incident taxonomy should adopt.

Act as an expert in philosophy of cognitive science who has worked with institutional policy committees. Think through how the three stances actually apply here, and briefly brainstorm the policy options before writing. Then draft the decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's committee meeting. It should recommend one concrete incident-classification and review policy, with a time frame for implementation and later review, and it should give the strongest version of the losing position exactly as its advocates would press it before explaining why the recommendation should carry. Keep it under about 900 words.
