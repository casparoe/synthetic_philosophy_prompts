I'm giving a departmental talk in a few weeks defending the claim that money-pump arguments don't actually establish any substantive requirement of diachronic rationality. The audience will be mostly decision theorists and formal epistemologists, plus two behavioural economists who've done work on preference reversals, and I know at least three of them think of the standard money pump as one of the best arguments we have. My line is roughly this: the pump only gets going against an agent who at each node just takes whatever option is currently preferred, and that's a form of myopia (making choices as if each node were a fresh problem) rather than intransitivity. If the agent is sophisticated or resolute and sees the whole decision tree at the start, no pump actually happens, or else the agent declines the trades up front, so the argument can't show the cyclic preferences themselves are irrational. What it shows, if anything, is that being exploitable in sequential choice is a cost — but the money-pump argument seems to presuppose backwards induction or some seperability assumption that's doing all the work. I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a general defence: the six or eight objections I'll actually get from this lot, ordered by roughly how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way a decision theorist or behavioural economist would put them, with a two-minute reply to each and a note on where each reply is softest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and tell me whether I should concede anything up front to keep the pump people on side.
