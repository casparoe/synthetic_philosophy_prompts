In most common-law systems, if someone is killed through negligence, the damages are built around the survivors' losses — lost financial support, funeral costs, sometimes their own grief — and the estate can claim for the victim's pain and suffering in the interval before death. But the victim's own lost future isnt itself compensated: a living person badly injured in the same crash can recover for the loss of enjoyment of the next twenty years, yet the person who dies loses all of it and the law usually puts no sum on that. What does this pattern presuppose about whether death is bad for the person who dies? Reconstruct the implicit theory — is it that death can't be bad for the one who dies, or just that you can't compensate a dead person, or something about what courts are for? And how much is strictly presupposed rather than underdetermined: could someone who thought death was a terrible deprivation still accept these rules, and what would a system that really treated death as a loss to the victim have to change?
