I have just come out of a long argument with a friend who works in criminal law, and it has forced me to sharpen a view I am now committed to defending in public. I am presenting at our department's workshop on action and responsibility, and my thesis is this: the Knobe-effect asymmetry, in which subjects call a foreseen harmful side effect "intentional" far more often than a foreseen helpful one, shows that the folk concept of intentional action is evaluatively loaded, with judgments of blameworthiness doing part of the work inside the attribution of intentionality itself rather than being applied after the fact. From this I will argue that theorizing about intentional action, including its role in legal responsibility, cannot assume the intentional/unintentional distinction is drawn on purely descriptive grounds and evaluation arrives only later. My argument in brief: I will defend a reading of the asymmetry on which it is neither a performance error nor a pragmatic artifact; I will maintain that it survives the standard debunking attempts built around confounds and redescriptions; and I will claim that even the armchair theorist owes an account of why the asymmetry is so robust rather than a dismissal of it. The audience will be mostly philosophers of action and moral responsibility, several of them committed to intentional action as a factual, descriptive notion, plus one empirically minded psychologist who suspects the finding is a vignette-wording artifact. Prepare me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how likely and how damaging, phrased in their terms, each with a short reply and a note on where that reply is soft, and close with the objection I should hope nobody asks.
