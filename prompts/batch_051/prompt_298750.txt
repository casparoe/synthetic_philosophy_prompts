In my graduate seminar on formal epistemology, the following argument was pressed on me, and I should like a rigorous diagnosis. Let R be the proposition that it rains in Manchester tomorrow, and let C be the proposition that a fair coin, tossed at noon, lands heads. The coin is known to be fair and known to have no causal or evidential bearing on tomorrow's weather; your evidence pins down your credence in R only as lying somewhere in [0.3, 0.7]. Consider this argument.

(1) Your imprecise credal state is the set of all probability functions p, defined on the relevant algebra, such that p(R) ∈ [0.3, 0.7] and p(C) = 0.5.

(2) On the imprecise view, updating on C works by conditioning every p in the set on C, yielding the set of conditional probabilities p(R | C).

(3) That resulting set contains values arbitrarily close to 0 and to 1. For any p with p(R) = 0.5, we can set p(R | C) anywhere in [0, 1] while keeping p(C) = 0.5; in particular, p(R | C) = 0 and p(R | C) = 1 are both consistent with the constraints in (1).

(4) So upon learning that the coin landed heads, your imprecise credence in rain is not [0.3, 0.7] but [0, 1]: you become strictly more uncertain about the weather after receiving information that you know to be irrelevant to it.

(5) A rational agent may not be made more uncertain about a proposition by learning the outcome of a fair coin that she knows is irrelevant to that proposition.

(6) Therefore imprecise probabilism is in serious difficulty. Either the agent should from the outset have restricted her set to probability functions on which R and C are independent—in which case her evidence in (1) did not fix her credal state, and she needed an exact structural commitment about dependence—or the view sanctions the plainly irrational update in (4).

Where, if anywhere, does this argument go wrong? Name the premise or step you reject, explain why it initially looks forced, and say what a defender of imprecise credences must surrender in order to escape the conclusion. Your answer should be written in British English.
