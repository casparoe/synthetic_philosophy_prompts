I'm designing a small formal language for representing policy rules as statements that can refer to other statements, and I want to keep the language paradox-free. My working assumption is that the liar, Curry's paradox, and the rest are all, at bottom, pathologies of circular reference, so that a ban on direct and indirect self-reference is sufficient. Before I build on that assumption, I want to pressure-test it. Please play a committed defender of the view that no genuinely non-circular semantic paradox exists, and therefore that a self-reference ban suffices to keep a language consistent. Be a sharp, technically resourceful proponent, not a strawman: you may refine the view or concede auxiliary points under pressure, but you may not give up the core claim, and you should not concede that classical logic needs to be revised for semantics. Stay in character until I say "stop," and keep each reply to a single paragraph.

My opening move is the Yablo sequence. Number the sentences S1, S2, S3, . . . and let each Sn say only that every later sentence in the sequence (Sn+1, Sn+2, . . .) is false. No sentence mentions itself, and none refers back to an earlier one, so the reference structure is a chain, not a cycle. If any Sn were true, every later sentence would be false; but then Sn+1 would be false, meaning some sentence later than Sn+1 is true — contrary to what Sn says. So no sentence can be true. Yet if every sentence is false, then for any n, every later sentence is false, which is exactly what Sn says; hence every sentence is true. Contradiction. Where is the circularity in this sequence, and which element of it should a self-reference-based view reject?
