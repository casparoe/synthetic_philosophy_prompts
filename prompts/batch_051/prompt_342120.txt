I’m a PhD student in moral philosophy and serve as the graduate student member of my university’s behavioral research ethics panel. The panel meets once a month and reviews about fifteen protocols per year, mostly from psychology, management, and communications researchers; the studies often involve deception, hidden recording, or recruitment of participants under financial stress. The other members are not moral theorists and have little patience for theory; each protocol gets roughly twenty minutes of discussion. I want a reusable screening instrument—something I can distribute as a two-page worksheet—that operationalizes Kant’s universalizability tests as a preliminary filter. The aim is not to replace the panel’s overall judgment about the ethics of a study but to catch proposals that should be rejected on the maxim alone, and to identify which proposals need full discussion because the universalizability tests cannot settle them.

Here is the passage I keep returning to. In Kant’s Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (also published as Fundamental Principles of the Metaphysic of Morals), in Thomas Kingsmill Abbott’s translation, Kant writes:

Everyone must admit that if a law is to have moral force, i.e., to be the basis of an obligation, it must carry with it absolute necessity; that, for example, the precept, “Thou shalt not lie,” is not valid for men alone, as if other rational beings had no need to observe it; and so with all the other moral laws properly so called; that, therefore, the basis of obligation must not be sought in the nature of man, or in the circumstances in the world in which he is placed, but a priori simply in the conception of pure reason.

I want the procedure to take that passage seriously: the first test should not depend on the likely consequences of the study, the vulnerability of the specific population, the value of the research, or the researcher’s intentions. It should test only whether the maxim on which the proposal relies can be universalized. At the same time, the procedure must be honest about how much that formal test can deliver.

Please give me a step-by-step decision procedure with the following structure. For each step, specify the exact question to ask, the information one needs to answer it, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with “no,” where it stops with “yes, so far as the universalizability tests are concerned,” and where it hands the decision back to the full panel because the step cannot be applied mechanically. The procedure should include at least these steps: extracting a maxim from the proposal; testing the maxim under the formula of universal law; testing it under the formula of the law of nature, distinguishing between a contradiction in conception and a contradiction in will; and stating clearly which failures correspond to perfect duties and which to imperfect duties in this setting. Where the distinction between a contradiction in conception and a contradiction in will fails to give a clean answer, say so and explain what kind of judgment is being made.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

(1) A study proposes to record conversations in a campus common area without informing those recorded, arguing that informing them would alter the behavior under study.

(2) A study proposes to pay two hundred dollars to unhoused participants for a ninety-minute session on financial decision-making, with the payment described as compensation rather than inducement.

(3) A study proposes to debrief participants but obtain consent for using their data only after the experimental session, on the grounds that pre-consent would reveal the hypothesis.

For each, show the maxim you extract, the result at each step, and what the procedure tells the panel to do next. At least one of the three should be a case where the procedure does not disallow the study even though a reasonable panel member might want to reject it. End by listing what this procedure cannot do—in particular, which parts of research ethics are not reachable by universalizability alone.

I need to be able to give this worksheet to non-philosophers, so avoid Kantian jargon where possible by translating it into plain questions.
