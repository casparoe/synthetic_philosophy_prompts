My philosophy of perception reading group is finally making two of its members write something down instead of arguing past eachother all term. One of them is a modularist: she thinks early vision is encapsulated, so beliefs, desires, and expectations can't literally get into perceptual experience itself—they only affect attention, interpretation, or the judgements we make afterwards. The other is a penetrabilist: he thinks cognition can reach down and alter the experience itself, not just the post-perceptual story we tell about it. They've agreed to produce an adversarial collaboration document, and I want you to draft it for them.

The document should have three parts. First, a numbered list of claims both can sign—not vacuous stuff like the claim that perception exists, but substantive things about the basic data, what counts as evidence here, and what the burden of proof is. Second, a table or list stating their exact disagreements, with each disagreement phrased so the person whose view it is would accept the wording (no strawmanning in either direction). Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that resolving one would actually move at least one of them. For each crux, give a concrete experiment or case that both agree bears on it, and state what each side predicts in advance.

They've both mentioned the memory-colour effect—people report a grey banana-shaped patch as looking slightly yellow—as a possible shared case. If you use it, say what each side predicts about it and where each side thinks the other mishandles the evidence. Also be careful to seperate disagreements that are genuine from ones that are probably just about words, like whether they even mean the same thing by cognitive penetration. And flag any crux where one side thinks the proposed experiment is decisive and the other thinks it's confounded, because that's likely where the actual dispute is hiding.

Use British English throughout. And don't adjudicate—the document should be one both of them could sign, not a verdict on who's right.
