Last night I had a long argument with a friend who is a committed physicalist. When I pressed her on what exactly "physical" means in the claim that everything is physical, I gave her the following dilemma — I have since seen it called Hempel's dilemma — and she kept insisting it was too quick without telling me exactly where it went wrong. I want the argument itself diagnosed, not a survey of the literature.

(1) Physicalism is the thesis that everything is physical. For this thesis to be evaluable at all, "physical" must have a determinate meaning.
(2) There are only two ways to give it a determinate meaning: either it refers to the entities and properties posited by current physics, or it refers to the entities and properties that some future, completed physics would posit.
(3) If "physical" refers to current physics, then physicalism is very likely false, because current physics is incomplete and has repeatedly revised its basic ontology; the thesis would say no more than that everything is among the things present-day physics happens to countenance.
(4) If "physical" refers to a future, completed physics, then physicalism has no content that can be stated now: we cannot specify what the completed physics will include, so the thesis becomes either the trivial claim that everything is whatever the finished science says there is, or a claim too indeterminate to evaluate.
(5) Therefore, physicalism is either likely false or too indeterminate to evaluate.

Is this argument valid? If it is valid, which premises should be resisted, and on what specific reading of "physical"? If it is not valid, which step fails, and is the failure an equivocation, a false dichotomy in step (2), a suppressed premise, or something else? In particular, is there a third way to define "physical" that is neither hostage to current physics nor vacuous, and would that third way leave the argument's conclusion intact?
