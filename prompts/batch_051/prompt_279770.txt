So I've been working through some of Wolfram's stuff on cellular automata, and this passage from A New Kind of Science, Chapter 12 (the "Computational Irreducibility" section) keeps tripping me up:

"In traditional science it has usually been assumed that if one can succeed in finding definite underlying rules for a system then this means that ultimately there will always be a fairly easy way to predict how the system will behave. Several decades ago chaos theory pointed out that to have enough information to make complete predictions one must in general know not only the rules for a system but also its complete initial conditions. But now computational irreducibility leads to a much more fundamental problem with prediction. For it implies that even if in principle one has all the information one needs to work out how some particular system will behave, it can still take an irreducible amount of computational work actually to do this."

My read is: once the underlying rules are known, you can still be stuck having to run the whole evolution to find out what happens — there's no shortcut. But then what is science actually doing when it tries to hand us compact equations and models for fluids, economies, brains? If a system is computationally irreducible, the only "explaination" of its behavior is the system itself running, which feels less like explanation and more like duplication. Does Wolfram's claim basically mean that for a large chunk of the natural world scientific understanding is impossible, and brute-force simulation is the best we'll ever get? Or can you keep irreducible systems and still have something worth calling explanation?
