I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am writing an explainer on the limits of expected-value reasoning. Rather than rehearse the familiar St. Petersburg game for readers, I want to work through a less familiar example and would like your help thinking through its implications.

Consider the following gamble. A fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads. If the first heads occurs on the nth toss, the player receives (−1)^(n−1) · 2^n / n dollars, where a negative amount means the player pays. The probability of first heads on toss n is 1/2^n, so the formal expected value is the sum over n of (1/2^n) · (−1)^(n−1) · 2^n / n, which simplifies to 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ... . This alternating harmonic series converges, but only conditionally: its positive terms alone sum to infinity and its negative terms alone sum to negative infinity. The expected value therefore has the form infinity minus infinity and is undefined under the standard definition.

Please describe the most important implications of this example for how we should reason about choices whose expected values are undefined or only non-absolutely-convergent. In particular, I would like to know: what, if anything, this shows about the completeness of expected utility theory as a guide to choice; whether there is a well-motivated way to assign a determinate value to such a gamble; and what practical lessons, if any, this carries for real-world decisions involving unbounded or poorly behaved payoffs.
