In my philosophy club our teacher left us with a puzzle about how to combine the two things that seem to make an experience matter: how happy it is and how conscious the creature is. She sketched three positions: additive (just add up happiness once consciousness is present), multiplicative (value = consciousness × happiness), and lexical (consciousness comes first, then happiness), but she wouldn't tell us which one she liked. Two seniors, Priya and Jordan, kept arguing after the meeting and I wrote their argument down. Could you go through it move by move and say who is right about what, and who is more right overall? I honestly can't tell.

(1) Priya said: if a barely-conscious fish and a fully-conscious person both feel the same amount of happiness, it can't be right that their experiences are equal in value. The person's experience is more "there," more awake. So value has to be something like consciousness times happiness.

(2) Jordan said: that puts consciousness on the same scale as happiness, but consciousness isn't another feeling you can trade off against a feeling. It's a precondition. No consciousness means no value; once something is conscious, the good or bad is in the happiness itself, not in how much awareness it's wrapped in. So multiplyings is a category mistake.

(3) Priya said: then you owe me a cutoff. If consciousness just has to be present, a one-bit flicker of awareness counts the same as full human awareness, which is arbitrary. And if you say it's graded, then your back to weighting by consciousness, which is multiplication in all but name.

(4) Jordan said: not necessarily. You could make consciousness lexically prior: rank experiences by consciousness first, and only compare happiness when consciousness is tied. That has no magic cutoff and no weird cross-multiplication.

(5) Priya said: lexical is even worse. It makes any gain in consciousness, no matter how tiny, outweigh any loss in happiness, no matter how huge. You'd have to say a microscopic shift in awareness matters more than a whole life of joy versus misery. That's the same fanatical tradeoff you accused multiplication of making, just now it's absolute.

(6) Jordan said: I don't have to take lexical that way. Use broad bands — like unconscious, minimally conscious, fully conscious — as the lexically ordered layer, and inside each band just compare happiness normally. Then tiny consciousness differences don't dominate, but you still don't throw awareness out of the picture.

(7) Priya said: now you've just moved the cutoff problem up a level. Either there's a sharp line between bands, in which case the tiniest difference around that line is infinitely important again, or there isn't, in which case your back to grading, which you called a category mistake.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the seven moves, say whether it's clearly mistaken, mostly wrong, mixed, mostly right, or clearly right, and briefly why; then say what each person is missing; then say who is more right overall. End with a line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase, e.g. "Verdict: Jordan, narrowly" or "Verdict: split."
