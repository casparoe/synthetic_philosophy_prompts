My colleague keeps a written log of her verdicts on deception cases, and I want to predict what she will say about a case we are discussing next week. Her four most recent entries are these. (1) A witness asked whether she saw the defendant leave at ten says "No," though she did see him leave, and the jury believes her; my colleague calls this seriously wrong. (2) A witness asked the same question replies "I was in the records office until quarter past," which is true, but she omits that she did see him leave and knows the jury will take her answer to mean she did not; my colleague calls this seriously wrong, though she has not said how it compares with (1). (3) A speaker says "I saw him at the library," meaning only that she saw him there earlier; the hearer, unexpectedly to her, takes her to mean he is there now and acts badly on that; my colleague says the speaker is not morally at fault. (4) A speaker tells someone a road is open, having checked an hour earlier; a recent flood, which no one anticipated, has since closed it; my colleague says there is no deception and no fault. The new case is this: a landlord asks a rental applicant whether he has ever been evicted. The applicant has been evicted once. He answers "The building was sold and the new owner renovated," which is true, though he knows the landlord will take it as "no," and he could equally well have said "No" outright. My colleague regards the answer as wrong; the only open question is how its magnitude compares with the direct lie in (1). Predict her comparative verdict — the misleading answer is less wrong than, as wrong as, or more wrong than the lie — and state which principle about the wrong of deception each possible verdict would commit her to, whether the wrong lies wholly in the induced false belief, in a broken assurance of truth, or in something about misleading that a lie lacks. Then specify a further case, not a variant of the eviction case, whose verdict would most cleanly settle which principle she holds.
