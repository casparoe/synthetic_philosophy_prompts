My wife recently announced that she is giving up alcohol for twelve months. Her drinking was never heavy or disordered by any ordinary standard, but she had noticed that it had become the fixed endpoint of the day, and she wanted to see what would happen if she removed it. The decision has changed our evenings and made questions about habit, desire, and self-command suddenly practical for me, so the topic is no longer abstract.

Last weekend, two friends got into an argument about her decision over dinner. Both agreed it was a reasonable thing to do in a practical sense. The argument was about what kind of trait — and what kind of achievement — the decision actually represents. I tried to follow it and found I could not referee. I would like you to act as a moral philosopher working on the virtues and the psychology of self-control, and to adjudicate the exchange point by point.

Here is the exchange, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Elena said the decision was a clear exercise of temperance. Self-control is not a matter of how much one consumes but of whether appetite is kept under the authority of judgment, and a bright-line rule is one form that authority can take. A person who can say no categorically, for a self-chosen reason, is exercising exactly the capacity that a temperate person has.

(2) Marcus replied that this has the wrong shape. If temperance is a virtue, it is a disposition about desires themselves, and a virtue lies between excess and deficiency. For a person with no history of addiction, a year of abstinence is not the mean; it is a swing toward the opposite extreme. A self-imposed prohibition can be prudent — like locking up the liquor — but prudently managing a desire is not the same as possessing the virtue that moderates it.

(3) Elena answered that "the mean" need not be a quantity. For this person, in these circumstances, with this particular history of habit, zero drinks may be the appropriate relation to the desire, because the mean is relative to the agent and the situation. She added that the line between prudence and temperance cannot be drawn from outside: a rule that restructures one's environment may be part of acquiring the virtue, not merely a substitute for it.

(4) Marcus countered that if "zero is the mean" is available for someone with no history of addiction, then the concept of the mean has come loose from desire and now simply names whatever a person decides, so "temperance" adds nothing. He also said that if the will is like a muscle, the prohibition removes the exercise rather than practicing it: moderation would require many small acts of resistance, whereas a one-time rule lets her avoid the decision on each occasion.

(5) Elena replied that the muscle picture is the weak step. A bright-line rule is not avoidance, because she still chooses each evening, and she chooses against a standing desire. The rule does not eliminate temptation; it alters it. And what matters in the end is that judgment, rather than appetite, governs the drinking — that is the point of the virtue, however the governance is arranged.

(6) Marcus said this last move quietly changes the subject from a trait of character to an outcome. A scaffold — a promise, a public announcement, a rule — can produce temperate-looking behavior in someone who has not yet acquired the trait, and praising the outcome as "temperance" flatters a strategy. He then raised a third possibility: if the desire is left intact and merely suppressed by the rule, then what is being practiced is not virtue but repression, and repression may surface elsewhere, as irritability, self-righteousness, or the swapping of one compulsion for another.

Neither speaker was able to land a decisive blow. I would like a careful, point-by-point adjudication. Please begin by briefly laying out the distinct senses of "temperance," "prudence," and "repression" that would have to be separated for the dispute to be tractable. Then go through the six moves in order, saying for each what is right and what is overstated or mistaken in it. Finally, state which speaker is overall more correct, and identify the single point on which the whole disagreement most depends.
