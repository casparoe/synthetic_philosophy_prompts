Last month I was appointed secretary to the ethics and compliance committee of Meridian Textiles, a privately held fabric and garment manufacturer. Our committee is currently reviewing the employee handbook, and we have been asked to resolve a live disciplinary matter. Section 6.2 of the handbook, unchanged since it was drafted in 1994, reads:

"Every employee owes the Company an undivided duty of loyalty. All information acquired by an employee in connection with the performance of his or her duties shall be kept strictly confidential and shall not be communicated to any person who is not an employee of the Company, nor used in any manner adverse to the interests of the Company."

Three years ago the Company joined the FairSource Assurance Scheme, a voluntary industry certification under which an independent examiner audits our supply chain and interviews our staff. When the Company joined, senior management sent a written message to all employees stating that the examiners would have access to our records and would speak with employees individually, and instructing employees to answer the examiners' questions truthfully and completely. A quality engineer, Ms. R, was interviewed and was asked directly whether she was aware of any supplier practice that fell below our own published sourcing standards. She answered that one of our principal suppliers subcontracts finishing work to a workshop whose working conditions fall below those standards, though the practice violates no law in the supplier's jurisdiction and does not appear to involve coercion. Ms. R had reported the same concern through the Company's internal channel two months earlier; the report was closed as "not substantiated." Her answer to the examiner was a substantial factor in the loss of our certification, and the Company lost a significant retail contract as a result.

The committee must decide whether Ms. R's statements to the examiner breached Section 6.2 and, if so, whether discipline is warranted. The clause was drafted at a time when the Company had no external auditors, certifiers, or monitors, and no one at the Company had invited employees to speak truthfully to outsiders.

Please act as a professional expert in applied ethics and the interpretation of professional and employment codes. Think carefully about the case. First state the strongest argument that she breached the clause and the strongest argument that she did not. Then work through what each of these approaches would yield: (1) the words of the text, (2) the purpose of the clause, (3) what the 1994 drafters would have said if asked about a company-instructed interview with a voluntary certifier, and (4) what a reasonable employee would have understood the clause to permit. Then say which approach should prevail and what the committee should decide. Mark clearly the points at which the answer requires a value judgment rather than a reading of the clause. Lead with a one-paragraph recommendation and keep the entire response under 750 words.
