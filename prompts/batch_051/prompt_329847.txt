A classmate in my seminar offered the following argument, and I have not been able to find the fault in it. Each step seemed innocent as he wrote it, but the conclusion cannot be right, so something must fail. Here is the argument as he numbered it on the board:

(1) A set is nonempty just in case there is at least one object that belongs to it; that is what nonempty means.

(2) Therefore, if {A_i : i ∈ I} is any family of nonempty sets indexed by a set I, then for each index i there is an object that belongs to A_i. Write that object x_i.

(3) Now define the function f on I by the rule f(i) = x_i. For every i, the value f(i) belongs to A_i.

(4) Hence every family of nonempty sets admits a choice function.

(5) The axiom of choice is just the assertion that every family of nonempty sets admits a choice function.

(6) Therefore the axiom of choice is a theorem of ZF.

(7) But the standard results say that, if ZF is consistent, then ZF proves neither the axiom of choice nor its negation.

(8) So ZF both proves and does not prove the axiom of choice, which is impossible unless ZF is inconsistent.

He said that, to avoid the contradiction, he would sooner give up (7) than give up (1) through (3), since (1) through (3) were merely unpacking what nonempty means.
