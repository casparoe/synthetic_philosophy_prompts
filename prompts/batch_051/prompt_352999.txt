I'm on the human-subjects review panel at a neuroscience institute that keeps getting whole-brain emulation (WBE) proposals — destructive scan-and-run protocols, non-destructive scan-and-copy, Ship-of-Theseus gradual replacement of neurons with engineered substrates. We get maybe six of these a year. The classification feeds into what the consent forms can say and whether we allow the protocol at all, so the stakes are basically life-or-death, and the chair, who signs off on every classification, won't sit through a metaphysics lecture. Half the panel are clinicians and engineers. Every time a WBE protocol lands on our desk we relitigate from scratch whether it's 'same mind continues' or 'original ends, new mind created', and our calls have been inconsistent. I've read the usual blog arguments about whether uploading is death, and they all stop at slogan level. I want a reusable decision procedure we can actually run in a couple of meetings, grounded in whatever the personal-identity-through-uploading literature actually shows, but translated into a checklist and a one-page flowchart — not a seminar.

Here's the landscape I half-understand and want operationalized. There's the psychological-continuity camp, which wants the right kind of causal chain connecting the later mind to the earlier one; there's the pattern-identity camp, which mostly cares whether the information is preserved; and there's the view that you need unbroken spatiotemporal continuity of the same substrate. Turn those into ordered questions: what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure returns a clear 'treat as survival', where it returns a clear 'treat as death-plus-copy', and where it stops and hands the call to the panel because the remaining disagreement is a value choice. The distinctions I want respected are at least: whether the original is destroyed or still around afterward (branching), whether the process keeps continuity in real time or reconstructs the mind from stored data after a gap, and whether the substrate is the same tissue being replaced bit by bit.

Then run the procedure on three protocols we might actually see: (1) a destructive whole-brain scan where the emulation is switched on only after the original brain is gone; (2) a gradual in-place replacement that keeps the subject awake and talking throughout, replacing a few percent of neurons at a time; (3) a non-destructive scan that leaves the original alive and instantiates a copy. Flag the spot where the procedure gives a answer the panel won't like, and be explicit about the cases it can't settle at all except as a judgment call.
