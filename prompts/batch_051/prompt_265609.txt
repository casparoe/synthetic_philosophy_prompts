I am a novelist. My work consists largely in tracking the relation between a character's words and the facts: which falsehoods are lies, which are self-deception, and which belong to the murkier register that Harry Frankfurt called bullshit — speech delivered with a kind of indifference to how things actually stand. Lately I have been struck by an asymmetry in ordinary judgment.

When a student, consultant, or politician speaks fluently on a matter he has not troubled to check, and what he says is false, we treat it as a moral and professional failing: he is a bullshitter, and we hold him responsible for the gap between his confidence and his inquiry. But when a large language model does something superficially similar — producing fluent, plausible, false sentences — our language changes register. We call it hallucination, an error mode, a glitch, a pathology. We do not call the model a bullshitter; many would resist saying it spoke falsely at all, as opposed to merely generating text.

I want to know whether anything justifies this asymmetry. The obvious candidate principles are that the model has no intention, no self to be indifferent, and has made no promise to its audience; the human has all three. But I can also imagine a less flattering explanation: the asymmetry lets institutions deploy the machine while disclaiming responsibility for what it says, which would be a way of bullshitting about the bullshit. I would like the strongest principle you can construct for treating the two cases differently, tested against hard cases — a model's output presented under a hospital's name as medical guidance, and a person who reads aloud text he knows was machine-generated and unverified. If the principle cannot survive those cases, tell me whether the asymmetry should be revised or abandoned, and what a novelist, or anyone, should call the machine's falsehoods instead.
