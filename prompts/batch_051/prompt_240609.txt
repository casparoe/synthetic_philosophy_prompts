I'm a PhD student in comparative psychology, and somethings been bugging me since a lab meeting last week. My advisor spent ten minutes warning us not to overinterpret a clip of one of our octopuses unscrewing a jar: with Morgan's canon in mind, we should prefer the leanest account—maybe the animal is just chaining a reinforced motor routine, not representing the lid and planning the twist. Then, in the same breath, he showed a phone video of his two-year-old dragging a stool across the kitchen to reach a biscuit tin and said, in so many words, that she genuinely understood what she was doing. The contrast stuck with me. A few days later, a friend described her dog as obviously feeling guilty when it slinks under the table, and then dismissed the idea that the rats in my lab might be remembering a hidden platform during a delay period. The dog knows, the rat is just conditioned. The steady pattern seems to be that rich mental-state talk is the default for human children and often for pets, while lean, deflationary talk is the default for lab animals, octopuses, and anything without a face we can easily read. I know there are easy things to point to—language, species membership, shared neural architecture, the fact that I was once a toddler but never an octopus. But I want the strongest principle that tracks a difference relevant to the attribution itself, not just a list of differences that happen to be there. So here is the question: is there a principle that justifies richly interpreting a preverbal child while applying something like Morgan's canon to an animal doing the very same task? Test it against a case that's awkward for whatever principle you give—an adult human with severe aphasia who can only gesture, or a dog that has lived in a human family for a decade. Give me the strongest case for the asymmetry and an honest verdict on whether we should keep it, reverse it, or mostly drop it.
