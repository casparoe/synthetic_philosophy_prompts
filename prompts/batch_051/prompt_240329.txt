I have a rule about my own future choices which has become a point of contention at home: I refuse to assign a probability to any decision I am still making. Last month, whilst I was in the common room, a friend asked how likely I was to attend her party that weekend. I was genuinely weighing it up, and I told her I did not think I could give a probability from within the deliberation, because any number I named would either reveal that I had already decided or start pushing me towards whichever option the number made look settled. Once I had made up my mind I was happy to say I was ninety per cent sure I would go. My brother calls this special pleading. He points out that I regularly predict my own behaviour in advance: I know, for example, that I will probably leave my revision until the last two days, and I can say so without its depriving me of a choice. He adds that other people can often predict me from the outside, and that my refusal amounts to declining to use evidence I plainly have. Why, he asks, should the mere fact that I happen to be deliberating at that moment make a probability suddenly incoherent? I reply that those predictions concern standing habits of mine, whereas in the party case I was actively deciding, and treating my choice as predictable from the outside seems to undermine the very activity I was engaged in. I want to know whether this policy is genuinely defensible or simply a flattering way of protecting the feeling that my decisions are open. Could you distinguish the cases in which assigning probabilities to my own choices is unproblematic from those in which it conflicts with deliberation, and say whether my rule, perhaps in some revised form, is reasonable? Please also say how confident you are in the answer you give and single out the point you are least sure of.
