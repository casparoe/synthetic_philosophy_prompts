I want to open the discounting chapter with the criteria rather than the usual tour of positions, so: what does an adequate normative theory of discounting the future have to do? I mean discounting as a claim about how we ought to weight later benefits and costs, not a description of how people actually choose. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it: dynamic consistency, stationarity, neutrality across generations, handling infinite or indefinitely long streams without the present value blowing up, keeping utility-discounting and consumption-discounting seperate, and whatever else you think is load-bearing. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preferences (zero pure time preference, exponential form, that sort of thing). The part I actually want is whether you think any of these are jointly unsatisfiable — I keep suspecting the neutrality-plus-finite-value pair does most of the work, and that says something about the field. Also, which single requirement rules out the most otherwise tempting candidates, like hyperbolic discounting? I'll save the comparison of existing theories for later; here I only want the standard itself.
