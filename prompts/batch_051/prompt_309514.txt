I am designing a scoring-rubric exercise for an interdisciplinary seminar on biological individuality, and I want it to force participants to compare hard cases rather than argue about definitions in the abstract. Construct the rubric in four steps.

1. Start with the nine candidate criteria below and assign each a weight from 0 to 1, with the weights summing to 1. The weight should reflect how central the criterion is to deciding whether something is one biological individual, not how easy the criterion is to measure.

2. Use your weighted criteria to assign each of the eight test cases a single organismality score from 0 to 100. Give enough reasoning for each score that I can reconstruct why it landed where it did.

3. Then answer three diagnostic questions. (a) Which one criterion is doing the largest share of the work across your scores, and is that a feature of the rubric or a problem? (b) Which two cases does the rubric handle most awkwardly, and what exactly makes them awkward? (c) At what score would you call a case an organism, or, if every cutoff is arbitrary, defend that conclusion.

4. Close with one paragraph on what your results imply for the question of whether organismality is a sharp kind or a graded cluster across normal organisms, holobionts, colonial forms, and superorganisms such as a eusocial ant colony.

Candidate criteria: genetic homogeneity — the parts share a genome; physiological integration — shared boundaries and coordinated regulation; a single-cell developmental bottleneck — the whole develops from a single cell; germline sequestration — a reproductive lineage is set aside, so body parts do not compete to reproduce; immune self/nonself discrimination — a maintained boundary between self and nonself; evolutionary individuality — the parts share a fitness fate and act as one unit of selection; metabolic autonomy — the whole governs its own energy and material flows; anatomical contiguity — the parts form one connected body; irreversible functional specialization — the parts are specialized and lose independent viability.

Test cases: an adult human, considered on its own; the same adult human plus its full resident microbiota; a eusocial ant colony (reproductive and worker castes with shared brood care); a Portuguese man o' war (a floating colony of specialized zooids); a lichen (a fungus joined with an alga or cyanobacterium); a quaking aspen clone (many stems connected by a shared root system); a coral holobiont (coral animal plus its photosynthetic symbionts and other microbes); a transient bison herd.
