I am a retired physical chemist who has recently begun to work through some philosophical texts. I have just read John Maynard Keynes’s essay “Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren” (1930). Early in the essay Keynes writes:

For I predict that both of the two opposed errors of pessimism which now make so much noise in the world will be proved wrong in our own time – the pessimism of the revolutionaries who think that things are so bad that nothing can save us but violent change, and the pessimism of the reactionaries who consider the balance of our economic and social life so precarious that we must risk no experiments.

The essay later develops a view about the economic problem, technological unemployment, and what human beings will do once the problem of scarcity is solved. My question is not about the accuracy of Keynes’s economic forecast. I want to know the most important implications of the view of the good life that Keynes develops in the later part of the essay, when that view is read in the light of the passage above. In particular, I keep finding myself in arguments between two groups of scientifically literate friends: one holds that economic growth is still our best instrument for reducing poverty and should be pursued as a priority; the other holds that the pursuit of growth has become a collective compulsion and that we should deliberately slow it down. What are the most important implications of Keynes’s account for how I should think about that disagreement? Please do not turn this into a summary of the essay or a set of reading recommendations; I want the conceptual implications drawn out.
