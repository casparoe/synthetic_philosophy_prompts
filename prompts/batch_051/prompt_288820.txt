I'm putting together a two-page proposal for a small research grant in philosophy of mathematics. The question I keep circling is whether the basic axioms of math might be inconsistent, but I want to pitch it as something more than the standard yes-or-no question about whether PA is consistent.

What's been bugging me: people talk as if there is a single question — are the axioms consistent? — with a single answer, and then they split into a skeptical camp and a defensive camp. The skeptics say we can't prove consistency from below, so our confidence is just induction on the fact that no contradiction has shown up yet. The defenders say we have relative consistency proofs and Gentzen-style results, or that the whole worry is unthinkable because if arithmetic were inconsistent we couldn't trust anything. But it feels like three different questions got stuck together: whether the axioms are actually consistent, what kinds of evidence could bear on that, and what a bounded agent should do if a contradiction actually showed up in a system it has been using. The third one isn't settled by the first. A proof of 0=1 in PA wouldn't by itself tell you which ordinary arithmetical beliefs to throw out or keep.

My angle is to stop asking whether the axioms are consistent and instead ask what a rational agent should do on discovering an inconsistency in a system it relies on. The options aren't just keep everything versus throw everything away: you could quarantine the offending axiom scheme, restrict induction, switch to paraconsistent logic, keep using the system instrumentally, and so on. Working through that would force a distinction between trusting a system because it's true and trusting it because its useful, and I suspect the consistency debate collapses once you draw that distinction.

I need a precise statement of the question, why the existing positions leave it open, and in particular why the two standard moves — pointing at a consistency proof, and pointing out that anything follows from a contradiction — are both unsatisfying as final answers. Also say why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives, like doing straight proof theory or a historical study of Hilbert's program, and give a month-by-month method, what would count as the project failing, and a realistic scope for one year. No bibliography; I'll add citations. Try to make it sound like something a rationalist-adjacent committee would find exciting rather than obvious.
