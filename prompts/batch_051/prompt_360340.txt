You're a political philosopher who works on theories of liberty, and I'd like you to think carefully about a modified version of Nozick's experience machine. Start by brainstorming a few first impressions, then work toward a precise answer, and dont worry about hedging too much at the end.

In the original thought experiment, you can plug into a machine that gives you any experiences you like while your body floats in a tank; the experiences feel completely real, you can't tell you're plugged in, and you can pre-select your life. Nozick thought most people would still refuse, because they want to actually do things, to be a certain kind of person, and to be in touch with a deeper reality.

Here's the tweak. Suppose the machine is not a private illusion but a shared, ongoing simulation — call it the Commons — inhabited by thousands of real people who have all chosen to plug in. Inside, your choices have real effects on other residents and theirs on you. Everyone knows they're plugged in; nobody is being deceived on that score. You may unplug whenever you like and return to ordinary life, but leaving is permanent: once you're out, you can never go back or communicate with anyone inside. The Commons is run by a private firm whose terms of service give it complete discretion to alter the simulation's rules — its economy, its physics, even the political arrangements the residents have built — at any time and without notice. In practise it never has exercised this power, and the residents have gradually come to think of the Commons as home.

Now take the three main families of liberty in turn: negative liberty (freedom as non-interference), positive liberty (self-mastery, being the author of you're own life), and republican liberty (freedom as non-domination, i.e. not living under another's arbitrary power). Is a resident of the Commons free, on each view? Does swapping the private simulation for a shared one move the three theories in different directions? In particular, does the republican view have something distinctive to say about the operator's dormant discretionary power, or does that objection just reduce to the negative-liberty worry about the mere capacity to interfere? Finally, say which tradition you think handles the case best, and whether the case exposes a weakness in one of them.
