I keep running into the same image in a reading group on scientific classification, and I would like it dismantled rather than merely praised or dismissed. The image is that the sciences carve nature at its joints: a successful taxonomy is supposed to divide the world the way a skilled butcher divides a carcass, following seams that were already in the animal rather than cutting to suit what the household happens to want for dinner. In our group this is doing real argumentative work. It is used to brush aside the thought that our categories are imposed by us, to support the claim that some classifications are simply wrong and others simply right, and to underwrite the conclusion that at least a privileged core of scientific kinds — chemical elements, perhaps, or biological species — exists quite independently of human interests.

I want an audit of how far this analogy can actually carry that conclusion. First, give me the correspondence in full: what plays the role of the carcass, the joints, the knife, the butcher's skill, and the customer's preferred cuts? Then catalogue the main disanalogies, and distinguish sharply between the ones that are merely superficial and the ones that infect the intended use. Here are the sorts of places I suspect the image slips: a carcass has a settled anatomy before any butcher arrives, whereas the boundaries between candidate kinds are often vague, graded or scale-dependent; different sciences cross-cut one another rather than yielding one canonical partition; and a butcher's purposes do not help determine where the joints lie, while scientific purposes plausibly do help determine where we draw lines. The image also quietly imports a single body, a pre-existing discrete structure, and a uniquely correct carving. Tell me which of these imports are illicit, and whether the realist conclusion survives once they are named. If it does not survive, say what a better analogy would be, or whether the conclusion would actually be better defended with no carving metaphor at all. Please write the response in British English.
