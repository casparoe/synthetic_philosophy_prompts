Timothy Williamson's Knowledge and Its Limits contains two commitments that are often thought to sit uneasily together. Early in the book he argues that knowing is a state of mind: knowledge, on his view, is a factive attitude that figures in the explanation of action and that cannot be decomposed into belief plus truth plus some independent anti-luck condition. Later in the same book he argues that no non-trivial condition is luminous, where a condition is luminous just in case whenever it obtains the subject is in a position to know that it obtains. His running example is the condition of feeling cold, which he treats as admitting borderline cases that defeat any such guarantee. In the chapters that follow, he uses considerations about margins for error to argue that the KK principle fails: from the fact that you know that p, it does not follow that you are in a position to know that you know that p.

These two commitments seem to pull in opposite directions. If knowing is a mental state, one might think it should be the sort of thing a subject can know about from the inside, much as we ordinarily take ourselves to know whether we feel cold; but the anti-luminosity argument denies exactly that kind of first-person access, and for the very case that is supposed to make mental self-knowledge secure. Are the two commitments genuinely in tension, or is the appearance of conflict generated by an assumption Williamson is entitled to reject? Lay out the strongest candidate reconciliations, distinguishing carefully between the claim that knowledge is a mental state and the claim that mental states are luminous or self-intimating. Then say which reconciliation is most faithful to the details of Williamson's arguments, and which amount to special pleading or to silently weakening one of the two commitments.
