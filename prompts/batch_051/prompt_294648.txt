I maintain a mid-sized open-source library with about a dozen regular contributors and a long tail of one-off pull requests. We already keep separate license notices for legal compliance; what I need is a procedure for the discretionary, human-facing CREDITS file and release acknowledgments. The recurring argument is over who deserves to be named. Some maintainers say every merged pull request gets a line. Others say only sustained contributors count. Others say the test should be whether the person’s code is still present in the current codebase. We release every six to eight weeks, the CREDITS file is public, and contributors occasionally treat it as a professional signal, so the stakes are high enough that the procedure has to be defensible to an angry contributor.

I have no formal philosophy background, but I recently read a paraphrase of Seneca’s De Beneficiis and found one distinction useful. As I understand it, he argues that a benefit is not the same as the material thing handed over: the same object can be given with different intentions, and what makes a gift a benefit is more closely tied to the giver’s will and to the relationship it creates than to the size or usefulness of the object itself. The specific passage that stuck with me claims, as I remember it, that a benefit should be measured by the will behind it, so that a small favor given freely may count for more than a large transfer made carelessly or for show. I am probably getting the details wrong, and I do not want the procedure to depend on exact textual questions, but I do want to test whether that basic idea can be operationalized.

Please write a decision procedure my co-maintainers and I could actually run before each release. It should be a checklist with questions asked in order, an account of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a definite no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is about intent rather than the observable record. The procedure should take a non-philosopher under fifteen minutes per candidate. It should not collapse into a license attribution check, since those are handled elsewhere.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a first-time contributor who fixes a single typo in the documentation and clearly intended to help; a developer who contributes a large feature that we rewrite so heavily that only the core idea survives, and who later says publicly that they did it mainly to strengthen their résumé; and a former maintainer who has not touched the project in four years but whose early architectural choices, made when the project was tiny, still constrain most current work by a mixture of foresight and luck. Tell me which result the procedure gives that I will dislike, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot do.
