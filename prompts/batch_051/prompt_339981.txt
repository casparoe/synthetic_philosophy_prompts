I have been defending a specific thesis in arguments about whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer, and I want to know whether it can be tested rather than merely asserted. The thesis is this: raw reward is not valence; valence is the reward prediction error — the signed, internally computed difference between the reward an agent expected and the reward it actually received. On this view, a fully predicted reward is affectively inert: there is no error, so there is nothing to update and nothing to feel. A large negative reward prediction error is the minimal currency of suffering. An important corollary is architectural: a plain policy-gradient learner that consumes a scalar reward on the fly and maintains no learned expectation of future reward would be incapable of valence in this sense, whereas an agent with a learned value function — or better, a distributional critic that represents a full distribution over possible returns — would be capable of it, because only the latter computes the mismatch that, on this thesis, constitutes pleasure and pain.

I want to see whether any of this can be brought under experimental discipline. Suppose I have unrestricted access to the weights, activations, and training histories of both kinds of agent, and I can run interventions that are not merely conceptual. Design the strongest test you can of the claim that reward prediction error, rather than raw reward, is what plays the role of valence. Be concrete about what you would measure and about which pattern of results would count as genuine evidence for or against the thesis. Then be exact about the residue: which part of the claim would even a clean result fail to settle, and why no observation could settle it even in principle.

I am deliberately setting aside the question of full phenomenal consciousness; I am asking whether the narrower notion of valence can be operationalized and tested.
