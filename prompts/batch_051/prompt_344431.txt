I am a PhD student in political philosophy working on parental authority and the limits of the liberal state, and I want to test whether an asymmetry I notice in ordinary moral judgment can be defended. Consider two parents. One refuses a medically standard, life-saving treatment for a young child; we ordinarily treat this as a serious wrongful failure, and we accept that the state may intervene coercively, with expert medical judgment carrying decisive weight about what the child needs. Another parent withdraws a child from school to educate her at home inside a comprehensively closed ideological system: science and history are presented selectively, rival views are taught only as error, and—let us stipulate—the child really does reach adulthood with little capacity to assess the worldview she was raised in. We ordinarily treat this as a legitimate exercise of parental discretion, and the state either does not inspect the child's epistemic environment or does so with far less deference to expert educational judgment than it shows to medical judgment in the first case. Both are exercises of parental authority over a child's development; both shape the future adult's capacities for autonomous thought and choice; both are hard to reverse after childhood. Yet we treat the second far more deferentially than the first. I would like the strongest principled justification for treating bodily development and epistemic development asymmetrically in this way. Formulate the best candidate principle, test it against nearby cases—for instance, a parent who denies a child care for a treatable broken bone, and a parent who refuses to teach a child to read or to let her encounter any rival view—and then give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or revised.
