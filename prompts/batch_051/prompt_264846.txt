Please explain a conceptual issue. People often treat doubt as a kind of intellectual or even moral duty, as if you're obligated to keep questioning your own politics, your religion, even plain common sense. But there's a snag: you can't decide to believe or doubt at will, any more than you can decide to believe your plane won't crash. So in what sense, if any, could doubt be a duty? I'd like you to separate at least two readings — a duty to have the occurent feeling of uncertainty, versus a duty to maintain certain inquiry practices like actively chasing counterevidence, checking how often your past settled judgments turned out wrong, and periodically re-running the argument — and say which reading survives the ought-implies-can objection. Also, does the duty to doubt get applied to itself, and does that produce a regress or self-defeat? Keep it conceptual, no citations.
