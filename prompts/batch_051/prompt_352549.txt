I've been reading around the social-construction literature — race, gender, that sort of thing — and a friend who works at a statistics bureau described a proposal for their next census. I want to know how you'd analyze it as a mechanism, not whether you'd endorse it as policy.

The proposal: drop the fixed race and ethnicity boxes. Every adult can write in any labels they take themselves to belong to. A label becomes a 'recognized kind' only if it clears two independent thresholds: (i) at least half a percent of the adult population applies it to themselves, and (ii) in a separate survey, adults who don't self-apply the label are shown anonymized profiles of people who do; at least half a percent of those adults must also apply the label back to the profiled person. Recognized kinds are then the only categories used for anti-discrimination monitoring, school funding formulas, health research grants, and districting. If a kind misses either threshold in two censuses running, it falls off the list. The rationale is that a kind only counts as real if it's recognized from both the inside and the outside.

What I'm curious about is how the thing would actually behave. What would it incentivize among advocacy groups, pollsters, employers, and ordinary respondents? Which new kinds would get manufactured and which existing ones would die off? Do the two thresholds pull in opposite directions? And what are the worst failure modes, and can they be fixed without abandonning the joint-recognition idea? Lead with your bottom line and keep the answer under 600 words.
