For the past year I have been defending a broadly sentientist, consequentialist view on which the badness of suffering does not depend on who undergoes it or on how it came about. Pain matters morally whether it occurs in a hospital bed, a factory farm, or a salt marsh; once we grant that it matters, the fact that most wild-animal suffering is natural, unobserved, and caused by biological processes rather than by any agent's choice begins to look morally inert. I am also unpersuaded that the doing/allowing distinction and the intending/foreseeing distinction carry anything like the weight they would need to carry to place human-caused harms and nature-caused harms in different moral categories. And yet I find myself with a firm intuition that a healthy ecosystem — full of predation, parasitic wasp larvae eating caterpillars from the inside, disease, starvation, and freezing — is not a moral catastrophe; that the right response to a crocodile drowning a wildebeest is not the same as the right response to seeing a person suffer in a comparable way; and that a project of systematically intervening in wild ecosystems to lower aggregate suffering, whether through sterilization, genetic modification, or managed habitats, would be a loss of something valuable rather than a moral triumph. I do not mean only that such interventions would backfire practically. I mean that the wildness itself seems to me to have a kind of value that welfare accounting does not capture, or at least a claim against our interference.

This clash leaves me with three live options. The theory may be overreaching, and may need to be limited by something like a principle against imposing our will on the spontaneous order of nature, or by an account of which suffering is ours to relieve. The intuition may be a cognitive artifact: status quo bias, scope insensitivity to astronomical numbers of small harms, an evolved reluctance to take on godlike control, or a romantic picture of nature that does not survive contact with the actual lives of wild animals. Or the two may be compatible, because the theoretical claim about the badness of suffering may not straightforwardly entail an interventionist conclusion — whether because of other values, because of the limits of foresight, or because our moral relation to wild animals differs from our relation to those whose lives we have already taken responsibility for. I want you to argue each side at its strongest, and then tell me which of the three resolutions you find most defensible. For each resolution, specify what would have to be true for it to be the one I should accept, and identify the weakest move in the path that leads there. Do not just tell me to bite the bullet or to trust the intuition. Please write the response in American English.
