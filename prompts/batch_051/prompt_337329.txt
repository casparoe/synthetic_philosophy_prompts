I'm giving a seminar talk next term to an interdisciplinary working group on games and ethics, and I need a Q&A prep sheet. The thesis I'm defending is that the most popular war shooters do not glorify war primarily through what they depict—uniforms, gore, licensed hardware, set-piece battles—but through what their systems ask the player to do and enjoy. Their mechanics turn war into a bounded tactical puzzle with legible states, clean feedback, and skill-based mastery, and the genre's characteristic pleasures are pleasure taken in that false legibility. On my account, the fun is not a harmless wrapper around neutral content; the fun is itself the misrepresentation, and the ethical question is whether and when it is a bad kind of delight to rehearse, with absorbed attention, a model of war as a solvable optimization problem. The talk does not claim that these games cause violence, that players confuse game and war, or that enjoyment itself is suspect. The argument's core moves are, first, that depictive content cannot by itself distinguish a sincere treatment of war from a glorifying one; second, that the genre's shared systems select for a specific model of war as a legible problem-space; and third, that the mastery-pleasure is not merely associated with that model but is pleased with it.

The room is not a media-effects audience. No one there will ask for proof that games make people violent, and any whiff of moral panic loses them. The group is mostly game studies scholars and working designers who already accept some version of the view that rules, interfaces, and mechanics carry meaning rather than only the story layered on top. They are suspicious of content-criticism—cataloguing gore or bad ideology in cutscenes—and they dislike earnest serious games that fail as games. A few are skeptical that you can attribute a claim or a misrepresentation to a system at all, rather than only to the framing the player brings to it. One or two military historians in the room will push back on any account that treats real war as uniformly chaotic, since soldiers themselves often describe operations in the language of targets, objectives, and manageable local problems. Almost everyone in the room has played these games at length.

Please write the prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this exact room, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way this audience would actually put them at the microphone, each with a two-minute reply, what to concede and what to hold, and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I should be most afraid someone will ask, and explain why it would do the most damage. The prep sheet is for me to study, not to read aloud.
