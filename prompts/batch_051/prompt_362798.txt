I keep seeing the same shape in two places and I can't tell whether I've actually found something or just pattern-matched on the word 'embedding.' In metaethics, the expressivist says a sentence like 'lying is wrong' doesn't descibe the world or have truth conditions; it expresses a non-cognitive attitude, roughly disapproval of lying. The Frege–Geach problem is that this breaks as soon as you embed the sentence in a conditional: consider 'if shoplifting is wrong, then encouraging your friend to shoplift is wrong.' Someone can accept that whole conditional without disapproving of shoplifting at all, so the antecedent doesn't express the attitude the expressivist assigned to it, the meaning of 'wrong' looks like it shifts between embedded and unembedded positions, and modus ponens stops going through. Blackburn's fix is to say the conditional expresses a higher-order attitude — approval of a pair of attitudes — and that validity is a constraint on which combinations of attitudes it's coherent to hold.

On the side I know better: in Haskell you can't stick an effectful expression where a pure value is expected, because `putStrLn "hi"` has type `IO ()`, not `()`, and a conditional that wants a plain `Bool` refuses to take it. The machinery that lets effectful things compose is bind, `m a -> (a -> m b) -> m b`, plus unit `a -> m a`. The compound is again `m b`, never `b`, and the monad laws force the sequencing to behave. That looks to me like exactly what Frege–Geach says expressivists can't supply: an embedding operation that says what the semantic value of a complex normative sentence is, given the values of its parts, one that keeps the compound the same kind of semantic thing (an attitude), plus coherence laws that guarantee modus ponens stays valid.

So my question: is Blackburn-style expressivism best understood as an under-specified monad for moral language — attitudes as the type constructor, the underlying value being the thing the attitude is about (the act, say), higher-order attitudes as bind — with Frege–Geach just the complaint that nobody wrote down the bind and the monad laws? If that reading is right, what do associativity and the identity laws amount to as claims about human attitudes, and do real attitudes actually satisfy them? If it's wrong, where does the analogy give out? My suspicion is that it dies at unit: in code there's a pure injection from any value into the effectful type, but on the attitude side there's no way to get a disapproval out of a bare fact without already having an attitude, and fixing that seems to sneak beliefs back in and stop being expressivism. Though maybe 'believing that p' is exactly the unit and I'm wrong. This smells like it could be a real structure rather than a loose analogy, but it also smells like the usual thing where anything with a type constructor gets called a monad — which is it?
