A friend of mine does freelance interactive-fiction consulting—he designs branching narrative structures for game studios. He wrapped up a gig last fall for a mid-size studio, and the contract he signed had a clause I keep turning over in my head. It reads:

"All ideas, concepts, methods, techniques, processes, and works of authorship, whether or not patentable or copyrightable, that Consultant conceives, develops, or first reduces to practice during the term of this Agreement and arising from or relating to the Services shall be the sole and exclusive property of Client."

During the project he worked out a general way of plotting tragic endings so the player's earlier choices stay meaningful. He never wrote the method down for the client and it wasn't in any deliverable, but he kept a private notebook during the engagement where he sketched the idea out for himself. After the contract ended he used the method to write his own interactive novella, and he now teaches it in a paid weekend workshop. The studio find out about the workshop and is threatening to enforce the clause. Their argument is that the method is a "concept, method, technique, or process" he "conceived or developed during the term," and that the notebook is a "work of authorship" they own because he first reduced the idea to practice while working for them. He thinks that's obviously absurd—you can't own the inside of someone's head, and the clause was basically boilerplate from the early 90s, drafted by people who probably never imagined a freelancer walking off with a method in his skull and selling it as craft.

I'm trying to figure out what the clause actually gives them. Can you walk me through how the plain wording, the purpose of the clause, what the original drafters would have said if someone had asked them about this exact case, and what a reasonable person signing the contract would have understood—would each of those readings reach the method itself, the notebook, the novella, or the workshop? And if the readings pull apart, which one should govern? Does the fact that he carried the method away in his head rather than as a file or document change how we should think about it, or is that distinction doing more work than it should?
