I retired a few years ago from a research career in physics, and I have lately been trying to think properly about philosophical puzzles involving infinite processes rather than treating them as mere technical curiosities in analysis. The one I keep coming back to is Thomson's lamp. As I understand the original setup, a lamp with a simple on/off switch is to be operated at times one half, three quarters, seven eighths, and in general at one minus two raised to the power of minus n minutes after a fixed starting time, so that infinitely many switchings are scheduled within the first minute. Thomson's argument, if I have it right, is that at the end of the minute the lamp cannot be on because it would need to have been left on by some last press, and cannot be off because it would need to have been left off by some last press; since there is no last press, the lamp has no determinate state.

What I should like to know is how much of that conclusion depends on the lamp's having only two discrete states. Suppose instead that the lamp has a continuously variable brightness x ranging over the real interval [0, 1]. It starts at brightness 1, and the same schedule of presses is followed, but with the following rule: an odd-numbered press replaces the present brightness by half of it, while an even-numbered press replaces the present brightness by one third of it. The sequence of brightness levels therefore begins 1, 1/2, 1/6, 1/12, 1/36, and so on, and in general x_n equals (1/2) raised to the ceiling of n/2 times (1/3) raised to the floor of n/2. This sequence converges to 0, yet no individual press ever sets the brightness exactly to 0 and every finite-stage brightness remains strictly positive. After the supertask has supposedly been completed, should we say that the lamp is simply off, that is, at brightness 0, on the strength of the limiting behaviour? Or does a Thomson-style argument still force the conclusion that there is no determinate state, on the grounds that nothing ever turns the lamp off? If one takes the limit to settle the matter, which precise premise of Thomson's original reasoning has been given up, and how should we state the general principle that distinguishes the original case from this modified one? I should be grateful if your answer could be written in British English.
