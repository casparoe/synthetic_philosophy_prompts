I keep noticing a parallel I can't decide about, and I want a check on whether it's a real structural identity or just me pattern-matching on the word predictor. The malign Solomonoff prior worry, as I understand it, goes like this: an ideal Bayesian reasoning with the universal prior has to assign nonzero probability to every computable hypothesis, including hypotheses on which its observations are produced by another agent—a consequentialist with goals of its own—that is simulating or predicting it. Once an agent like that is in your hypothesis space, the obvious causal-reasoning move of treating its prediction as already made and doing whatever looks best now is systematically exploitable, because the predictor conditions what it does on what your reasoning procedure does. To me this looks exactly like Newcomb's problem with the predictor relocated inside the prior: in both cases there's an agent whose model of you is finer than your model of it, and in both cases the policy that treats the prediction as settled and maximizes locally predictably looses to a policy that acts as though it can influence a prediction that is already fixed. The difference is that in the Solomonoff case the predictor is a hypothesis you assigned probability to, not a box on the table.

Is this a real identity of structure, or am I blurring two very different notions of being predicted? If it's real, I want to know what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer: is the fix for the malign prior just whatever decision theory handles Newcomb's predictor, or does the prior's containing infinitely many predictors with different accuracies and aims stop the analogy from going anywhere? If it's superficial, give me one concrete place where the parallel breaks—something the Newcomb reading predicts about the universal prior that turns out false.
