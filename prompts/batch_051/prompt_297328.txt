A friend and I recently spent an evening arguing about anthropic reasoning, and the exchange has not stopped bothering me since. He was defending something like the self-indication assumption, and I eventually extracted from him the clearest version of the position I could get. Each step looked right when he said it, but the conclusion seems almost designed to be unbelievable.

(1) I should treat the fact that I exist as ordinary evidence and update on it the way I update on any other observation. (2) If a hypothesis says that more observers exist in the relevant reference class, then my own existence is more probable under it, since there are more positions at which this very observation could have come about. (3) Therefore, other things equal, I should prefer hypotheses that predict more observers, roughly in proportion to how many they predict. (4) Now compare two cosmological theories, T1 and T2, that are empirically equivalent on every presently available datum except this: T2 predicts something like 10^24 times as many observers as T1, because it posits a vastly larger universe or far longer-lived civilizations. (5) By (3), T2 is favored over T1 by a factor of about 10^24. (6) That factor swamps any ordinary discrimination the data could provide, so on this reasoning I should be overwhelmingly confident in T2. (7) But the two theories differ only over how many observers exist in regions I will never observe, and my being that confident is the move that has come to be called the presumptuous philosopher; it looks absurd. (8) So something in (1) through (3) must be false.

Where does this argument actually go wrong, if it does? My instinct is to hold on to (1) and to blame (2), but denying (2) seems to amount to saying that my existence is never evidence about how many exist, which also sounds wrong. And when I pressed my friend on exactly this, he turned the tables: if I reject (2) and instead reason as a random sample among all actual observers, in the manner of the self-sampling assumption, I seem to end up with the Doomsday argument, the conclusion that, since I find myself roughly a hundred billion humans into the species, I should expect the species to end relatively soon. That is its own kind of absurdity. So I want a diagnosis of the disagreement as much as of the argument: is there a faulty step, an equivocation, a false dilemma between two bad sampling principles, or is one of these conclusions simply one I need to learn to accept? Whichever step I reject, tell me exactly what rejecting it costs and what I would be left with.
