Moore says in the preface to Principia Ethica:

"It appears to me that in Ethics, as in all other philosophical studies, the difficulties and disagreements, of which its history is full, are mainly due to a very simple cause: namely to the attempt to answer questions, without first discovering precisely what question it is which you desire to answer."

He thinks mixing up questions is the main source of trouble, so I tried to be careful and sort out the questions in this argument. And now I'm worried it either proves the is-ought gap is fake, or I'm making exactly the mistake he warns about. Here's my argument:

(1) People in fact tend to approve of the actions that would help them survive and reproduce.
(2) Saying "that action is right" is just a way of reporting that the action is the kind that gets this sort of approval.
(3) So actions that help you survive and reproduce are right.
(4) If an action is right, you ought to do it.
(5) So you ought to do whatever helps you survive and reproduce.

That conclusion is an ought, and every premise sure looks like an ordinary factual claim. So either the is-ought gap is bogus, or the argument is falling apart somewhere. But I can't tell where — (2) seems like the obvious suspect, but if (2) is just a definition of the word "right," shouldn't it be harmless? And if the problem is (4), isn't that just what the word "right" means anyway? Tell me exactly which step, if any, fails, and on what reading of it. Keep the answer under 500 words.
