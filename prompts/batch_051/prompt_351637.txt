I got into a long argument after a lecture about Nozick's experience machine, and I want to see whether a tweak I've been thinking about actually matters. In the standard version, the machine gives you whatever subjective experiences you choose, but nothing you seem to do really happens and nobody you meet is real. The usual reasons for not plugging in are roughly that we want to actually do things rather than merely have the experience of doing them, we want to actually be a certain sort of person, and we want some contact with a reality beyond our own mental states. So far so familiar. But suppose the machine is upgraded so it doesn't run canned hallucinations. Instead it runs a fine-grained simulation of a world with its own physics that no one controls, and the other people in it are genuinely conscious and have their own projects. Inside the simulation you really can improve at something through practice, fail from your own laziness, keep a promise, or hurt someone, because your simulated actions are real causal events in that world, with real consequences for other conscious beings. The only thing you give up is contact with the particular physical world where your body floats in the tank. My question is what this does to the original argument. Does refusing to plug in still make sense, or does Nozick's argument lose most of its force once the experiences are no longer illusions in the relevant sense? Work through which of the three reasons survives the variation and which doesn't, and say whether hedonism, desire-satisfaction theory, and objective-list theory come apart on this version in a different way than they do on the original.
