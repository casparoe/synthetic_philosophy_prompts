I have been testing my own judgments about which of the following sentences a person can utter without sounding absurd or being irrational, and I cannot find a clean principle behind them. Here are the cases as I see them.

(1) “It’s raining, but I don’t believe it is.” A person who says this sounds badly wrong — not logically inconsistent, since the rain and the absence of belief could jointly obtain, but as if the sentence undermines itself in the saying.

(2) “It’s raining, but she doesn’t believe it is.” Perfectly fine. It reports that someone else is out of the loop.

(3) “It was raining, but I didn’t believe it was.” Also fine. This sounds like an honest correction of a past mistake.

(4) “I believe it’s raining, but it isn’t.” I want to call this fine — someone owning up to a currently false belief — but I cannot shake the feeling that it is (1) reversed, and that it may be just as bad.

(5) “It’s raining, but I don’t know that it is.” Not fine; it feels almost exactly like (1), perhaps a shade less bad.

(6) “It’s raining, but I’m not sure it is.” This also seems not fine, though I am less confident than with (5), since “sure” feels less stringent than “know.”

(7) “It might rain, but I don’t believe it will.” Fine. One can allow that something is possible without expecting it.

The case I keep going back and forth on is: “It’s probably raining, but I don’t believe it is.” Does the “probably” make it acceptable, or does the second half still pull the rug out from under the first?

What I want is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that draws the line in the right place — something precise about how assertion, belief, knowledge, and certainty interact. Which principle gets all or most of my verdicts? Where would it force me to revise a verdict, especially about (4) or (6)? And what does the best principle say about the “probably” case, and about whether the defect in the bad cases is logical, semantic, or something about the act of assertion itself?
