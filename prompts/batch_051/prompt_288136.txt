I sit on the records and acquisitions commitee of a small regional archive in a former mill town in the north of England. We've been offered the papers of a textile-manufacturing family, the Hargreaves (not their real name), spanning roughly 1820 to 1930: ledgers, wage books, letters between owners, and two albums of mill photographs. The wage books are, as far as anyone knows, the only surviving record of who worked in the town's mills in that period, but workers appear only as names and sums, never as people with their own documents. There are no diaries, letters or photographs from the workers' side. The family's descendants want to gift the collection, and they've made it clear they hope we'll showcase the family's contribution to the town. I'm new to the committee and hadn't quite appreciated how much of what we call custodianship is actually editorial decisions. Our existing holdings are already heavy on mill owners, merchants and clergy, and thin on everyone else.

The committee is split three ways. One group says take it: however one-sided the collection is, it's the only trace of these workers' lives, and refusing it simply deepens the silence. A second group says accept only on condition that the archive describes and interprets the material so that what's missing is made visible, perhaps paired with a community-led project; but some of them worry this is us inadvertantly putting our thumb on the scale. A third group says accepting the collection at all makes the archive complicit in reproducing an old power structure, because keeping the masters' papers is exactly how the workers got left out of the record in the first place, and taking more of the same is not neutrality, it's a choice that sides with the powerful. Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before next week's meeting. It should recommend a concrete decision, whether that's accept, decline, or accept with specified conditions, with a timeframe and criteria a future acquisitions committee can actually apply, and it should engage the strongest argument on the losing side seriously rather than just knocking it down.
