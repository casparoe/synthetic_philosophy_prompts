In the preface to *The Will to Believe* (1897), William James writes:

"Real possibilities, real indeterminations, real beginnings, real ends, real evil, real crises, catastrophes, and escapes, a real God, and a real moral life, just as common-sense conceives these things, may remain in empiricism as conceptions which that philosophy gives up the attempt either to 'overcome' or to reinterpret in monistic form."

Treat this passage as James’s criterion for an indeterminism that matters ethically: indeterminism counts for free will and moral life only insofar as it leaves room for real possibilities, real beginnings, real evil, real crises, and real moral life—not merely for physical randomness. What are the most important implications of this criterion for the contemporary view that quantum-level indeterminacy in the brain provides the openness that free will requires?
