Two of my coworkers got into it at lunch last week over a third teammate, Ravi. Over a few months, Ravi had cycled through three keyboards. He traded keyboard A for keyboard B and threw in $25; a month later he traded B for C and threw in $30; a month after that he traded C back for A and threw in $15. He ended up holding the same keyboard he started with, $70 poorer.

One coworker, Dana, said the whole sequence was irrational no matter how it felt at each step: Ravi paid to end exactly where he began, and if a single trade that returned the same object minus cash would obviously be a mistake, slicing the mistake into three steps doesn't cure it.

The other coworker, Marcus, said that's an artifact of watching from outside. At the time of each swap, Ravi preferred the keyboard he was getting to the one he already had; that's all a preference can be. People discover what they actually like only by trying alternatives, and trading back when you miss the original is just updating, not inconsistency.

Dana came back: if a sequence of individually reasonable exchanges is guaranteed to leave you worse off by your own later lights, the pattern itself is the problem, not the individual steps. Somebody could keep offering Ravi the same rotation and collect $70 each round. A person whose choices loop like that is exploitable, and exploitable preferences are irrational preferences.

Marcus replied that once the loop is pointed out, Ravi would presumably refuse the next round; noticing a pattern changes his future choices. He also said the $70 wasn't a pure loss — it bought Ravi something real, namely knowledge about which keyboard he actually likes, plus three months of using each one. If you call that irrational, then any purchase you later regret becomes irrational, which would make rationality impossible.

Dana said that defense can be attached to anything: someone could sit in a dealership swapping cars at a loss indefinitely and call each loss tuition. At some point, coherence across time has to constrain an agent, or the word "mistake" loses its meaning. Marcus answered that coherence across time treats Ravi at month one and Ravi at month three as if they were the same evaluator with the same information, which they aren't; rationality constrains how you move from what you know now, not whether your past selves form a tidy ranking.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Please go through the exchange move by move: say who is right about which points, rate each point, and say what both of them are missing — or who is more right overall.
