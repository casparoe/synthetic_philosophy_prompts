I keep coming back to a puzzle in how my own country is governed, and I would like you to help me think it through rather than settle it.

On one side, we treat long political experience as dangerous. There are term limits for some offices, and candidates campaign as outsiders, as if a career spent in politics must make someone worse at the job. Yet nobody applies this logic to surgery or aviation: we want the pilot with the most hours and the surgeon with the most operations, and we would consider it insane to fill those roles by lottery.

On the other side, we do fill some of our most serious decision-making roles by lottery. Juries are drawn at random and asked to decide whether someone goes to prison. In several countries, citizens' assemblies chosen by lot have been asked to deliberate on complicated matters such as abortion law or climate targets — exactly the kind of question we usually say requires expertise.

So the same society seems to say three inconsistent things at once: governing is a demanding skill that rewards practice; ordinary people with no special training can be trusted with momentous judgments; and long service in office is a defect that the law should prevent. I want to know what theory of political judgment and representation these practices presuppose, and how much of that theory they actually force on us. What would have to be true about the difference between political judgment and technical skill for the whole pattern to be coherent? Could more than one theory license the same practices, and if so, where exactly is the choice between them left open? And what would have to change in practice if we replaced that implicit theory with the view that governing is a trained profession like medicine — or, in the opposite direction, with the view that ordinary citizens should make the decisions directly and officials are merely their temporary staff?
