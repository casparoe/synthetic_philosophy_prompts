I sit on the independent content-policy advisory body of a large video-sharing platform. We must decide on a university research group's request to build a public, searchable archive of decontextualized propaganda clips—recruitment videos, manipulated news segments, and synthetic-media examples—for use in media-literacy teaching and disinformation research. The researchers argue that stable access to primary material is needed to study how propaganda persuades and to train people to recognize manipulation; they maintain that reproducing the material for critical analysis is not endorsement, and that current takedown practice leaves researchers with a moving target. Our head of trust and safety opposes the archive on the grounds that the clips will be downloaded and redeployed as propaganda, that hosting them may violate our own harmful-content policies, and that some clips revictimize the people depicted. At the core of the dispute is whether a label and access controls can change what the reproduction actually does, or whether they merely attach a disclaimer to the same underlying harm. The question became concrete for me recently, when a colleague found her likeness manipulated into a smear clip that would fall inside the proposed archive. Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate before our vote. Recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions (access, watermarking, duplication, takedown), with an actionable bottom line and an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side.
