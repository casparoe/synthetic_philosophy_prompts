I'm taking a philosophy of mind course and we just did functionalism and multiple realizability, and I realized my gut reactions to the usual cases dont obviously hang together. I'm hoping you can reverse-engineer what principle I'm actually tracking, or tell me which of my reactions have to go. You're a professional philosopher of mind who's good at this exact kind of careful reflective-equilibrium work. Think through it slowly, and maybe brainstorm a couple of candidate principles before settling on one—dont just jump to a canned answer.

Here are my verdicts. They probably conflict; thats the point.

1. Neuron-by-neuron silicon replacement. If you replaced each of my neurons, one at a time, with a tiny silicon chip that takes in the same signals and sends out the same signals the original neuron would have, I think I'd stay conscious the whole time and it would still be me. No fading lights.

2. Whole-brain emulation. If you scanned my brain and ran a simulation of all my neurons and their synaptic connections on a computer, with the same internal dynamics, I think that simulation would be conscious and would be me in some real sense.

3. The giant lookup table. Suppose you build a machine that contains, for every possible sensory input I could ever get, the exact behavioral output I would produce, and it just looks up the current input and spits out the reply. I think there's nothing it's like to be that table. It's not conscious and it's not me, even if it passes every test you throw at it.

4. The billion-person brain. Same idea as the China brain thought experiment where a billion people pass slips of paper or talk over radios to implement the same pattern of connections as my brain for a while. I don't think that system feels anything. Something is off, though I cant say exactly what.

5. The thermostat wired up for pain. If you wire a thermostat so its few internal states stand in exactly the causal relations that, on some simple functional theory, define being in pain, I still don't think it feels pain. The functional role is there but the thing is too simple.

6. The alien. If we met an alien whose chemistry was completely different—silicon and liquid methane, whatever—but which had internal states playing the same causal and functional roles as our pain, belief, and desire states, I'd say it can be in pain. I'm not a biological chauvinist about this.

My tentative principle is that what matters is real causal role, not input-output matching. That would rule out the lookup table and maybe the thermostat, and let in the silicon replacement, the emulation, and the alien. But I can't see why the billion-person brain doesn't satisfy that too—it has real internal causal structure, it's just made of people. So either my principle is missing something, or my billion-person-brain verdict has to go.

Is there a coherent principle that gets all or most of these verdicts? Which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? And what would it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: replacing my neurons one at a time with tiny homunculi—little people who mechanically do exactly what the neurons do—rather than with silicon chips?
