I sit on my university's research policy committee, which must decide a contested intellectual-property dispute before next month's meeting, and I have been asked to circulate a short written opinion recommending a concrete course of action. The case is this: a doctoral student in computational biology presented a lab seminar describing a novel method for training a model to predict protein–protein interactions. She has a dated laboratory notebook entry from six weeks before the seminar and had asked two colleagues to keep the method confidential. A postdoc who attended the seminar later built the method into a published computational pipeline funded by an industry partner and listed himself as sole inventor. The postdoc argues that ideas cannot be property because they are non-scarce and do not spoil, and that whatever is disclosed openly in a seminar enters a common intellectual stock. The student's advocate appeals to John Locke's labor-mixing argument in the Second Treatise of Government, claiming that the notebook entry is exactly the kind of labor through which one fixes property in what was previously common. But the same chapter contains a qualification our committee has kept coming back to: in Chapter V, Section 31, Locke says that the right of appropriation from the common stock is bounded — a person may take only as much as she can make use of for any advantage of life before it spoils, and whatever is beyond that is more than her share and belongs to others. We are divided about whether this proviso cuts for or against either party, since a research idea neither spoils nor is diminished by another's use. Draft the short opinion I will circulate to the committee: it should recommend a decision on whether the student's claim has merit under our policy, whether the postdoc's conduct was wrongful, and what remedy, if any, the university should impose — and it should engage the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly rather than simply asserting that ideas are or are not property.
