In my history and politics classes I keep noticing a mismatch I cannot resolve on my own. We learn that a state legitimately holds the highest coercive powers over a territory: it may make and enforce law, tax, judge, keep an army, and go to war. But when we studied chartered trading companies, the same powers were described as a scandal. A joint-stock company, answerable to its shareholders, was allowed to govern whole territories — it raised troops, made treaties, collected taxes, ran courts, and used force, all while pursuing profit. This looks like the very same bundle of powers described twice: once as the definition of sovereignty, once as an illegitimate privatisation of it. Is the difference in our judgements tracking anything real? Is a modern state, considered as an organisation claiming supreme coercive authority over a territory, relevantly different from such a company, or does the company look worse mainly because it answered to shareholders rather than to a diffuse public? I would like a committed answer: if there is a principled distinction, state it precisely and test it against cases like a for-profit prison or a private military contractor; if there is none, say why the company was condemned while the state was not, and whether we should revise either judgement. Please write in British English.
