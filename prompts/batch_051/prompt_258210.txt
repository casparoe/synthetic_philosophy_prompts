I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar and last week we got stuck on self-deception, of all things. The textbook puzzle is something like: if self-deception means getting yourself to believe P while somewhere also knowing not-P, then you'd have to be both deceiver and deceived at once, which looks impossible. One classmate keeps answering every case with "You can't really lie to yourself." When someone brought up the person who genuinely believes her spouse isn't cheating while also checking his phone on the sly, the classmate said "that's not lying to yourself, that's just being wrong about yourself with some motivated reasoning mixed in." I don't think she has one clear claim in mind, but I suspect there are several different true-ish points hiding in the slogan.

Act as a professional philosopher who works on self-deception and philosophy of mind. Start by brainstorming a list of the distinct claims "You can't really lie to yourself" could be making — from trivial points about how the verb "lie" works, to substantive claims about whether one part of a mind can keep a secret from another, or whether a present self can deceive a future self. Then take each reading and say whether it's a platitude, a defensible but contentious thesis, or probably false, and why. Think carefully about which candidate readings collapse into each other, and about the difference between saying self-deception never happens and saying the label "lying" is just a bad label for a real thing. Finish by telling me which of the readings (if any) would actually support her move of pushing the motived-belief cases outside the category of self-deception, and which readings would only rule out something nobody in the seminar was defending. Give me a seperately itemised menu I can bring back to class, not a single verdict.
