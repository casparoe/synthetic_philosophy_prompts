I am a PhD student in philosophy of technology, and I have been working through an interpretive problem that would benefit from a careful, methodical analysis. Please act as an expert in the philosophy of law and digital ethics. Take your time, begin by laying out the possible interpretive approaches, and reason through each one before offering a considered judgment.

The problem concerns a clause in the community standards of a long-running social virtual world. The clause reads as follows:

"You may not engage in any conduct within the platform that causes another user to suffer significant emotional distress."

The drafters of this clause clearly had in mind direct harassment: threats, stalking, hateful speech, repeated unwanted contact, and similar interpersonal abuse. But I am interested in a case they plainly did not anticipate.

A user, without threatening or contacting anyone, constructed a meticulous virtual replica of another user's childhood home, which the second user had described in a public forum as a place of great personal significance following the death of a parent. The first user then invited the second user to a virtual viewing, and, while the second user watched, burned the replica to the ground in an act of symbolic destruction. The second user reports that the experience caused genuine and lasting emotional distress, including intrusive memories and difficulty sleeping. No rule against destroying virtual property was violated: the replica was built and destroyed entirely within the first user's own virtual land.

The platform's moderation team is divided. Some say the clause plainly reaches the conduct because the second user suffered significant emotional distress, and the first user's conduct caused it. Others say this is not what "conduct" was meant to cover, and that reading the clause this broadly would make virtually any upsetting creative or expressive act a violation. Still others say the case reveals a deeper problem: the clause assumes a straightforward distinction between "virtual" conduct and "real" harm, but this case shows the two cannot be cleanly separated.

I am less interested in the practical outcome than in the interpretive structure. What would each standard approach to interpreting such a rule—its plain words, its purpose, what the drafters likely had in mind, what a reasonable reader would understand it to cover—yield in this case? And what does the difficulty of applying the rule here tell us about the underlying question of whether virtual harms are real harms? Please be explicit about which of the possible readings would actually support the second user's complaint, and whether any coherent reading does so without collapsing the rule into a general prohibition on causing offense.
