I'm putting together a module on language change and I keep tripping over the same question: if you trace a chain of speakers from, say, 200 CE Rome to 1300 CE Florence, every child understood their parents, and yet at some point you'd call what they're speaking Italian and not Latin. I want to use five candidate "boundary markers" for when Latin becomes Italian and rank them from best to worst at actually carving the continuum at a joint (or at least being honest that there's no joint). The five are: (1) the first surviving texts that editors treat as a Romance vernacular rather than Latin; (2) the loss of Latin's case endings in everyday speech as a grammatical threshold; (3) the point at which ordinary Romance speakers could no longer make out liturgical or classical Latin without special study; (4) the point at which speakers stopped calling their language "latino" and started seeing it as a distinct "volgare"; and (5) the moment a written standard got codified and claimed for high-prestige functions like literature and administration (a Dante-type move). Rank these five, and for each give a sentence or two on why it lands where it does. I'd like the answer as a numbered list with exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
