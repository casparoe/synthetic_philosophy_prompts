I am an undergraduate in a course on biotechnology and the future of value, and our seminar has spent several weeks on David Pearce's The Hedonistic Imperative. We keep circling around a dispute about his response to the contrast objection—the idea that happiness is meaningful only in relation to unhappiness. Pearce responds in section 4.0 partly through an analogy with Anton's syndrome. The passage around which the seminar dispute turns is:

People with Anton's Syndrome not only become blind; they are unaware of their sensory deficit. Furthermore, they lose all notion of the meaning of sight. They no longer possess the neurological substrates of the visual concepts by which their past and present condition could be compared and contrasted. Our genetically joyful descendants may, or may not, undergo an analogous loss of cognitive access to the nature and variant textures of suffering.

In the seminar, one side reads the analogy as doing the work Pearce needs. On their view, the analogy shows that a state can be wholly positive from the inside even when the capacity to represent its negative contrast is lost: Anton's patients are not made miserable by their blindness, and analogously, posthuman ecstatics would not be made less happy by their loss of cognitive access to suffering. The other side responds that the analogy trades on an equivocation. Anton's patients have a serious deficit, and their unawareness of it is part of their pathology; what the analogy shows is only that a loss can go unfelt, not that the loss is no loss in value, nor that a life without the capacity for contrast is thereby better or just as good. The defenders of the analogy then reply that Pearce is not using Anton's syndrome to justify the posthuman state; he is using it only to refute a specific causal premise about felt contrast being necessary for felt happiness. At that point the disagreement seems to change shape: one side is arguing about what the analogy is meant to establish, and the other is arguing about what it actually establishes, and neither move seems to land.

We do not disagree about the clinical facts of Anton's syndrome, about Pearce's text, or about whether a state can be happy without felt unhappiness. The impasse feels like a disagreement about the adequacy conditions for an answer to the contrast objection. I have a hypothesis: one side is asking whether the loss of contrast causes unhappiness, and the other is asking whether the loss of contrast is an evaluative loss even if it isn't felt. But I am not sure whether that is a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two different questions under one heading. Please diagnose the disagreement with reasons drawn from what each side actually says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress—a conceptual distinction, a reformulation of the objection, an empirical finding, or something else.
