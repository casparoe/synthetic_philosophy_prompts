For my moral psychology seminar I'm putting together a handout for the unit on psychopathy and moral knowledge, and I need a case that does one specific job. Construct a realistic, ordinary-life scenario about an adult with no diagnosed psychopathy, no brain lesions, and nothing science-fictional, in which two accounts of moral knowledge come apart. The first account is a cognitivist/tracking view on which moral knowledge is roughly correct moral belief acquired in the right way; the second is a motivational-internalist view on which no state counts as moral knowledge unless it includes at least some disposition to be moved. The scenario should make the subject a clear case of knowing on the first account and a clear case of not knowing on the second. She should meet every standard epistemic condition for knowing that what she is doing is wrong: she can state the rule she is violating, explain why it is wrong, distinguish moral rules from merely conventional ones, and when the act is described to her under a neutral label she classes it as wrong without hestitation. But the case should render her motivationally flat about that knowledge—she treats the wrongness as a bare fact with no more grip on her than the fact that the sky is blue. Don't stipulate that she knows or doesn't know; the details have to generate the verdicts. After the scenario, run the verification. State the exact belief, how she formed it, and then show carefully why the first account yields the verdict that she knows and the second yields the verdict that she does not. Then flag which single feature of the case is doing the real work, what the case looks like if you remove it, and how a committed motivational internalist would most plausibly attack the case without begging the question. Last, in a sentence or two, say what the case should teach about whether the psychopath who says he knows it is wrong but does not care is being sincere. Begin with the scenario itself, no preface, and keep the whole thing under a page.
