I work as the sole program officer at a mid-sized family foundation with a deliberately broad mission—to improve lives—and no geographic or issue-area restrictions. We receive roughly 150 unsolicited grant inquiries a year and can make perhaps a dozen substantial grants, with an annual grant budget of about $10 million. I run the initial triage: I have at most one hour per inquiry, one junior analyst to help, no in-house subject-matter experts, and a six-member board that is bright but non-expert, impatient with jargon, and suspicious of anything that looks like a numerical scoring system. The board signs off on every grant, but in practice it leans heavily on my one-page recommendation. I have heard that cause prioritization frameworks evaluate proposals along three dimensions—importance, tractability, and neglectedness—but I have never seen the framework turned into something I can actually run in my job, across proposals as different as local arts programs and tropical disease control.

I would like you to write me a reusable triage procedure grounded in the importance/tractability/neglectedness framework. It should be a kind of flowchart in words: the questions I ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a clear "decline for now," where it produces "advance to full due diligence," and where—because the question is one of values rather than facts—it hands the decision to a person with explicit acknowledgment of the value judgment. The procedure must be usable by a generalist who does not have the theory, must fit in my one-hour-per-inquiry budget, and must produce a one-page board-facing rationale without collapsing everything into a single score the board will distrust.

Please build in guidance for the hard cases: missing evidence; very high uncertainty with high potential upside; proposals whose outcomes cannot be placed on a common scale with one another; the difference between a cause being neglected in general and there being room for more funding from us in particular; and the temptation to double-count the same consideration in more than one dimension. Mark each point where judgment cannot be eliminated as such, and say what the procedure should do when good faith judgment still leaves me uncertain.

Then run the procedure on all three of these proposals, stating for each what the procedure yields and which precise step or consideration drives the result. The first is a $400,000-per-year, three-year request from a local after-school music instruction program serving 250 low-income children in the foundation's home city; the stipulated facts are that rigorous evaluations suggest small but real improvements in school attendance and non-cognitive skills, the cost is about $1,600 per child per year, three other local funders and the city already support similar programming, and additional study would be unlikely to change what we know. The board chair knows the applicant and has spoken warmly about the proposal. The second is a $500,000-per-year, four-year request from a national NGO to deliver school-based treatment for schistosomiasis to 1.5 million school-age children in a district where no major international donor is currently active; the stipulated facts are that the disease burden is high, the intervention costs roughly $0.60 per child per year, randomized evidence shows large reductions in anemia and malnutrition, the national government is cooperative, and the main risks are logistics and political instability in neighboring provinces. The third is a $1,200,000-per-year, three-year request to place ten mid-career procurement specialists in health ministries in low-income countries, on the theory that better purchasing will make existing health budgets go much further; the stipulated facts are that the leverage would be large if the theory is right, the organization already has six major funders, and the only evidence for the model is the organization's own before-and-after estimates rather than an independent evaluation.

Please make sure at least one of the three cases is one in which your procedure gives an answer I or the board will not like, and say plainly what it is about the framework that compels that answer. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of proposals it will systematically mishandle, the places where its outputs could be mistaken for more objective than they are, and the decision it can never make for me.
