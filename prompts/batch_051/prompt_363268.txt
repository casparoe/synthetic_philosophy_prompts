I sit on the board of a small family foundation in a mid-sized city. We make four to six grants a year, mostly between fifteen and forty thousand dollars, in the areas of housing and food insecurity. I have no training in policy, economics, or program evaluation, and neither do most of the other trustees. But over the past few years I have kept coming back to a distinction I do not have tidy words for: some of what we fund helps people get through bad conditions as they are, and some of it tries to change the conditions themselves. It is not simply the difference between charity and advocacy; it is closer to the difference between buying a better mop and turning off the tap. I can feel our board defaulting to the mop because the mop's results are visible next month, while the work of turning off the tap is slow, uncertain, and hard to attribute to us.

I would like you to turn this distinction into a decision procedure we can actually run on each proposal. We review proposals on a rolling basis; I can give each one about an hour, and the other trustees will give it less. Please do not explain the distinction to me as an essay. Give me a tool: a checklist or a set of questions in the order they must be asked, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than fact, and where it should force us to write down an assumption we would otherwise smuggle in. It should end in a short written rationale I can hand to the other trustees, who will not have read the source material. It should not require us to become policy analysts or to search out information we do not have, but it should say which missing information would change the answer and what to do when we cannot get it. It should be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as routine, and about where the procedure will misfire.

Then run the procedure on three proposals:

1. A food pantry asks for twenty-five thousand dollars to keep its Saturday warehouse open for another year. It serves roughly four hundred households a month, turns people away when the budget runs out, and would spend the money on food, fuel, and one part-time coordinator.

2. A tenants' organization asks for thirty-five thousand dollars over two years to build a base in two neighborhoods and press the city council to adopt a just-cause eviction ordinance and publicly funded legal counsel for tenants facing eviction. It has a concrete outreach plan, a part-time organizer, and a list of council votes it would need to flip, but no promise of success and no short-term results it can show us.

3. A coalition asks for twelve thousand dollars for a public awareness campaign about the root causes of hunger: billboards, a website, and a speaker series. It describes the campaign as systems-focused, but it cannot identify which decision-maker the campaign is meant to influence or what change would follow.

I suspect the procedure will return an answer I do not like on at least one of these; do not soften it. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do—what my board will still have to decide for itself even if we follow it perfectly.
