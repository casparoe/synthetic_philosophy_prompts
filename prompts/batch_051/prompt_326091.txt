Two friends of mine got into one of those arguments at a dinner party last weekend that's better than it had any right to be, and I've been turning it over every since. The trigger was banal: our host mentioned that an old classmate of his had just sold his second startup, and someone at my end of the table joked that second startups are bought, not built. Maya said something like, sure, but it's also true that with family money and the right safety net, founding a company is a completely different game; it's not exactly pulling yourself up by your bootstraps. Owen jumped in and said that was just envy dressed up as social criticism. I stayed out of it, but what followed was weirdly rigorous for a dinner party. I'm probably not remembering the exact wording, but the moves went roughly like this:

(1) Owen: "Resenting someone else's success is sour grapes unless you can point to actual wrongdoing. Wanting the thing they have isn't an argument about justice; it's a confession."

(2) Maya: "That assumes the only way an inequality can be unjust is if somebody broke a rule. A system can be rigged by its setup — who gets bailed out, who has the safety net, whose network opens the doors — and resentment can be the feeling that first alerts you to that. Rawls has this idea of excusable envy: when a society's inequalities aren't the kind that benefit the worst off, the kind his difference principle would allow, then the envy of the people left behind isn't what we should be moralizing about. The inequality itself is the problem."

(3) Owen: "But even on Rawls's own picture that's off. Rawls treats envy as a nonmoral emotion, not a moral sentiment like resentment. And excusable doesn't mean accurate. A feeling can be understandable — of course the person who loses out is bitter — without being any evidence that the process was unfair. You still need an independent standard of unfairness."

(4) Maya: "But we don't require that standard before taking anger seriously after a broken promise. We think the anger is both a reaction and a sign that the promise mattered. The double standard is my whole point: we hand epistemic standing to the feelings of the comfortable, and strip it from the people below them because their feelings are inconvenient."

(5) Owen: "There's still an asymmetry. The kind of moral anger you're appealing to can be third-personal — I can be angry about a wrong done to a stranger on the other side of the world. Envy can't do that; it's always about my own standing relative to the person I envy. If the successful person lost everything overnight, the envious person would feel better even if nothing else in the world changed. That shows it's tracking the gap, not the injustice."

(6) Maya: "Only if the gap itself can't be the injury. If a rigged economy makes self-respect positional, then being stuck below someone in a rigged ranking just is the harm. The fact that the envy would disappear if the ranking collapsed doesn't prove what you think — you could say the same about a bullied student who's relieved when the bully loses his power."

They went in circles for a while after that, and the evening sort of dissolved before either landed a clean hit. I'd be curious how you'd referee it. Go through the moves one by one and assign each a verdict — clearly right, mostly right, half right, mostly wrong, clearly wrong, or something in between — with a sentence or two of justification. Then step back: who is more right overall, and what are both of them missing? For instance, are they running together being excusable, being justified, and being accurate? Is there a stable line between resentment and envy here, and does that line depend on which theory of justice you already accept?
