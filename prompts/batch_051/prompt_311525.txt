Last term I was in a seminar on reproductive ethics, and the discussion kept sliding between whether a certain policy was morally right and what it would actually do if implemented. I want to keep those questions apart and ask only the second. The scheme below is a deliberate mechanization of procreative beneficence, in something like the spirit of liberal eugenics: the state steers parents with subsidies rather than coercing them, and the only penalty for refusing to be steered is financial. I would like you to analyze it strictly as a mechanism — what it incentivizes, who it attracts and repels, how it would be gamed, where it fails, and what second-order effects it sets off. Then commit: say which failure mode you think dominates, why, and which one or two changes would most improve the scheme without gutting it. Please don't give me a literature review or citations; the details of the proposal should be enough to reason from.

Here is the proposal. A country creates a public Preconception Welfare Fund. Any prospective parent may apply for fully subsidized IVF with preimplantation genetic testing for a fixed panel: medically serious monogenic conditions, common chromosomal abnormalities, and, where available, polygenic risk scores for a dozen common diseases, together with a polygenic index for educational attainment used as a rough proxy for expected life prospects. As a condition of full subsidy, applicants must use a state-published Expected Child Welfare Index to rank the transferable embryos, and must transfer the highest-scoring one. Embryos whose scores fall within one percent of the highest score count as ties and may be chosen freely from the tied set. Applicants who prefer a lower-scoring embryo may still choose it, but they then owe a repayment equal to five percent of the subsidy for each percentage point between their embryo's score and the highest available score, capped at eighty percent of the subsidy. Clinics receive a small per-cycle bonus when a child conceived through the Fund is later assessed as free of the tested conditions; parents may withdraw before transfer and receive no subsidy rather than a reduced one. The Index is revised every five years by a standing panel of statisticians, clinical geneticists, and representatives of disability organizations.

Walk through what this does to who applies, who declines, and what clinics, test manufacturers, and the panel itself are incentivized to do. Where does the first gaming or scandal most plausibly appear? What happens to the political and clinical meaning of the Index over time? If the scheme tends to convert a moral choice into a priced choice, is that conversion a fixable bug in the mechanism or a stable feature of it? Give a definite answer on each of these rather than a survey of possibilities.
