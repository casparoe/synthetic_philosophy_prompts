People have this double standard about mind uploading that I can't figure out. If I describe a procedure where over thirty years your neurons get swapped out one at a time for synthetic ones that behave identically—no noticeable change day to day, the new ones slowly takeing over the causal role—most people say sure, I survive, the person at the end is me. But if I describe a procedure that reaches the exact same endpoint faster, say a complete scan and then a replacement of every neuron with equivalent hardware in a single afternoon, same skull, same result, they say no, that killed the original and the thing that wakes up is a duplicate. The only variables that differ are speed and whether the replacement overlaps with the old stuff in time. Is there any principle that actually lets you draw a line somewhere between thirty years and one afternoon, or is the slow-safe / fast-death split just an intuition we should give up? Try to state the strongest justification for the double standard you can, run it through some awkward cases (a week? a day? an hour? a minute? under anesthesia?), and then commit: where would you draw the line, or do you bite the bullet that the slow replacement kills the original too?
