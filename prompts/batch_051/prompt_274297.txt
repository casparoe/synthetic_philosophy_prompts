I am a novelist rather than a philosopher, and I am trying to make a practical decision about where to put my research time. The novel I am about to draft follows a single character through one day in which she repeatedly misidentifies her own emotions: she calls envy grief, takes a bodily unease for romantic longing, and mistakes the sinking of shame for fear. The book will stand or fall on whether these errors read as psychologically exact rather than as the author moving pieces around. In thinking about what it would be to render such states exactly, I have been reading around in the philosophy of emotion, and I keep circling a familiar three-way split: that an emotion is essentially an evaluative judgment or appraisal, that it is a perception or quasi-perception of value, or that it is at bottom a felt bodily condition. Each of these would obviously push a scene in a different direction — a judgment view toward what the character believes and how those beliefs could be revised; a perceptual view toward what the emotion presents and how it might misfire the way an illusion does; a feeling view toward the sequence and texture of bodily change. But beyond that first mapping, I do not know which disputes in the field would actually change my drafting and which would only rearrange terminology.

So my question is an agenda-setting one. If I have six to eight weeks to absorb the parts of this literature that would most improve the book, which two or three open questions or lines of work should I prioritize, and which much-discussed debates are overrated for my purposes because they would leave the prose largely untouched? I already suspect that some apparently technical disagreements may matter more than they look: for example, the treatment of recalcitrant emotions — fear that persists while the person sincerely denies anything is dangerous — is used as near-decisive evidence by some and dismissed as a side problem by others. Please defend the ordering against explicit criteria — for instance, how much settling the question would alter the rendering of a scene; how widely the difference would ramify across a whole novel rather than a single kind of beat; and how stable the consequence is, rather than a nuance that dissolves once it is translated into narrative. I would also ask you to be self-critical in a specific way: tell me which argument or new consideration would most likely lead you to change the ranking, and where the reasoning behind your ordering is most likely to fail.
