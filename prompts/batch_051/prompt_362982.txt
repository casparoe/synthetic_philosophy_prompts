I help run a small amateur league in which teams argue contested policy questions before lay audiences and are scored on the quality of their reasoning rather than on delivery or partisanship. Next month I'm presenting to the program committee of a foundation that funds civic education and public media: I'm asking for a pilot grant to scale this into a spectator-friendly competitive argument format — teams assigned positions by lot, mixed panels of subject experts and lay judges, broadcast rounds with post-round commentary on the reasoning, and a season-long ranking. My thesis is that a well-designed competitive format for argument would, over several years, improve how ordinary people argue about disputed questions and would attract people who are currently put off by both formal debate and televised shouting.

The committee: former journalists, high school educators, and civil servants, plus two donors. They are sympathetic to improving public discourse but skeptical of "gamification"; several have seen school debate formats reward speed and technical point-scoring, and they suspect we would be building a more elaborate version of that. They will not respond to formal epistemology or decision-theoretic arguments, and they will care about abstract incentive questions only insofar as they bear directly on the pilot's design.

Prepare me for the question period as a prep sheet, not a defense of the idea. Structure it as follows:

1. Objections (about 70% of the response): the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each does to the proposal. For each, give (a) the objection phrased as a former journalist or educator on this committee would put it; (b) what to concede immediately; (c) a two-minute reply; and (d) one sentence on where that reply is weakest.
2. The question I should hope nobody asks (about 15%): the single most damaging question, why it is dangerous, and the best available answer.
3. The reply that has to land (about 15%): the one point that must be persuasive for the proposal to survive, stated as I should deliver it, and what signal tells me whether it landed.
