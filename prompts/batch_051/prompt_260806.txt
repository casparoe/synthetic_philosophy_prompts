I'm a PhD student in philosophy, mostly working on love and practical reasons, and my cousin recently sent me the script from his wedding. He and his wife wrote their own vows, and the line I keep turning over is the one they both said: "I take you as you are today, and I promise to love and cherish you through every change of who you are, in sickness and in health, for as long as we both live." They told me afterward that they put "through every change of who you are" in on purpose, because they didn't want to promise only to love the person standing at the alter; they were picturing the ordinary drift of a life—aging, a big career change, becoming parents, maybe a slow political swing.

The case none of us were thinking about at the rehearsal dinner is this. Five years in, one of them has a severe frontal-lobe injury. After rehab the person is physically present and the memories are mostly intact, but the traits the other one had fallen in love with are gone: the patience, the humor, the particular way of responding to things, the second-order cares that gave this person their edges. In their place are mostly apathy, irritability, and small cruelties. A neurologist at one point says, offhand, "the person you married isn't really in there anymore." Assume for the argument that this is unproblematically the same human being—the puzzle is about the reasons for love and the promise, not numerical identity. The vow's illness clause doesn't obviously let anyone out, since it says "in sickness and in health"; and the "every change of who you are" clause looks like it reaches the case or maybe even was built for it. But can the healthy spouse still love "you" when the "you" has been hollowed out, or does the promise slip from being a promise to love into a promise to perform care for a stranger in the same body? My seminar this term has been reading Frankfurt, Velleman, and Kolodny, and this has become my private test case for the appraisal-vs-bestowal split: on an appraisalish view, love tracks the beloved's valuable qualities, so when those are obliterated there's nothing left for the promise to be responsive to; on a bestowalish view, the commitment itself is partly what makes the beloved's value, and this is precisely the kind of case a vow is supposed to survive.

So here's what I'd like you to do. Work through what the clause actually commits the healthy spouse to under three different interpretive approaches: the plain text of the words; the purpose the couple drafted it for, including what they would have said if someone had asked them at the altar about this exact injury; and what a reasonable guest at the wedding would have understood them to be promising. For each, tell me whether the vow applies, lapses, or partly applies, and whether it requires love itself or only care and fidelity. Then explain how the appraisal/bestowal disagreement is doing the real work under the surface, and which reading should win here and why. I don't need legal analysis or clinical advice, and I only want the philosophical argument.
