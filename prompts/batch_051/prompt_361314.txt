I cover money and ideas for a magazine, and theres a puzzle I keep coming back to. A person with a good printer runs off a few thousand pounds in counterfeit notes and spends them on groceries, petrol, a second-hand car. The notes circulate. Every pound he passes on dilutes the purchasing power of every genuine pound held by everyone else by a tiny sliver. We call this counterfeiting: he goes to prison, and almost nobody thinks that is a miscarriage of justice. Now look at the same underlying action — expanding the stock of money so that each existing holder has a claim on a slightly smaller slice of real goods — when done by the Bank of England via quantitative easing. The Bank buys bonds from financial institutions with newly created reserves. The new money works its way into the economy, pushes up asset prices, and quietly transfers some purchasing power from people holding cash or fixed incomes to whoever gets the new money first. Its a technical choice by unelected officials, not a crime, and plenty of people thought it was simply necessary after 2008 and again during the pandemic.

So here is the question. Is that asymmetry actually defensible, or have we just learned to tolerate the big, legal version of the very thing we punish in its small, private form? I can already think of the obvious candidate justifications: state authorisation, differences in scale and skill, the macroeconomic purpose, transparency, and the fact that a central bank creates reserves against an asset it buys rather than out of nothing. But I'm not sure they survive a bit of pressure. Suppose a counterfeiter printed his notes openly, published his balance sheet, and spent the proceeds only on public goods the community wanted — would that cure the moral problem? If not, maybe the things we cite are doing less work than we pretend.

I'd like you to take the role of a professional philosopher working on the ethics of money and think carefully about this. Start by brainstorming every reason you can find for treating the two cases differently, test each one against nearby cases (goldsmiths who issued receipts for more gold than they held, local currencies, families paying children in home-made tokens), and then tell me whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned. Please write your answer in British English.
