I run a lunchtime study group at work, about 12 people — mostly software engineers, a couple data folks, nobody with formal philosophy — and we meet for 90 minutes. We've been trying to get thier heads around the thought that computational limits might matter for what it even means to be rational (bounded rationality, ideal agents, that stuff). One specific idea I keep circling: checking a proposed solution to a constraint problem is easy — you scan the pairs and make sure none of them conflict — but producing a solution can be shockingly hard; and when you've been searching for 15 minutes with nothing, you cant tell whether you're failing because the problem is hard-but-solvable or because no solution exists. I want people to feel that gap in the room, not hear me explain it.

So: design a runnable 90-minute game for this group that does that. I'd like it built around graph 3-coloring in disguise — assigning maybe 15-20 "features" or "jobs" to three machines/environments where certain pairs get a card saying they can't be placed together. Materials should be printable office stuff only (cards, sticky notes, a whiteboard), no software and no real money. I imagine two phases: first a phase where some people only verify a proposed placement while others are stuck trying to produce one, and then a sealed-envelope phase where two teams each get a problem, one solvable and one with a hidden conflict that makes it impossible, and neither team knows which they have. But feel free to change the phases if you have something better that gets the same effect.

The debrief is important. I want it to map specific moments in the game onto the actual ideas — verifying vs finding, witnesses/certificates, why the inside experience of failing gives no signal about hard-vs-impossible — and then land on the philosophical kicker: if a real agent can't tell hard from impossible, and can't feasibly search the whole space, then "pick the option that maximizes expected utility" may be asking the agent to solve a problem it can't solve. That last step I know we have to supply after the fact; no game fully delivers the ideal-rationality claim. Please be honest about what the activity can't reproduce (for example, the worst-case/asymptotic feel of hardness, or the fact that one hidden little clique is a toy certificate and not a claim about unsolvability in general), and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it.

Give me the rules, materials list, a timeline, a procedure for generating the two problem instances myself without accidentally making them both trivial (or both impossible), the debrief script, the likely failure modes, and the honest "what this can't teach" note.
