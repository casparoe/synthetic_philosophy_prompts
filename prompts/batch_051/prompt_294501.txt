I work on the staff of a regional planning body in a drought-prone area that has suffered repeated supply-chain breakdowns, and I have been asked to stress-test a proposal before it goes to a vote. The proposal, called a Resilience Compact, is explicitly framed by its designers as an attempt to institutionalize an old ideal of self-sufficiency: they gesture at the Greek notion of autarkeia and at the Stoic and Epicurean view that the self-sufficient person is free because they do not depend on what lies outside their control. Under the Compact, any household may enroll voluntarily. Enrollees commit to an annual audit establishing three things: that they produce at least one quarter of their own food calories (or an audited equivalent in energy, water capture, or repair of household systems); that the household can keep essential functions running for 72 hours without grid power; and that at least one adult holds a basic repair-and-maintenance certification. Enrollees in return receive a property-tax abatement scaled to their degree of self-provisioning, priority access to water and emergency services during declared shortages, and seats on a rotating board that shapes drought, infrastructure, and emergency-policy decisions. To avoid excluding apartment dwellers and landless households, up to one third of the threshold may be met by buying credits from a communal fund that finances shared infrastructure such as community solar, tool libraries, seed banks, and neighborhood water storage. Audits are conducted by other enrolled households on a rotating committee, and suspected gaming is referred to the board, which can revoke benefits.

I am not asking whether self-sufficiency is a good ideal in the abstract. I want a concrete account of how this institution would actually behave. What would it incentivize, and who would be most likely to enroll, to free-ride through the credit market, or to game a neighbor-audit system in which the person auditing you may be audited by you next season? What happens to enforcement once the power to revoke benefits sits with the most committed and materially invested enrollees? What second-order effects should we expect on land prices, on the labor market for repair skills, on the political weight of enrolled households, and on the meaning of self-sufficiency once it is certified, quantified, and traded? Which of these failure modes could be fixed by changing the audit structure, the credit cap, or the abatement, and which are intrinsic to attaching material rewards and political power to an ideal such as autarkeia? Finally, would the resulting practice still track anything the philosophical ideal values, or would it predictably turn into something else?
