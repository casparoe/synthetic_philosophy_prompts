An economist friend of mine is always on about Rawls's veil of ignorance, and his whole line is that it's just expected utility in fancy dress. His argument goes something like this: asking what principles you'd choose if you didn't know who you'd be is exactly like choosing among lottery tickets. Rational people, when choosing among lotteries, maximise expected value rather than obsessing over the worst possible prize. So, he says, the impartial choice has to be average utilitarianism, and Rawls's difference principle is just irrational risk-aversion wearing a fairness costume. I know Harsanyi turned something like this into a proper theorem — roughly, if everyone's preferences and society's preferences both satisfy the expected-utility axioms, and society respects Pareto indifference, then social utility has to be a weighted sum of individual utilities, and adding an equal-probability veil makes the weights equal. But I can't tell whether the lottery analogy is what's actually doing the argumentative work, or whether the theorem's axioms are quietly doing it all.

So I'd like you to audit the analogy, the way you'd audit 'the market is an ecosystem' or 'the brain is a computer'. Give me the correspondence table explicitly: what maps onto the lottery tickets, the outcomes, the probabilities, the chooser, and the utility function? Then lay out the places where the mapping breaks. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the conclusion he wants from the ones that are only surface-level. And then tell me whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the whole thing. If it doesn't hold up, say what you think Harsanyi's theorem actually shows, and whether there's a better analogy for it.

I'd like a committed answer: take a definite position on how far the analogy holds and whether the average-utilitarian conclusion is licensed, and give the reasons for it, rather than laying out both sides and leaving me to pick.
