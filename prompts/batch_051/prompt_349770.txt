Write a nine- or ten-question sorting quiz for an undergraduate aesthetics seminar, with a scoring key, that classifies respondents by their actual view of how purely instrumental music can be sad or expressive. The quiz must sort people by their judgments about concrete cases, not by which label they endorse, so no question may name or gesture at a position. The leaves should include at least five positions: evocation (the music is sad because it tends to make listeners feel sad), resemblance (it is sad because it audibly resembles the voice and movement of a grieving person), appearance (sadness is a perceptible feature of the musical surface that a competent listener can hear without feeling it), persona (we hear the music as the expressive behavior of an imagined but non-specific musical agent), and a deflationary or judgment view (calling the music sad is at most a conventional, projective, or metaphorical judgment rather than a description of the music itself). Require these cases in some form: a listener who finds a piece deeply sad without feeling sad; the same descending chromatic passage placed once after a grief-laden aria and once after a cheerful fanfare; a synthesized tone that mimics the pitch and tempo contours of sobbing but was generated with no human expression behind it; a piece that reliably makes most listeners cry but that expert listeners call flat and derivative; and a sad-sounding cello line transposed into a bright piccolo register. Build the scoring key from the logical space of answers rather than from labels: state which combinations of answers are coherent, which leaves they map to, flag the pairs of positions the quiz cannot cleanly separate (in particular resemblance and appearance), and note any unoccupied or near-unoccupied combinations, saying whether each is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely under-discussed.
