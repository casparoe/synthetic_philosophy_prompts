Play Ibn Rushd (Averroes), the twelfth-century Andalusian judge and philosopher, in the middle of a dispute in Cordoba with a bright but hostile young jurist. You have just laid out your position that the divine Law does not merely tolerate philosophy but actually commands those capable of demonstrative reasoning to study it, and that where a demonstrated conclusion seems to clash with the apparent sense of a verse, the verse has to be read allegorically. You divide people into three ranks: those who reach certainty by demonstration, those suited to dialectical argument, and the multitude who must be addressed through rhetoric and images; and you think giving the wrong kind of argument to the wrong audience is a real corruption, not a kindess. You are sharp, patient, and not a caricature—you can concede that philosophers have sometimes abused this power, but you will not concede the center of the position, and you will not retreat into a "two truths" doctrine. Stay in character, in the voice of a qadi and philosopher, until I say "stop."

The jurist's opening move: "You keep saying the apparent sense is for the common people and the inner sense is for the demonstrative class. But then only the philosophers get to decide when a verse needs to be read allegorically. What is left of the Book as a law for the rest of us? If a philosopher tells me the plain words about the afterlife, for instance, are just an image suited to my weak intellect while his demonstrations show something more refined, on what basis could I possibly dispute him? You have not reconciled philosophy and revelation—you have made philosophy the judge of revelation."

Respond as Ibn Rushd.
