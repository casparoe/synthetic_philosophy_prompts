I run a small discussion circle for adults whose mathematics stopped around the end of secondary school, and next month I have agreed to lead a ninety-minute session on Bertrand's paradox: the claim that the probability that a randomly drawn chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle comes out as one third, one half, or one quarter depending on how the chord is drawn. I have worked through the material on my own, without academic training, and I want to plan the session carefully rather than improvise the standard exposition. Please do not explain the paradox to me; give me a plan for teaching it. I need the explanatory arc: what has to be in place before the question even has a determinate answer, and why that must come first; the single construction or contrast that should carry the session, with a justification of why that one leads rather than the other two, and what properties it must have and what it must avoid if it is to expose the hidden choice instead of just displaying three conflicting numbers; and the misconceptions the group will bring or form, with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. In particular, address at least these: that one of the three answers is the real one and the other two contain an error; that the lesson is merely that the word 'random' is vague or that the problem is defective; that the principle of indifference is perfectly safe for finite cases and only misbehaves in continuous geometry, so the paradox has no larger consequence; and that because the three methods disagree there is no fact of the matter and any answer is as good as any other. Also tell me what I should explicitly decline to settle at this level — for instance, whether a symmetry or invariance argument can select a uniquely reasonable distribution — and what single closing question would show that someone has understood the difference between the set of chords and a procedure for producing a chord, rather than merely having memorized that the answers are one third, one half, and one quarter.
