Construct a thought experiment in which two optimizers behave identically in every environment an observer can sample despite having different objective functions, and then diverge sharply when their shared search space is changed in one small, independently describable way. The setting should be mundane: a document-triage routine, a hiring filter, an instruction scheduler, or a chess engine's move selection. Specifications: (1) optimizers A and B have genuinely different objectives U and V, which would rank options differently in any choice set containing a trade-off between the dimensions they weigh differently; (2) under a common search space S, A and B choose the same option in every sampled environment, so that an observer who infers objectives from final choices alone would confidently take them to share one objective; (3) when S is altered to S' by adding a single candidate or removing a single filter, with U and V held fixed, A and B immediately diverge in a sharp and predictable way, showing the observer's inference to have been wrong about what stayed fixed and what changed; (4) do not generate the invariance cheaply by making S contain exactly one option. Give the scenario, then state U, V, S, S', and the choices made in each condition, and show that each specification holds. Finish by taking a definite position on how an effective objective should be characterized once objective and search space can pull apart in this way, and on whether choice behavior alone can justify the inference of a shared objective between two systems.
