I'm a novelist, not a planner, but I got roped onto my city's heritage comission, and now I'm the one who has to draft the memo for this week's vote. The case is an 1847 house in the old quarter that has protected status. Over the years its owners have replaced nearly all the fabric—joists, sills, cladding—so maybe a fifth of the original material is left, and now they want to replace the last original load-bearing timbers. After that, by matter, it'll be about six percent 1847. Our ordinance protects "the structure as it has existed historically." Half the commission says it's obviously still the same house: the designation attaches to the continuing thing, not to the wood. The other half says it's a replica, and that this is just the Ship of Theseus with a tax abatement. I keep thinking of the house as a character who survives every replacement—which is probably why I'm stuck.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Friday's vote. Lead with a concrete recommendation, then work through exactly what an endurantist and a perdurantist would each say about whether "the same house" still exists once the last timbers are swapped, and then say honestly where the metaphysics stops doing work and the policy judgment has to step in. Give the strongest objection to your own recommendation a fair run rather than setting it up as a straw man. Keep it under 700 words, and don't refuse to choose.
