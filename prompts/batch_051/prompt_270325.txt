I am a PhD student in linguistics, and for the past year I have been facilitating a good-faith adversarial collaboration between two colleagues in my department: a qualified prescriptivist, who holds that there is a defensible and non-arbitrary sense in which a sentence can be bad grammar — a sense that does real work over and above tracking which forms happen to be socially stigmatized — and a descriptivist, who holds that "bad grammar" as a claim about a language is scientifically empty, because every native and near-native variety is rule-governed, and evaluative judgments are facts about speakers, registers, and social histories rather than about grammars. Write the joint statement they should have produced at the end of that collaboration. It should read as a document both could sign without irony: not a diplomatic compromise, but a text that makes their residual disagreement as exact as possible.

The statement should contain five parts. (1) A numbered list of substantive claims both parties can sign. Do not let this be a vacuous middle: it should include claims about what human languages share, about the systematicity of stigmatized varieties (for example, that negative concord and habitual be are rule-governed in their home varieties even though they are nonstandard in prestige written English), about the historical contingency of the standard, about slips and performance errors, and about the real social penalties attached to nonstandard usage — each stated so that neither party has to hold their nose. (2) A disagreements table, giving for each residual dispute a formulation each side would accept for its own view and a formulation the other side would accept as a fair statement of that view. (3) A section on which of their disagreements are merely verbal. I suspect a good deal turns on "grammar" sliding among at least three things: an internal computational system, a codified standard, and an observed pattern of usage in a community; make explicit which notion is in play for each residual dispute and what survives once the three are kept apart. (4) The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one party, rather than merely relabel the matter. (5) For each crux, the concrete case, corpus pattern, or experimental paradigm both parties would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Plausible crux areas include whether a workable notion of error can be relativized to a named register without collapsing into prestige, whether any stigmatized variants carry measurable comprehension or processing costs, and what patterns of hypercorrection such as between you and I show about whether the standard's rules are psychologically real. Do not confine yourself to those; derive the cruxes from the agreed claims themselves.
