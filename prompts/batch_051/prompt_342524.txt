Ok so I've been reading about how in Greek myth the gods swear their most serious oaths on the river Styx, and breaking one of those oaths has consequences even for them—the story goes that a god who swears falsely by the Styx is laid out for a year and then shut out of the gods' feasts and councils for like nine more. And the weirdest part is the backstory: the power of the Styx oath is itself something Zeus supposedly set up, as a reward to the river for siding with him against the Titans. So here's what bugs me. These are gods, the most powerful beings in the system, and nothing else can punish them. So what does this practice presuppose about what makes an oath binding at all? Does binding force have to come from outside the person swearing, or can they bind themselves? Why would naming a river make a god's word more binding that just saying it—is the river a witness, a threat of enforcement, or something else entirely? And is there a tension in the fact that the oath's power seems to be created by the very being it is supposed to constrain? Reconstruct the implicit theory of obligation here, say where the practice strictly presupposes that theory versus where it merely fits with it, and tell me what would have to change in the practice if you replaced the Styx with an ordinary human penalty like perjury law. Also flag where the practice is underdetermined: what rival views of promising could still make sense of it?
