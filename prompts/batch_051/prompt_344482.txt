I'm a PhD student in psychology, and the frequentist–Bayesian dispute in my department has been stuck for as long as anyone can remember, even though the replication crisis gave both camps a natural occasion to revisit their foundations. The frequentists read the crisis as a failure of error control — underpowered designs, undisclosed flexibility, inflated false-positive rates — and treat the fix as preregistration and corrected thresholds. The Bayesians read the same crisis as a failure of coherent belief revision — researchers drawing conclusions no rational posterior would support — and treat the fix as putting priors and posteriors on the table. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are not arguing one substantive question at all: they are answering different questions, one about long-run error guarantees, the other about what it is rational to believe after this particular data. I suspect the real crux is a hidden principle about optional stopping, namely that the frequentist treats the sampling plan as part of the inference while the Bayesian holds that, once the data are in, the evidence is all in the likelihood function. Diagnose the disagreement: is my hypothesis right, is part of it a verbal dispute about the word 'probability,' or is there a substantive empirical or formal question underneath that could settle it? And be as specific as you can about what, if anything, would count as progress rather than another round of exchange.
