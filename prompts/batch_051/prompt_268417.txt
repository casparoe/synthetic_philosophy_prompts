I am a novelist, and I have spent my working life trying to make readers feel things — grief, dread, pity, rage — with a deliberateness I would never wish on them in their actual lives. The same readers who weep at the end of a novel and call the book devastating, meaning it as high praise, will a week later describe their own tearfulness as something to be managed, medicated, or treated as a failure of coping. They pay for the sadness they would pay to be rid of. This is not a small inconsistency in taste; it is built into the craft, which treats the shudder a story induces as a mark of excellence, while no one treats a stranger's panic on the street as an achievement.

The division has an old philosophical shape. One tradition, of which the Stoics are the cleanest example, regards the passions as disturbances of the soul — false or excessive judgments to be extirpated rather than cultivated; the wise person may register misfortune without being carried away by it. The Romantic tradition, by contrast, treats intense feeling as inseparable from a full and truthful life and suspects that a person no fiction can unsettle is missing something central. Ordinary readers, as far as I can tell, try to occupy both positions at once, and the border between them is simply the frame of the artwork: grief, dread, and rage count as noise, symptom, or burden when they arise in a life, yet count as value — sometimes the principal value — when they arise before a novel, a play, a song, or a film.

My question is whether anything justifies this asymmetry, or whether it is only a contradiction we live with for historical or commercial reasons. The best answer would identify the most promising candidate distinction — distance or safety from real consequences, insight purchased by the feeling, control over when it begins and ends, the transforming effect of aesthetic form, or something else — test that distinction against nearby cases (a grief memoir rather than a novel, a documentary of actual violence rather than a horror film, a sad song one deliberately puts on, a real loss mourned with deliberate ritual), and then say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
