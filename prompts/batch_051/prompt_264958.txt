I keep noticing a structural parallel between two ways of thinking about word meaning, and I would like help determining whether it runs deep or is only a shared picture. In distributional semantics and word-embedding models, a word's meaning is often treated as its position in a high-dimensional vector space, and the familiar offset equation king − man + woman ≈ queen is used to suggest that semantic relations can be represented as vector differences that recur across the vocabulary: the same man-to-woman vector could in principle be added to brother to reach sister, which seems to imply that meanings are not atoms but coordinates in a space of separable, recombinable contrasts. On the other side, there is the older componential-analysis tradition in lexical semantics, which analyzes each word into a bundle of primitive semantic features — man as something like +human, +adult, +male and woman as +human, +adult, −male — and in which moving from one lexeme to another is a matter of flipping or replacing features, so that a single feature difference such as male/female recurs across many lexical pairs.

I want to know whether this parallel is deep or superficial. If the embedding picture really reproduced componential analysis, we should be able to transfer results between the two frameworks: we could read interpretable semantic features off the axes of an embedding space, predict in advance which offsets will land on existing words, and model feature-flipping as vector addition without residue. If the parallel is only superficial, where exactly does the mapping come apart? I am especially unsure about one point: componential features are supposed to be primitive, binary, and relatively few, whereas embedding spaces are learned, continuous, and have no privileged axes, since any rotation of the space should preserve the relevant distances. Is that a fatal disanalogy, or is it an implementation detail that leaves the underlying structural claim intact?
