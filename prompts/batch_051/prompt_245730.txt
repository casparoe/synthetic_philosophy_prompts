I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about when it's okay to randomize patients between treatments in a clinical trial, going by my gut on cases. Give me a principle if you can, not just vibes.

My first pass was something like: you can randomize whenever the wider expert community is genuinely split on which treatment is better. But that already feels too simple for a couple of these.

Here's where my gut lands:

1. Testing a new blood-pressure drug against placebo, when there are already two cheap, proven blood-pressure drugs patients would normally get. Gut: not fine. You're giving half the patients nothing.

2. Head-to-head trial of the new drug against the standard one, where the field is genuinely split on which is better. Gut: fine.

3. Add-on trial: everyone gets the standard drug, and on top of that half get the new drug and half get placebo. The real question is whether adding the new drug does anything extra. Gut: fine, even though there's a placebo group, because nobody is denied the standard.

4. Testing a cheaper new drug against placebo in a country where the standard drug isn't available because it costs too much. Gut: not fine, but less bad than case 1, and I honestly can't say why it feels different.

5. A surgeon is randomizing people between a new operation and the old operation, and the specialty is split about 50/50 on which is better. She herself is convinced the new one is better, based on her own uncontrolled series of patients. Gut: fine. Her hunch is her problem; the field is still split.

6. Same surgeon, same split field, but she tells a colleague she wouldn't let her own daughter be randomized — she'd push for the new operation. She still enrolls her own patients. Gut: not fine. I can't make this consistent with case 5 and its driving me up the wall.

7. Testing whether a new drug is not much worse than the standard, where nobody thinks it's better, but it would cost a quarter as much. Gut: fine.

Is there a principle that gets all or most of these? What does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a trial starts with genuine uncertainty, but halfway through an independent monitoring committee sees the new drug pulling clearly ahead on survival — not enough to trigger the pre-specified stopping rule, but enough that you'd personally bet on it — and the trial just keeps going to full enrollment. Is that fine?

And be honest: does the principle force me to give up any of my verdicts, or does it have to get complicated to save them all?
