I teach an advanced undergraduate seminar on chance and modality, and I need a sorting instrument — specifically a decision tree with a compact scoring key — that classifies a person's view about how possibility and objective probability relate, on the basis of their answers to concrete cases, not on which label they choose for themselves.

Assume the respondent grants a modest starting point: there are objective chances at least for some physical set-ups (coins, dice, radioactive decay), and the respondent has a working pre-theoretic notion of "possible" before the tree begins. Assume no training in measure theory, but the respondent can handle "probability zero" and "probability one" as standard probability textbooks define them.

Each internal node of the tree must be a question or short case answerable without technical vocabulary; prefer a vignette followed by a forced choice. The following cases must appear somewhere in the tree:

1. A fair coin is tossed; the respondent is asked whether landing exactly on its edge is possible, and what chance, if any, it has.
2. A device produces a real number chosen uniformly from the interval [0, 1]. Before the run, is it possible that the output will be exactly 0.7, given that any particular real number has probability zero under the uniform distribution? Does "probability zero" settle the question?
3. A fair lottery has one winner among a billion tickets. After the draw, the holder of a losing ticket says, "I could have won." Does that statement require that the ticket had a nonzero chance of winning, however tiny, or could it be true even if the ticket's chance were zero?
4. A fair coin is flipped infinitely many times. Any particular infinite sequence — all heads, say, or a perfectly alternating sequence — has probability zero. Is it possible that the sequence will be all heads? Is this the same sense of "possible" as in case 1?
5. A statistical-mechanics story: all the air molecules in a classroom spontaneously move into one corner. The event is assigned an astronomically small probability, but not literally zero. Do you call this event possible? Is that a different sense of "possible" from the one at work in cases 2 and 4?

Build the tree from the logical space of answers to these cases first, and treat the following as target regions to locate within that space. The tree should terminate in no more than six leaves, and at least these four views must appear as leaves, under whatever labels you judge apt:

(a) the regularity view: a possibility always has positive probability, and apparent counterexamples are to be redescribed rather than accepted as genuine possibilities;
(b) the null-possible view: probability-zero events can be genuinely possible, and "almost surely" does not mean "necessarily";
(c) an epistemic-possibility view: most ordinary "possible" talk tracks information or credence rather than objective chance, so the question usually changes subject before it can be answered;
(d) a modal-separatist view: possibility is a modal notion that does not by itself place any probability measure over outcomes, and asking for a probability adds structure the modal claim never committed to.

You may add up to two further leaves if you find distinct stable answer patterns worth separating. For each leaf, give a short name (or "unnamed" if no established label fits) and one sentence stating the most awkward commitment the holder cannot easily dodge.

Include a scoring key: for each leaf, state which pattern of answers to the branch cases takes a respondent there. Then add two diagnostic notes. First, identify at least one combination of answers that the tree treats as consistent but that you suspect no systematic view in fact occupies, and say whether it is unoccupied because it is unstable, merely unexplored, or actually incoherent. Second, flag any pair of leaves your branch cases cannot decisively separate, and specify a further concrete case that would split them.

Keep the finished instrument usable by a student who has never encountered the terms "regularity," "measure," "modality," or "chance–credence norm": the sorting must be driven entirely by the cases.
