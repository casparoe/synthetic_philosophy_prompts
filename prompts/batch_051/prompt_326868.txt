The question I set was:

In the Pensées, Pascal argues that it is rational to believe in God even if you assign only a very small probability to God's existence. Reconstruct the argument in decision-theoretic terms, then present and assess what you take to be the strongest objection to it. Does the wager successfully show that belief (or trying to believe) is the uniquely rational course of action?

The student wrote:

Pascal's argument is best read as an expected utility argument. There are two states, God exists and God does not exist, and two acts, believe and don't believe. If you believe and God exists, you get an infinite payoff (salvation); if you believe and God does not exist, you lose finite worldly pleasures. If you do not believe and God exists, you get infinite punishment; if you do not believe and God does not exist, you keep finite worldly pleasures. So the expected utility of belief is (p × ∞) plus a finite loss, which is infinite for any p > 0. This means belief strictly dominates non-belief, because its expected utility is infinitely larger than that of non-belief, which at best is finite. That is why Pascal can say the rational thing is to believe even if you assign God a very small positive probability. The strongest objection is the many-gods problem: the same reasoning would favour any god that promises infinite rewards, including incompatible ones, so the wager can't single out Christianity. But I don't think this objection works, because Pascal is addressing a specific audience who already operate within a monotheistic framework and are choosing between the Christian God and something like atheism, not between every conceivable god. The other standard objection is that you cannot believe at will. Pascal has a response here too: you can cultivate belief through practice—going to mass, taking holy water, acting as if—and belief will eventually follow. So the wager succeeds in showing that belief, or at least the project of inducing belief, is uniquely rational.
