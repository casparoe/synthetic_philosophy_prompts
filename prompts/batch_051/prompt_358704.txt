I have spent the past several weeks inside a question I used to address only from the lectern. My partner has just been offered enrollment in a randomized trial for a cancer whose standard therapy is only modestly effective, and though I am not on the treating team, I am a physician with more than a passing knowledge of research ethics — and I find I have a private hunch that the experimental arm will prove better. The treating team is genuinely divided; their collective uncertainty does not seem to require that any one of them, or I, be perfectly balanced between the arms, and I am trying to understand what, if anything, it does require.

Please write a Petrarchan sonnet from the point of view of someone in my position. Let the octave give voice to the older, tempting demand — the one I keep falling back into — that a patient may be randomized only if the individual clinician is personally in equipoise, exactly balanced between the two arms. Let the turn into the sestet enact the realization that this demand places uncertainty in the wrong subject: what legitimates the trial is not any single doctor's flattened belief but the expert community's honest, unresolved disagreement. The sestet should work out what a private leaning may and may not do once that distinction is granted, and the closing couplet should state, as precisely as it can, what must be surrendered and what may be kept.

The poem should be accurate enough that a research ethicist would recognize the distinction between personal equipoise and clinical equipoise without wincing, and the pull of the private hunch should remain audible rather than being solved away.
