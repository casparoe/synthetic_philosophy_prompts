I help run a small religious community whose founding code, adopted a century ago by founders who brought it with them from the old country, includes a purity rule about food. In the handbook's translation the rule reads: "No member shall eat the flesh of the swine, and any vessel in which that flesh has been cooked shall be broken; for the uncleanness of the swine is in its flesh, and it passes from the flesh to the vessel and from the vessel to whatever food is put in it." We now face a dispute the founders plainly did not foresee. A member of the community works for a company that grows cultivated pork: cells taken from a pig decades ago are multiplied in bioreactors into meat that has never been part of a living, slaughtered animal — no blood, no carcass, no contact with any kitchen vessel that has held traditional pork. She wants to serve the product at a community meal, and the elders have split. Some say the rule does not reach it, because what the rule separated us from was the practice of slaughtering and eating the swine, and no animal was killed or even touched here. Others say the rule is about the flesh of the swine, and this product is, cell for cell, the flesh of the swine; if the rule does not reach it, then the rule loses its grip on exactly the case where technology makes the forbidden thing available without the forbidden history.

Please act as a philosopher of law and religion with expertise in the interpretation of purity codes, and think through the dispute carefully rather than reaching for the first plausible verdict. Begin by laying out the readings that follow from (a) the plain words of the rule, (b) the purpose the rule was meant to serve, (c) what the founders would have said if the case had been put to them, and (d) what a reasonable member of the community now understands the words to mean. Then say which of these approaches should govern here, and why. I am less interested in the practical ruling than in what the case reveals about what a purity rule of this kind was protecting all along — real contamination, or the maintenance of symbolic and sacred boundaries, the management of disgust, and the identity of the community — and whether that is a kind of value an ethical community should keep enforcing once it has been written into its founding law.
