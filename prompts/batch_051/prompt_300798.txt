I would like an essay-length case analysis of the 1989 cold fusion controversy, read through Paul Feyerabend's challenge to the view that science is governed by a single fixed method. The exercise should be a patient, two-way mapping between the theory and the episode: use Feyerabend to diagnose the case, and use the case to stress-test Feyerabend.

Here are the salient facts, which you may treat as given. In March 1989, the chemists Martin Fleischmann and Stanley Pons announced at a press conference, before ordinary peer review, that a tabletop electrolysis apparatus involving palladium electrodes in heavy water produced excess heat, which they attributed to nuclear fusion at room temperature. The announcement generated enormous public and scientific interest. Many laboratories attempted replications; most failed to find the reported excess heat; a United States Department of Energy review found no convincing evidence for cold fusion; the field was thereafter treated by most scientists as pathological and was relegated to the margins, while a small group of researchers continued to investigate the claimed effect.

Feyerabend's position, as I want you to understand it for this exercise, is the following. He held that there is no universal scientific method — no set of rules that has governed good science and could not be violated in the course of progress — and that the attempt to enforce such rules is historically inaccurate and normatively harmful. His slogan anything goes was not meant as a positive rule of practice; it was a deflationary remark about what is left once one abandons the demand for universal methodological rules. On his account, appeals to method often function socially to exclude anomalies and heterodox traditions rather than to secure truth.
