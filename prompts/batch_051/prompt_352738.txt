I have spent the past few months leading an adult education course on Reformation theology at my church, and sola scriptura has come to feel less like a settled inherited slogan and more like a live difficulty of my own. The principle attracts me because of its simplicity and its confidence: the Bible is a public text, God is not the author of confusion, and what a person needs for salvation can be grasped by a serious reader without surrendering conscience to an institution. In our sessions, though, the same group of people, whilst all affirming that Scripture is our final authority, divides week after week over what the text actually requires. We have split over how the church should treat divorced and remarried members, over the nature of Christ's presence at the Lord's Supper, and over the relation of faith to works, each side insisting that it is merely reading what is plainly there and each side accusing the other of smuggling in an unacknowledged tradition. The usual resolution offered to me is that obscure passages must be interpreted in light of the clear ones, Scripture being its own interpreter; but I have come to realise that this presupposes the very thing it is meant to deliver, since deciding which passages are the clear ones is itself an act of interpretation, and the rule for making that decision is not, so far as I can see, written anywhere in the text. I do not want a verdict on who is right. I want to understand what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that is given up, what it would take for the principle to be amended or abandoned, and whether there is a defensible reading of the perspicuity of Scripture on which my confidence in the text and my sense that interpretation does not come to an end in the text can both survive.
