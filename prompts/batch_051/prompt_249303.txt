A friend and I have just spent a long argument on a question about public reason and religious appeals in politics, and we came down on opposite sides; I want a careful written analysis to break the impasse.

A city council's Standing Orders contain this clause, Section 12(c):

"In a public session of the Council, a member may invoke a religious or other comprehensive doctrine in explaining her position on an item of business, provided that, before the Council votes, she also states a justification for that position that citizens of any reasonable comprehensive doctrine could be expected to accept."

At the first hearing on a housing ordinance, a council member supports the ordinance by declaring that her faith commands care for the stranger and that her vote is an act of obedience. A two-minute clip of the statement goes viral and quickly becomes the main public frame for the ordinance. At the continued hearing, just before the vote, she reads into the record a secular-sounding justification for the ordinance. The text was drafted by a staffer using a large language model; the member did not review it before reading it into the record, does not regard its reasons as the real basis of her position, and has not checked its empirical claims. The justification is entered into the record, but the viral clip remains the dominant public representation of the issue.

Has she complied with Section 12(c)? Please work through what the plain words of the clause, its evident purpose, what its drafters would most plausibly have said if asked, and a reasonable citizen's understanding each imply, and then say which approach should control here and why. Also tell me which argument or consideration would most likely change your conclusion, and where your reasoning is most vulnerable to failure. Please write the answer in American English.
