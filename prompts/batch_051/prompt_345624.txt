One of the more influential arguments for the possibility of aligning very capable AI systems rests on what is often called factored cognition. In its standard form, the argument claims roughly this: even if no single human can evaluate the whole behavior of a system far more capable than any human, that difficulty can be dissolved by decomposing the task into a tree of smaller sub-tasks, each small enough to be checked by a human using only a limited context. If a model is then trained to reproduce that tree of human judgments—each leaf checked, each combination checked, and so on up the tree—then no part of the resulting behavior has gone unexamined, and the system can outstrip its overseers without escaping their oversight. Treat that argument as the target.

Now suppose we change the regime in which the argument is applied. The system's abilities are no longer produced by any explicit decomposition; instead, a single large model is trained on an enormous corpus of human text and acquires something like whole-task competence—drafting, reasoning, planning—as an opaque, monolithic capability. Its intermediate processing is not naturally organized into a tree of human-checkable steps, and humans often cannot articulate the sub-questions into which the work ought to be factored. Does the factored-cognition argument survive this shift? Identify which premise is under the most pressure: the claim that tasks are decomposable in the required way, the claim that each leaf is reliably checkable by a human with limited context, or the claim that the training signal must recapitulate the decomposition in order for oversight to transmit upward. Then say whether the argument should be treated as dead, as an idealization that still earns its keep, or as a normative ideal of deliberation rather than an empirical hypothesis about how competent systems will be built. If a weaker version survives, state that version precisely and say what work it can still do.
