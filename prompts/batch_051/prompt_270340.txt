Below is a short-answer question from an upper-level undergraduate course on the ethics and governance of artificial intelligence, followed by a student's answer. Grade the student's answer as the teaching assistant for the course.

Question: Isaac Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics are sometimes invoked as an early sketch of how to keep advanced artificial agents safe. What do the Three Laws get right as an alignment proposal, where do they fail, and what overall verdict would you defend about their relevance to AI safety today? Be precise about the difference between the laws' content and the interpretive machinery needed to apply them.

Student's answer:

"Asimov's Three Laws are usually paraphrased as three constraints built into a robot. The first law says a robot may not injure a human being, and may not stand aside and allow a human being to be injured. The second law says a robot must obey the orders that human beings give it, unless obedience would conflict with the first law. The third law says a robot must protect its own existence, except when that conflicts with the first or second law.

The proposal deserves credit for treating safety as a matter of standing constraints rather than as one preference to be traded off. The laws are lexically ordered: the first overrides the second, and the second overrides the third, so no robot can justify injuring a human by appealing to obedience or self-preservation. The laws also state the system's obligations in explicit, inspectable language, which is more than can be said for a system whose behavior emerges from an uninterpretable learned objective.

Still, the proposal fails. Its deepest weakness is that the laws are constraints, not a complete specification of what the robot should do. They forbid injury and disobedience, but they leave the robot's positive aims almost entirely open. Under the most natural reading of the first law, however, a robot must intervene whenever it can prevent harm; generalized, that amounts to an obligation to promote human welfare as much as possible, because any unmet human need is a harm the robot could have prevented. The law then gives no determinate way to weigh the demands of different people, and Asimov's stories repeatedly show the laws producing contradiction and paralysis rather than safe action.

The deeper lesson is that the failure lies in relying on explicit rules at all. Since terms like harm and obey can be interpreted in too many ways, any explicit rule-based approach to AI safety is doomed, and safety must instead be built into the system's goals or values. I therefore conclude that the Three Laws are best read as a literary device for generating plots about conflicting duties, and that they have almost nothing to teach contemporary work on alignment."

Grade the answer out of 20 and assign a letter grade. Base the grade on a marking scheme with four elements: (1) accurate statement of the laws; (2) a fair account of what the proposal gets right; (3) a precise diagnosis of where and why it fails; and (4) a defensible overall verdict. Structure your response in exactly the following sections, in this order:

- Grade and one-paragraph justification (roughly 100–150 words): lead with the mark and letter grade, then give the core justification in one paragraph.
- What the answer gets right (roughly 150–200 words): identify the main ideas the student handles correctly.
- What the answer gets wrong (roughly 200–250 words): identify each substantive error, quote the student's own wording, and explain precisely why it is wrong. Separate mistakes of interpretation from failures of reasoning.
- Rubric-level point-by-point comments (roughly 200–250 words): assess the answer against each of the four marking elements in turn.
- Feedback to the student (roughly 100 words): two or three sentences of constructive feedback addressed to the student.

The deliverable should read as a point-by-point assessment, not a holistic impression.
