I encountered the Dennett–Caruso exchange on free will and punishment in the free-will threads of the rationalist forums I have read for years, and I now want to teach it through a novel rather than through argument alone. I run a discussion-based evening course for adults who have each read Crime and Punishment at least once but have no background in academic philosophy. In our next three-hour session we are pairing the novel with that debate, and I need a reading I can actually teach from, one that puts pressure on both positions instead of letting the book stand as an illustration of one of them.

The two positions, as I understand them, are these. Caruso is a free-will skeptic: he holds that no one possesses the kind of free will that would make them morally responsible in the basic-desert sense, where desert means deserving blame or suffering just in virtue of the act and not for the sake of future goods. Retributive punishment is therefore unjustified. What remains is a public health quarantine model on which dangerous or destructive behavior is handled something like a contagious disease: society may restrain people to protect others, but only as a last resort, under the least restrictive conditions compatible with safety, and always with prevention, rehabilitation, and reintegration as the aim. Dennett replies that this attacks a contra-causal, libertarian free will that practically no one needs. On his view the capacities worth wanting—self-control, responsiveness to reasons, the ability to revise one's behavior in light of criticism and to choose under pressure—are real, are compatible with determinism, and are enough to make people proper objects of blame and punishment. He is willing to say that someone who had those capacities and failed to exercise them can deserve censure, though he does not think punishment needs a metaphysical foundation beyond the kinds of responsibility those capacities support.

Write me the reading. Where does Crime and Punishment itself side with, dramatize, or undercut each picture? Work through the murder, Raskolnikov's rationalizing article and his conversations with Porfiry, the confession to Sonya, and the epilogue's movement toward some kind of renewal: does the novel treat Raskolnikov's suffering as punishment he deserves, as a kind of quarantine or treatment, or as something neither debater can name? I am especially interested in where the novel resists the lens—for instance, whether its Christian grammar of sin, confession, expiation, and rebirth through another person depends on a notion of desert Caruso rejects and Dennett cannot quite secure. Do not summarize the plot or spend words on the novel's publication history; assume we know the novel, and give us the analysis. American English, please.
