A friend and I were arguing about what it means to say "the cup is on the table." I said it means the cup stands to the table in a certain relation. She said: fine, but then what's the difference between there being the cup, the table, and the relation of being-on, all sitting there as three seperate items, and its actually being the case that the cup is on the table? I said the relation has to actually relate them. She asked what makes it relate them. I wanted to answer "instantiation," but then I realized instantiation is just another relation, and now we need to know what makes instantiation hold between the cup, the table, and being-on, and it never stops. The same shape came up in a class on propositions. A sentence like "the cup is on the table" is supposed to have parts that combine into something that can be true or false. But combining them seems either to leave you with a mere list of parts, or to require some extra ingredient that does the combining, and then that ingredient needs to be combined with them, and so on again.

I can't tell whether these are two faces of one problem or just two similar-looking problems. I know "infinite regress argument" is a much broader category, so I'm not asking for that. Is there an established name for this specific thing, and how exactly does it differ from a generic infinite regress? Also, is the proposition case the same problem as the cup-and-table case, or a cousin? A bare label won't help me much—I want to know where the edges are.
