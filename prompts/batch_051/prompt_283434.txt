As a retired natural scientist—I spent most of my career in physical chemistry—I have recently been reading philosophy of mind, and one argument keeps troubling me. It is built around the well-known Chase–Sanborn coffee-tasting example, though I want to reconstruct it in my own words to see whether it is sound. It seems to show that qualia, understood as intrinsic and directly knowable qualitative properties of experience, are an incoherent posit. That conclusion is too strong, but I cannot find the step that fails.

Here is the argument:

(1) If a quale is a real, intrinsic, private qualitative property of an experience, then a competent subject can, under cognitively favorable conditions, tell whether that property has changed or remained the same across two experiences.

(2) In the Chase–Sanborn case, two expert coffee tasters both report a change in their assessment of the same coffee. One attributes the change to his standards having become more demanding, while insisting that the coffee tastes exactly the same to him as it always did. The other attributes the change to a shift in the taste itself, while insisting that his standards have not moved.

(3) No further behavior, verbal report, or physiological measurement could settle which taster is right, because any additional evidence would itself need to be interpreted either as revealing a change in standards, a change in the experience itself, or a change in the subject's access to or memory of the experience.

(4) Therefore the distinction between "the taste itself changed" and "my standards changed" is not a distinction between two independently discoverable states of affairs.

(5) If that distinction is not independently discoverable, then the supposed quale—the very taste property that was introduced to explain the difference—has no stable identity conditions and is doing no genuine explanatory work.

(6) Therefore we should abandon qualia.

(7) But this proves too much. The same style of reasoning would apply to a patient who says "the pain changed from sharp to dull" or a subject in a psychophysics experiment who says "the patch now looks more orange than red." In each case we could reinterpret the report as a change in the subject's standards for applying words rather than a change in the experience itself. Yet we do not think such first-person reports are systematically meaningless or merely about word use.

(8) Since that is unacceptable, something must have gone wrong in (1)–(6).

Please respond as a professional philosopher of mind who specializes in consciousness and qualia. Think carefully and begin by brainstorming the possible readings of the key terms—especially "directly knowable," "standards," and "the experience itself"—before committing to a diagnosis. Tell me which premise or step you reject, why it initially looked plausible, and what accepting the alternative costs. If the argument is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to give up and why. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would most likely lead you to a different verdict, and where your own reasoning about this is most fragile. I would most like to keep the idea that people can be correct or incorrect about changes in their own experience, but I do not want to take that as simply obvious.
