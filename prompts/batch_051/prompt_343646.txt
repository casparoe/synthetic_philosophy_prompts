I underwrite specialty commercial liability for a mutual insurer, mostly six- and seven-figure limits. Six months ago I moved from a book of business with decades of loss data to a line in which one or two files a month present exposures with very little usable history: energy-storage installations, autonomous delivery pilots, volunteer-staffed music festivals. Two recent renewals forced me to admit that my standard actuarial method, which works when the probabilities are unknown but estimable, comes apart when the probabilities themselves are unsettled. I have since read enough about the Ellsberg paradox and ambiguity aversion to recognize the feeling it describes: I was treating all uncertainty alike, either pretending the ambiguity wasn't there and pricing as if one clean probability existed, or letting it turn into blanket pessimism that no one could audit.

I want you to turn this distinction into a reusable underwriting procedure I can run in about two hours per risk, with sign-off from our pricing committee, and that a non-philosopher colleague could follow from the instructions alone. I don't want a literature survey or a reading list; I want the reasoning itself. The procedure should: (a) classify a proposed risk as mostly risk, mostly ambiguity, or mostly model-less, and say what evidence would place it in each class; (b) state how ambiguity aversion should change the price or the decision to quote at each level, without pretending there is a unique probability to feed into the model; (c) identify when a point estimate is worse than no estimate; and (d) mark which steps are genuinely judgments about risk appetite rather than calculations dressed up as judgments. Then run the procedure on three applications: a commercial bakery with twenty years of claims data; an energy-storage installer with three years of scattered industry data and conflicting engineering reports; and a one-off drone-delivery pilot for which we have no comparable exposure. Make at least one application come out in a way I will not like, either by declining a risk I would rather take for the relationship or by charging a premium I expect to lose the account. End by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do, especially where it cannot replace the firm's decision about how ambiguity-averse it is willing to be. Please write the answer in American English.
