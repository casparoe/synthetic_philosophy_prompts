I am a novelist and serve on the seven-person jury of a national prize for first novels. We meet in three weeks to settle our screening rules for this year's cycle, and because I am the jury member who works as a writer rather than as a critic or scholar, I have been asked to draft the decision memo. The case is this: 141 novels were submitted. Our paid readers have prepared, for each one, a roughly 250-word report giving a summary, a judgment of the prose, and a recommendation. We will never get seven jurors through 141 books, and even a longlist, if honored seriously, will strain us. One juror, a translator, insists that a reader's report cannot even provisionally justify advancing a book. Literary value, he says, lives in the experience of reading; a judgment that a book is good cannot be transferred the way a fact can, and a report gives you evidence only of what the reader felt, not of how the book is. To vote a book forward without having read it is to treat borrowed feeling as knowledge. Another juror, a critic, calls this mysticism. We rely on expert reports in law, medicine, and countless practical matters when firsthand inquiry is impossible, she argues; here the alternative is not that every book gets read, but that our longlist merely mirrors the accident of what seven busy people happened to open. She proposes that reports count as legitimate but defeasible evidence, sufficient to advance a novel to the longlist on a majority vote, but never sufficient to exclude one without at least one juror's direct reading; and that no novel may win unless at least two jurors have read it in full. As a novelist, I feel the pull of both positions. Please draft the memo I will circulate: it should set out a clear policy with procedures (how reports may and may not be used, who may advance or block a novel, and what the winner-level reading requirement should be), give the reasoning in plain terms, and honestly answer the strongest argument the policy rejects.
