Something's been bugging me about the way machine learning papers treat their labels. They'll have a bunch of crowdworkers look at an image or a sentence and click a category, and then the paper calls those labels the 'ground truth' — as if the world itself had handed us the answer. The model gets trained and scored against that. But the same pipeline also report inter-annotator agreement, throws out examples where the workers disagreed, and sometimes lets annotators click 'unsure.' So the ground truth is pretty obviously manufactured by people with concepts, training, and biases, not just read off the raw data. I'm trying to figure out what theory of perception or knowledge this practice presupposes. Is it committed to something like Sellars's Myth of the Given — the idea that there's a raw, self-justifying layer of experience that can anchor everything else — or is 'ground truth' just a convenient idealization that's compatible with a more Sellarsian, anti-given picture where the labels are themselves just fallible judgments in the space of reasons? If you took the anti-given view seriously, what would a machine learning eval pipeline have to do differently, or is the whole practice philosophically underdetermined?
