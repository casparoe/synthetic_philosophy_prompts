I work as a software engineer, and I help run my organization's incident-review process. Our official policy is a blameless postmortem: when a production failure results from ordinary mistakes—a misread dashboard, a bad configuration, a change that passed all tests but failed in an unforeseen interaction—we do not discipline the individuals involved. The stated reasoning is that punishing people for outcomes they could not have anticipated or avoided only makes them hide errors and destroys the information the organization needs. But the same organization does discipline, and sometimes fires, people in other cases: an engineer deliberately deploying code after being told not to, or someone knowingly disabling a safety control before a failure occurs. The working distinction most people around here seem to rely on is whether the person had a genuine alternative at the moment of acting—whether they could have done otherwise.

Recently a friend told me about a family of thought experiments, I believe called Frankfurt cases, in which a person could not have done otherwise because an outside mechanism would have forced them to act if they had hesitated, yet the mechanism never actually intervenes and the person still seems responsible. That unsettles me, because our incident policy looks as though it is built on the idea that responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise, and that idea is exactly what those cases are meant to challenge. What theory of responsibility does our accountability practice actually presuppose? Does it strictly presuppose that a person can be blameworthy only if they could have done otherwise, or is that principle just one convenient way of drawing the line? If Frankfurt-style cases are correct, what would a coherent incident-review and discipline policy have to change, if anything? And are there other theories of responsibility that could justify our current line between blameless error and blameworthy conduct without relying on a could-have-done-otherwise test?
