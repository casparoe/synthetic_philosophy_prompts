So my friend has this hobbyhorse about what counts as genuinely moving versus just moving relative to something, and she's drilled me on a few cases she's sure about. She says that in Newton's bucket setup — once the water has sped up and is rotating along with the bucket, sitting still relative to it while the surface climbs the sides — the water is really spinning, not the bucket and not the room. She says a rock completely alone in an empty universe can't really be spinning, or really be doing any motion at all; with no other stuff around, saying it spins is like pinning a label to nothing. And she says a person on a normal fairground carousel is genuinely rotating — being thrown against the outside wall is what rotation is, according to her.

The case I keep asking her about and can't get a straight answer on is this. Picture a windowless trailer out in space, spinning at a steady pace relative to the stars. Somebody is strapped into a chair bolted down near the outer wall of the trailer, so they aren't moving relative to the trailer at all, but they're pressed against the chair the whole time anyway. Do you think she'd say that person is genuinely rotating? I'm less interested in the yes or no then in the rule she's actually running about rotation, what other rules would fit her three verdicts just as well, which of these cases does the real work seperating the rules, and what a further case would be that would settle which rule she's using.
