A friend summarized an article defending the right to exclude as follows: "Countries are like clubs. Just as you have no right to walk into a private club and demand a membership card, no outsider has a moral claim to settle in a state without that state's permission. Because states are self-governing communities, their freedom of association and their collective self-determination are really the same thing: control over who belongs. Without that control, a country would gradually dissolve into a different community, so open borders would destroy the very thing self-determination is supposed to protect. The article concluded that, since exclusion is just an exercise of self-determination, any immigration policy a state chooses is legitimate — and that anyone who objects to the right to exclude is thereby denying that political communities may govern themselves at all."

How fair is that as a summary of the philosophical case for the right to exclude? Please give exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Across the five points, say which parts a careful defender would accept, which are common simplifications, which are overstatements or misrepresentations, what a defender would likely object to, and whether the final inference — that critics of the right to exclude are committed to denying all self-government — follows from anything accurate in the summary.
