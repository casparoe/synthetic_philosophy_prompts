I am a PhD student in political theory, working on the way post-Enlightenment thinkers have tried to hold onto a notion of moral progress after the catastrophes of the twentieth century. In a seminar last week, I argued that a certain old teleological confidence in universal history no longer survives contact with manifestations like industrialized slaughter or the possibility of human extinction. A colleague responded by quoting the line that the arc of the moral universe is long but it bends toward justice, and then said my problem is that I am measuring a curve by one of its local minima. The room largely accepted this as a refutation.

It strikes me that the sentence is doing a great deal of work quietly. It could be read as an empirical claim about a long-run trend in some measurable quantity (abolition, declines in violence, the spread of rights), as a claim about a contingent tendency of human institutions, as a providential or theodical claim about a hidden order that redeems past horrors from the standpoint of the end, as a pragmatic or existential commitment about how one ought to act when the evidence is ambiguous, or as something else entirely. Some of these readings are almost trivially true, some are substantive and contestable, and some may be false or unfalsifiable in a way that matters for whether the slogan is even doing argumentative work.

I would like a systematic disambiguation. What are the distinct precise theses that "the arc of the moral universe is long, but it bends toward justice" could be expressing? For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and note which of these readings are the ones people usually intend when they deploy the line against historical pessimism, and which reading, if any, actually supports dismissing someone who points to catastrophic periods as exceptions or local minima.
