In my statutory interpretation course we have been working through the absurdity doctrine, and I would like your help with a case I have devised. Suppose a city ordinance from 1902 reads: "It shall be unlawful for any person to drive, push, or otherwise propel any vehicle upon the public boardwalk." The council enacted it after a horse-drawn carriage struck a pedestrian, and its members had horses, carts, bicycles, and early automobiles in mind. Today the only such vehicle to enter the boardwalk is a powered wheelchair, and it is its user's only workable route to her job. On a broad dictionary reading the text plainly reaches her: a powered wheelchair is a means of carrying a person, and she is propelling it. The literal result is that a safety measure has quietly become a barrier to her getting to work.

I am less interested in the separate disability-law question than in the interpretive one. Could you work through how the main approaches — the ordinary meaning of the words, the evident purpose of the ordinance, what the 1902 council would have said if asked about powered wheelchairs, and what a reasonable person reading the ordinance today would understand it to mean — each treat the case? Then discuss whether the absurdity doctrine (or the related principle that courts should not read a text to yield a patently unreasonable result) is properly triggered here: what has to be true about the outcome before a court may disregard what the text plainly says, and why should a court not simply enforce the text and leave the repair to the legislature? Please write the answer in American English.
