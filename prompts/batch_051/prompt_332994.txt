I have been developing a hypothesis about generic sentences—bare-plural generalizations such as "ravens are black," "ducks lay eggs," and "mosquitoes carry malaria"—and I would like an honest assessment of it. I call it the U-shape hypothesis. The claim is that a generic of the form "Ks are F" is acceptable to ordinary speakers only when the prevalence of F among Ks sits at one of two extremes: either F is nearly universal among Ks, with only negligible exceptions, or F is rare but practically significant—dangerous, costly, or otherwise decisive for how one must interact with Ks. Middle frequencies fail to license generics, as does low prevalence without practical significance.

The hypothesis explains why "ravens are black" and "people are right-handed" are accepted, while "ravens are albino" and "people are left-handed" are not. It explains the low-prevalence cases by the second arm: "mosquitoes carry malaria" and "sharks attack swimmers" are accepted because, although only a tiny minority of the kind has the property, the property is urgent. It also predicts that "mosquitoes are harmless to humans" should be rejected, even assuming it is true of almost all individual mosquitoes, because the property is non-urgent and so cannot use the low-prevalence arm.

I know the hypothesis is crude. Please assess it honestly. Does something like this already exist in the semantics or psychology of generic language, and under what name? Where exactly does it break? Does "ducks lay eggs" refute it, since only mature females lay eggs and only in season, yet the generic is accepted? Are there middle-frequency properties that speakers accept generically, or extreme-frequency properties they reject? Is "practical significance" the right criterion for the second arm, or a placeholder for something like danger, norm violation, permanence, or explanatory depth? Reconstruct the strongest version of the view you can, name the nearest existing ideas, and give an honest verdict on how much of it survives.
