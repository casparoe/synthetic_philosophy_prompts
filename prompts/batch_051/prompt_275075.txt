I am preparing an episode of a philosophy podcast whose guest is Ibn Rushd (Averroes), the Andalusian philosopher and jurist; the conversation will focus on his treatise arguing that the religious law commands those capable of demonstration to study philosophy, and that demonstrated truth cannot genuinely conflict with revealed truth. The audience already knows the broad shape of his position, so the conversation should not be introductory or exegetical.

Write eight to ten interview questions that force him to do real work where the position strains. Include at least one question on each of the following, and give every main question a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. Write the questions as actual questions, ready to be spoken, not as summaries of expected answers.

- The apparent circularity: he derives the obligation to philosophize from the same religious law whose interpretation is at issue, and then uses philosophy to disclose the law's deeper sense.
- The threefold division of modes of assent — demonstration, dialectic, and rhetoric — and the suspicion that this turns religion into an instrument for managing the masses while philosophers possess the real truth.
- The practice of taʾwīl (allegorical interpretation): the status of the apparent sense when it conflicts with demonstration, and who may be told that the apparent sense has been set aside.
- His quarrel with the mutakallimūn: he accuses the dialectical theologians of corrupting the vulgar by publicizing interpretations they cannot handle, yet his own treatise is a public document.
- The later double truth reading associated with Averroism, and whether his reconciliation preserves a single truth or two different standings for one proposition.
- The asymmetry between the two sources: whether demonstration can correct a practical command or ritual obligation, or whether philosophy is always the interpreter and revelation always the interpreted.
- The role of consensus (ijmāʿ) in his claim that no one may be convicted of unbelief over matters of interpretation, and where the outer boundary of permissible interpretation lies.
- A closing question about what would count, on his own principles, as a real conflict between demonstration and revelation rather than a merely apparent one.

No biographical or scene-setting questions; each question should make him solve a problem or defend a boundary, not restate a doctrine.
