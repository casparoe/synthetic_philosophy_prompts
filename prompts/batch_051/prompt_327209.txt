I sit on the research ethics committee of a teaching hospital, and I am the member expected to hold the line on clinical equipoise while the statistician worries about power and the safety officer about adverse events. I vote on two or three randomized trial protocols a month, often after only ten or fifteen minutes with the part of the protocol that matters, and my vote usually decides whether the chair gives final approval; a wrong call means either exposing patients to a trial that should not have run or blocking one that should. Our committee already has adequate checklists for consent, privacy, and risk minimization. What I need is a reusable decision procedure for the question those checklists do not settle: whether the trial is approvable on grounds of clinical equipoise. I understand the principle to be that randomizing a patient between arms is acceptable only when there is genuine and honest conflict in the relevant community of experts about which arm is better, so that no one is knowingly assigned to a treatment that expert opinion already regards as inferior. I do not want a survey of what people have said about equipoise; I want you to translate that principle into steps a non-philosopher can run on a protocol, and I want you to commit to definite rulings rather than laying out considerations for me to balance.

Please write the procedure as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. It should have a fixed order of questions, and for each question it should say what kind of answer counts as good enough to move on and what kind of answer stops the procedure with a no. It should also say where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts, and it should be honest about which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. I particularly want two things from the procedure. First, do not let it treat equipoise as one undivided thing. Say which level you propose to test when a protocol is approved: disagreement among the clinicians who would actually enroll patients, the trial taken as a whole, or something about the individual patient's own values, and explain which of these belongs to protocol approval and which to the enrollment of a specific patient. Second, give me a real answer to drift: a trial that honestly starts in equipoise can lose it as the data accumulate before any pre-specified stopping boundary fires, and the committee then has to say whether continuing to randomize remains ethical. Tell me how the procedure detects that condition and what it instructs the committee to do when it is detected.

Then run the procedure on three protocols and take a committed position on each, with reasons, and do not offer me a menu of options. The first is an industry-sponsored trial that would compare a new antiplatelet drug against a deliberately low, subtherapeutic dose of an established drug; the dose is inside the labeled range but is not what any cardiologist in our hospital would actually prescribe, and the comparator was plainly chosen to make the new drug look effective. The second is a pragmatic trial randomizing patients with uncomplicated appendicitis to laparoscopic or open surgery, where the surgeons in the hospital are genuinely split and the real trade-off is recovery time against cost and training burden. The third is a randomized trial for a rare cancer with no approved therapy, comparing a new drug with placebo after an interim look showed a survival trend strongly favoring the drug but below the trial's own statistical stopping threshold, and the sponsor, whose registration plan depends on completing the trial, wants to continue to the planned endpoint. For at least one of these, I expect the procedure to give a verdict I will not like; do not soften it. Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot decide for me and where the committee's own values have to do the remaining work.
