Ever since I caught myself calling one of my own decisions "intentional" when its side effect went badly but refusing the word when an otherwise similar side effect went well, I have been preoccupied with the Knobe effect and the debates around experimental philosophy. I have constructed the following argument, and I cannot tell where, if anywhere, it goes wrong.

(1) In the classic Knobe pair, subjects are told about a chairman who approves a program whose foreseen side effect will harm the environment in one condition and help it in the other, and who says he cares only about profit. Subjects say he harmed the environment intentionally but do not say he helped it intentionally.
(2) The two conditions are matched on the agent's beliefs, desires, and causal role; the only difference is the moral valence of the side effect.
(3) If the folk concept of intentional action were morally neutral, then a difference in nothing but moral valence could not change whether an action falls under the concept.
(4) The Knobe pair shows a difference in classification produced by nothing but moral valence.
(5) Therefore, the folk concept of intentional action is not morally neutral: moral evaluation partly drives the application of "intentional."
(6) If moral evaluation partly drives the application of "intentional," then no analysis of intentional action in purely non-moral vocabulary can be correct.
(7) Therefore, no purely non-moral analysis of intentional action can be correct.

I half-suspect the argument conflates how a concept is applied with what the concept contains, but I cannot articulate the point precisely. Is the argument valid? If not, state the first step at which it fails, give the strongest reading of that step on which it is true, and explain what the argument would still establish on that reading. If you take it to be valid, say which premise a defender of a morally neutral analysis should reject and why.
