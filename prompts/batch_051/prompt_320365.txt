I'm in a high-school political theory club, and for this week we had to read a chunk of Edmund Burke's Letters on a Regicide Peace, Letter 1 (1796). The passage that stuck with me is this:

> Manners are of more importance than laws. Upon them, in a great measure, the laws depend. The law touches us but here and there, and now and then. Manners are what vex or soothe, corrupt or purify, exalt or debase, barbarize or refine us, but a constant, steady, uniform, insensible operation, like that of the air we breathe in. They give their whole form and color to our lives. According to their quality, they aid morals, they supply them, or they totally destroy them.

Our club leader asked us each to write down our own "political profile" so the rest can see if it's internally consistent. Here's what I came up with:

1. I mostly buy Burke's point: the informal stuff—manners, habits, trust, unspoken rules about how to disagree—shapes a society more than explicit laws do.  
2. But I'm basically a liberal pluralist: I don't think the government should be in the business of making everyone live a single vision of the good life, and I want protections for people with rival values.  
3. I think social peace is much more fragile than people in my school's debate club admit; fast changes in custom and public etiquette can do real damage even when the laws stay the same.  
4. I also think marginalized groups need to be able to challenge majority customs, including by being rude, refusing to perform politeness, or breaking etiquitte when the norm feels like a trap.  
5. I'm drawn to the Confucian idea that rituals and small ceremonial habits—maybe like the li we learned about in world history—shape our moral feelings before we even get to arguments, so public habits are morally formative rather than just surface stuff.  
6. I sometimes suspect "harmony" talk is used to shut down legitimate conflict—but I don't think that means harmony is always a bad ideal.

So: audit this set. I need you to act like a professional political philosopher and think carefully before answering. Start by reconstructing each commitment in your own words, then go through the pairs and tell me which ones are genuinely in tension, which pairs only look like they conflict, and which tension is the most severe. For each real tension, say what the cheapest fix would be and what it would cost me, and then tell me the single revision that would clean up the most at once. I don't know where the problem is, so don't assume I'm willing to drop any particular one.
