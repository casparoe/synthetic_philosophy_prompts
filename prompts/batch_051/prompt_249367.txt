I'm in an aesthetics seminar and I keep coming back to the transparency debate about photography, which seems to have been stuck for decades, and my working theory is that the two sides are mostly doing different things with the word "see" rather than disagreeing about anything in the world. The transparency camp says photographs are more like mirrors or telescopes than like paintings: the image is mechanically caused by light from the scene, so under the right conditions, looking at a photo is literally looking at the thing itself — your late grandmother, not a picture of your late grandmother. The other camp says that's exactly the move that should be resisted: when you look at a photograph you are looking at a flat object covered in dye or pixels, and since the grandmother isn't present in your visual field, calling that "seeing her" stretches the concept until it snaps; a photo is a trace or a record, not a window. Both sides agree about the optics, the exposure chemistry, and the phenomenology; neither side is confused about how a camera works; and still nobody moves.

I don't want a verdict on who's right. I want a diagnosis: is this a substantive disagreement about perception, a covertly verbal one, a fight about which paradigm cases (mirrors versus paintings) get to anchor the concept, or a difference about what an explanation of seeing is supposed to accomplish? And under your diagnosis, what would count as actual progress — a distinction that dissolves it, a new experiment, a taxonomy of intermediate cases, a better question — or should we expect no movement, and why? The thing that bugs me seperately is boundary cases: people say a live CCTV feed or a mirror lets you see something, but a photo from half a second ago doesn't. Does the transparency view have a principled story about where that line goes, and does noticing this push the debate anywhere? Keep it under 800 words, and open with your bottom-line diagnosis.
