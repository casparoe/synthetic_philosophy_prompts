I'm putting together a lecture on the principle of indifference and I've realised my own committments on it are a bit all over the place. Before I teach it I'd like someone to poke at the set. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(a) If you're genuinely ignorant about which of several mutually exclusive outcomes will occur, you should split your credence evenly between them.
(b) This extends naturally to continuous magnitudes: if I'm ignorant of the value of some quantity, I should use a uniform prior over it.
(c) Bertrand's paradox doesn't show the principle of indifference is incoherent. It just shows that a chord chosen at random is underspecified, and the three answers (1/2, 1/3 and 1/4) correspond to three genuinely different randomisation procedures.
(d) A single state of ignorance can be described in more than one way. If I'm ignorant of a coin's bias, I might describe it with the bias p or with the log-odds log(p/(1-p)), and a uniform prior over one is not uniform over the other.
(e) But rational credences shouldn't depend on an arbitrary choice of how I describe the problem.

I can't tell whether this lot hangs together. Could you go through them and say which pairs are actually in tension, which only look that way, and what the cheapest revision would be? Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, ordered by how severe the tension is, with no introduction or conclusion, and in British English.
