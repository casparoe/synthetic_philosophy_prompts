So I've been down a rabbit hole lately on Bayesian updating, and one thing keeps bugging me: the textbook story says you observe E and condition on it, but in real lab life you never observe E flat out. You get a noisy readout and your confidence in E goes from, I dont know, 0.4 to 0.8, not to 1. I gather Richard Jeffrey's fix (probability kinematics, I think its called) is that you keep your conditional probabilities fixed and average over the observation partition, weighted by your new uncertain confidence in each possibility. I'd like my group to feel why the naive pretend-I-saw-it-for-certain move is wrong, rather than just hear me say so.

I run a philosophy of science reading group at our community center: about a dozen retired folks, mostly lab or engineering backgrounds, no formal philosophy, two hours including coffee. Can you design a game or activity for us that makes participants run into the problem of uncertain evidence themselves? Ideally they start by doing the intuitive thing — treating a noisy observation as if it were certain — and only notice from the scoring that something's off, then stumble toward the averaging fix before you name it. Use a proper scoring rule if you can, so overconfidence actually costs points, and the lesson shows up in the scoreboard rather than in a mini-lecture. Small stakes are fine (losers wash the cups) but don't force it.

Equipment should be just paper, cups, colored tokens or cards, maybe a coin. Hold the name Jeffrey conditionalization until the debrief. In the debrief, map specific moments in the game onto the formal pieces — the prior, the likelihoods, the uncertain observation, the weighted average over the partition. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when we run it (someone refuses to give a number, scores are noisy with only twelve people, that sort of thing) and how to handle it on the spot. And be straight about the gap I already suspect: in the game the confidence in the observation will come from a known noise process, but Jeffrey's rule just takes that confidence as an input and says nothing about where it comes from — so flag whatever else the game can't reproduce and the facilitator has to supply.
