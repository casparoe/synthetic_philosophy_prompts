Here is an exam question from an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of biology course, along with a student's answer. Grade the answer as the course instructor or a grader would.

Exam question:
Explain the difference between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function. Which of the two, if either, can make sense of the claim that a heart malfunctions when it fails to pump blood, and why?

Student's answer:
The etiological account holds that the function of a trait is the effect that explains why the trait is there and persists: the trait was selected or maintained because it produced that effect. Because function is fixed by this history, the account supports a normative notion of functioning, since a token trait can have a function it is failing to perform. The causal-role account holds that the function of a trait is its contribution to some capacity of a containing system, where which capacity matters is settled by the explanatory project at hand. It is deliberately non-historical and non-normative. On the malfunction question, the etiological account is clearly in better shape. A heart's function is to pump blood because hearts were selected for pumping blood, so a heart that cannot pump is malfunctioning. The causal-role account seems to lose malfunction, since a defective heart still makes some causal contribution to the body—it occupies space, alters pressure, and so on—so it still has a function on that account. But I think the causal-role account can be repaired: if we fix the relevant circulatory capacity as delivering oxygen to tissues, then pumping is the heart's normal role, and a heart that fails to pump is malfunctioning relative to that capacity. So in the end the two accounts differ mainly in temporal orientation—etiological functions look back to selection history, causal-role functions look at present contributions—and the choice between them is mostly a matter of explanatory preference.
