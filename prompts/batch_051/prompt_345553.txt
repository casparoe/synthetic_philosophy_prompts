I am a graduate student member of my university's emergency hardship committee, which awards one-off grants from a donor-funded account to students who have suffered serious financial shocks. We have no written policy, and we decide each application on its own facts while trying to stay consistent with our own track record. One member argues that we have been running, without quite saying so, a luck-egalitarian fund: on his account, we compensate brute bad luck and refuse to clean up the consequences of informed, voluntary gambles. Another member says that description flatters us, and that our real operating principle is a basic-needs floor: protect any student who would otherwise go without housing or food, whatever choices got them there. We have a new application this week, and the committee wants to know what our precedents actually commit us to.

Our recent decisions are these. We compensated a student whose laptop, books, and notes were destroyed in a fire that started in the next unit's faulty wiring; the student could not have foreseen or prevented it. We compensated a student with a congenital condition for a year of medication not covered by insurance. We compensated a student who had been defrauded in a pyramid scheme, even though the student entered the investment voluntarily, because the promoter had lied about the risks. On the other side, we denied a claim from a business student who lost his savings in a leveraged cryptocurrency position after signing the standard risk disclosures; his tuition, housing, and meals were untouched, and he retained a family backstop. We denied a student who chose to live in a flood-prone off-campus building to save on rent and declined inexpensive renters' insurance; when a flood destroyed her possessions, we held that the loss came from a fully informed gamble. And we denied a student who asked for a food-budget top-up because he had deliberately cultivated expensive organic tastes and felt worse off than peers on ordinary diets, though he was not actually undernourished.

The pending case: a doctoral student was offered a guaranteed emergency grant of $4,000 after her lease fell through, but she opted in writing — after the university had disclosed the odds and answered her questions — for a lottery giving her a 50 percent chance of $10,000 and a 50 percent chance of nothing. She lost. She now cannot cover next month's rent and has no family able to help, so she asks us for an emergency grant. Please work this through as a political philosopher who specializes in distributive justice and luck egalitarianism. Start by laying out the candidate principles that could fit all our past decisions, not only the two my colleagues mentioned; say what each principle would require in the lottery case; and identify the closest precedent. Then recommend what we should decide if we want to keep faith with our own track record, and tell me how confident you are in that recommendation. If our precedents underdetermine the case, say what principle we would be adopting with each option. Propose the further hypothetical case that would most cleanly discriminate among the remaining rules. And if you think the framing itself is confused — for instance, that a humanitarian emergency fund cannot be run on luck-egalitarian lines, or that I have described one of the precedents in a way that already stacks the deck — challenge that directly rather than working around it.
