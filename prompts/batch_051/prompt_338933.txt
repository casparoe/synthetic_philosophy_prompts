I'm an undergraduate in a cognitive science course on concepts and categories, and our current unit is on the Letter Spirit project's question of what makes every 'a' an 'a' despite enormous variation in shape, style, and handwriting. For a course project I need to build a "which theory of letter identity do you actually hold?" quiz aimed at readers with no background in the field. The quiz should have eight to ten concrete cases—for example, a two-story printed 'a' versus a one-story handwritten 'a', a cursive 'a' that looks nearly identical to an 'o' or a 'u', a heavily stylized logo 'a' with one part omitted or distorted, a newly designed letterform that is nevertheless read as 'a', and a scrawl ambiguous between 'a' and another letter—each with two or three answer options phrased in ordinary language. The scoring key should sort answer patterns into at least five positions: a classical feature-list view (identity fixed by a checklist of geometric parts), a prototype view, an exemplar view, a generative role-and-style view (identity fixed by abstract part roles such as bowl and stem that a style parameter realizes), and a deflationary use-based view (identity fixed by the practices of readers and writers). Please build the quiz from the logical space first: identify the two or three underlying separations (for example, is there a single best example? can recognition proceed by rule? are parts defined by geometry or by role? does community use settle identity?), lay out the combinations of answers they define, locate the five positions among those combinations, and briefly diagnose any unoccupied combinations as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any two positions the quiz cannot separate and say why. No question should ask readers which label they prefer; every question should be a case or claim someone can answer without knowing the theory names.
