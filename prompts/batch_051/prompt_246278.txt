I have been working through Aristotle’s account of money in Nicomachean Ethics V.5, the passage where he discusses exchange, need, and the origin of currency. As I understand it, Aristotle argues that exchange requires unlike goods to be commensurable, that need is what makes them commensurable, and that money was introduced as a representative of need so that exchange can take place even when the parties do not need each other’s goods at the same time. Money, on his account, acts as a kind of guarantee for future exchange: the person who has it can obtain what is needed later, and its value rests on convention rather than on the nature of the thing itself. The temporal mismatch between needs seems essential to his argument.

Now the parallel I keep noticing. In asynchronous programming, a Future or Promise is an object that stands in for a value that has not yet been computed; it allows a program to continue executing now by treating the future value as if it were already available, and to bind that value only when it actually resolves. The Future is a placeholder defined by a protocol rather than by the final value; it can fail to resolve, can be composed with other Futures, and can be awaited. It struck me that Aristotle’s money is doing something structurally similar within a network of exchangers: it is a placeholder for an unspecified future good, accepted by convention, that lets exchange proceed now despite non-coincident needs, and whose value can collapse if the convention fails.

Is this a genuine structural identity, a loose analogy, or a confusion? To make the question more precise: does a monetary economy stand to barter as asynchronous composition stands to synchronous call-return? What would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer from one domain to the other? And is there a predicted consequence of the analogy that turns out false — for example, money has no well-defined resolution event the way a Future does, or Futures do not generally serve as a store of value between unrelated computations — and if so, what does that failure reveal about the limits of the parallel?
