In a seminar on intergenerational justice this week, the conversation turned to whether climate damages in 2100 should count for less than the same damages today, solely because they happen in the future. The person sat next to me — an economics masters student — rolled his eyes and said 'a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush, no?', then moved on as though that settled it. I've since heard the same line used three different ways: as a point about not gambling a sure thing on an uncertain bigger thing, as a defence of ordinary impatience, and as something close to the claim that future people's welfare simply matters less than ours. I'm fairly sure these aren't the same claim, but I can't articulate the differences sharply enough to actually respond.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that one-liner might be expressing, ordered roughly from least to most controversial — uncertainty about whether the future good will arrive, opportunity cost, diminishing marginal returns, mortality, plain impatience, pure time preference that discounts welfare itself merely because of its date, extinction risk, and anything else that seems to be doing work — and give a verdict on each: trivial platitude, substantive and defensible, or probaly false as stated? The part I most need is which of these readings would actually license discounting the well-being of future people in a public-policy calculation, and which would only support something much narrower, like discounting my own expected payoffs under uncertainty. I'd like a menu I can take back to the seminar rather than a single 'correct' reading.

Could you also tell me, for each verdict, which argument or consideration would change your mind, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail? Please write the response in British English.
