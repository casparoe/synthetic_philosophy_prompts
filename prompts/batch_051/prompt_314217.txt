I work as a software engineer, and the multiplayer game I play in the evenings has started to produce a philosophical discomfort I don't have quite the right vocabulary for. The game has a serious internal economy: items are scarce, prices float according to supply and demand, and rare equipment can take months of play to acquire. The company's terms of service are emphatic that none of this is owned by players. Virtual currency, weapons, and characters are described as data to which a player holds only a limited, revocable license; the company can modify or delete them at any time, and a player who sells them to another player for real money will be banned. From my professional point of view, this is natural: a sword is a row in an items table with a foreign key to an account, and it would be a category mistake to say that a row was stolen.

But the same company does not consistently act as though that is all a sword is. When a server bug allows someone to duplicate a rare item, the company bans the exploiter, removes the copies, and describes the incident as damage to the economy rather than as unauthorized writes to a database. When an account is hacked and its inventory is emptied, customer support restores the missing items and talks about the player's loss and recovery in terms that sound closer to a drained bank account than to corrupted data. The company also sells some of these items directly for real money in its official shop while forbidding players to resell them, so an item has a cash price when the company sells it but is supposed to be pure licensed data when the player tries to transfer it. The practice therefore seems to treat virtual objects as unreal for some purposes and as genuinely ownable, stealable, and economically significant for others.

I am trying to work out what theory of virtual objects and virtual harms this combination of policies presupposes, if it presupposes any single theory at all. Is the company implicitly committed to the view that virtual items are a kind of property and that taking or duplicating them is a real harm to a person, rather than merely a terms-of-service violation or a game-balance problem? Or can all of these behaviors be explained cleanly as contract enforcement and economy maintenance, with the language of theft being only a metaphor? And if the company were forced to hold one view consistently, what would its policies have to look like — in particular, could it still forbid player resale while asserting that a hacked player has genuinely lost something?
