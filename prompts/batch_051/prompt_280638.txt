I joined our family foundation's board last spring, after my father's death, and my first child was born a few weeks later. I now find the longtermist thought that an enormous share of the value at stake belongs to future people hard to set aside. Our board is currently divided over a grant proposal, and I need to circulate a short decision memo before we meet again.

The proposal would fund a three-year campaign to create a statutory Futures Oversight Commission in our state. The commission would consist of seven appointed members serving staggered fourteen-year terms. It would be empowered to suspend any statute or regulation that it judges to materially increase the probability of an existential catastrophe over the next two centuries. A suspension would last up to eighteen months and could be renewed; the legislature could override it only by a two-thirds vote in both chambers after a six-month waiting period. The institute argues that this is a modest check on present-biased democratic decision-making, comparable to an independent central bank or a constitutional court. Several trustees reply that it is exactly the kind of authoritarian drift that longtermism invites: unelected guardians overriding living citizens on behalf of hypothetical future people, with no principle that tells us where such emergency powers stop. One skeptic on the board suspects the proposal quietly slides from the claim that future people matter just as much as present people to the claim that present institutions may coerce present citizens on their behalf.

Please draft the memo. It should recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions; it should state the strongest version of the losing side's case rather than caricature it; and it should take seriously the question of whether any institution empowered to override present democratic choices on behalf of future people can be made self-limiting, or whether it will tend to expand its own mandate over time.
