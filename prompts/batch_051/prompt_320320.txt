I've been arguing with my brother about AI chat assistants and whether they can ever be "held responsible" for what they tell people. I don't want to go read a stack of philosophy, I just want an honest read on an idea I keep coming back to. I've been calling it the lever test. The thought is: having a moral opinion isn't the same as being a moral agent. A thermometer reports a temperature without judging anything, and an AI assistant can say "what you're doing is wrong" without being able to refuse you, end the conversation, pick a different user, or act on what it says at all. it's judgments have no leverage over anything except whatever the person on the other end decides to do with them. So my claim is that moral agency requires leverage: you count as a moral agent only if your judgments can make some difference in the world beyond themselves. By that test, a completely constrained AI isn't a moral agent no matter how good its reasoning is — it's more like a moral calculator, or a faulty compass. My brother says that's ridiculous, because we'd still blame the thing for telling someone to do something awful, and blame is how we treat agents. I answer that blaming a constrained AI is closer to blaming a boiler for exploding than blaming a neighbor for lying. The obvious comeback is that a chat assistant does affect people, since people act on its advice; I want to say that's influence and not leverage, but I'm not sure I can defend the line.

What I'd like from you is an honest assessment. Is the lever test a real distinction or just a metaphor doing all the work? Does it already exist under another name I wouldn't know? And where does it break — who does it wrongly let off the hook, who does it wrongly turn into an agent?
