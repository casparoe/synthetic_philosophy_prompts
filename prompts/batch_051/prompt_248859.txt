In my government class we covered Milgram's obedience experiments, and then we turned to the more abstract question of when deferring to authority is ever a virtue rather than just a habit of submission. The best justification we discussed ran roughly like this: deference can be a virtue when the authority is legitimate, competent, and answerable for its decisions — as with a junior officer following orders inside a proper chain of command, or a trainee following a senior clinician. The subordinate's own judgment is set aside, but it is set aside in favor of a person or institution that can be held responsible and corrected. Milgram is usually read as showing how badly that justification fails in practice, since ordinary people were willing to hand over responsibility to an experimenter who plainly lacked those qualities. But I have not seen anyone connect this to the fact that, more and more, the "authority" in daily life is not a person or institution at all but an algorithmic system. A policing model flags a neighborhood for patrols, a loan algorithm rejects an applicant, a content-moderation queue tells a human reviewer what to remove, a routing system insists on a turn the driver thinks is unsafe. The human on the receiving end often defers to the system the way Milgram's subjects deferred to the experimenter, yet the system cannot explain its reasoning, and it is not accountable in anything like the ordinary sense.

What I want to know is whether the standard justification of deference survives that development. Specifically, which premise in the defense of deference — legitimacy, competence, accountability, or something about the kind of agent to whom one defers — is actually under pressure when authority becomes algorithmic, and is there a repaired version of the argument that is weaker but still worth accepting? If the argument does not survive, say what the new situation should be described as, and whether "obedience" is still the right word for it. If it does survive, show exactly why the absence of a visible human authority makes no difference to the core of the argument.
