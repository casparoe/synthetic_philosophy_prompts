I'm a retired chemist, and after a lifetime of being a bit impatient with philosophy I've finally got round to reading Mill properly. One thing that keeps nagging me is how his harm principle maps onto speech on large online platforms, which obviously didn't exist in 1859. I'd like you to think through what Mill would most plausibly have said about a social media company removing or downranking somebody's account for hateful but not directly inciting speech — the sort where you might say it is degrading a group, but nobody is being told to go and do anything in particular. The awkward part for me is that Mill's main enemies in On Liberty are the state, the law, and the informal pressure of majority opinion; he didn't have a category for a private corporation that isn't a government but functions rather like a public square. Would he see deplatforming as the platform exercising its own liberty (its right to choose its company, so to speak), or would he see it as exactly the kind of social pressure he wanted to restrain? And just how far could his notion of harm stretch without collapsing into a licence for censorship: can it cover cumulative, structural harm to a group, or would he have insisted that only conduct which threatens a reasonably assignable individual counts?

I'm not after the standard undergraduate line that offence is not harm and therefore speech should be almost never restricted. I want the genuinely uncomfortable version, where you have to say something precise about the corn dealer example: Mill seems to treat the same words as protected in print and punishable when addressed to an angry crowd outside the dealer's house, so the question becomes whether algorithmic amplification is closer to print circulation or to the crowd scene. Work through how he might have reasoned about that, and whether private moderation, because it isn't state action, escapes the harm principle altogether, or whether Mill's worry about public opinion would make him treat platforms as a new organ of social control. I suspect Mill's text underdetermins this, and I want to hear how you would fill the gap without pretending the text settles it. Please write the answer in British English. Also, having spent decades around lab results, I'd rather you be explicit about epistemic confidence: say how confident you are in the overall picture, and single out the point you are least sure of.
