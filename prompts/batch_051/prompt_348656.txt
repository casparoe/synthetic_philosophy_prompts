A friend walked me into this one and I can't find the exit. Here's the argument as I remember it, cleaned up a little:

(1) A corporation is a legal person: it can hold property, enter into contracts, and sue or be sued in its own name.
(2) But a corporation has none of the things people usually say matter for personhood — no mind, no consciousness, no interests of its own, no capacity to suffer.
(3) So legal personhood can be granted purely on the basis of a bundle of legal capacities, not on psychological features.
(4) A sufficiently capable AI system can be given that same bundle by statute: a registered agent, an account, standing to sue, the works.
(5) Therefore, if corporate personhood is legitimate, the usual objections to AI legal personhood (it has no mind, no interests, no suffering) are already foreclosed.
(6) And once you've got a legal person, it's arbitrary to deny it the constitutional rights other legal persons like corporations have, including free speech.
(7) So we seem stuck saying a chatbot has a First Amendment right to spend money on electioneering ads, which I find hard to accept.

So either corporate personhood is a mistake, or we have to swallow AI constitutional rights. I'd like to keep (1) and (2), and I'm pretty reluctant to give up the idea that corporate personhood is legitimate. Tell me which premise or inference you'd reject and what rejecting it costs. If the argument is actually valid, tell me which premise is least costly to give up and why.
