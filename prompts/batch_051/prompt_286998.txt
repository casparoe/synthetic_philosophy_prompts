I'm running an online adversarial collaboration between two people who've been arguing for months about whether Otto Neurath's picture-language project could ever actually work. One of them — call her M — is the Neurathian: she thinks the interwar Vienna ISOTYPE program (pictograms for public statistics, meant to be international and somehow neutral, non-persuasive, free of the emotional pull of words) was onto something, and that a picture language could in principle carry genuine semantic content rather than just decorating natural language. The other — call him C — is the critic: he thinks pictures can't do the things that make a language a language (negation, tense, conditionals, abstraction), and that whatever ISOTYPE achieved was good infographics riding parasitically on the natural languages it was supposed to bypass.

They've agreed to do the adversarial collaboration properly, and your job is to write the joint statement both of them could actually sign at the end. You're an expert in philosophy of language with a second head for visual notation and diagrammatic reasoning — think about compositionality, about what makes a notational system semantics rather than mere decoration. Take your time; before you draft, sketch candidate cruxes and discard the ones that are confused or merely verbal.

The document should be theirs, not a verdict on who wins. No bibliography or footnotes — it's an internal working document. It must have these sections in this order, with these rough lengths:

1. Preamble (2–3 sentences): what the collaboration covers, and what the document is for.
2. Agreed claims (numbered, 9–12 items; 350–450 words total). These have to be substantive, not safe mush. Make them cover what both sides now accept about the original ISOTYPE system (it could show quantities by repeating identical icons, and simple comparisons, but it had no direct way to display negation, temporal order, modality, or definitions of abstract terms), about interpretation always requiring learned conventions, and about what a notational system needs before it can count as a language.
3. Disagreements table (roughly 500 words). Columns: Issue; The Neurathian's wording; The critic's wording; Why this one still matters. Phrase each side's row so that side would sign it without wincing — no steelman caricatures.
4. Cruxes and tests (350–450 words). Pick three or four genuine cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of the two — and for each, give a concrete case, image, or design task both would accept as bearing on it, then write each side's prediction in advance. I suspect at least one crux will be the 'not' symbol, at least one will be about composionality (what you can understand when known pictograms get combined in new ways), and maybe one about whether the system's claimed neutrality survives a look at what its pictograms silently choose.
5. Where the dispute was verbal (100–150 words). Say which part of their year-long argument turned out to be about how demanding the word "language" should be, and which part survives even after the terminology is cleaned up.

Write the thing flat-out as the joint statement itself, not as a memo about how to write it.
