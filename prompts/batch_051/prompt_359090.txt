I am drafting a novel set in a world where a machine can insert a true belief into a person's mind directly: the belief arrives with full conviction but cut loose from evidence, method, and any sense of where its correctness might end. Two physicians in the story receive the same correct diagnosis, one from the machine and one from years of training. Under stable conditions they act identically, but when an unexpected complication arises the trained physician notices the diagnosis beginning to fail at its edges and revises it, while the machine-implanted physician clings to it past the point of damage and then drops it all at once. I have built a small theory from this observation, and I want it tested before I let the novel rest on it.

I call it the seam criterion of knowledge. A true belief is knowledge to the degree that the process that produced it also left behind a rough map of its own possible failure: which observations would loosen it, which facts it depends on, which neighboring beliefs support it, and what would have to be different for it to turn out false. Knowledge, on this view, is not more valuable than true belief because it is more certain or more justified; it is more valuable because it fails differently. A bare true belief is brittle: it shatters without warning and leaves the believer with nothing to revise. Knowledge is seamed: it tears along the lines of its own construction, and the tear shows the believer what to mend. The real advantage of knowledge over true belief is that knowledge fails well.

I recognize that this may simply be Plato's answer in the dialogue Meno, where true opinion is compared to an unfastened statue of Daedalus, apt to run away until it is fastened down by an account of the reason why. Please answer me as a professional epistemologist would: first reconstruct the strongest version of the seam criterion, then press it. In particular, tell me whether it genuinely answers the question of why knowledge is better than true belief or merely restates it; where it breaks, for instance cases of genuine knowledge that would fail badly or of bare true belief that would fail informatively; and whether it collapses into an existing account, and if so, which. Work through the two or three strongest objections, not the easiest ones. Then state how confident you are in your overall assessment, and single out the one point you are least sure about.
