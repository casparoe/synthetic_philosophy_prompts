I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but expected-value calculations have become a recurring problem at work, and I've been reading around the St. Petersburg game. In the standard version, a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, and the payout is 2^n dollars if the first heads comes on toss n; each outcome contributes (1/2^n)(2^n) = 1 to the expectation, so the expected payout is infinite. Recently I came across a variant called the Pasadena game, where the payout on toss n is (-1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n. Its expected-value series is 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which is conditionally convergent: the terms can be rearranged to sum to any real number or to diverge, so the expected value is undefined in the usual sense. That struck me as a deeper problem than a merely infinite expectation.

Here is the variation I'd like to understand. Take the Pasadena game and change one parameter: let the coin be biased, with probability p of tails on each toss (so the first heads occurs on toss n with probability p^(n-1)(1-p)). As far as I can tell, for p < 1/2 the expected-value series converges absolutely, at p = 1/2 it is the conditionally convergent Pasadena case, and for p > 1/2 the terms do not even go to zero, so the series diverges. If that is right, the paradox seems to live at exactly p = 1/2. Please work through the convergence behavior carefully, then explain how the standard ways of responding to St. Petersburg and Pasadena fare under this variation. Does an arbitrarily small bias away from fairness make the game unproblematic, and if so, should we really treat the fair coin as a discontinuous special case where rationality changes character? Do resolutions based on log utility, finite bankrolls, or resistance to pricing games by raw expected monetary value change their force when the issue is not an infinite value but an order-dependent one? If I have the convergence facts wrong, or if I'm misstating what makes the Pasadena case paradoxical, tell me that directly; I would rather have the setup corrected than receive an answer to a broken version of the question.
