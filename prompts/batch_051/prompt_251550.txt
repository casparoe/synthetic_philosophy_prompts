I'm in a third-year moral philosophy module and we've just done trolley problems and the doctrine of double effect. After the seminar, two people in my study group, Aisha and Marcus, got into a long argument about the loop variant, and I honestly couldn't referee it, and its been bugging me since. The loop case goes like this: the trolley is heading for five people, you can switch it onto a side track that loops back onto the main line before it reaches the five, and there's one person on the side track whose body would stop the trolley; if he weren't there, the trolley would loop round and kill the five anyway. Most of the seminar thought switching is permissible here, even though the one's being hit is causally necessary for saving the five.

The argument went like this. (1) Marcus said the loop case is a counterexample to double effect: the doctrine says you may not intend the one's death as a means, but here the plan only works because the one gets hit, so either the doctrine forbids switching (which nobody accepts) or people are quietly gerrymandering 'intention' to get the answer they already want. (2) Aisha said no, the doctrine only forbids intending the death itself; in the loop the agent intends to stop the trolley, and the one's death is a side effect of his being in the way, whereas in the footbridge case the agent's plan includes throwing the man into the path, so the death is intended there. The loop agent's plan would be just as good if the man somehow survived. (3) Marcus replied that this test doesn't separate the cases: in the bridge case the plan would also be just as good if the man survived and the five were saved, so 'would be glad if he survived' tells us nothing. He said the only difference Aisha had given is that the loop agent gets to describe her plan as 'stop the trolley with something heavy' while the bridge agent's plan gets described as 'throw the man', and that's a choice of words, not a fact. (4) Aisha said there is a fact underneath: in the loop the agent moves the trolley away from the five and the man is already on the track it is redirected onto, while in the bridge the agent moves the man into the threat's path. You may redirect a threat, but you may not redirect a person into it. (5) Marcus answered that 'already in the path' is now doing all the moral work, not intention, so DDE has been replaced by a different principle, and one with its own problems: what if the man is on the loop only because you paid him to stand there an hour earlier, or what if he fell there by accident? The doctrine was supposed to explain why that line matters, not just take it as basic. (6) Aisha conceded that the loop case shows the standard DDE needs supplementation, but said it doesn't show the distinction is useless, since redirecting a threat and making a person into a threat are still different kinds of agency. (7) Marcus said that concession is fatal: if DDE needs the supplement to get the right answer, then DDE wasn't doing the explaining in the first place, and the thing doing the explaining is whatever principle about paths and threats Aisha is now leaning on.

I'd be really grateful if you could go through this move by move and say who's right about what at each stage, which moves are strong and which are weak, and then give an overall verdict on whether the loop case sinks the doctrine of double effect or not.
