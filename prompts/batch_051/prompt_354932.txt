There's a classic move in linguistics where you define language by running down a checklist of design features — the idea being that every human language has them and no animal communication system has the whole set. For a long time one of the entries on that list was that language is vocal-auditory: produced by the vocal tract, perceived by the ears. Then signed languages got taken seriously as languages in their own right, and it became pretty clear ASL and others have syntax, morphology, phonology (just in the manual-visual channel), productivity, displacement, the works. So my question is whether the design-features argument actually survives that development. Which premise does the sign-language case pressure — is it just the vocal-auditory entry, or is it the deeper assumption that these features hang together as a checklist in the first place? And if a repaired version is possible, what should it look like: do you just swap in something like, language must organize some material channel, or does that end up saving the list by making it so thin it no longer does the original work of sorting human language from everything else? I want a verdict, not a survey of the linguistics literature.
