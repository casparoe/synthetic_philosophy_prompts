I've been going back and forth on selected-effects vs causal-role accounts of biological function — the ones that say a trait's function is what it was selected for, versus the ones that say a function is just the contribution a trait makes to some system capacity you care about — and nearly every supposed divergence either collapses once you pin down the background system, or just turns into the feathers-are-exaptations example again. I want a case built specifically to keep the two accounts apart under pressure.

Construct a thought experiment that meets these specs:

1. A population fixes a trait T by drift alone. No selection on T for any effect — not in the lineage, not via pleiotropy, not via hitchhiking. If a selected-effects theorist tries to find a selection history, there shouldn't be one to find.

2. Right now T makes a specific causal contribution C to some organism-level capacity S of the sort a Cummins-style causal-role account would count as a function. Name S and C explicitly and say exactly what system you're analyzing T in. A selected-effects account must judge that T has no function at all at this time.

3. Now modify the case: starting today, a change in the environment makes C the difference between leaving surviving offspring and not. There is (say from a modifier locus) heritable variation in how much C T produces, so selection can act on it. After many generations the trait is maintined by selection for C. Say precisely what the selected-effects account says now: does the function exist from the first generation selection acted, or only after some later point, and does it get applied retroactively to the pre-selection copies of T and to their unselected descendants? Say what the causal-role account says at each time as well.

4. No stipulating the theories' verdicts; the drift history and the C-to-S contribution are the features that have to generate them. No designers, no foresight, no exotic biology — gene duplication, neutral mutations, structural drift, novel toxins, and new predators are all fine.

The case is for a short argument about whether selected-effects accounts have a principled answer to the question of when a function comes into existence, so flag any point where you're forced to make a stipulation instead of deriving an answer.

After you've given the scenario, run the reasoning for both accounts at both time points, then tell me which structural feature of the case is doing all the work. If you remove the drift-then-selection structure so T is selected for C from the start, does the whole thing collapse into a boring heart-pumps-blood agreement? And how would a defender of the selected-effects account most plausibly push back — by denying that C is a function rather than a side-effect, by broadening their notion of selection history, or by saying the function begins exactly when selection does? Say whether that move looks ad hoc or independently motivated.
