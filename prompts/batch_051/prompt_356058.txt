I keep noticing something with chatbots and i can't tell whether it has a name. You give a model a reasoning problem, it gives an answer, and then you ask it to walk through why it gave that answer. It produces a tidy step-by-step story — "first I noticed A, which means B, so the answer has to be C." Totally fluent. But when i run the same problem again with the answer choices in a different order, or with a stray sentence at the start that makes the wrong option superficially tempting, the model often lands on the same final answer but the whole walkthrough changes to match: different first step, different intermediate claim, sometimes a different account of what the question even was. And if you ask it "did you actually reason in that order before answering?" it says yes, every time. Where I've had access to token probabilities, the answer sometimes looks basically settled after the first few tokens, before the explanation could have been assembled. So the explanation looks like it's being fitted to a decision that already happened, not read out from the causes of that decision.

I know this is somewhere near confabulation, and near rationalization in the ordinary sense, and near the old psychological point that people don't have direct access to why they did something. But those concepts were built for people, where you can't open the hood; here you partly can. Is there an estabished term for this specific thing — fluent, self-consistent, but causally unfaithful explanations of one's own outputs — and how exactly does it relate to confabulation and rationalization? Does it even make sense to call it unfaithful, given that the model doesn't have some private "real reasoning" to be unfaithful to? I don't just want a label; I want the boundaries, and a sense of which nearby term is the least misleading.
