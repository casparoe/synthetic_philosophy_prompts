Ok, so I'm teaching a unit on pain next week—philosophy of pain, not the clinical stuff—and I want to put a poem on my last slide, something to send them out with. I'd rather the form do the philosophical work than have the poem just decorate a summary. Could you write a villanelle from the point of view of someone with pain asymbolia? In case it helps: the sensory-discriminative component of pain is intact in these patients, so they can locate the pinprick and grade its intensity, but the affective-motivational component is gone—it doesn't feel bad, there's no urge to withdraw, no suffering. It isn't analgesia and it isn't congenital insensitivity to pain; they feel something and can identify it as the kind of thing other people call pain, but the ouch has gone out of it.

Here's the structural part I care about. Let the two refrains do the work. Make one refrain the sensory side—flat, a little clinical, naming the stimulus or its place and intensity. Make the other refrain the missing badness—something like how it doesn't matter or doesn't pull at them. In someone with ordinary pain those two would be fused into a single experience, but the villanelle should keep them apart with its returning lines, so that when the refrains finally come together in the closing couplet it doesn't read as a healing or a return to normal; it reads as the dissociation itself. Someone who works on pain should recognize the phenomenon, and a philosopher should see the wedge it drives between the sensation of pain and the badness of pain—the standard move is to say asymbolia shows the badness is a seperable component rather than something built into the raw feel. Don't resolve that. Leave open whether this person has lost something essential to pain or whether the rest of us were wrong to think the badness was part of the sensation from the start.
