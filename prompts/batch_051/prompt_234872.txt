For the past year I have been working in public-health communication, and I keep noticing that the same advice spreads or stalls depending almost entirely on who delivers it. A handwashing mnemonic I introduced was ignored for months until a senior nurse began repeating it; within a fortnight it was everywhere. The same content, unchanged, went from dead to ubiquitous, and no feature of the mnemonic itself had altered. That observation pushed me toward a distinction I now want tested. I call it the difference between intrinsic and positional selection among cultural variants. A variant is intrinsically selected when it spreads because of properties carried in the variant itself: it is more memorable, more emotionally charged, easier to say, or more directly useful. A variant is positionally selected when it spreads primarily because of where its carriers sit in a social network: a teacher, a celebrity, a person at a structural bottleneck, or a member of a trusted in-group. I am aware that this overlaps with the cultural-evolution literature's distinction between content bias and model-based or context bias, but I want to press the point further than I think that literature does. My thesis is what I have been calling positional dominance: the claim that the striking successes usually offered as examples of memetic selection — catchphrases, taboos, fashions, rumours, public-health advice — are overwhelmingly positional effects dressed up as content effects. On this view, a Darwinian theory of culture misleads when it models selection as occurring in a shared pool of variants competing on their intrinsic merits, rather than as the differential exposure produced by a structured network. I expect the decisive counterexamples would be variants whose content clearly drives their spread even when their carriers are peripheral, and I would be most persuaded by a case in which identical content succeeds from weak positions and fails from strong ones. What I want is an honest, committed assessment in British English: is the distinction coherent and does it do real work, has it already been developed under another name, what exactly does positional dominance get right, and where does it break? I would rather you took a definite position on whether the thesis is a genuine advance or a redescribed piece of existing theory, and defended it, than that you laid out alternatives and left me to choose.
