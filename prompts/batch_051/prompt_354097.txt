That taxation is theft is a slogan, so I want you to price a sharpened version of it rather than grade the slogan. Give me your actual credence in the following proposition: for the median full-time worker in a developed democracy, the state's extraction of the portion of their pre-tax earnings that funds transfers to other able-bodied adults, as opposed to public goods the worker themselves consumes, wrongs the worker in the same fundamental way theft wrongs its victim — by non-consensually appropriating what is rightfully theirs — even though it differs in publicity, incidence, and appropriate remedy. Treat the revenue as earmarked for those transfers, since tax money is fungible. Then decompose the number. I want at least these components: how much weight rests on (a) whether any non-consensual authority can acquire legitimate jurisdiction over a person's earnings; (b) whether the pre-tax distribution is even the correct baseline rather than a convenience of the legal order; (c) whether hypothetical consent or benefits received can ground a real obligation to pay; and (d) whether the wrong, if any, lies in the extraction itself or only in the redistributive spending. Assign a rough credence to each, show how they combine into the final number, tell me which single component the final number is most sensitive to, and say what argument or evidence would move that component the most.
