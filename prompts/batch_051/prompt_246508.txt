Two people in my reading group got into it last week about whether radical skepticism can actually be answered, and I genuinely can't tell who was winning. It started when J said you can't non-circularly defend trusting the senses: any argument for their general reliability would have to use premises the senses supplied, so the whole defense is dead on arrival. (1) K replied that the same circularity takes the skeptic down too, because the skeptic's examples of illusion, dreaming, and the evil demon are all gathered through the senses, memory, and reasoning, so the skeptic isn't standing on any firmer ground. (2) J said that's not symmetric. The skeptic isn't claiming to know anything; he's just pointing out that the defender hasn't met the defender's own standard, and that's enough to lower confidence. (3) K said that move only works if "justify from no trusted sources at all" is a demand that makes sense, and the problem of the criterion is exactly a reason to doubt that: you can't identify good sources without a criterion, and you can't defend a criterion without already treating some sources as good. So the standing-start demand is a philosophical preference, not an obligation the defender has to meet. (4) J said fine, then you'll start from particular "clear cases" like seeing your own hands, but those are precisely the cases the skeptic is putting in doubt, so starting there is just prejudice. (5) K said it isn't mere prejudice if we can't do otherwise. After the skeptical argument is over we go right back to believing in hands and tables; that was roughly Hume's point, that the doubt doesn't survive ordinary life. So the demand for a total, source-neutral justification is an artifact of a philosophical project nobody needs. (6) J said that's a causal story about what we can't help believing, not a justification of the belief. "Can't help it" is perfectly compatible with "unjustified," so you've changed the subject. (7)

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the seven moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who, if anyone, is right about which specific point, and what both of them are missing.
