I'm teaching a unit on peer disagreement and someone in my reading group keeps insisting 12 Angry Men is basically a tidy counterexample to conciliationism. Their line is: the one holdout faces eleven apparent peers who all disagree with him, he doesn't lower his confidence, and he turns out to be right, so conciliationism's norm about deferring to your peers obviously fails. I think this is too quick, but I can't fully articulate where it goes wrong, and I also don't want to overcorrect and say the film is only about prejudice rather than evidence, because it plainly isn't. Walk me through the film against conciliationism versus steadfastness. Specifically: (1) Are the other jurors epistemic peers of the holdout in the sense the literature actually cares about, or does the film quietly stack the deck? (2) When he produces the switchblade, the old man's limp, and the passing elevated train, is that new private evidence the others didn't have—which even a conciliationist could act on—or just reweighting of shared evidence the majority already had? (3) Is there a point in the film where someone genuinely ought to conciliate, and do they? (4) How much of the later vote-flipping is conciliation versus plain social pressure, given the unanimous-verdict setup and the fact that the votes happen in public? And the practical question: can you use this film in a philosophy of disagreement unit without smuggling in the lesson that holding firm against a majority is always epistemically heroic? I'm happy to hear its a mess as an illustration, but I want the mess mapped.
