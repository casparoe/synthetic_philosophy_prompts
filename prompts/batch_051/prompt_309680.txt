In an ethics unit in my world history class, we stumbled onto a contrast in our own judgments that I cannot shake. Everyone in my group agreed that a soldier who, in wartime, lined up ten unarmed civilians and shot them to break the enemy's will would be a murderer — even if it worked, and even if the enemy surrendered a week sooner because of it. But when the conversation turned to the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, most of us, myself included at first, felt the bombs were horrific yet defensible: they were dropped to end the war, and the alternative, our teacher explained, was thought to be a longer campaign costing even more lives. The soldier up close and the bomb at a distance both kill civilians deliberately to force a surrender, yet we wanted to call one a murderer and the other a tragic necessity, and in public commemoration the leader who authorized the bombs is usually remembered as decisive rather than condemned as a killer of innocents.

The usual distinctions do not seem to be doing the real work. Scale cannot decide it, because we also said that massacring one town to save several larger towns would be wrong even if the arithmetic came out favorable. Technology cannot decide it, because making a killing more efficient or more remote is not normally a moral defense. And authority cannot decide it, because the fact that a superior gave the order is the same excuse we said we would not accept in the up-close case.

So here is what I want to know. Is there a defensible principle that makes the deliberate killing of civilians murder when it is done face to face by an individual, but at least arguably permissible when it is done at scale and at a distance by a state? If you think the asymmetry should be kept, defend it against the hardest case I could invent: a single soldier ordered in wartime to burn one neighborhood of civilians with a flamethrower, because his commander has solid evidence that the atrocity will terrorize the enemy into immediate surrender. If you think the asymmetry should be given up, tell me which side to let go of — the condemnation of the soldier or the toleration of the bomb. I am less interested in the history than in which side of the inconsistency is the superstition.
