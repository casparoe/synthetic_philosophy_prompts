I am currently taking a course in democratic theory, and a disagreement in a student organization I help found has started to feel like more than a procedural squabble. We are drafting a constitution for a new student cooperative, and one founder wants to include a rule under which certain core commitments—for example, that the organization remain a cooperative and that surplus be reinvested rather than distributed—can be changed only by a three-quarters vote of the entire membership and only after a two-week delay between the proposal and the vote. Her argument is that without such a rule the organization's founding purpose could be undone by a passing wave of enthusiasm or by an influx of new members who happen not to share the original aims. Another founder objects that the rule is illegitimate in a deeper way: it lets the people who happen to be here now bind future members who never consented to these restrictions and who should be free to govern themselves by ordinary majority vote. We are stuck on whether the proposed rule is a reasonable way to protect the organization's stability and founding values, or an unjustified way for one cohort to limit the freedom of later cohorts. What would you advise us to do, and on what grounds? If such a rule can ever be legitimate in a small voluntary association, what distinguishes the legitimate cases from the illegitimate ones?
