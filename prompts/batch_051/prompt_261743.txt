Construct a thought experiment in which a person who acts on a mere true belief achieves a better practical result than a person who acts on knowledge of the very same proposition. The case should be designed as a challenge to the familiar Platonic answer in the Meno to the question of why knowledge is more valuable than true belief. That answer runs, roughly, as follows: for guiding any particular action, true belief is no worse than knowledge, so knowledge's distinctive value must lie in its stability; knowledge is anchored by an account of why the proposition is true, whereas a mere true belief is apt to wander off. I want a case in which this anchor itself, rather than any independent defect in the knower, explains why the knower does worse.

Constraints. The scenario must be ordinary and realistic—a workplace, a hospital, a farm, a household, or similar—with no science fiction and no contrived moral dilemma of the trolley variety. The knower must genuinely satisfy a mainstream account of knowledge: the proposition is true, the knower believes it, and the justification or reliable method is in good order; do not build the case on a Gettier-type accident or on a false lemma buried in the knower's reasoning. The true believer must fail to know for a clear epistemic reason, such as holding the proposition on a hunch, on weak authority, or through a lucky route, but the belief must be true and must concern the same proposition the knower knows. The two agents should be otherwise equally competent and equally well motivated; whatever drives the difference in outcomes should be a difference between knowing and merely truly believing, not a difference in nerve, skill, or values. Keep the scenario to one paragraph.

After the scenario, do the following. (1) State the proposition, the two agents, and the method by which each holds it. (2) Explain why the first agent knows and why the second agent's belief falls short of knowledge. (3) Trace the reasoning by which each agent acts, and explain precisely why the true believer's outcome is better. (4) Identify the single feature of the case that does the work—the feature that reverses the usual ranking—and say what happens if that feature is removed. (5) Present the strongest reply that a defender of the distinctive value of knowledge would give to the case, and say whether it succeeds.

Please write your answer in British English.
