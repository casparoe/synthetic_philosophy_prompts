Early in The Subjection of Women, Mill describes the legal subordination of one sex to the other as intrinsically wrong and one of the chief obstacles to human improvement, and he maintains that it should give way to a standard of complete equality between the sexes. Later in the same essay, when he discusses the married household, he appears to treat a wife's management of the home and children as her chosen occupation, entered into by marriage in the way a man might choose a profession, and as carrying with it the renunciation of aims incompatible with that work. Are these two commitments in genuine tension? Give the strongest readings under which the tension dissolves, say what the standard of complete equality has to mean for the later passage not to be a retreat from it, and give a verdict on which of these readings can honestly be attributed to Mill and which is special pleading.
