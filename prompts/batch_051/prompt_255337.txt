I'm taking a philosophy unit on Kant's universalization test, and we keep coming back to the question "what if everyone did that?" I decided to try the test on a rule I actually live under. My school's philosophy club has a constitution, written years before I joined, whose conduct clause reads: "No member shall take any action if that action, were it taken by every member, would prevent the club from carrying out its activities." The clause was drafted when the club was small and met in person, and I think its authors were picturing free-riding — someone skipping setup or leaving others to do the work.

The case I can't settle is this. A member has written a small script that submits one automated room-booking request to the school's online calendar system for a lunchtime meeting. One submission does no harm at all; the system handles it easily. But if all forty members ran the script at the same time, the simultaneous requests would crash the server, and the club would be unable to reserve rooms for its meetings. The authors plainly did not foresee automated scripts or server crashes. I can see three ways to read the clause. The first is literal: the script is prohibited, because it would be harmful if everyone did it — but that reading makes the clause turn on facts about the school's server rather than on anything about the member's conduct, and it would also reach perfectly blameless conduct if enough members happened to do the same harmless thing at once. The second is purpose-based: the clause targets members who benefit from the club while relying on others to do the work, and the script is not that. The third is what the drafters would have said if asked: probably that they never meant to regulate harmless one-off computer use.

I want to know which interpretation is best and why, whether there is a revised clause that would catch the script case without catching the blameless cases, and what the difficulty of drawing that line says about the limits of "what if everyone did that?" once it stops being a rough moral heuristic and has to function as an actual rule. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
