I am taking a moral philosophy course, and we are about to begin the unit on whether there can be genuine moral dilemmas. Before I pick a side, I want to know whether the following argument against their possibility works, because each step seems individually compelling but the conclusion may just be an artifact of the formal machinery. Please test it.

A genuine moral dilemma is a case in which an agent has two distinct acts A and B such that, all things considered, she ought to do A; all things considered, she ought to do B; and she cannot do both.

The argument:

(1) If a situation is a genuine moral dilemma, then there is an agent who, all things considered, ought to do A, all things considered ought to do B, and cannot do both A and B.

(2) If an agent all-things-considered ought to do A and all-things-considered ought to do B, then she all-things-considered ought to do the conjunctive act A-and-B. This is an agglomeration principle.

(3) If an agent all-things-considered ought to do some act C, then she can do C. This is ought-implies-can, applied here to a conjunctive act.

(4) Therefore, if a situation is a genuine moral dilemma, the agent can do A-and-B.

(5) But if a situation is a genuine moral dilemma, then by definition the agent cannot do A-and-B.

(6) Therefore, there cannot be genuine moral dilemmas.

I suspect the trouble is in step (2) or in the definition of a genuine moral dilemma, but I cannot make the suspicion precise. I especially want to know whether step (2) trades on an equivocation between the all-things-considered reading of ought and the pro tanto reading, where a duty is genuinely present but may be outweighed. If the defender of genuine dilemmas rejects step (2), is that just another way of asserting exactly what is at issue, or can she motivate a non-question-begging restriction on agglomeration? Does ought-implies-can need to be restricted to individual acts rather than conjunctive acts, and would such a restriction be principled or ad hoc? Is there a version of this argument that shows only that a certain deontic logic rules out dilemmas, rather than that morality itself does?
