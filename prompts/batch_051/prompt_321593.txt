i'm a retired ecologist — spent my career between marine stations and an invertebrate zoology course, and lately i've been reading philosophy of biology for fun. every paper i pick up wants to tell me what an organism is, and most of them settled the question before they'd met the awkward cases. so before i read another one, give me the desiderata. what must an adequate theory of biological individuality do? i want the list of requirements, and for each one the case or consideration that forces it — the things it has to classify right, or at least explain why the classification is genuinely unstable. make sure you cover at least the portuguese man o' war, a lichen, a eusocial insect colony, the aspen clone Pando, a cellular slime mold fruiting-body aggregate, and a holobiont (me plus my gut flora, say). add others only if they earn their place. then tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just some camp's prejudice wearing the costume of a constraint. and give me an honset look at whether the list is jointly satisfiable — i have a hunch the single-genome desideratum and the physiological-integration desideratum collide, especialy once holobionts are on the table, but i'd like to know whether the collision is real or i'm confusing two senses of 'individual.' if you want, close with a quick scorecard of the usual moves — genetic uniqueness, physiological integration, reproductive/evolutionary individuality, immune self — but keep the weight on the criteria, not the scorecard. and please tell me how confident you are in each major requirement, and which single point you're least sure of.
