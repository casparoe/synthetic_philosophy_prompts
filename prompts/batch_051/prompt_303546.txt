A charitable foundation decides to run itself on explicitly negative-utilitarian principles. It holds a fixed annual endowment, and every grant decision follows a single procedure. An applicant estimates how many "severe suffering-hours" the project would prevent per $1,000 spent; a severe suffering-hour is an hour of conscious experience at or above an intensity threshold set by the foundation's board. A panel assigns each project a severity multiplier: 100 for states near the worst end of the scale, 10 for moderately severe states, and 1 for mild suffering. Projects whose main expected effect is additional positive experience rather than reduced negative experience may receive no more than five percent of the annual budget and get no multiplier. To deter inflated forecasts, the foundation re-evaluates a random sample of funded projects after three years, commissions its own retrospective estimate of suffering-hours actually prevented, and pays staff and evaluator bonuses from a pool that shrinks when portfolio-wide predictions turn out overconfident.

Suppose this scheme runs unchanged for a decade. Trace the likely evolution of the grant portfolio, the language applicants use to describe their work, and the internal culture of the staff who have to defend severity judgments in public. Identify the first point at which the rules get gamed, the least obvious pathology, and the bad outcome that nobody has an incentive to report. Then say which failure modes could be patched while preserving the mechanism and which are intrinsic to trying to operationalize a suffering-focused criterion at institutional scale. Finally, indicate one realistic change to the rules that would make the foundation worse and one that would make it better, and explain why.
