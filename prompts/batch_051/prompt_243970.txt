My friend describes herself as holding the regulative principle of worship: in public worship, an element is permissible only if Scripture commands it, and whatever is not commanded is forbidden. She adds that mere circumstances of worship — time, place, seating, the order of events — are not elements and may be arranged by prudence. Over the years I have collected her verdicts on particular cases:

- A congregation singing a recently written hymn, whose words are not from the biblical Psalms: permitted.
- A piano accompanying the congregational singing: forbidden.
- A choir singing an anthem by itself while the offering is collected: permitted.
- A short play enacting the parable of the Good Samaritan during the service: forbidden.
- The minister reading a lengthy passage of Scripture aloud: permitted.
- Lit candles on the communion table: forbidden.
- A printed order of service listing the day's hymns and prayers: permitted.
- The minister praying extemporaneously rather than from a set form: permitted.

Next week she has to rule on a new case: a member of the congregation standing during the service to give a brief personal testimony about how she came to faith.

I am less interested in the one-word answer than in the principle she is actually running. Identify the candidate rules that fit her verdicts so far, say what each predicts about the testimony case, give your best guess about her answer and your reasons for preferring one rule over the others, and name one further case that would cleanly separate the rule you favor from its nearest rival.
