I'm a novelist, and I've been chewing on W. E. B. Du Bois's claim that every piece of art is propaganda whether the artist admits it or not. I used to hate this; now I think it's true but not for the reason people usually give.

I keep making a distinction I want checked. I call it recruiting fiction verses ratifying fiction. Recruiting fiction aims at a reader who doesn't already hold the view: it has to charm, argue, lower defenses, and make the unfamiliar position feel like the only sane one. Ratifying fiction aims at a reader who already holds the view: it doesn't convert them, it deepens the conviction, makes it feel weightier and more inevitable, and gives the converted a shared vocabulary—the sense that their side has the best sentences.

Here's the claim I keep making in bars and not really defending: most politically serious fiction that gets called "courageous" is actually ratifying. It's read by people who already agree, and it works on them like a liturgy rather than an argument. My last novel was about a woman leaving a tight religious community. I told myself it was recruiting fiction for secularism, but every letter I got was from someone who had already left, saying I'd told their story. I dont think I moved a single person out of the faith. So the real ethical question for a novelist might not be "will my book change minds?" but "whose existing leanings will my book strengthen?"

And that's what I now think Du Bois was getting at. Read through the ratifying lens, his claim that art is always propaganda means: no novel really converts a hostile reader; it confirms and intensifies what the reader already half-believes. So the artist's job isn't to choose between propaganda and art—it's to choose which audience gets ratified.

Is the distinction coherent, or does it collapse once you press on it? Does it already exist under some name I've missed (it feels too obvious not to)? Is my reading of Du Bois fair, or am I just flattening him into something cozier? Where does it break?
