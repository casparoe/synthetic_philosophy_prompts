I just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about Adam Smith's attitude toward the British empire in the Wealth of Nations. My friend held that Smith was, at bottom, a reluctant imperial reformer who thought the empire could be made just by admitting the provinces to the British parliament and taxing them fairly. I maintained that the text says something closer to the opposite.

Near the end of the Wealth of Nations, in Book V, Chapter III, Smith considers a plan for extending the British system of taxation to all the provinces of the empire, accompanied by representation. He asks "in what manner a general union of this kind might be likely to affect the happiness and prosperity of the different provinces comprehended within it," and then he writes: "Such a speculation can at worst be regarded but as a new Utopia, less amusing certainly, but not more useless and chimerical than the old one."

Earlier in that same chapter, Smith has described British dominion over the American colonies in quite different terms—as the mere blueprints of an empire rather than an empire itself, the blueprints of a gold mine rather than a working mine, a project that has cost, continues to cost, and is likely to keep costing immense expense without profit.

These two verdicts seem to cut against each other. Please say whether they can be reconciled, and at what cost. Give the strongest reading of each passage, then say which reconciliation is honest to Smith's argument and which is special pleading.
