Since I retired from the lab I've been working through some philosophy of science, and I keep getting stuck on Milton Friedman's essay The Methodology of Positive Economics, the one that gave us what people now call the F-twist: that you shouldn't judge a theory by wether its assumptions are realistic, only by how well it predicts. An economist friend of mine keeps defending it with an analogy I'm sure you've heard. A model is like a map. Nobody complains that a map leaves things out or distorts things; a map that showed every tree and rock would be useless, and it wouldn't even be a map anymore, it would just be the territory again. So idealizations—frictionless planes, perfect competition, rational agents—are like the simplifications a cartographer makes, and objecting to them is like complaining that a road atlas doesn't show every pothole. He thinks that's enough to make the F-twist stick.

What I'd like from you is a careful audit of that analogy, because it feels like its doing a lot of work below the surface. Give me the correspondence table first: model, target system, idealization, modeler, the purpose the thing gets put to, and whatever else seems load-bearing. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and which breaks matter for the particular use he's making of it—defending the F-twist—versus which breaks are just interesting but harmless. Finally, do the honest thing: once the disanalogies are all on the table, does the F-twist survive, or was the map analogy carrying the whole conclusion? If the analogy can't carry it, is there a nearby analogy that would do better, or does its failure point to something the F-twist needs that no analogy can supply?
