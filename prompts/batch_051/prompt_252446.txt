I have spent a good deal of time reading rationalist-adjacent blogs and forums, and I keep noticing an asymmetry in how people deploy Darwinian ideas about culture. When the target is an idea the speaker rejects — a viral conspiracy theory, an advertising slogan that has outcompeted more accurate information, a fashionable therapy with a weak evidence base, a religious movement that keeps mutating rather than dying out — the memetic lens comes naturally. People talk fluently about ideas as replicators: certain ideas spread because they are catchy, because they exploit cognitive biases, because they are easy to transmit and outcompete rivals for attention, whether or not they are true. For the ideas they reject, selection explains the spread, and considerations of evidence enter later, if at all.

Yet when the same lens is aimed at convictions the speaker actually holds, the frame abruptly changes. Their political views are conclusions they reached by weighing the arguments; their dietary ethics are a response to evidence about suffering; their views on emerging technologies are the result of careful reasoning from stated premises. They may allow that some of their co-believers are in it merely for group membership or aesthetics, but they treat their own psychology as running on reasons. The asymmetry is stark: bad ideas spread by contagion, while good ideas — at least the ones I happen to hold — spread by being right.

I want to know whether anything can justify this asymmetry rather than merely explain it. The strongest principled defense I can devise is something like this: contagion explanations are the appropriate default for beliefs whose content is disconnected from whatever would make them true, whereas reason-based explanations are warranted when the belief's content and the evidence available to the believer make its adoption probable — and people are sometimes correct that their own beliefs sit on the second side. But that principle may itself smuggle in the conclusion. Please make the strongest case available for a justified asymmetry, test it against nearby cases (a scientist who adopts a theory for social reasons and yet is right; a conspiracy theorist who reasons carefully from a false premise; a belief one holds on evidence but that also happens to be extremely memetic), and then give a committed verdict: should we keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or abandon it? I want a definite, defended position, not a survey of the moves.
