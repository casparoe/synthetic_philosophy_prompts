I keep running into a version of the old upstream/downstream river story in EA meetups and rationalist forum threads, deployed to defend a strong claim about where effort should go. In the story, you find people drowning in a river; you can spend your time pulling them out one by one, or you can walk upstream and stop whoever is throwing them in. The people who tell it use it to license the conclusion that marginal, downstream interventions — direct delivery, treatment, relief, funding a top bednet or cash-transfer charity — are ultimately palliative, and that a rational actor should strongly prefer systemic work: policy change, institutional redesign, altering the incentives that produce the problem. I share the general orientation, but I suspect the analogy is carrying more argumentative weight than it can bear, and I want it audited rather than accepted.

Please do four things. First, lay out the mapping explicitly: what corresponds to the rescuer, the drowning people, the thrower, the river, the walk upstream, and the decision about how to spend the afternoon. Second, identify where the mapping breaks, and in particular which features the story quietly imports that real cause areas often lack — a single identifiable thrower, a contained mechanism, victims visible at the moment of choice, near-certain local effects, no serious cost or uncertainty in walking upstream, no competition among rescuers — and say which of these disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion and which are incidental. Third, take a definite position, with reasons: does the analogy support a general presumption in favor of systemic or upstream interventions over marginal ones, or does it only support something much narrower, such as comparing interventions by expected value rather than visibility, with the upstream/downstream distinction doing no independent work? If the truth lies in between, state exactly where the line falls. Fourth, if there is a better analogy that does more honest work in this debate, give one and explain how it handles the disanalogies you identified.
