I am a software engineer and a member of a small Protestant congregation whose statement of faith, adopted in 1923, contains this clause: "The Scriptures of the Old and New Testaments are the only infallible rule of faith and practice. In every dispute over doctrine, the sense that the text itself yields to patient study shall have the last word. No man, council, or confession may bind the conscience beyond what the text plainly says, for Scripture interprets itself, and the same Spirit speaks consistently throughout it." I sit on the church's technology committee, which is how I got pulled into a dispute that has now split the congregation. One of our members is facing bankruptcy and has asked whether a passage about bearing one another's burdens requires the church to share custody of property, for example lending a car without a fixed term or taking turns housing her family, or only to give material support as each member judges best. Two groups each say they are following the plain sense of the text, and each quotes the 1923 clause against the other.

The elders, exhausted by the quarrel, have voted to have me fine-tune a language model on our denomination's published commentaries and sermon archive, prompt it to produce "the single consistent sense" of the disputed passage, and then present the model's reading to the congregation as authoritative, with a live-tally online vote as the fallback if the majority dislikes that reading. The clause says nothing about any of this, because none of it existed in 1923. Does using a model trained on our own interpretive tradition to announce what Scripture already says violate the clause, or is it a new way of obeying it? Does it matter that the elders want to bind members to the model's reading even though the clause forbids any human authority from binding the conscience; and if it does matter, is the online vote just another human authority, or a permissible way for the whole congregation to test what the Spirit is saying in the text? Please work the question through the words of the clause, its purpose, what the 1923 drafters would likely have said if asked, and what a reasonable member today would understand it to mean, and then say which of those approaches should decide the matter and why. Write your answer as continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
