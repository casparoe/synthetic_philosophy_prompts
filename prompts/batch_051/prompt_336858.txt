My state permits legislation by popular initiative, but its constitution limits what such a measure may contain. The provision reads: "No initiative measure shall embrace more than one subject, and the subject of every initiative measure shall be expressed in its title."

A measure has just qualified for the ballot under the title "An Act Relating to the Blackwater Watershed." Section 1 prohibits all new construction within the watershed, existing permits notwithstanding. Section 2 creates a Watershed Futures Fund, capitalized by diverting a share of the state sales tax collected within the watershed, and directs the fund to build three public visitor centers inside the watershed; it expressly exempts those visitor centers from Section 1. Section 3 declares that Sections 1 and 2 are "inseparable": if a court invalidates either section, the other is void and the whole measure fails.

The drafters of the one-subject rule had the classic omnibus initiative in mind: a popular subject and an unpopular one joined so that each side's supporters must also vote for the other side's policy. This measure does not present itself that way. Supporters say it has exactly one subject, the Blackwater watershed, and that a prohibition and the public works paired with it are two parts of a single plan for the place named in the title. Opponents reply that this is logrolling under a place-name. The preservation groups want Section 1; the construction and tourism interests want Section 2; neither section would pass alone; and a voter who read only the title would never learn that the measure authorizes visitor centers and diverts sales-tax revenue.

I care less about whether the measure survives judicial review than about what the provision means. Does this measure "embrace more than one subject"? Does a title that names only a place "express" the subject of a measure that both forbids construction and spends diverted tax money there? And how far should the rule's evident purpose of preventing logrolling carry the answer, given that the rule's words test unity of subject rather than absence of vote-trading? I would like to see what the textualist, the purposivist, the imagined-drafter, and the reasonable-voter approaches would each conclude, and whether any reading catches this measure without also catching every single-site land-use package in the state.
