I'm running a film-and-philosophy night with some grad students and we're doing Twelve Angry Men next month. I want to pair it with the Condorcet jury theorem, but not in the usual 'juries are wise' way. The theorem says that if each voter is more likely right than wrong and they vote independently of each other, majority rule gets more reliable as the group gets bigger. The film seems to open as a kind of counterexample: eleven jurors vote guilty, one holdout votes not guilty, and over the course of the movie hes the one who turns out to be right. So what does that do to the theorem? I'd like you to read the Lumet film (and the Rose play, if you know it) as a pressure test of the theorem's assumptions: independence, roughly equal competence, a shared goal, sincere voting. Tell me where the film respects those and where it quietly violates them. I'm especially curious about the unanimity requirement: the theorem is about majority rule, but this jury has to be unanimous to convict, so does the unanimity rule change what the theorem would predict, or is it doing something entirely different? And heres the loaded part: the film treats deliberation as what saves the jury from a bad verdict, but the theorem treats deliberation as beside the point or even a contaminant, since it correlates the votes. Is the movie an argument that deliberation can correct correlated bias, or is it just a fantasy about one heroic dissenter? Give me a reading that takes the film and the formal result both seriously, and is willing to say where the film resists the theorem's picture of what a jury is.
