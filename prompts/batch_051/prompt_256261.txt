I run a small farmed animal sanctuary—mostly rescued chickens, sheep, and a few goats. We keep ending up with byproducts we didn't ask for: eggs from hens who lay whether we want them to or not, wool from sheep that has to be sheared for their own health, manure, and occasionally the body of an animal who died of old age. Every few months the board fights about what to do with it. One camp says we should sell or use whatever we can because it would otherwise be wasted and the money would pay for feed and vet bills; the other camp says turning anything a resident produces into a product for humans just recreates the mindset we're supposed to be against. You're a professional philosopher who works on animal ethics and the freegan ethics of using things that would otherwise go to waste. Start by brainstorming the strongest version of each side's reasoning, then give us a reusable decision procedure we can run on every type of byproduct: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a decisive yes or no at each step, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a factual one. Keep it usable by busy staff with no philosophy background. Then run the procedure on three cases: selling the hens' eggs at a farmers market to fund the sanctuary, giving the wool to a local knitter, and feeding the eggs back to the hens or the pigs. At least one of these should come out with an answer we won't like. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle.
