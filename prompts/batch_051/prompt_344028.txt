I am a PhD student in linguistics, and I am currently trying to reconcile two uses of the word "grammatical" that I encounter every week. In the introductory syntax course I teach, "grammatical" is a property of a speaker's internalized grammar: a string is grammatical relative to a grammar, which is why negative concord counts as a well-formed output of some English varieties even when it is stigmatized in others. In the sociolinguistics seminar I attend, by contrast, prescriptive norms are treated as genuine social facts—conventions maintained by editors, teachers, and institutions—with real histories and real effects on people's prospects. The phrase "bad grammar" therefore seems to hover between two ideas: a string's failing to be generated by any native competence, and a string's violating a norm that some community has chosen to enforce. I want to know whether the ordinary concept of a "grammatical error" can be given a single principled definition that does justice to both uses, or whether the phrase is doing two jobs at once.

Please give me a working definition of a grammatical error (or of bad grammar, if you prefer), and test it against the following cases: (i) a habitual speaker of African American English saying "Nobody ain't told me nothing"; (ii) "Between you and I" in a formal essay by an otherwise educated writer; (iii) a native-speaking child producing "I goed to the store"; (iv) a native-speaking adult typing "teh" for "the" in an email; (v) "Every student should bring their laptop," with a singular antecedent and plural pronoun; (vi) an adult learner of English producing "He go to school every day"; (vii) the western Pennsylvania construction "The car needs washed"; (viii) the hypercorrection "Whom shall I say is calling?" from a speaker who is carefully trying to apply case rules; and (ix) "I literally died laughing," where "literally" functions as an intensifier over a metaphor. I have settled intuitions about only a few of these: I am confident that (iii) and (iv) are errors in some sense, and I flinch at calling (i) or (ix) errors in the same sense; the rest leave me genuinely torn. Propose the definition, run it through all nine cases, say explicitly where it yields a verdict you can defend and where it yields one you would want to revise, identify which verdict is least secure, and tell me whether a prescriptivist and a descriptivist are ultimately disagreeing about one thing or talking about two.
