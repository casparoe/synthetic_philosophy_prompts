I've been tinkering with a way of restating Peirce's four methods of fixing belief, and I want a candid appraisal before I keep using it. I call the idea 'reopening cost.' I got there by asking a pragmatic-maxim-y question: what practical difference does your method of settling belief actually make? My answer is that it makes a difference to what you'd have to give up or risk before you let yourself genuinely doubt a settled question. Tenacity sets that price at infinity; the method just is refusing to entertain the question, so no evidence can buy a doubt. Authority sets the price as the social cost of contradicting the authorities: you can reopen 'the earth goes around the sun' but you'll pay in scandal or punishment. The a priori method charges coherence: you may reopen a belief if you can do it while keeping the rest of your system consistent with your preferred principles. Science is the odd one because it has no fixed price — it'll let you reopen anything, provided you're willing to pay the cost of actually running the inquiry and taking the result. This makes 'that person is irrational' translatable into 'their reopening price is set too high from where I'm standing,' and it predicts that the same person can be scientific in one domain and tenacious in another, because reopening costs are set per-domain and are not a global personality trait. I know Peirce says a belief is just what you're prepared to act on, so maybe my 'cost' is just an overelaborated restatement of habit and I've added nothing. So: is this a real distinction or a metaphor doing all the work? Does it already exist under another name (pragmatist literature, belief-revision talk, anywhere else)? And what are the three strongest objections — is any of them fatal, or is it salvageble as a pedagogical gloss rather than a substantive claim? Also, if I've gotten the tenacity method wrong, tell me.
