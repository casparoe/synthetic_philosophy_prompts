I recently read a book on rationality and the epistemology of disagreement whose chapter on Aumann's agreement theorem left me unsure how much to trust. The chapter, as I understand it, reports that the theorem proves rational people cannot agree to disagree. It says: suppose two people are each fully rational and start from the same prior probabilities, and suppose they tell one another everything they know about some question. Then as soon as each hears the other's opinion, they must assign the same probability to that question; any remaining gap can only be a temporary lag while they finish updating. From this the author draws a larger moral: persistent real-world disagreement, about things like climate change or the causes of a war, is always a sign that at least one party is irrational, is withholding information, or is refusing to update on what the other side says.

I suspect some of this is unfair to the actual theorem, but I don't know enough to say which parts. Please give me an accuracy audit: which clauses state the result correctly, which quietly replace one condition with another, which add a condition the theorem doesn't need, and which overstate what follows. In particular, does the theorem really require the parties to share all their information, or is its interest that they need not? Does it describe a process of convergence over time, or a condition that holds at a single moment? And does the moral about real-world disagreement survive once the theorem is stated accurately, or does it rest on assumptions the theorem does not license?
