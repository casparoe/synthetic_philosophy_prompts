In my international relations seminar we were given a working definition of terrorism with five criteria: (1) violence or the threat of violence against persons; (2) the victims are non-combatants, so not soldiers or police in the field; (3) a political, religious, or ideological goal; (4) the act is meant to influence an audience beyond the immediate victims, by terrorizing or coercing a wider public or a government; and (5) the perpetrator is a non-state actor, a group or individual rather than a state.

Here's the case I can't get out of my head. A regional water authority — a public body, part of the state — wants to break a long-running blockade of a dam project. It hires a private security firm to contaminate the drinking water of the neighborhood at the center of the blockade. The contaminant is chosen to cause severe intestinal illness over a couple of weeks but isn't expected to kill anyone; the idea is to make the area temporarily uninhabitable so the blockade disperses, and to signal to other communities that blockading is a loosing strategy. The authority keeps the contract at arm's length, and the firm's own operatives are the ones who actually dump the agent into the reservoir.

Go through the five criteria honestly and say whether this counts as terrorism. Where the answer is uncertain, say whether the problem is with the case, with the criterion, or with the checklist-definition itself. And tell me whether this example shows the non-state-actor criterion is doing real work, or whether it's just a way to keep state violence out of the word "terrorism."
