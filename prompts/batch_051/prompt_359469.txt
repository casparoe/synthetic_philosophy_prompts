Two people in my reading group got into an argument about the common prior assumption in Bayesian game theory. (1) The first defended it as little more than a consistency condition: if two agents genuinely begin with the same prior and update by conditionalization on their private information, then any difference in their posterior beliefs can be traced entirely to the difference in what each has observed; common priors simply make explicit what it means for disagreement to be informational rather than a clash of irreconcilable starting points. (2) The second replied that this drains the assumption of content, since there is no independent criterion for saying two agents have the same prior apart from stipulating it; on this reading, the claim that rational agents cannot agree to disagree is either a tautology or a result about a stipulated object, not a constraint that could ever bind an actual disagreement. (3) The first answered that the content lies in cross-agent restrictions: given the full schedule of what each agent would believe after each possible private signal, it is not automatic that a single shared prior could generate all of those beliefs, so the common prior assumption is a substantive ex ante condition on the agents' entire belief profile, not a label applied after the fact. (4) The second said this merely moves the problem back a step: choosing among rival priors that each rationalize the observed disagreement requires an independent principle about which priors are admissible, and that principle is a substantive, contestable commitment; the agreement theorem therefore never shows that rationality alone forces convergence, only that convergence follows once a shared starting point has been assumed. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the four moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating; then identify anything both sides are missing and state who is more right overall.
