I work on a product team that is building an AI assistant meant to learn what individual users actually want, not just what they click. In a design meeting about whether to lean on user interviews or behavioral logs, a senior engineer ended the discussion with "actions speak louder than words." The phrase was doing real work there: it was being used to justify treating observed behavior as more trustworthy evidence of a user's preferences than anything the user says about themselves. I'm not sure it is one claim rather than several. Sometimes it sounds almost tautological (whatever you do just is what you prefer); sometimes it sounds like an empirical claim about self-knowledge (people can't reliably report their own motives); sometimes it sounds like an economic point about signaling (talk is cheap, behavior is costly, so behavior is the more credible signal); and sometimes it seems to apply only in narrow cases like temptation or social pressure, not to action in general. There also seems to be a deeper problem hiding underneath: a person's choices are produced jointly by their goals, their beliefs about their options, and the constraints they face, so the same pattern of behavior can look optimal under quite different "wants" depending on what you assume about the beliefs and constraints. If that's right, "let the behavior speak" doesn't settle what an AI should optimize for; it just moves the ambiguity one step back. I want to push back with precision. Lay out the distinct precise claims "actions speak louder than words" could be making, from the trivial to the radical, and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible empirical claim, or likely false, and what kind of evidence would bear on it. Then say which reading or readings would actually license my colleague's inference that behavioral data is generally a better guide to user preferences than self-report, and flag any gap between "behavior reveals what someone wants" and "behavior reveals the values an AI should optimize for." If the saying is too loose to admit a stable set of readings, or if my colleague's use rests on a premise that doesn't survive scrutiny, tell me that directly rather than giving me a tidy menu.
