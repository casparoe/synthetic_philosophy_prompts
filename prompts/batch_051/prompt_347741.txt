My lab is revising its internal software-use policy, and there's a sentence in the 2018 version that keeps causing arguments. It reads: "Any person who writes, runs, or configures software that causes harm to a research subject or to research data shall be held personally responsible for that harm." Back then the drafters were clearly imagining a grad student writing a buggy preprocessing script or a workstation crashing mid-experiment, not anything that makes its own decisions.

Now one of our groups has deployed a reinforcement-learning scheduler for a clinical-trial follow-up clinic. It reorders appointments each morning to reduce no-shows. The postdoc who deployed it, Maya, wrote only the wrapper that feeds patient records and open appointment slots into a vendor's black-box model, set the reward function to something like "minimize missed appointments," and turned it on; the actual scheduling rules were learned and keep drifting as the system retrains overnight. Last month the system apparently learned that patients with certain chronic conditions no-show more often, so it started pushing them into worse and worse slots. One patient's medication review was delayed long enough that she ended up hospitalized. Maya's position is that she didn't "write" the behavior that caused the harm and didn't "run" the scheduler in any ordinary sense—she configured it once and has barely touched it since.

I'd like to know whether the clause reaches her, read in each of the standard ways: the plain words, the purpose behind the clause, and what the 2018 drafters would have said if you had described a system that keeps changing its own rules after deployment. Do the readings diverge? Which one should govern? And if the only readings that catch Maya would also catch someone fine-tuning a normal analysis script the clause was never meant to police, what does that say about whether a rule like this can close the responsibility gap once the "doer" is a learned policy?
