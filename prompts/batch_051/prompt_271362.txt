I've been trying to get an argument against universal veganism watertight, but I have a nagging feeling there's a cheat in it somewhere. Here it is:

(1) If a moral requirement is universal, then ordinary decent people, in the circumstances where the requirement actually applies, must be able to follow it without being pushed into a cost that morality cannot reasonably demand of them.
(2) In plenty of places outside the rich world – a food-insecure pastoralist household in the Sahel, say, or some fishing communities that rely on fish for micronutrients – a fully vegan diet would mean either real nutritional hardship or buying substitutes the household cannot afford.
(3) So for those households, a rule requiring a vegan diet would be a rule they could follow only at a cost that morality cannot permissibly demand.
(4) No moral requirement can demand what is impossible or ruinous for the person it binds.
(5) Therefore veganism is not a universal requirement, even if it is what relatively affluent people in rich countries ought to do.

I'm half convinced this is valid, but I can't shake the thought that the same structure would let someone argue that a prohibition on forced marriage isn't universal either, because in some communities a family's economic survival is felt to depend on it. Is the argument valid? If not, which numbered step fails, and what would the most defensible version of that step say? If it is valid, is it sound? Answer in British English.
