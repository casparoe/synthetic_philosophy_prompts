I spent thirty years in geochemistry and am now retired and slowly working through philosophy on my own. I keep running into the slogan 'deliberation crowds out prediction,' and a friend who did some decision theory tried to explain it to me over lunch. His gloss was:

'When you're actually deciding what to do, you can't assign a probability to what you'll do without changing the situation. Deliberation asks "what should I do?" while prediction asks "what will I do?", and you can't occupy both stances at once. If you give yourself, say, a 70% chance of taking the job, you've either already made up your mind or you're treating yourself as an outside observer; either way the number isn't a genuine expression of uncertainty, because you're the one who will settle the matter. So until you decide, it's not just unknown what you'll do—it's indeterminate. That's why assigning intermediate credences to your own choices is incoherent, and why decision theory, which wants to put probabilities over acts, is subtly broken. It also explains the weirdness of self-prediction in cases like the prisoner's dilemma: once you predict you'll defect, that very prediction changes your evidential situation and tends to make you defect, so predicting your own choice is a self-fulfilling prophecy rather than a neutral measurement.'

The bit about two different stances sounds right to me, and the idea that a measurement can disturb the thing it measures is familiar from the lab. But the jump from 'it's weird to predict while deciding' to 'there's no fact of the matter until you decide' or 'self-prediction is impossible' looks like a non-sequiter to me: I can predict with high confidence that I'll have coffee tomorrow morning, and I don't see why that would be incoherent. I'm also suspicious of the claim that decision theory is broken, since plenty of people seem to use it for real decisions without assigning probabilities to their own acts.

Can you go through my friend's summary sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a philosopher who actually defends 'deliberation crowds out prediction' would accept, which parts are overstatements or conflations (and of what), and whether the strong conclusions about indeterminacy, incoherent self-credences, and broken decision theory follow from the accurate parts? I don't need a bibliography or a history of the view, just an accuracy audit I can take back to him.
