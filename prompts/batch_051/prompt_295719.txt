Suppose tomorrow a cheap pocket device appears—call it the Verdict Box—that answers any moral question you ask it: yes/no, permissible/impermissible, or a number for how bad something is. Assume for the sake of the scenario that moral questions have determinate correct answers and the box always gives them. But it gives no reasons, no explanations, and no one can reverse-engineer it's underlying principles any better than we can now. So it's like the moral testimony debate with the reliability problem solved and the understanding problem left completely untouched.

Predict the next fifty years for: moral philosophy, how parents raise kids, criminal sentencing, and ordinary blaming and praising between friends and strangers. I want the chain of reasoning, not just the endpoints: where do you expect people to happily defer to the box, where do you expect stubborn resistance, and what explains the difference? Which predictions are you most confident in, and which hinge on assumptions about human psychology (e.g., whether people can actually outsource their moral judgments, or whether they keep trusting their gut and just use the box to rationalize afterwards)? What would have to be true about moral understanding—or about what blame is for—for the whole trajectory to come out differently?
