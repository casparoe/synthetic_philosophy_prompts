Having come to philosophy late, after a career in the natural sciences, I have been working through the idea, associated with the rights-as-trumps tradition, that an individual right does not simply weigh heavily against collective welfare: it removes certain trade-offs from the table, so that a government may not override a person's interest merely because the aggregate benefits of doing so would be large. That argument was framed for a world in which an identifiable official or institution deliberates and then decides to override the interest. Today a great deal of public decision-making — benefit eligibility scoring, pretrial risk assessment, fraud screening, the allocation of social housing — is delegated to algorithmic systems in which nobody sits down and weighs this person's interest against that collective benefit; the trade-off is instead embedded in objective functions, training data, thresholds, and feature choices. I would like your assessment of whether the rights-as-trumps argument survives this shift. Which premise is really under pressure: that a right speaks to an agent who can decline to perform a dangerous calculation, that the encroachment must have a locus, or something else? Is there a repaired version that still does serious work, for instance by treating rights as constraints on how such systems are specified and audited rather than on how a decision-maker reasons at the moment of decision? And does the new setting perhaps expose a flaw that was always present in the trump picture? Please include the appropriate self-critical caveats: tell me which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail. I would prefer continuous prose throughout, as in an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
