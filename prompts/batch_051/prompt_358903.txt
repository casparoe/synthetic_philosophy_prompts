I'm a junior doctor in an emergency department, and the decision I make forty times a shift is whether the chest-pain patient in front of me can go home or stays for serial troponins and observation. The cost structure is lopsided: sending someone home who then infarcts is catastrophic, keeping someone an extra night is expensive and annoying but survivable. I've got maybe ten minutes a patient, and most of my discharge decisions aren't reviewed by anyone before the patient leaves — my signature is the review.

I once read about Williamson's anti-luminosity argument and I can't shake it. As I remember it: there are no interesting luminous conditions — conditions such that whenever they hold you're in a position to know they hold. Knowledge is subject to a margin for error: if I know this patient is safe to discharge, then in the nearby cases where the history or exam is only a hair different in a way I couldn't detect, I'd still be right. And the KK principle says that if I know, I should be able to know that I know. I'm half-convinced of all this, but I have no idea how to run it at 3am when I've seen thirty patients, my feet hurt, and the man in bay 7 keeps telling me his "pressure" is gone now.

So: write me a reusable procedure for deciding when I genuinely know a chest-pain patient is safe to send home, as opposed to when I'm pattern-matching on a normal ECG and a reassuring story. Make it a checklist or an ordered set of tests I can run in under ten minutes without a calculator, and that a new intern could follow without having read any philosophy. For each step, mark it as either a check I can actually run or a judgment call dressed up as a check. I want at least one margin-for-error step, at least one KK-ish step, and an instruction for what to do when they pull apart. Then apply it to three cases: one where it says discharge, one where it says keep, and one where it tells me to keep someone I badly want to send home because the chart looks fine. Be specific about what the procedure can't settle — e.g. the patient who can't give a history, or the one whose story is fine but its the third time this week, or when my own fatigue should count as evidence.

End with a one-sentence summary of the procedure as the last line, beginning 'Summary:'.
