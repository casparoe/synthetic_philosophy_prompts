I am a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I am comfortable with evolutionary biology — gene flow, clines, reproductive isolation, and the species problem — but I have only a non-specialist's grasp of historical linguistics. I am working on an essay about the question of when Latin becomes Italian. Set aside the standard dates, texts, and attributions; I want the conceptual structure of the problem, not the historiography. The puzzle is that a continuous chain of speaker communities, each broadly intelligible to its near neighbours in time and place, seems to carry a language from one name to another without any generation ever observing the break. I want you to restate the debate over whether the Latin-to-Italian boundary is a real event or a retrospective convention, using the vocabulary I know from evolutionary biology. Treat a language as a population of idiolects; treat sound change, grammatical change, and lexical borrowing as mutation, drift, and gene flow; treat regional variation as a cline; treat mutual intelligibility as reproductive compatibility; and treat the Latin-to-Italian transition as a case that might be decided differently by different species concepts and modes of speciation. Be as faithful as you can, and flag every point where the biology has to be strained, simplified, or supplemented to fit the linguistics. Then give me the residue report. Which claims in the language-change and identity debate translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in the evolutionary vocabulary at all — I suspect that written standardization, a literary canon, administrative and educational institutions, and the deliberate assertion of a national language belong in that last group. Treat the residue as the interesting part. Finally, tell me whether the residue points to a shortcoming of the evolutionary framework, or to something in the original question that a purely population-level analysis cannot see.
