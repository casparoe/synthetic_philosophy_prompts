Section 7(2) of a major donor country's development act provides: "Assistance under this Act may be furnished only where its principal effect is the lasting reduction of extreme poverty in the recipient country, and no assistance may be furnished where the programme is designed, in whole or in part, to advance the donor's security, export, or energy-supply interests."

The provision was enacted in the 1980s after a scandal in which food aid was conditioned on the recipient's signature of a long-term supply contract with a donor-based grain exporter. The drafters assumed that funded programmes would be grants for clinics, schools, water systems, or seed distribution, and that the donor's interests, when present at all, would be incidental side-effects rather than part of the design.

Now consider a proposal the drafters did not foresee. A consortium of the donor's technology firms proposes to "co-fund" a digital-identity and mobile-payments system in a lower-middle-income country. On the consortium's own projections, the system would let several million extremely poor people prove their identity, receive cash transfers, and open accounts, reducing leakage in benefit distribution and lowering the cost of remittances. The same firms would operate the system under a twenty-year concession, store the resulting data, and use the rollout as a reference case for public-sector contracts in other middle-income countries. The recipient government offers no cash contribution, on the ground that the concession itself is its contribution.

I care less about the verdict than about the interpretive structure. Under each of the following readings, is the system barred, permitted, or permissible only if redesigned: (a) a plain-words reading of "principal effect"; (b) a purpose-based reading that asks what the 1980s drafters would have said if confronted with a programme whose benefit to the poor and return to the donor are generated by the very same infrastructure; (c) an official-purpose reading that looks only to the stated aim of the arrangement rather than to the operators' collateral business objectives; and (d) a reasonable-recipient reading that asks whether the poorest intended beneficiaries would regard the system as aid or as a commercial concession. If the readings diverge, which is most faithful to the Act, and what does that tell us about whether the Act can distinguish foreign aid from subsidized market access? Finally, might a programme be genuinely poverty-reducing and nevertheless fail to be "assistance" in the sense the Act embodies — and, if so, what would that show about what rich countries owe poor ones?
