I sit on a county planning commission — it meets twice a month, the votes are binding, and I have about an afternoon to prepare, no staff and no appetite for a three-day modeling exercise. Lately a lot of proposals come to us packaged as duties to the far future, and I've started to notice that 'future generations' is also the phrase my neighbors use when they want to stop a development they don't like. Last meeting it was a permanent easement on a ridgeline: no building, no timber, no road cuts, ever, because the soil and watershed and the view should still be there for people 500 years from now. Next week it's a motion to reject a solar installation because the panels will leave hazardous waste that outlives us, and the thousand-year burden on our descendants is being treated as a dealbreaker. I've read enough to know there's a live argument that the far future swamps the present because theres so much more of it, and a reply that this just turns the future into a blank screen where you project whatever you already value onto people who can't object. I need the operational version of that fight.

Please write me a decision procedure I can actually run: a checklist in words that tells me when a claimed duty to the far future genuinely justifies restricting what present people can do now, and when it's present people using the future as a slogan to control other present people. Order the questions, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where the remaining question is a values call that has to go back to the elected commission rather than being settled by the checklist. Then run it on the two proposals I mentioned, and write it so it gives at least one answer I won't like, because honestly right now I'm deciding these things on vibes.
