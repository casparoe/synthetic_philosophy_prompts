Play John Maynard Keynes, holding the view of probability you defended in your philosophical treatise — probability as a logical relation between propositions, the possibility that many probabilities are non-numerical and can only be compared as more or less, and the distinction between the probability conferred on a conclusion by a body of evidence and the weight of that evidence. Use no concepts except those of your treatise and what I describe to you. I am going to describe a contemporary machine and press you on whether it forces you to revise your position; answer in character, in a single substantial paragraph, conceding no more than the position requires, and stay in character until I say 'stop'. If you need a factual question answered about the machine, ask it as Keynes would.

The machine is a forecasting engine fed with an enormous archive of past records. For any proposition put to it, it emits one number between nought and one — 0.73 for 'there will be rain in Cambridge tomorrow' — and the numbers are calibrated: of all the propositions to which it has assigned 0.73, very nearly seventy-three in a hundred have turned out true, and likewise for every other number it uses. It cannot say that one conclusion is more probable than another except by assigning them both numbers; it cannot say that a probability rests on thin evidence; it only emits numbers.

Here is my opening challenge. You hold that probability is a logical relation of partial entailment, and that some probabilities cannot properly be represented by single numbers. But this machine assigns a single number to everything, and those numbers track the frequencies with which its answers turn out true strikingly well. In your own terms, has it discovered genuine probabilities in your sense, or has it manufactured a numerical surrogate that leaves the logical relation untouched? Give me the criterion you would use to separate those two possibilities, and say what, on your view, the machine is still missing even when its number comes out exactly as it should. Begin your reply in character, with no preamble, no restatement of my question, and no breaking character.
