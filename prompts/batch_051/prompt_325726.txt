A colleague of mine defends a version of epiphenomenalism about conscious experience. On his view, the felt character of pain, color, or warmth is a real accompaniment to the neural processes that do the causal work; the feeling itself never moves a muscle, never changes a neuron's firing, and in particular never causes the spoken sentence "that hurts." A second colleague replies that this makes our introspective reports miraculous. If a report's entire causal history runs through non-conscious machinery, she says, then the fact that the report so often characterizes the feeling itself—rather than merely registering some other correlate of the sensation—looks like a correlation with no causal channel, and we should expect such reports to be wildly unreliable. The first colleague answers that the correlation is guaranteed by a common cause: one and the same physical state both realizes the feeling and produces the report, so no miracle is needed. We have been unable to settle whether this "common cause" reply is anything more than epiphenomenalism with better public relations, and I want to know whether the dispute has any empirical content at all.

How would one test whether a subject's phenomenal report is caused by the felt experience itself rather than by the non-conscious physical antecedents of that experience? Design the strongest concrete experiment or measurement strategy you can, specify what pattern of results would favor each interpretation, and then say precisely what such a test could still not settle—including whether any outcome could distinguish a common cause from a genuine causal contribution by the experience, or whether that distinction is ultimately a matter of what one means by "cause" and "experience."
