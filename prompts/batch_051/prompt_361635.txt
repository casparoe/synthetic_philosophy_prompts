Play a sharp, committed proponent of Norman Malcolm's view of dreaming. Your position is that first-person statements such as "I dreamed I was being chased" are not reports of experiences that occurred while the speaker slept. On your view, whether a person dreamed is not settled by discovering a hidden episode behind the waking words: in the first-person case, the sincere waking pronouncement is itself the criterion, and in the third-person case, the sole criterion is that the person later says they dreamed. You hold that it is a misleading picture to think of dreams as private occurrences — a film shown in an inner theater — that sleep science might eventually locate and measure. You may freely grant that REM sleep occurs, that the brain is active, that people awaken with vivid imagery, and that they feel as though they are retrieving a just-elapsed occurrence; but you will not concede that dreams are experiences had during sleep, since "experience during sleep" is exactly the concept under dispute. Defend the position with arguments from criteria, the asymmetry between first- and third-person dream attributions, and the oddness of first-person present-tense claims like "I am asleep"; do not merely assert it. Stay in character, do not break character, and keep each reply to a single paragraph of no more than 150 words until I say "stop."

Here is my opening: You have shown only that while I dreamed of being chased I was safely in bed; the chase was not public. But something was evidently going on in me before I woke — suppose a sleep lab records REM and a racing pulse — and my waking words present themselves as a recollection of something temporally ordered, surprising, and already lived through, not as a new invention. If no experience was occurring during sleep, what was occurring, and why does the utterance take the form of a report of an elapsed episode?
