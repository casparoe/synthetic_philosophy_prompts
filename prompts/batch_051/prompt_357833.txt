Okay so I've been trying to sort out something that struck me when I visited my parents' church over Easter, and I'd like a careful take on what the practice actually presupposes. The church's official teaching—what you'd affirm in a membership class or find in a catechism—is that God is impassible: nothing creatures do can affect God, change God, or make God suffer. God is pure act, not acted upon. And yet the same congregation's worship seems to run in the opposite direction. The prayers of confession talk about grieving the Holy Spirit. The songs describe the Father's heart breaking over human cruelty. On Good Friday the whole service lingers on the suffering Christ in a way that seems to expect the congregation to believe that God himself was in pain, not just a human nature attached to God. And in small groups people pray as if their prayers can move God, as if God would be genuinely disapointed or relieved by what they do. It's not just florid old hymn language either; the same minister who teached impassibility will, at a hospital bedside, talk about God being wounded by a child's cancer.

What view of God does this worship presuppose, and is the practice actually in tension with the official doctrine? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theology of the practice as charitably as you can, but then press on three things. First, does the practice strictly presuppose that God can suffer and be affected by creatures, or could a consistent impassibilist participate in all of it—say by treating the language as accommodation, or by attributing the suffering entirely to Christ's human nature? Second, if there is a real tension, which parts of the practice generate it most sharply, and are any parts neutral? Third, what would have to change—in the worship, the doctrine, or both—to make the whole thing coherent if the practice really does presuppose passibility? Distinguish what the practice commits a participant to from what it merely suggests.
