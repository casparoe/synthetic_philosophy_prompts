I am a novelist drafting a near-future legal thriller, and I need to get a conceptual distinction right. I have been working with these criteria for a violation of mental privacy: (1) the material accessed is produced by the person's cognitive processes rather than inferred from their outward behavior; (2) access occurs without the person's present, specific consent; (3) a reasonable person, on learning of the access, would feel surveilled at the source of thought rather than merely observed from outside. Now consider a case in the novel. A speech-assistive brain–computer interface worn continuously by a person with aphasia monitors motor and premotor cortex; the manufacturer insists it decodes only neural preparations for movement, not propositional thought. But in discovery, investigators show that the system's logs contain a running, semantically specific model of the wearer's silent, sentence-like inner speech—unspoken rebukes, private reactions, things she deliberately did not say aloud—formed seconds before any visible behavior. Does this constitute a violation of mental privacy under my criteria? Work through each criterion in turn, say where the criteria leave the verdict underdetermined, and then tell me whether the case shows the criteria themselves need revision—perhaps because a technology that operates upstream of outward behavior makes the line between observation and surveillance itself unstable.
