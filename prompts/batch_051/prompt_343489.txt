I teach an undergrad applied ethics seminar, about 25 students, 75 minutes, no prior reading. We're doing a unit on everyday conventions — queueing, tipping, jaywalking — and whether they can generate moral obligations, plus the visitor problem: does a tourist or newcomer have to follow local conventions they didn't grow up with and maybe don't even know? I want a classroom activity that makes students feel the force of the idea that breaking a mere convention can be a real wrong, and that lets the visitor question come up inside the activity rather than me lecturing it at the end. No names for the positions up front, no readings.

The activity should probably have a coordination/free-rider structure. Maybe a queue for something mildly desirable (points, snacks, early dismissal), with roles: locals who've been told a rule, visitors who haven't, and one or two observers who can impose small sanctions. Use points or food as payoffs, not real money. Give me exact rules, what each role is told and not told, materials, and a timeline that fits the 75 minutes including setup and cleanup. The debrief should map what actually happened onto distinctions like convention vs moral rule, mutual expectation, reliance, arbitrary-but-binding, and the difference between being blameworthy and just being sanctioned. It should also get at the visitor side — e.g. is the obligation different when the visitor reasonably didn't know, when the local rule is invisible (like a tipping percentage), when locals disagree, or when the visitor is only passing through and doesn't benefit from the convention being maintained. Try to have the students generate those edge cases themselves during the debrief.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — e.g. students colluding, nobody caring about the payoff, the visitors guessing the point early and ruining the reveal, locals policing in a way that escalates into actual annoyance. And be honest about which features of the real thing the activity can't reproduce (low stakes, weak sanctions, and the fact that a real visitor brings power and colonial baggage the game flattens don't come through). I'd rather have a debrief note on that then pretend the game captures everything.
