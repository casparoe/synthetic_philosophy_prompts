Hey, I'm putting together a short "where I stand" memo for a political theory seminar on the role of religious arguments in democratic politics, and I'd rather have somebody poke at my position before the class does. I can't tell which of my commitments are actually doing damage to each other, and I don't want to just quietly drop whichever one feels hard. So treat these as my sincere, simultaneously-held views and give me an audit: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and what would have to give for the cheapest fix.

Here's the package:

(1) The state may not coerce citizens on matters of basic justice unless the justification for that coercion could in principle be accepted by reasonable people from different faiths and from none. So fundamental laws need to rest on reasons that are shareable across worldviews, not just on the fact that a majority shares one religion.

(2) But ordinary citizens in the public square should be fully free to argue from their religious convictions. Telling them they have to translate their faith into neutral-sounding language before speaking is a real civic burden, and a lot of important moral progress came from people refusing to do exactly that.

(3) Officials are a special case. A legislator casting a vote, or a judge writing an opinion, should hold themselves to a stricter standard and set aside their private comprehensive doctrines, acting only on grounds that could be shared across worldviews.

(4) At the same time I'm not interested in policing souls: if there's a genuinely adequate public justification available for a policy, then it doesn't matter for the law's legitimacy that many of the voters who supported it were actually moved by religious reasons — what matters is the public case that can be made, not the private motives behind the votes.

(5) But I also think the line between public and religious reasons can't be drawn in a principled, religion-neutral way. What counts as a reason accessible to all depends on prior decisions about what counts as shared standards of evidence and authority, so the sorting itself tends to quietly favor secular worldviews while presenting itself as neutral.

Go through and tell me which of these are in real tension, which only seem to be, and rank whatever real tensions there are by severity. For each tension, say what would have to be revised and what the least costly repair is. If two of them turn out to be compatible only once one is re-interpreted, tell me what the reinterpretation is and what I'd lose by making it.
