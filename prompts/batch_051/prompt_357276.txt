Consider the familiar argument, often associated with Roger Ebert, that video games cannot be art: a work of art is an authored object offered for contemplation, whereas a game is a system of rules and objectives with win/lose conditions, and the experience is largely the player's own doing rather than the author's expression. Now take a later development that was barely visible when that argument was most influential: the walking simulator and the ambient interactive piece, where scoring, winning, and failure states are mostly or entirely withdrawn (think of works like Dear Esther, Gone Home, or Mountain). In these works the author designs an environment, but what carries the experience is the player's agency—choosing where to go, what to attend to, how long to linger, whether to act at all. Does the Ebert-style argument survive this development? Distinguish two premises: (i) games are contests with objectives and therefore not art, and (ii) interactivity cedes authorship to the player and therefore blocks art. Say which premise is actually under pressure, whether the argument is dead, salvageable in a weaker form, or still sound with its scope clarified, and what the weaker form would be.
