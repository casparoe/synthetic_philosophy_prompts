I run an informal ethics discussion group at a mid-sized software company, and we keep coming back to the same argument: do we owe more to our own country's citizens than to foreigners, and if so, why? The latest version started when someone proposed shifting our charitable matching budget from local food banks to global health charities on cost-effectiveness grounds, and it split the room. One side says a state's job is to serve its own people, and it is obviously acceptable to fund a local shelter or to hire a citizen over a somewhat less qualified foreign applicant. The other side says that where somebody was born is morally arbitrary, and that treating compatriots as more important is just discrimination with a larger perimeter. The discussion stalls because people seem to be answering different underlying questions even when they use the same vocabulary, and we never separate issues of personal choice, company policy, and what the state may do.

I would like a taxonomy of the positions in this debate, laid out as a decision tree that sorts a person by how they answer concrete cases or claims rather than by which label they pick. Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of familiar school-of-thought names. Use splitting questions such as: whether compatriots have any special claims at all; whether any such claims are duties or merely permissions; whether they are grounded in the shared political system that makes binding decisions for everyone inside it, in shared identity or culture, or only in the greater effectiveness of helping nearby; whether they may be overridden by urgent global need; whether the relevant unit is the nation-state or a smaller community; and whether the special relationship also permits imposing extra burdens on compatriots (taxes, conscription) that it would be wrong to impose on outsiders. Phrase each splitting question so that someone can answer it without knowing any position names.

Each split should be labeled with the concrete question that separates it, and each leaf should carry a name (or "unnamed") plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Make sure the tree ends up distinguishing at least these commitments: the view that compatriot preference is only a useful heuristic for doing good overall; the view that it is a real duty grounded in shared political institutions; the view that it is grounded in shared identity rather than institutions; the view that special claims attach to small face-to-face or cooperative communities rather than to the nation-state; and the view that favoritism toward compatriots is acceptable in personal life but not in state policy. Work concrete cases into the diagnostic questions somewhere: a local food bank versus a more cost-effective global health charity when only one can be funded; a company choosing between a qualified citizen and a more skilled foreign applicant; a government deciding between improving its own retirees' healthcare and preventing famine abroad; pandemic vaccine procurement; and a conscription decision. Flag any logically possible combination of answers that no position occupies, and say for each whether it looks incoherent, merely unappealing, or unexplored. Finally, flag any standard positions your tree cannot separate, and say what additional question would separate them.
