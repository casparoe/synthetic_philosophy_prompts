I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so if I'm about to reinvent a wheel or butcher Berkeley, tell me straight. I've been reading George Berkeley's Querist, the one where he argues for a national bank in Ireland. The bit I can't shake is where he keeps coming back to the idea that money is just a ticket or counter — real riches are the land and the labour and the actual stuff, and the coins or notes are just marks that let you move those riches around. I'm paraphrasing, not quoting, because I don't have the book in front of me, but that's the gist. I've turned it into a position I call ledger-first money. The idea is that the fundamental object is a ledger: a record of who has done what for whom and who has a claim on what. Metal coins, paper notes, private keys, even gold bars — those are just the persistence layer. The system is the rows and the constraints. The token is not the data.

My central claim is that most arguments about money go wrong by treating the persistence layer as if it were the ledger. Saying we need more money when the real problem is that the ledger isn't moving is like saying the database is broken because the disk isn't spinning. Inflation, trade deficits, central bank policy, crypto, and a lot of the goldbug stuff all seem to make this same error, just in different directions. One side thinks printing tokens changes the row count; the other side thinks making tokens scarce fixes the database. Both mistake the disk for the system.

So, my questions: Is ledger-first money a real concept or just a metaphor with extra steps? Has someone already worked this out under another name (I'd guess economics has something, but I don't know the field)? And where does it actually break — what does it get wrong about money, credit, prices, or trust that a token-first picture gets right? Give me the strongest version and then tell me where it folds.
