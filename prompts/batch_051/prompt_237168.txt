Before I read another book on polarization or epistemic bubbles, I want to know what I'm actually asking a theory to do in the first place. You're a professional philosopher who works on political epistemology and disagreement — think carefully, and start by brainstorming candidate requirements before you commit to a final list. Don't just brainstorm; prune.

The theory has one job as far as I'm concerned: tell me when I owe someone on the other side continued engagement — listening, steelmanning, answering back — and when I can walk away because they're not arguing in good faith. I'm not after a theory of what the state may ban, or what an ideal reasoner with infinite time would do. I want adequacy for an ordinary person navigating a polarized, algorithmically sorted public sphere.

Anchor it on this bit of Mill, On Liberty, chapter II (1859):

"And the world, to each individual, means the part of it with which he comes in contact; his party, his sect, his church, his class of society: the man may be called, by comparison, almost liberal and large-minded to whom it means anything so comprehensive as his own country or his own age. Nor is his faith in this collective authority at all shaken by his being aware that other ages, countries, sects, churches, classes, and parties have thought, and even now think, the exact reverse. He devolves upon his own world the responsibility of being in the right against the dissentient worlds of other people; and it never troubles him that mere accident has decided which of these numerous worlds is the object of his reliance, and that the same causes which make him a Churchman in London, would have made him a Buddhist or a Confucian in Pekin."

Your deliverable is the desiderata list. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than just some camp's taste. Use the Mill passage as one of the motivating cases — interpret it — and specifically tell me whether the observation in the last sentence should actually be accepted as a desideratum, or whether Mill overstates a more limited point and the requirement should be weakened or replaced. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are negotiable. Flag any two requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied; thats the part I most want to see. Then briefly say which single requirement does the most to knock out the popular views, and leave it at that — no citations or scorecards, I'll add references myself.

One last constraint: don't let the list be a long-winded way of saying "be reasonable." If a requirement reduces to that, drop it or give it teeth.
