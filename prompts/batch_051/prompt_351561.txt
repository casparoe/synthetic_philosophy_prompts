I am a retired experimental biologist, and I have only recently begun reading academic philosophy with any seriousness. A disagreement in my family has exposed a question I cannot answer. My daughter has been married for eleven years. Two years ago her husband suffered a stroke; he survived and has recovered much of his physical function, but he is not the man he was. The quick wit, intellectual ambition, and irreverent energy that she used to say she loved are greatly diminished. Yet she insists that her love has not diminished, and that the stroke clarified rather than weakened their bond. When her brother asked what her love is now based on, she said that she loves him—the particular man—not a list of attributes that might in principle be found more fully in someone else. Her brother, an economist by training, replied that this is confused. Either her love responds to attributes of her husband, in which case a change in those attributes should rationally require a change in the love, and she should be able to say which attributes support it now; or her love responds to no attributes at all, in which case it is arbitrary and could as well have fixed on a stranger. I suspect this is a false dichotomy, but I cannot yet state the missing alternative precisely. In my old field I studied systems that track a target quantity and correct deviations from it; I keep asking myself what her love is tracking, and I suspect that frame itself may be the problem. How should a philosopher analyze this? Specifically: can a love remain answerable to reasons when the properties that originally drew the lover have largely disappeared, and what sort of reasons could these be if they are not the present properties of the beloved?
