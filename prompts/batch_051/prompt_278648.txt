I am a novelist, and I work in three languages: English, Polish, and German. I recently came across the language of thought hypothesis, the idea that cognition runs on an inner mental code — Mentalese — that is richer than, and prior to, any natural language. The idea appeals to me because it matches my sense that a fully determinate thought exists in my mind before I find the sentence for it. But I cannot tell how literally to take the language part. If all thought is conducted in Mentalese, does that mean my language of thought is simply whichever natural language I am composing in at the moment, so that drafting a chapter in German amounts to thinking in German, and revising it in English amounts to thinking in English? And would a monolingual speaker of a language with few words for a given subtle emotion therefore be unable to have that thought at all? I would like an expert in philosophy of mind and cognitive science to think through this carefully, and to begin by brainstorming what the hypothesis is actually committed to about the relation between Mentalese, inner speech, and the natural languages we write in. Then press the novelist's question: how should I model the difference between the sentence on the page, the inner speech in my head, and the underlying representation of the thought — and does the hypothesis, correctly understood, survive the evidence that bilinguals report experiencing their own thinking differently in different languages?
