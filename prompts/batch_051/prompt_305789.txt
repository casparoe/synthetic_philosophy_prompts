I follow a single rule when I want to know what someone actually values: I ignore what they say and read their values off their choices. If a colleague says she cares about reducing inequality but persistently votes against redistributive measures whenever it would cost her money, I take the votes to be the real preference. If a friend says he hates his job but keeps turning down lower-paid alternatives, I conclude that he values the salary more than he admits. I apply the rule to myself as well: when I am unsure whether I really want to finish a project, I look at how I spend my evenings rather than at what I tell myself. In practice I am not running controlled experiments; I am judging people I know from the partial record of choices I happen to see.

A friend of mine calls this naïve behaviourism about the mind. His objection is that choices are produced jointly by preferences, constraints, beliefs, and self-control, so no single action reveals a single valuation. He offers cases: a person who buys expensive insurance need not be revealing a high valuation of security, because she may have misunderstood the risk; a person in poverty who reports being content with very little may have adapted her aspirations downward rather than revealed a modest true preference; an addict's repeated choice to drink need not express what he most wants; and a vote can express identity or loyalty rather than a judgement about expected consequences. My reply is that words and self-reports are cheap, whereas choices carry costs and therefore information.

I want to know whether my policy is defensible or whether it is a bias dressed up as a rule. Please assess it as a policy: under what conditions does choice genuinely reveal valuation, when does it systematically mislead, and can the rule be restated so as to keep its benefits without its costs? Is my friend right that stated preferences should retain some weight even when they conflict with observed choices, and, if so, how should the two kinds of evidence be combined? I am especially interested in whether "utility" in the choice-theoretic sense is even meant to be an inner quantity that choices reveal, or whether it is merely a compact way of summarising consistent choice — in which case my whole metaphor of "reading off real values" may be confused.

As well as giving your verdict, please say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of. Please answer in British English.
