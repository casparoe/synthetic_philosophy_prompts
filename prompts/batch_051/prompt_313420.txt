I'm a novelist, about eighteen months into a book set in a Lancashire mill town in the 1840s, and the archive work has left me with a set of views I can't tell whether they're coherent or not. Here they are, as close as I can get to what I actually believe.

(1) An archive is a record of what institutions noticed and thought worth keeping, not a sample of what happened, so its silences are made by power and are not random.

(2) A documentary absence is still evidence of something: it tells me a person was on the wrong side of the recording machinery. But it tells me almost nothing about their inner life, or about how much they mattered to the people who knew them.

(3) When I write the inner life of someone who left no first-person trace, I'm not recovering anything from the record; I'm inventing it, and I try to be honest with myself about that.

(4) A novel that refuses to imagine those people from the inside, just because the archive is silent, is siding with the archive, so I have an obligation to imagine them anyway, inventing where the record thins out.

(5) The traces they do leave — a court deposition, a workhouse register, a line in the local paper — are so thoroughly captured and rewritten by authority that what they mostly reveal are the recorders, and I can't treat them as windows onto an interior life.

(6) But I also believe a novel can do something the archive can't: it can make a reader feel the shape of a particular missing life without claiming to have restored the voice that isn't there.

I'd like an audit, not reassurance. Which of these are genuinely in tension and which only look that way when I'm using recovery or evidence loosely? Order the real ones by severity and tell me what has to give to make the set hang together. Please write continuous prose only, no headings, bullets, bold or tables, the way you'd write a letter. And please answer in British English; the book's set in Lancashire and I'm keeping my ear in.
