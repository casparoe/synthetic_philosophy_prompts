I'm a novelist plotting a scene where my protagonist, a lapsed Catholic, argues with his uncle who still goes to Benediction, and I want the uncle's argument to be sharp, not a straw man. The uncle says: (1) After consecration, if Christ is really present under the appearance of bread, this presence cannot be detected by the senses or by the intellect; as Aquinas says in the Summa Theologiae, III, q.75, a.1: "The presence of Christ's true body and blood in this sacrament cannot be detected by sense, nor understanding, but by faith alone, which rests upon Divine authority." (2) But if a presence cannot be detected by sense or understanding, then it is, from any natural standpoint, indistinguishable from a merely symbolic presence. (3) If two states of affairs are indistinguishable from any natural standpoint, natural reason gives no grounds for preferring one over the other. (4) Therefore natural reason cannot support the claim of real presence, and the position reduces to bare faith, not knowledge. My protagonist thinks this is valid, and I half-suspect he's right. But step 2 feels like it does too much—it seems to slide from "not detectable by sense or intellect" to "no rational grounds at all." Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, under what reading, and what would a Thomist replace it with?
