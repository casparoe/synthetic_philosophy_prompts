I sit on a working group that is trying to frame a policy position on personal data, and one faction has been arguing that we should simply embrace the ownership framing: information about a person belongs to that person in roughly the way a parcel of land or a bank account does. On their view, treating personal data as property is the load-bearing move, and the rest follows from it — a company would need a license or a lease to collect and use data, people could sell, license, or withhold their data on their own terms, and collection without authorization would be a kind of trespass or conversion. For them the analogy is not rhetorical decoration; it is what lets data governance inherit the settled machinery of property law, from clear title and consent to transfer to remedies for intrusion and a market in which the rightholder sets the price.

That move seems to me to import a lot of quiet assumptions, but I am not confident which of them are fatal and which are only surface noise. I would like a structured audit of how far the data-as-property analogy actually holds. Please give an explicit correspondence table: what is supposed to play the role of the owner, the owned thing, the boundary or fence, the deed or title, the rent or sale, and the trespass. Then lay out where the mapping breaks. The trouble spots I can already see include the fact that data about a person is non-rival, since taking a copy deprives no one of the original; that most records about a person are co-produced with platforms, devices, employers, and other people; that the commercially valuable thing is often not a raw fact about me but an inference about me made inside someone else's model; and that property law is built around bounded, excludable, alienable objects, with ownership understood on a bundle-of-rights view as a cluster of rights to use, exclude, transfer, and destroy that need not all have data analogs. But I do not trust myself to weight these.

Please sort the disanalogies into those that matter for the faction's conclusion and those that do not, and then say whether the conclusion — that people should own and control information about them, with the associated rights to sell and exclude — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was being carried by the analogy the whole time. If the conclusion can survive only under a different analogy, or should be defended without the analogy, say what that better framing would be.
