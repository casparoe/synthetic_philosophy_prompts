I am a high school student drafting an essay on the surprise exam paradox, and I would be glad for comments on the central argument, especially on whether it genuinely dissolves the paradox or only relabels it.

My essay works from this version of the paradox. On Friday, a teacher announces that there will be an exam on exactly one school day in the following week, and that the students will not be able to know, before the day itself, which day it is. The students reason backward. If no exam has occurred by Thursday evening, they could infer that the exam must be Friday, which would mean knowing the date in advance, so Friday is ruled out. With Friday ruled out, they could infer Thursday on Wednesday evening, so Thursday is ruled out; and similarly for Wednesday, Tuesday, and Monday. They conclude that no exam can occur. The teacher then gives the exam on Wednesday, and the students are surprised.

My essay's thesis is that the backward induction secretly depends on treating the teacher's announcement as two separate statements that never have to be true in the same situation. There is a factual part, that an exam will occur on one of the five days, and an epistemic part, that the students will not know the day in advance. To rule out Friday, the students must suppose that on Thursday evening they would still be able to use the factual part as a premise: no exam has occurred so far, an exam is still to come, therefore it is Friday. But being able to use the premise in that way just is knowing the day in advance, which is exactly what the epistemic part says they will not be able to do. So the first step derives its force from a state of knowledge the announcement itself rules out. I want to argue from this that the announcement is not an ordinary prediction but a reflexive one: its second half makes a claim about the epistemic effects of accepting its first half, and the students' own reasoning is one of the processes that determines whether the second half is true.

The essay then draws the general lesson that the paradox is better classified with self-referential epistemic puzzles than with puzzles about induction or about the definition of knowledge. The exam does not vanish; what vanishes is the students' right to treat the no-knowledge clause as a fixed background fact while they reason through the week.

My questions are these. First, is my description of the first step fair, or does it misrepresent what the induction assumes about the students on Thursday evening? In particular, does the induction really need the students to be able to rely on the factual part in that counterfactual situation, or can the whole step be run as a purely hypothetical reduction without committing the students to any actual state of knowledge? Second, does calling the announcement reflexive actually explain anything, or have I simply attached a name to the difficulty? Third, where would an examiner most plausibly attack the essay, and how could I preempt that attack without making the essay too technical?
