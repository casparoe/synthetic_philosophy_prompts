I keep coming back to Goodman’s grue example, and whats bugging me is that the standard response—that grue is not projectible because it isn’t entrenched in our practices—feels like it changes the subject. It tells me what we habitually project, not whether there is a fact of the matter about which predicates are projectible. So I want to know if the claim that some predicates are genuinely projectible and others aren’t can be tested, rather than just settled by looking at our history of word use.

Here’s the version of the claim I’m interested in: for creatures or systems that learn from experience, using green-like categories leads to better inductive performance than using grue-like categories, and this difference isn’t just due to which categories happen to be common in their training data or community. Could someone design an actual test of that? For instance, take a bunch of reinforcement-learning agents or predictive models and give them different sets of primitives—some that carve the world at ordinary joints, and  some that use a grue-ish temporal cutoff like “emerald-looking before time t, sapphire-looking after.” Vary what counts as successful prediction or control. What pattern of results would count as evidence that projectibility is doing real work, and what pattern would suggest it is only a stylistic or historically contingent feature of our language? Also, if a test came out one way or the other, where exactly is the gap between the behavioral result and the conceptual claim? I want the strongest possible test and an honest account of what it still wouldn’t settle.
