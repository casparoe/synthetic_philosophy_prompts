Consider the claim that habitat destruction is, on net, good for wild animal welfare rather than bad, because it prevents the existence of additional wild animals whose lives would in expectation contain more suffering than they are worth. I want to know how much of my attitude toward conservation and development should be set by this claim, and the main bottleneck is figuring out which sub-question to settle first. I've provisionally broken the issue into five candidates:

(a) Is the average wild animal life net positive or net negative, and by roughly how much?
(b) If wild lives are net negative, is the badness driven mostly by rare acute events (predation, parasitism, starvation) or by chronic low-level states (hunger, fear, thermal stress, disease)?
(c) When habitat is destroyed, does the total number of wild animals actually fall, or does the population mainly shift toward species whose individual welfare is higher or lower?
(d) Should population ethics count the foregone lives of animals that would have existed if the habitat had been kept, or only the welfare of animals that do exist?
(e) How large is the suffering caused by the destruction process itself and by edge effects, relative to the ongoing difference in the size and composition of the animal population?

Rank these — and add anything you think is more fundamental — by priority for resolving the bottom line. Then say which one or two you would actually try to settle first given a year, no new fieldwork, and access only to existing observational data and models. Defend the ordering in dependency-aware terms: which answers would change the most downstream conclusions, which are tractable, and which are load-bearing mainly because of a contested philosophical assumption. End with a line in the form \answer{(a) > (c) > ...}, with highest priority first.
