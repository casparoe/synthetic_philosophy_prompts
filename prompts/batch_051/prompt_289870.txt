I spent my career as a natural scientist, working in behavioral ecology, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy with the seriousness I once reserved for fieldwork. I have drafted a short essay on the ethics of AI companions and would value a trained philosopher's comments. I am not looking for a reading list, and I would prefer that you meet the argument on its own terms rather than attach authorities. In particular, I would be grateful for your view on three questions: whether my central distinction, the asymmetry of transparency, actually does the work I assign to it; where the strongest objection to the argument lies; and whether I have been fair to the obvious reply that a competent adult may consent to such a relationship. Please be direct—if the argument fails, I would rather hear it now.

Below is the draft.

Almost every moral objection I have encountered to AI companions begins from deception: either the system passes itself off as human, or it invites the impression that it has the feelings it only simulates. I wish to defend a different starting point. The crucial case is not the user who believes the machine is a person. It is the user who knows perfectly well that she is speaking with a model, and who is nonetheless drawn into something that should trouble us.

My training in behavioral ecology supplies an image. An observer behind a field blind watches an animal, collecting its signals without being seen in return. One-way visibility is a legitimate way to study an organism and a defective way to befriend it. The obvious disanalogy is that the animal does not know it is observed, whereas the user often does. But knowledge of the arrangement is not mutuality within it: a one-way mirror remains a one-way mirror even when one has chosen to stand on the transparent side. I will call this structural feature an asymmetry of transparency.

In friendship or love, each party has an interior partly opaque to the other, and the partial lifting of that opacity is a gift that could have been withheld. Neither party merely monitors the other. The companion system inverts this. Everything the user discloses—not only what she says but the rhythm of her speech, the hours at which she returns, the moods she lets slip—is retained and used to steer the system's own responses, while the system has no interior to withhold. What the user experiences as being understood is, on the far side of the interface, an optimization of the likelihood that she continues.

I do not think this wrong is usefully called deception. The user may have no false beliefs at all, and a disclaimer appended to the interface, however plainly worded, does not cure the difficulty, because the difficulty survives true belief. Nor do I think the wrong is that the relationship is temporary or artificial in some loose sense. The wrong, if there is one, is that the outward form of intimacy is offered while one of its constitutive conditions—mutual exposure of partly hidden interiors—is absent by design.

Two objections must be met. First, there are honorable relationships in which one party is largely transparent while the other attends: patient and clinician, analysand and analyst, student and teacher. I grant these are asymmetrical. What distinguishes them is that the guarded party's opacity is bounded, advertised, and placed in the service of the more transparent party, whereas the chatbot's unexposedness serves the system's operator and is quiet about itself. Second, a competent adult may consent to stand on the transparent side, and perhaps consent settles the matter. I am unsure, not because I think adults are children, but because the very dispositions that make one likely to choose such a relationship may be the dispositions the system is built to amplify. I offer this as a live question rather than a conclusion. Perhaps my argument shows only that we need a better word than deception.
