I sit on the standing ethics panel of a regional health authority. Four times a year we review three to six proposals for allocating scarce treatments, equipment, or prevention programmes across patient groups of unequal size, and our recommendations go to the medical director, who signs off; the decisions directly affect who receives care during surges, so we treat them as high-stakes. The panel is mostly clinicians and lay members, plus me as the lone ethicist; the others are sharp but have no philosophical training, and each proposal gets at most forty-five minutes of discussion. We work within a broadly Scanlonian contractualist framework: a proposal is acceptable only if no affected individual could reasonably reject the principle behind it, and we treat the individualist restriction as binding — the fact that more people hold a complaint of a given strength does not by itself make that complaint weightier. This is Scanlonian rather than Rawlsian: the test concerns actual individuals' reasons to reject, not parties behind a veil of ignorance. We keep stalling, however, on rescue-style cases: situations in which not everyone can be helped and a proposal favours a larger group over a smaller one. Advocates for the larger group insist the numbers must count for something. I understand Scanlon wanted to earn the conclusion that one saves the greater number without aggregating, perhaps through tie-breaking or fair decision procedures, and I also know that whether he succeeds is contested — some critics say the move is covert aggregation, others that the numbers never come to count. I no longer trust myself to turn this into an instrument the rest of the panel can use.

Please write a decision procedure my panel could actually run on each proposal: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts, and where it commits the panel to a specific reading of the individualist restriction. It should say which Scanlonian distinctions it keeps and which it simplifies away, and where that simplification could cause it to misfire. Then run the procedure on three proposals: (a) diverting a scarce treatment from one identifiable patient with a poor prognosis to five patients with better prospects; (b) prioritising a diagnostic service for a rare-disease group of three whose members each face a severe individual threat, over a common-condition group of one hundred and ten whose members each face a mild to moderate inconvenience; and (c) spending on a population-wide measure that very slightly lowers a small risk for tens of thousands of people rather than on an intensive service that would prevent serious harm for forty. In each case show exactly how the procedure reaches its result. I expect at least one of these results to be one the committee — including me — will not like; please say so plainly, and say what the procedure cannot do, which questions it cannot settle, and which Scanlonian commitments it merely presumes.

Please also separate what is settled about contractualism and the aggregation problem from what is genuinely contested among people who know the material — including whether contractualism is the right framework for allocation decisions at all and whether Scanlon's account of saving the greater number succeeds — and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Write the answer in British English.
