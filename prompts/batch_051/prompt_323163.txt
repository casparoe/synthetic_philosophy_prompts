Over the past few months I have been leading a small reading group of friends, none of us with any formal training in philosophy, through Kurt Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five, and I keep returning to what the Tralfamadorians teach Billy Pilgrim about time. As I understand them, they deny that moments pass: nothing ever comes into being or slips out of it, every event simply exists, complete and simultaneous, so that a person who dies in one stretch of time is nonetheless alive in others, and the appropriate response to any death is the novel's recurring phrase of acceptance. This reminded me, from my own scattered reading, of Parmenides, who reached an even more drastic position by argument rather than by aliens. If I have him right, he held that what is could not have come from what is not, and cannot pass into what is not, so nothing is ever generated or destroyed; and because any division between two things would require a seam of non-being running through reality, what is must be a single, undivided, unchanging whole, with plurality, motion, and before-and-after belonging only to the way things misleadingly appear.

I suspect, however, that these two positions part ways in a manner I cannot quite articulate. Parmenides seems to want to erase the many altogether and leave only the One, while the Tralfamadorians keep all the many moments and merely deny that one of them has the special status of now; and Vonnegut's repeated phrase of acceptance begins, as the novel goes on, to sound less like wisdom and more like something wounded or numbed — strange company for an anti-war book built out of the firebombing of Dresden. Could you write a close reading of Slaughterhouse-Five through the Eleatic denial of plurality and change? I would like it to do three things: show where the Tralfamadorian view genuinely is Parmenidean, show where it diverges into what I understand as a block-universe picture of many co-existing moments rather than Eleatic monism, and say what the novel's irony reveals about the cost of that view — what it would actually do to a person to accept the impossibility of change — that the bare Eleatic position leaves unstated. The reading should be faithful in both directions: it should not flatten the novel into a philosophy illustration, and it should not turn Parmenides into a science-fiction fatalist. It should end by saying what fiction offers here that the argument alone cannot.
