I'm a PhD student in political philosophy working on exploitation and global labor standards, and I've been roped into preparing a teaching module on sweatshops. Before the undergrads read the serious defenses of sweatshop labor, I want them to read something satirical that gets the structure of the debate into their heads. So: write an "Ethical Sourcing FAQ" (or "Supplier Code of Conduct") for a fictional apparel company—call it Meridian Threads or something equally bland—that has enitrely internalised the mutually-beneficial-exploitation line and converted it into deadpan corporate compliance prose. The target shouldn't be any particular author; it's the style of argument that says the work is okay because both parties gain and the worker consents, since the job is better than the next-best alternative. The document should perform the moves rather than name them: the baseline set by the worker's outside option, the slide from "we didn't make them worse off" to "we treated them fairly," the convenient shrinking of the comparison class, the idea that exit and consent settle everything, the celebration of a wage premium measured in cents an hour, and so on. An undergrad with no background should get the joke, but a specialist should recognize that you actually understand the arguments—the humor has to come from the real logic, not from generic evil-corporation caricature.

Shoot for around 650 words, formatted as numbered clauses or FAQ entries, in the polite, metrics-obsessed voice of a real sustainability page. Before drafting, take a couple of minutes to brainstorm the standard moves in the mutually-beneficial-exploitation debate and pick the four or five that inflate best. Act as someone who knows the philosophical literature and is also a sharp satirist. And I know its a big ask, but keep the irony deadpan throughout—no winking, no "to be clear, this is satire" line.
