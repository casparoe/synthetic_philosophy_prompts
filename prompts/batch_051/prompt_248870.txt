In a discussion about whether several inherited institutional arrangements should be abolished or merged, a colleague kept invoking the slogan 'Chesterton's fence' — the maxim that you should not take a fence down until you know why it was put up. I can see the weak, prudential reading of this, but he seemed to be using it to establish something far stronger: that inherited institutions enjoy a standing presumption in their favour, and that the burden of proof always lies with those proposing reform. Please pull this slogan apart for me. What are the distinct, precise claims it could be used to assert? Range from readings that are nearly trivial — e.g., a purely epistemic rule that one should learn what function a practice presently serves before disrupting it — through more substantive readings, such as the Burkean thesis that institutions can encode widely dispersed, hard-to-articulate experience that no single deliberator can reconstruct, and up to strongly anti-rationalist readings according to which individual reason is systematically ill-equipped to redesign inherited practices. For each reading, say whether it is plausible, whether it is distinctively conservative, and what it would actually entitle someone to claim in an institutional-reform debate. Most importantly, tell me which of these readings, if any, can bridge the gap from 'understand it before you change it' to 'presume it should stay', and whether they are strong enough to shift the burden of proof in the way my colleague wanted. Please write the answer in American English.
