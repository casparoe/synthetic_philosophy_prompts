I keep seeing people argue about whether a digital mind could matter morally by pointing at one thought experiment—a whole brain emulation, an RL agent that says it suffers, a chatbot that passes the right tests—and then using it as a proxy for the whole question. I'd like the full spec first. Write the desiderata that a theory of moral status for digital minds has to meet to count as adequate for the practical question of how we should treat possible future AI systems, including uploads, whole brain emulations, reinforcement learners trained to minimize aversive reward signals, and very slow simulated minds. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or just a preference of a particular camp. I want the list to cover at least:

- which cases it has to get right
- which distinctions it can't collapse (having status vs how much, moral patient vs moral agent, welfare capacity vs current welfare, sentience vs sapience, etc.)
- which empirical or computational results it can't contradict
- what it has to say about when a system goes from no status to some status, if it ever does

Then flag which single requirement does the most work excluding popular positions, and whether any pair of desiderata look like they cant be jointly satisfied. Skip the scorecard; I'll do that part myself. The point is to have the list against which the one-case arguments are supposed to be checked, not another one-case argument.
