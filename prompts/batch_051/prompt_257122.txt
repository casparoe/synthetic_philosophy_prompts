Lately I have found myself stuck on a pattern in the way we talk about ordinary objects, and I would like to know whether philosophy has a precise name for it — and, more importantly, how the boundaries of the concept are drawn, because a bare label will not settle the cases below.

Here is the pattern. A piece of stuff is shaped into a particular object. The stuff and the shaped object seem, in one sense, to be exactly the same thing: they share a location, a weight, a history from the moment of shaping onward, and I cannot point to any extra bit that belongs to one and not the other. But in another sense they differ. The stuff existed before the object came into being and will survive the object's destruction; the object did not and will not.

Example one. On Monday I buy a lump of clay and leave it in a box. On Tuesday I mould it into a statue of a horse and set it on a shelf. I am inclined to say there is exactly one thing on the shelf. But the lump was around on Monday while the statue was not, and if I now squash the thing, the statue is destroyed while the lump remains. So the lump and the statue do not have the same history or the same prospects. If they were strictly the same thing, how could they differ in what happened to them? If they are two things, then two material objects occupy the same place at the same time, which seems like double-counting.

Example two, which feels milder but raises the same difficulty. I fold a sheet of paper into a crane. The crane is destroyed when I unfold the sheet, but the sheet survives. Again I want to say there is one thing on the table, not two, yet the sheet has a past that the crane lacks.

What I am trying to pin down is the specific relation between the piece of stuff and the thing made from it when the stuff can survive the destruction of the thing but not vice versa. Is there an established name for this kind of case? And how do philosophers decide what to say about it without either multiplying objects or ignoring the apparent differences?

I would also like to know where the edges are. Does the pattern require the stuff to be rearranged rather than destroyed and replaced? Does it matter whether the shaped object is valued or named, or would a knot in a rope or an accidental dent in a car door raise the same issue? Is a word written in a stretch of sand an instance, or is that somehow only an arrangement and not a thing? Are there everyday cases where even someone committed to there being only one object would feel forced to admit two? And is the source of the discomfort in the world, or in something about how we count and classify things? I want the carving, not just the terminology.
