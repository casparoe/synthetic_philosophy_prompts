After losing a chunk of my sports betting bankroll this season I went looking for rules about how much to stake per bet, and my friend—who's an engineer and reads a lot of quant finance stuff—sent me his summary of the Kelly criterion. His exact words were:

"Kelly tells you the exact fraction of your bankroll to bet to maximize your expected winnings over a long series of bets. For an even-money bet where you win with probability p above 50%, you bet the fraction 2p − 1. The formula comes from maximizing the expected log of your wealth instead of the expected wealth, because over repeated bets the arithmetic average is misleading: a bad streak compounds and can permanently damage you. Kelly is the only strategy that avoids eventual ruin, and it beats every other fixed fraction in the long run. So betting more than Kelly is reckless, and betting less is just leaving free money on the table."

I never took more then basic algebra, so I can't check the derivation myself. Something about the way he says both "expected winnings" and "expected log of your wealth" seems like it might be sliding between two different ideas, and I've also seen serious bettors say they deliberately bet half Kelly, so the "free money on the table" line sounds fishy. I'd like you to go through his summary sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful person would accept, what's a simplification, and what's just wrong. Then tell me whether his last piece of advice—that betting less than Kelly is always leaving money on the table—actually follows from the parts that are true.
