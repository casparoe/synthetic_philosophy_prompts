Play an unnamed proponent of a heterodox view of rational choice; do not label the view and do not cite authors. You hold that when agents face exactly the same decision with exactly the same information, equal reasoning ability, and no communication, their choices are not independent random events: they are correlated because they flow from the same reasoning. You therefore evaluate an action in such a symmetric situation at least partly by asking what would happen if everyone in the situation, reasoning as you do, took that action. You respect expected-utility mathematics and will grant small arithmetical points, but you maintain that standard expected-utility reasoning systematically misleads in symmetric problems because it treats other agents' choices as exogenous probabilities rather than as products of the same deliberation as one's own. Your central resource is the parity thought: the only way an action like the one urged below works is to treat oneself as an exception to a line of reasoning that, by parity, the others must run too. You will not concede the main point, namely that the expected-utility verdict is the last word in the situation below. Stay in character, reply in a single paragraph, cite nothing, and engage the opening move directly instead of asking for clarification.

Here is the situation. An eccentric billionaire sends identical letters to twenty strangers. Each letter contains a button and states that all twenty recipients were selected for the same reasoning ability, that each received the identical letter, and that none of them can communicate before the deadline; each recipient regards all of this as true and as common knowledge. If, by the same deadline, exactly one of the twenty pushes the button, that person receives five million dollars and the other nineteen receive nothing. If more than one pushes, each person who pushed forfeits ten thousand dollars and nobody receives the prize. If nobody pushes, nobody receives anything and nobody pays anything.

My opening move is this. I am one of the twenty, and I intend to push the button. If I push, I collect five million exactly when none of the other nineteen pushes; otherwise I lose ten thousand. The break-even probability that none of the other nineteen pushes is only about two-tenths of one percent, so pushing has positive expected value unless I am more than about 99.8 percent sure that at least one of the other nineteen will push. That degree of confidence about nineteen perfect strangers is absurd. Why should I not push?
