Two colleagues of mine, an economist and a political philosopher, spent a long lunch arguing about the axiomatic foundations of bargaining. They agreed on the setting: two parties, a disagreement point normalized to (0,0), and a compact, convex set of feasible utility pairs, not necessarily symmetric. The economist defended the Nash bargaining solution. His opening move was that Nash's solution is pinned down by four axioms—Pareto efficiency, symmetry, scale invariance, and independence of irrelevant alternatives—and that only the last is genuinely contestable. The philosopher replied that scale invariance is less innocent than he thinks, because it rules out appealing to any comparison of utility gains across parties, and that independence of irrelevant alternatives is exactly the wrong axiom: it makes the outcome insensitive to how much each party could have achieved. The economist said that insensitivity is a virtue, since agreements should not be reopened just because an unchosen option is deleted or added. The philosopher countered that the options are not irrelevant; the maximal payoff each party could realistically reach sets the baseline for measuring concession, and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution—which keeps Pareto efficiency, symmetry, and scale invariance but replaces independence of irrelevant alternatives with individual monotonicity—uses exactly that information: it selects the maximal feasible payoff pair lying on the line from the disagreement point to the point whose coordinates are the two parties' highest individually attainable payoffs. The economist's final move was that this lets a party manipulate the outcome by adding outlandish alternatives that raise her own maximum, even if no one would ever accept them; the philosopher responded that feasible alternatives change the structure of the bargaining problem, so moving with them is what a monotone rule should do, and calling them outlandish presumes the Nash conclusion. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: identify which claims are right, which are overstated or question-begging, and whether the underlying disagreement is ultimately about different fairness commitments or whether one side has the better argument on neutral grounds.
