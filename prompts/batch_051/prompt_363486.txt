Since retiring from a career in the physical sciences, I have been reading my way through political philosophy and, more recently, through the empirical literature on policing. What I find genuinely perplexing is that the reform–defund–abolish debate does not seem to converge, even among serious people citing largely the same evidence: crime trends after budget adjustments, officer-involved shootings, randomised trials of body-worn cameras, and evaluations of crisis-intervention teams. The reformer concludes that the institution can be corrected through better training, accountability and procedure; the defunder concludes that the police are being asked to handle problems they are structurally unsuited to solve and that resources should be shifted toward housing, mental-health care and social work; and the abolitionist concludes that the institution is inseparable from domination and should be dismantled rather than improved. My working hypothesis, for what it is worth, is that this is not fundamentally a disagreement about facts nor even about values, but about the unit of analysis: the individual officer, the organisation, or the social function that policing performs. I am not confident in that diagnosis, and I would value a more rigorous one.

I am therefore asking for a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, and why it has remained so intractable. Are there hidden empirical questions doing the work — about deterrence, about the counterfactual behaviour of serious crime in the absence of police, about the tractability of reform in large hierarchical institutions — such that the debate would largely collapse once those questions were settled? Or is the impasse better explained by different standards of evidence, different burdens of proof, or bedrock intuitions about state coercion and legitimate hierarchy? And, on the basis of your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a particular study design, a conceptual distinction, a reformulation of the question, or an honest acknowledgement that the three positions are rival political commitments rather than rival predictions? I am not looking for a survey of the literature; I am looking for an account of the structure of the disagreement itself. Please write your answer in British English.
