For a final paper in the biomedical ethics unit of my high school's history of science elective, I have to analyze a single historical case through an ethical framework rather than simply narrate it. I chose the Tuskegee syphilis study. My understanding of the basic facts is this: beginning in 1932, the United States Public Health Service enrolled roughly six hundred African American men in Macon County, Alabama—around four hundred with syphilis and around two hundred without—telling them they were being treated for a condition people in the area referred to as bad blood. In fact, the men with syphilis were not told their diagnosis, were not treated for it, and were not offered penicillin after it became the standard cure in the 1940s; the study ended only after it was exposed in 1972. I am not asking for another retelling of the history, and I am not asking for a list of sources.

What I want is a careful case analysis using the account of valid consent that says consent is valid only when the person has the capacity to decide, is adequately informed, and gives it voluntarily. Take each major feature of how the researchers treated the participants and sort it under deception, coercion, or decisional-capacity failure, then say which of those three categories does the largest explanatory and moral work in accounting for what was wrong. Then press on the framework itself: what does the three-part account capture well about this case, and what does it distort or leave out—things like structural racism, extreme poverty, or the fact that the wrong was not only a defective consent process but also the deliberate withholding of a known cure? Finally, evaluate whether Tuskegee shows that the tripartite account of valid consent is strong enough to serve as the central safeguard in human-subjects research ethics, or whether the case reveals that concentrating on consent lets other ethical failures hide. If you had to keep one element—capacity, information, or voluntariness—as the deepest normative core of this episode, which would it be, and what would that choice imply about how research rules should be designed?
