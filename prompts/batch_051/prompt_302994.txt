I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I maintain the documentation and code-review standards for a platform team. I keep noticing an asymmetry in ordinary technical practice. If an example in our docs defaulted to a particular race or ethnicity — every "customer" had the same surname, or the diagrams always showed "the manager" as one ethnicity — reviewers would treat it as a clear incident and block the merge. Yet the same docs routinely use "he" for the generic user, developer, and manager ("each user changes his settings"), and that gets treated as a legacy quirk or style nit at most. Hofstadter's "Person Paper" presses on this sort of double standard by rerunning the usual defenses of sex-generic English with race swapped in for sex, and the defenses appear to collapse. Give me the strongest candidate principle that could justify treating race-based defaults as severe while treating sex-based defaults as merely outdated, test it against nearby cases, and then give a committed verdict: does the principle succeed, or is the asymmetry mostly a historical accident we should stop enforcing — and what should a style guide actually do if the latter is the case?
