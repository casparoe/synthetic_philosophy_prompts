In my graduate seminar on the philosophy of physics, a visitor presented a reconstruction of the hole argument against substantivalism — the view that spacetime points are real individuals over and above the metric field — and I cannot decide which step to give up. Every premise seems acceptable on its own, and yet the conclusion seems unacceptable. The reconstruction ran as follows.

(1) A model of general relativity is a pair (M, g): a differentiable manifold M of spacetime points, together with a Lorentzian metric g satisfying Einstein's field equations. (2) Because the field equations are tensorial, they are diffeomorphism-covariant: if (M, g) solves them, then so does (M, d*g), where d is any smooth one-to-one map from M onto itself with smooth inverse and d*g is the metric dragged along by d. (3) Choose d to be the identity everywhere outside a region H — say, to the future of some spacelike surface S — and non-trivial only inside H. Then (M, g) and (M, d*g) agree everywhere outside H, while inside H the same spacetime points are assigned different metric roles; the global pattern of geometry is unchanged, being merely shifted from point to point. (4) If substantivalism is true, the points of M are real things, so these two models describe distinct physical worlds: one and the same point p inside H plays one geometric role in one world and a different geometric role in the other. (5) The two worlds are exactly alike outside H — including the entire region before S — and differ only inside H. So from identical conditions before S the theory leads to different futures. (6) Therefore substantivalism implies that general relativity is radically indeterministic: fixing everything outside H does not fix what happens inside H. (7) But general relativity is not radically indeterministic in this way; the standard practice is to treat diffeomorphically related models as representing one and the same physical situation. (8) Hence substantivalism is false.

I would most like to keep (7), and I would also like to preserve as strong a version of (4) as possible, but I cannot see how to hold both. Which step or steps should be rejected, and what does each available rejection cost? More specifically, where in the argument is the choice really forced, and is there a version of substantivalism that can accept the argument's premises about the formalism while denying that determinism is lost? Please keep your answer to at most 600 words, naming the step or steps by number before defending your diagnosis.
