I retired from a career in evolutionary ecology, and for the past few years I have been reading my way through philosophy of science and the literature on probability and inference. The problem that keeps tugging at me, somewhat unexpectedly, is the Fermi paradox. The standard resolutions I encounter—abiogenesis is rare, tool-making intelligence is rarer still, civilizations reliably destroy themselves, or they are deliberately staying quiet—all strike me as either too convenient or too dependent on a single unmeasured parameter.

I have been toying with an idea I provisionally call "technological crypsis." In ecology, crypsis is the phenomenon whereby an organism evolves a form or behavior that makes it difficult to detect by predators or prey. My suggestion is that detectability is a phase rather than a destination for technological life. A species that survives its noisy, expanding, energy-wasteful adolescence may, for entirely local reasons of thermodynamic and computational efficiency, settle into a mature configuration that is compact, self-contained, and close to invisible at interstellar distances. Broadcasting and galaxy-spanning construction become overhead once a civilization fastens on whatever optimization targets actually matter to it. On this view, the Great Silence is not evidence of our solitude, nor of universal self-destruction; it is close to what a mature universe should look like from the outside. Silence is an attractor.

I cannot tell whether I have found a mechanism or merely renamed a hunch. Is "technological crypsis" coherent enough to be taken seriously? Has it already been worked out under another name, and if so, what distinguishes my version, if anything? Finally, where does it break—which step overreaches, and what observation would tell against it? Please write your answer in American English.
