I am clearing the ground before evaluating any particular theory of history, and I want the specification first: what an adequate theory of historical law would have to get right. Treat "law of history" in the strong sense Popper attacked under the name "historicism" in his book The Poverty of Historicism — I will use "historicism" in his sense, not in the sense of the historicization of concepts. A theory of historical law, for present purposes, claims there are law-like regularities in the course of human history on the strength of which the future could be projected, at least conditionally.

Please write the desiderata for such a theory. For each desideratum, give:

- a precise statement of the requirement, not a label;
- the case or consideration that makes it a requirement — what the theory loses if it drops it;
- whether it is non-negotiable, negotiable, or only the preference of one camp (and if so, which camp);
- which family of theories it presses hardest against, if any.

Then do the part I most want: identify any subset of the desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, or that can be jointly satisfied only by weakening the theory until it no longer deserves the name "theory of historical law." State each tension precisely and give the best available compromise.

There are points I already regard as important, but I want them placed in a full list rather than allowed to do all the work alone. They should appear, though you may push back on their status:

(a) the trend/law distinction: a trend is a statement about a particular historical sequence and does not by itself license projection unless boundary conditions are specified;
(b) the distinction between conditional scientific predictions and unconditional historical prophecies;
(c) the Oedipus effect — public knowledge of a prediction can alter the event predicted — so any purported historical law must say whether it is invariant under its own publication;
(d) Popper's argument that the course of history is shaped by the growth of knowledge, and that future knowledge cannot be predicted because that would mean knowing now what will only be known later.

I also want requirements from outside Popper, or at least not obviously his: about falsifiability, about the openness of social systems to exogenous influences, about the theory's reflexivity (does it characterize its own emergence and acceptance?), and about vacuity or triviality. Include these where they are genuine, or argue that some are not genuine requirements at all.

Then score two positions briefly: historicism as Popper characterizes it, and either historical materialism or a stage theory of civilizational development, whichever you can assess with the least guesswork. State in one line any factual assumption you are making about your chosen second view. Keep the scoring to one row per requirement; the requirements themselves should carry the weight.

Format the entire answer as a JSON object with exactly these fields, and no text outside it:

- "purpose": one sentence fixing the job the theory is being asked to do;
- "requirements": an array of objects, each with:
  - "id": a short identifier such as "R1";

  - "grounds": the case or consideration that makes it a requirement;
  - "status": one of "non-negotiable", "negotiable", or "camp-specific";
  - "presses": which family of theories it causes the most trouble for, and why, in one sentence;
- "unsatisfiable_subsets": an array of objects, each with:
  - "ids": an array of identifiers from the requirements list;
  - "conflict": a precise statement of why they cannot all be fully met;
  - "compromise": the best available weakening, or an explanation of why no weakening is acceptable;
- "most_exclusionary_requirement": an object with:
  - "id": the identifier of the single requirement that does the most to exclude candidates;

  - "id": the requirement identifier;

  - "second_view_assessment": assessment of the chosen second view against the requirement, in one sentence.

Write every English string value in British English (spellings and punctuation); this request is written in American English, but I would like the answer itself to use British conventions.
