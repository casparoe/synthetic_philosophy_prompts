Consider two workers in a platform's trust-and-safety operation, both doing the same job: reviewing uploaded videos and judging whether each violates the platform's ban on non-consensual intimate imagery. The first is a human moderator. On Tuesday she reviews a video, judges that it violates the ban, and believes it should be removed immediately; but her role gives her no authority to remove content. She can only apply the violation label and file it in the same automated queue as everyone else. The video stays up for two days, and the person in it is identifiable throughout. The second is the platform's content-moderation AI, running in the same queue. On Wednesday it reviews an indistinguishable video, produces the same violation label with the same confidence, and likewise has no authority to remove the video; it can only pass the label along. The video again stays up for two days.

Many people hold both of the following judgments without strain: the human moderator has done something morally weighty — there is something she failed to do, or at least a reason for her to lose sleep or resign, and her label is a judgment for which she can be called to account — while the AI has not done anything morally weighty; its label is a technical output, and the responsibility belongs entirely to the humans who built, deployed, and staffed the queue. Both are constrained agents in the relevant sense: each can judge that the video should be removed, and neither can remove it. Is there a principle that generates both judgments, or only a psychological explanation for an inconsistency we happen to live with? Give me the strongest principle you can find, test it against nearby cases, and reach an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.

Nearby cases to test a candidate principle against include: a moderator whose labels are automatically discarded by a faulty queue; an AI that is authorized to block content whenever its confidence exceeds a threshold; a human moderator who could email the board but would be fired and has no savings; an AI that can explain its labels in natural language and reports something resembling distress at the outcome; and a human hired specifically because she follows the rule without moral qualms. If you defend one of the familiar justifications — the capacity to act otherwise, the capacity to suffer or care, answerability to reasons, being the right kind of target for reactive attitudes such as blame and resentment, or the claim that the AI's output is not a genuine judgment because it lacks the right history, understanding, or ownership — say so explicitly and say where it breaks.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on moral agency and responsibility. Do not present a contested view — for example, about whether phenomenal consciousness is necessary for moral agency, or about whether the AI's label is a genuine judgment rather than merely behaviour — as if it were the consensus. If your verdict depends on taking one side of such a dispute, mark that.
