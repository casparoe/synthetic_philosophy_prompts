I am a PhD student in philosophy working on the epistemology of AI, and I follow a research rule that I cannot fully defend. The rule is: I do not let a large language model produce the first pass of my understanding of anything I intend to cite or build on. Concretely, I will not read an LLM's summary of a paper, its map of a literature, or its list of objections to an argument until I have written my own summary, map, or objection list by hand. Afterwards, I treat the model's output as a check: where it differs from mine, I take that as a signal about what to reread, not as a correction to adopt. My rationale is that compressing an argument, seeing which premises carry the weight, and anticipating objections are exactly the abilities my training is meant to produce, and outsourcing the first pass would let those abilities decay; the offloading worry about eroded understanding, in other words, is not just a theoretical claim for me. I also suspect the model's frame, if it arrives first, would anchor me and make it harder to notice where its summary quietly distorts the paper.

But I am not sure this is a principle rather than a protection of my own vanity. I do not insist on doing long division before using a calculator, and I rely happily on search engines and reference managers. The model is faster and often more comprehensive than I am, and my rule has real costs when I am behind. So: is this policy defensible as a protection of understanding, or is it a bias—a preference for my own summary simply because it is mine? More concretely, which features of a task (summarizing, mapping, generating objections) make first-pass outsourcing more or less corrosive to understanding? And what would a more defensible version of the rule be: under what conditions should I let the model go first?
