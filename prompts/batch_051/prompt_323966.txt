It is often argued that some experiences are transformative in two distinct ways at once: they are epistemically transformative, because going through them teaches you something about what the experience is like that no amount of outside description could have supplied; and they are personally transformative, because they change your preferences, values, and sense of self. Paradigm cases include deciding to have a child, undergoing cochlear implantation, enlisting in the military, taking a monastic vow, or immigrating to a radically different culture. On the strongest version of the argument, such decisions are rationally underdetermined: before the fact you lack both the relevant phenomenal knowledge and the values by which your future self will judge the outcome.

Suppose that in 2040 a technology called the PreView becomes widely and cheaply available throughout the developed world. The PreView works as follows. When a person is considering a candidate transformative experience, the device induces a roughly ninety-minute, fully immersive, first-personal simulation of what it is like to be in the target state. The simulation samples the phenomenal character of the state with high fidelity, and the subject later remembers it in ordinary episodic detail. The device is safe and has no significant direct side effects. Two constraints are built in by design. First, the PreView can sample only a representative slice of the target phenomenology; it cannot simulate the long-run, second-order effects of the experience, such as the way parenthood, military service, or conversion gradually rewrites a person's preferences, relationships, and self-conception over years. Second, the PreView operates under a spectator warranty: the subject remains aware throughout that this is only a preview, that their current identity and values are intact, and that they will step out of the simulation unchanged.

Give a reasoned prediction of how the availability of the PreView changes both the philosophical debate and the actual practice of life-shaping decisions. Address the following.

1. To what extent does the PreView dissolve the epistemic half of the argument, and does removing only that half leave the rational-choice problem standing? Identify any residual ignorance that still matters for the rationality of these decisions, and say why it matters.
2. Over roughly twenty years, what would you expect to change in at least two of the following domains: decisions to become parents, elective medical procedures that alter sensory capacities, career changes, or religious conversion? Distinguish predictions you would defend with high confidence from those that depend on assumptions about how people actually use information about future selves.
3. Describe at least one new failure mode the PreView might create, such as people using previews as a substitute for deciding, over-updating on a short sample, or a market in misleading previews. State the conditions under which this failure mode would become severe rather than marginal.
4. State explicitly which part of the original transformative-experience argument survives this counterfactual and which part turns out to have been an artifact of the epistemic obstacle. Also state what would have to be true about preference change or memory for your central prediction to be wrong.

Argue from mechanisms—about experience, memory, preference change, and decision-making—rather than by surveying positions.
