I run a reading group on self-locating belief for advanced undergraduates—about 18 students, one 90-minute meeting. We will have read Adam Elga’s paper “Self-locating belief and the Sleeping Beauty problem.” Design a structured in-class activity, not a lecture, that makes the participants feel the force of this passage from Elga:

“Notice that the difference between your being in T1 and your being in T2 is not a difference in which possible world is actual, but rather a difference in your temporal location within the world.”

The activity should stage the Sleeping Beauty setup with real choices and small stakes (course participation credit, not money), so that the group has to act from inside a waking before the distinction between H1, T1, and T2 is named. The idea should show up in what participants do, not in what the facilitator says afterward. Give me: materials, rules, a timeline, a payoff scheme that doesn’t require real money, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity to the quoted passage and to the claim that one’s credence in Heads ought to be 1/3. By the end, participants should come away seeing why this belief change is not a response to new information about the world. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be explicit about which feature of the self-locating belief problem the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply. If the task as posed rests on a confused premise—for example, if you think a classroom game cannot genuinely separate possible-world uncertainty from self-locating uncertainty without a covert lecture—say that plainly instead of giving me a bad activity.
