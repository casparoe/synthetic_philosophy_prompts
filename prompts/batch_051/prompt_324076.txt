I teach an undergraduate philosophy of mathematics seminar with about twenty students, mostly mathematics and philosophy majors who can verify a proof line by line but cannot say much about what the proof as a whole is doing. Design a ninety-minute classroom activity, requiring no more than high-school mathematics, built around an elementary proof such as the irrationality of the square root of 2, that makes students feel the difference between checking that each step follows and understanding why the theorem is true. They should run into the distinction themselves before it is named: have them verify, reconstruct, or modify an argument and hit the limits of purely local checking, with no framing lecture and no technical vocabulary until the debrief. In the debrief, map concrete moments in the activity onto the difference between procedural fluency and explanatory understanding—why one can confirm every line yet fail to adapt the argument to a nearby case, or fail to say which hypotheses are doing real work. Also say what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of understanding a proof, such as seeing why the proof is natural rather than merely correct, the activity cannot produce and the facilitator must supply.
