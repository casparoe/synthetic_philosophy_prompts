After a long career in the physical sciences, I have been spending my retirement reading philosophy systematically, and I have become stuck on Boethius's treatment of divine foreknowledge in the final book of the Consolation of Philosophy. As I understand the standard interpretation, Boethius resolves the apparent conflict between God's knowledge and human freedom by denying that God literally foreknows anything: God is eternal rather than everlasting, and he sees the whole temporal order at once, as someone stationed on a height sees the whole road, while the travelers below see only the stretch before them. Since God's knowledge of my act is not temporally prior to the act, it does not constrain the act.

I find this picture elegant, but I have come to suspect that it is only a change of metaphor, not a solution. The argument below expresses my worry, and I find its conclusion so unacceptable that I suspect a proof has gone wrong somewhere. I want to know which step you would reject and what rejecting it costs.

Let W be the proposition that I will walk before breakfast tomorrow, a future act I take to be free and of no moral significance.

(1) On Boethius's picture, God sees from eternity whether W.
(2) God's seeing whether W does not make W true. W owes its truth, if it has one, to my walking, and it would be true even if no mind, temporal or eternal, knew it.
(3) Suppose W is true. Then it is impossible that W should be true and I should fail to walk; the two states of affairs cannot both obtain.
(4) But if it is impossible that W be true and I fail to walk, and W is true, then it is impossible that I fail to walk. For if I failed to walk, W would be false, which contradicts the supposition that W is true.
(5) If it is impossible that I fail to walk, then I cannot refrain from walking; and if I cannot refrain, I do not walk freely.
(6) Nothing in (2)–(5) depends on whether the knower is temporal or eternal. The truth of W does all the work, and an atemporal knower adds nothing to it. So Boethius's move to eternity leaves the argument exactly where it was.
(7) W was an arbitrary future act of mine. Therefore, either there are no truths about future free actions — future-tensed statements about them are somehow neither true nor false — or no one ever acts freely.

Both alternatives repel me, yet I cannot find the misstep. I can see at least three junctures at which the argument might be broken: the move from God's seeing whether W to W's being true apart from any mind (1)–(2); the move from the truth of W to the impossibility of my failing to walk (3)–(4); and the claim that a temporal foreknower and an atemporal seer are on all fours with respect to this argument (6). Each of these seems plausible until I press on it, and each costs something I would not otherwise give up.

So my question is: which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the decisive failure is the move from truth to impossibility, I want the diagnosis pushed all the way down: state exactly which modal inference is invalid, why it looked valid, and whether it can be blocked without surrendering the ordinary thought that the past is fixed and that truth is truth. And I would like to know whether, after the analysis is done, Boethius's thesis that God is eternal does any genuine work, or whether it is a vivid way of drawing a distinction that had to be drawn in any case.
