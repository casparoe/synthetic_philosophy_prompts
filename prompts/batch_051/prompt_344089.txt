I direct human resources at a midsized software firm, and roughly once a week my team has to decide what happens after one employee harms another: a public outburst that humiliates someone, a careless error that lands a colleague in trouble, an abusive late-night message, or a slow pattern of undermining. We have to decide both what the company does — a reprimand, a performance plan, termination, a referral to the employee assistance program — and, just as importantly, what stance managers and the harmed colleagues should take toward the person afterward. Are they to be treated as someone who owes an apology and can work to repair trust, or as a problem to be managed, monitored, and kept away from certain situations? The stakes are high: bad calls have cost us good people and nearly led to litigation. The committee includes me, two HR business partners, and the relevant department head; none of us has a philosophy background, legal reviews our recommendations for consistency, and we usually have about five working days to reach a disposition.

I have been reading P.F. Strawson's essay Freedom and Resentment, and I am interested in his distinction between holding someone responsible as a participant in ordinary human relationships — with resentment, hurt, forgiveness, and the demand for good will — and taking what he calls the objective stance, in which we set those reactions aside and treat the person as something to be handled, managed, or treated, as we might treat a hazard or a patient. Strawson observes that this shift is warranted by different kinds of considerations: excuses, which show that a particular act did not really express the person's settled attitudes or intentions (an accident, a good-faith mistake, acting under coercion or misinformation), and exemptions, which show that the person is not currently a fit participant in these relationships at all (severe mental disorder, immaturity, extreme stress or deprivation). But he also suggests that the objective stance is never simply forced on us by the facts, and that something important is lost whenever we take it toward another adult.

I want to turn this into a reusable procedure my team can actually run, not an essay. Write us a decision procedure — questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a particular outcome, and where it explicitly hands the decision to a human as a values judgment — that distinguishes when the company should keep someone fully inside the participant stance (accountable, answerable, capable of repairing trust) from when it should shift to the objective stance (manage, monitor, treat, and probably end the working relationship without framing it as retribution). Ground it in the Strawsonian considerations above, but explain any jargon as you go. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a senior engineer, working from ambiguous and contradictory instructions, publicly tells a junior colleague that her work is not close to production quality; it is technically accurate but humiliating, and she files a complaint; (2) an employee sends a stream of abusive messages to a coworker during a documented acute manic episode, the first such episode; (3) over eight months, a manager quietly excludes one of the few nonwhite employees from important meetings, leaves his name off documents he contributed to, and describes him in writing as not a culture fit while treating everyone else warmly. The procedure should yield a clear disposition for each case, and at least one disposition should be one a typical HR team would not like — say why the procedure nonetheless requires it. Finally, say plainly which parts of this procedure are genuine judgment calls wearing the costume of a checklist, and what the procedure cannot decide: for example, whether resentment actually fades, or whether a harmed employee is ready to forgive.
