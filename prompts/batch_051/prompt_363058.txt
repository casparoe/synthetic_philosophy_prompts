I just got back from a long argument with my friend Marcus about whether individuals who eat meat are off the hook because no single purchase changes how many animals are raised. His line was that demand gets aggregated and smoothed, so my one burger—or even my whole year of burgers—doesn't map onto any extra cow; it just moves the rounding around. I pushed back that if everyone thought that way the system gets a free pass, but he said that's a generalization argument and he isn't responsible for what everyone would do if everyone thought like him, only for what he actually causes. By the end I couldnt tell how far the objection was supposed to reach. This isnt abstract for me either, because I sit on the university's dining and sustainability commitee, and we vote Thursday on a proposal to make all three dining halls plant-based by default, with meat and dairy still available at one request-only station. Our internal estimate says it would switch maybe 15–20% of entrees and cut food-related emissions by a few percent of an already small slice of the campus total, and one member has basically adopted Marcus's argument at the institutional level: the university is just a slightly bigger individual, and a few hundred thousand meals won't reach any production threshold either, so the policy is mostly symbolic paternalism. Another member says that's confused because a default isn't a consumer choice—it's a structural change that bundles many choices into one decision point, and institutions are allowed to use a different causal story than individuals. I need to draft the short memo I'll circulate before the meeting: recommend approve, reject, or approve with modifications, and honestly engage the strongest version of the loosing side rather than just the climate math. Where should we land, and which causal claim can we actually rest the decision on?
