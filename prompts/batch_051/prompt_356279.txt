In thinking about how the value of a conscious state is built from two ingredients—how conscious the subject is, and how good or bad the state feels—three templates present themselves: add the two together, multiply them, or order them lexically so that one strictly dominates and no amount of the other can compensate for its absence. I keep finding myself pulled toward the multiplicative template, but I also have a firm intuition that it gets a familiar kind of case wrong, and I want a negotiated verdict rather than a victory for either side.

Let me set the terms precisely. Let C measure the subject's level of consciousness: the depth, richness, or standing sensitivity of the awareness a creature brings to its experience generally. Let Q measure the felt quality of a particular state—its valence and intensity, how good or how bad it actually feels to the one undergoing it. The multiplicative account says that the welfare contribution of the state is the product, V = C × Q. Its virtues are real and systematic. It gets the limiting case right immediately: where there is no consciousness at all, C = 0, and nothing is good or bad for the subject. It also avoids the crudeness that would say every sentient experience counts the same, since a richer awareness amplifies whatever falls within it. And it gives one continuous function with no arbitrary threshold at which value suddenly switches on, which generates the rough cross-species ordering most people accept: a dog's pain weighs more than a bee's, yet the bee's pain is not dismissed as worthless.

But here is the intuition I cannot shake. Suppose a fish and a dog are each in pain, and the pain feels exactly as bad to the fish as the dog's pain feels to the dog—same subjective intensity, same dreadfulness, just as fully felt. It seems to me that those two pains are equal harms. The fish's globally dimmer form of awareness should not discount this pain, because the moral role of consciousness is to make an experience count at all, not to rate each experience by the standing wattage of the mind that hosts it. If I stipulate that the felt intensity is the same, then the feature of consciousness that matters for how bad the pain is has already been counted inside Q. Multiplying by C again looks like double counting: either C is nothing more than how intensely the state is felt, in which case it is already in Q, or C is something further—global cognitive richness, self-awareness, representational depth—in which case it is not obvious why it should scale a pain that is stipulated to feel exactly as bad. Put differently, I want to say that consciousness is a gate, not a gain.

I am not asking you to announce which side wins. I am asking you to negotiate. Give me the strongest version of each position, including the best case for why the multiplicative theory has the virtues I listed and the best case for treating the equal-felt-intensity intuition as a genuine datum. Then tell me, step by step, what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that gives—what kind of debunking argument would have to succeed, and how I would know it was a real debunking rather than a rationalization for keeping a tidy formula. And tell me what it would take for the multiplicative theory to give: whether it should collapse into a gated or lexical view, or be amended by distinguishing the local intensity of a token experience from a creature's standing level of consciousness, and what each amendment would lose. I want a precise account of what each move would have to look like, what would justify it, and which negotiation—if any—lets both sides survive.
