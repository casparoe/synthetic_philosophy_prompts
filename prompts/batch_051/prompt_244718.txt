I'm a novelist. My next book is set thirty years out, in a world where moral trade is as ordinary as carbon offsets, and before I outline I want someone to play out the logic for me, not just the vibes.

Here's the setup, so we're using the term the same way. Toby Ord's idea is that people with different moral priorities can trade actions the way countries trade goods: if I think a meatless month is a huge moral gain and you think it's trivial, while you think funding deworming pills is a huge moral gain and I think it's trivial, we swap — I fund pills I think don't matter much, you go meatless for a month even though you think that doesn't matter much, and by both our lights the world got better. Add self-offsetting too, people doing extra good to cancel out their own bad acts at some exchange rate.

Now the counterfactual. Suppose that in 2038, in the US and Western Europe, a startup builds a clearinghouse with actual verification: proof the other side did the thing, escrow, fractional credits, a reputation system. Within five years, a quater of affluent adults use it at least monthly. Walk me thru the next thirty years, to 2070 or so. I want the causal chain, not a list of outcomes: what adapts first (employers? insurers? churches? the courts?), who resists hardest, where the first big scandal comes from, and then the second-order effects — moral licensing, status competition, arbitrage between moral communities, regulatory capture of the certifiers. For each link in the chain tell me how confident you are, which effects are robust across different assumptions about human nature and which are guesses, and what would have to be true for the whole thing to collapse in five years instead. And give me the one most novelistically promising but still plausible pressure point — the specific place the system could curdle into something people actually fight over.
