I'm in a reading group that has been circling around the American Dream versus the Nordic idea of Jantelagen (the Law of Jante), and we keep getting stuck on what "success" even picks out. The American Dream side treats success as something an individual achieves and should be celebrated for; the Jantelagen side treats it as a comparative, almost suspicious thing — roughly, don't think you're anyone special or better than the rest — and the Australian tall poppy syndrome is the same reflex from the other direction: the moment someone stands out, we want to cut them down. So I want a working definition of "success" that doesn't quietly beg the question between those two pictures, or if the word is doing two jobs, a two-part definition that says which cases trade between them.

Here are the cases I want the definition to classify: a surgeon who develops a life-saving procedure and gets national honors; a woman who wins $120 million in the lottery and lives comfortably doing nothing; a small-town teacher who is loved by her students but never promoted or recognized; a trust-fund heir whose instagam fame gets him brand deals; a startup founder who is worth millions and then gets convicted of fraud; a painter who is ignored her whole life and declared a master after she dies; an athlete whose Olympic gold gets stripped after a doping finding; a drug runner who retires at forty and goes legit; a one-hit wonder who now plays nostalgia festivals; and a gardener with no money or recognition who says she is exactly where she wants to be. My two fixed points are that the surgeon is in and a man who spent thirty years on a book he never finished and calls himself a failure is out; for the rest I keep flipping depending on whether I'm in a "did they do something" mood or a "how does it sit with them socialy" mood.

Propose the definition (or the two), run it through each case, and mark which verdicts are forced. Then tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and what consideration would make you revise the definition — say, a strong argument that success is really about social standing rather than achievement, or about self-assessment rather than outputs.
