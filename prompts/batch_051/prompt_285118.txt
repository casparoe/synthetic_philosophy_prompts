A friend and I spent the evening arguing about a coincidence from a few months ago: on a long train ride, the stranger in the next seat turned out to have grown up in the same small town, in the same year, and to have attended the same narrowly specialized summer program he had almost attended himself. He took this as evidence of hidden structure — some social or logistical mechanism that put them in adjacent seats — while I spent the evening deflating the event along familiar lines, and by the end I was struck by how little my own deflation satisfied me. Our disagreement has since settled into a fixed shape. On his side, once you fix the details of the event — the town, the year, the subfield, the near miss — the probability of that exact conjunction under chance is tiny, and it seems perverse to assign it no weight at all simply because one cannot yet name the underlying mechanism. On my side, the calculation is illegitimate because the event was not specified in advance; the proper denominator is not this exact conjunction but the enormous class of striking correspondences that any two strangers could turn up in each other's histories, and the birthday paradox together with the law of truly large numbers implies that such correspondences are almost inevitable somewhere, even if each particular one is improbable. I also pressed the asymmetry of attention: we notice, remember, and retell the hits, and silently discard the vastly more numerous near misses and non-matches. He accepts the mathematics, I accept his description of the facts, and we accept that some standard of evidence is needed; neither of us moves.

I have begun to suspect the real disagreement is not about probability at all, but about what an anecdote can rationally do. He seems to be asking what a situated person should conclude after being struck by a particular, richly specified event; I seem to be asking what a detached observer — a journal referee or a skeptical third party — should take the event to support about some general mechanism. We also cannot agree on how to fix the reference class against which an unrepeatable event like this should be assessed, which suggests part of the impasse may be a disagreement about which questions are even well posed. I am not asking for a verdict on the train encounter, and I do not want another survey of debunking arguments. Diagnose the structure of the disagreement itself: is it verbal, factual, a difference about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, about whether a hypothesis must be formulated before the evidence arrives, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress?
