Give me a working definition of what it takes for an oath to actually bind someone—morally, not just legally, so breaking it reads as a genuine wrong or betrayal—that gets all the following cases right. (A) A god in the Greek setup who swears by the river Styx: break it and he lies insensible for a year and is cut off from nectar, ambrosia, and the company of the other gods for nine more, so enforcement is massive. (B) A witness in a modern courtroom who affirms rather than swears on a scripture they regard as ordinary paper. (C) A marriage vow between two secular people who don't expect anyone to punish them from above. (D) A politician taking an oath of office who has already decided in private he'll break it later. (E) An oath a mugger extracts at gunpoint. (F) A deathbed promise to a parent, kept for twenty years with no one else ever knowing. (G) A seven-year-old's cross-my-heart-and-hope-to-die. (H) An actor speaking the words of an oath onstage as part of the play. My gut says A and H are the easy endpoints, E and F are the ones that break any single definition, and C is the one that makes me suspect 'oath' is doing at least two jobs at once—something like a precommitment device and something like invoking a punisher or a witness. But I keep flipflopping. Propose the definition, run it through all eight, and tell me which verdict you're least confident about, especially if the definition has to be patched to handle one of these.
