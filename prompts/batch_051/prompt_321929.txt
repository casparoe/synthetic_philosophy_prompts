I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about whether Mozi’s impartial care is genuinely a rival to Confucian graded love, and I left convinced that the standard Confucian defenses can be regimented into four moves, but not convinced that they exhaust the resources in the Mencius text. I want you to help me find a fifth move.

Here is the passage I want the question to turn on. From Mencius, Book III, Part I (Teng Wen Gong I), in James Legge’s English, where Mencius quotes Confucius on a superior’s grief:

“What the superior loves, his inferiors will be found to love exceedingly. The virtue of the gentleman is like the wind; the virtue of the small man is like the grass. The grass must bend when the wind blows upon it.”

My list of Confucian arguments for graded love against Mozi’s impartial care, as they came out in last night’s argument:

1. The root argument: care needs a single root in parent-child affection, and impartial care has two roots or none.
2. The developmental argument: family love is the only reliable training ground for the habits that later benevolence requires; Mozi’s demand skips the necessary training.
3. The special-obligations argument: role relations generate asymmetrical reasons; one’s own father and another man’s father are not interchangeable, so impartial care either ignores or coerces these reasons.
4. The social-order argument: graded care secures filial piety, and filial piety produces orderly families and states, whereas impartial care undermines the familial loyalties that stabilize public order.

I think the wind/grass passage suggests a fifth argument for Confucian graded love that is not on this list and is not just a restatement, special case, or combination of these four. Give me that argument. Then tell me whether it is any good and how much work the passage does in it: would the argument survive without the passage?

- "use_of_passage": string, one or two sentences saying how much work the Mencius passage does and whether the argument would survive without it.
