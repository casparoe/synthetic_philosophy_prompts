So I got into a long argument with a friend last week about whether a government can actually do wrong, or whether that's just loose talk and only the individual people inside it can do wrong. The arguement kept coming back to the same legal facts, and I couldnt get them to hang together. In the US, and in plenty of other places, the default rule is that you can't sue the state in tort unless the state has consented to be sued — sovereign immunity. But the state has consented for a bunch of things: under the main federal tort claims law, you can sue the government for negligence by its employees, except that discretionary decisions and intentional torts are carved out, and you can't get punitive damages. You can often sue the individual officer who actually did the harm, but only if the law was clearly established at the time; otherwise they get qualified immunity. And the state is perfectly happy to be a plaintiff — it sues people all the time — so the law doesn't seem to think the state is incapable of being a party to a lawsuit. Meanwhile a private corporation is treated completely differently: if a company's employee injures you in the course of the job, you sue the company under respondeat superior and nobody finds that weird, and companies can even be prosecuted as companies.

My friend kept saying the law just doesn't think the state can be a wrongdoer the way a person or a corporation can be, and I kept saying the law obviously does think the state can do wrong — otherwise why set up the waivers and carve-outs, and why do judges talk about what the government did wrong all the time? I want someone to actually work out what view of the state as a wrongdoer this whole setup presupposes. Does sovereign immunity really presuppose the old common-law idea that the sovereign can't be guilty of a legal wrong, or is that just a leftover slogan while the actual practice presupposes something more like "the state can do wrong, but we protect the public treasury and official discretion for separate reasons"? Is the view even coherent across its parts, or do different parts presuppose different things? And if we replaced the underlying view with the idea that the state is a full moral agent — like a corporation that can be liable and punished — what exactly would have to change in the doctrine, and would anything important be lost?
