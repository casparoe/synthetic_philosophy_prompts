I've been stuck on a question about presupposition and I can't figure out how to even test it. The standard picture, at least as its been presented to me, is that when I say something like "Sam has stopped stealing office supplies," I take for granted that Sam used to steal them. If my hearer didn't already believe that, she can accommodate the presupposition: she just quietly accepts it, and from then on we both proceed as if it had been in the background all along. The idea is that this updates the common ground—the conversational scoreboard—which is supposed to be a public, shared record of what's been accepted for purposes of the conversation.

But what I can't get clear on is whether that scoreboard is anything over and above the contents of the participants' heads. One view says it's just shorthand for a tangle of mutual beliefs: I believe P, you believe P, I believe you believe P, you believe I believe P, and so on. The other view says no, there's an actual shared record—abstract, public, whatever—that can come apart from any individual's beliefs, the way a score in a game isn't a belief in any player's head. I keep thinking these two should make different predictions somewhere, but I can't design the situation. The closest I got: if a third person C arrives mid-conversation and we keep talking as if the presupposition is in force for C too, maybe that shows it's genuinely public; but maybe that just shows we each updated our private models in synchronized enough ways.

Design the best test you can—experiment, corpus pattern, formal argument, whatever you think is strongest—that would bear on this. Say exactly what pattern of results would count for the shared-record picture and what would count for the mutual-beliefs picture. Then be explicit about what the test would not settle, even in principle: for example, whether talk of a scoreboard is just a useful model, or whether a "shared" state that nobody fully represents is even coherent. And if I've posed the question in a way that already smuggles in an answer, or the distinction collapses on closer inspection, please just say so rather than pretending the test is well-formed.
