Two friends of mine, both working mathematicians, got into it at dinner last week about the four-colour theorem, and I ended up stuck as the referee. Here's the argument as best I can reproduce it. (1) David said he can't bring himself to call the theorem "really" proved, because the original proof rests on a case check that is far to large for any human to actually survey; a proof only counts as a proof, he says, when a single mind can hold the whole chain of reasoning in view, and a printout the size of a phone book just isn't that. (2) Maria replied that this is basically a superstition about brains: a proof is just a derivation from axioms by valid rules, and it doesn't matter whether the steps are executed in neurons or on a chip, as long as the rules were implemented correctly. (3) David shot back that "implemented correctly" is doing all the work: you still have to trust the program, the compiler, the hardware, and the translations between them, and nobody can survey any of that either, so the unsurveyability has just been pushed one level down. (4) Maria said that's equally true of a two-hundred-page paper: nobody surveys the referees' checking, the journal's systems, or even the author's all-too-human attention to detail, so if David's standard puts the four-colour theorem beyond proof, it puts an awful lot of ordinary mathematics beyond proof too. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating, then say what both of them are missing. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
