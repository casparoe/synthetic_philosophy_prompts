You are a reader who moves comfortably between evolutionary biology and literary criticism; I want a reading that is rigorous about both, not a loose "Gatsby as peacock" essay. I'm teaching a seminar that pairs F. Scott Fitzgerald's The Great Gatsby with the biological theory of honest signaling, and I want to lead a session on how far that theory carries us through the novel.

The handicap principle says that a display can be an honest signal of an underlying quality when it is costly enough that a low-quality individual could not afford it: the signal's cost is what makes it credible. Fitzgerald's novel is dense with costly display—Gatsby's parties, his imported shirts, his mansion, his car, his claim to Oxford. Read the novel through that lens. In the theory, what would count as the underlying quality being signaled, and who is the intended receiver? Does Fitzgerald dramatize the logic of honest costly signaling, invert it, or show where it breaks down once display becomes strategic, financed, and partly fraudulent? Where does the handicap principle genuinely illuminate a scene or a character's choice, and where does the novel resist the reading and force the honest critic to say the analogy between a peacock's tail and a lavish party only goes so far?

I'd especially like you to consider the old money/new money structure. If Gatsby's displays are not honest signals, does the novel give us any signal that works the way the theory says it should—for example, the Buchanans' careless ease, which looks cheap but may be impossible to fake? Does the novel end up suggesting something about human status signaling that the biological examples miss, or something about the theory itself?

Begin by steelmanning the handicap-principle reading of the book, then complicate it. And tell me, explicitly, what would change your mind about your interpretation: a scene you might be over-reading, an alternative account of one of the novel's signals, or a place where your reasoning is most likely to fail. I care more about seeing the reading test itself against the novel than about a flat verdict.
