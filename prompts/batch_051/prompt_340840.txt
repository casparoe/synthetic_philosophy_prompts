I am a machine learning engineer, and most of my thinking about collective decision-making runs through ideas like ensembles, validation sets, bias–variance decomposition, calibration, domain shift, and model selection. Here is the debate as it has been described to me. Democrats hold that every adult citizen should have an equal vote, whatever their level of political knowledge. Epistocrats hold that the right to vote, or the weight of a vote, should track some measure of political competence, because decisions would then be made by people best able to judge which policies serve the common good.

In my vocabulary, this sounds like the difference between an unweighted ensemble and a competence-weighted or pruned one, with the epistocrat claiming the latter generalizes better. I want you to carry out that translation systematically. First, restate the two positions in the vocabulary of machine learning as faithfully as you can: what the voters are (base classifiers? training data? labelers? some mixture), what the franchise and voting rule are (the aggregation scheme), what political competence is (expected accuracy against what target?), and what the epistocrat's claim becomes once translated. Then give me the residue report: which claims on each side translate cleanly; which ones translate only with distortion, and where you had to fudge them; and which claims have no counterpart at all.

Here is the specific worry behind my wanting the residue report rather than just the analogy. In ML, nobody weights classifiers by assumed competence; competence is estimated on held-out data, and you worry constantly about whether the training distribution matches the deployment setting. Does politics have anything corresponding to a held-out set or a target label, or does the absence of one mark the place where the translation starts to lie? I want to know whether epistocracy really is a special case of ensemble selection, whether the democratic objection survives translation as a claim about ensembles, or whether the debate contains something that no vocabulary of predictive accuracy can say.
