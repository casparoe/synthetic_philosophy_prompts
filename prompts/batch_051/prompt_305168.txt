I'm teaching a graduate epistemology seminar and we're two weeks into the regress problem, and I keep returning to this passage near the start of Hume's Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding: "They think it a reproach to all literature, that philosophy should not yet have fixed, beyond controversy, the foundation of morals, reasoning, and criticism; and should for ever talk of truth and falsehood, vice and virtue, beauty and deformity, without being able to determine the source of these distinctions. While they attempt this arduous task, they are deterred by no difficulties; but proceeding from particular instances to general principles, they still push on their enquiries to principles more general, and rest not satisfied till they arrive at those original principles, by which, in every science, all human curiosity must be bounded."

I want a thought experiment that makes that foundationalist ambition look structurally ill-suited to ordinary empirical justification. Construct a case with these specs: the protagonist is a working scientist or clinician, not a sceptic, trying to justify one specific empirical belief—something like "this calibration curve is reliable" or "the patient's symptom is functional, not organic." The case must be ordinary: no Cartesian demons, no infinite time, no perfect memory, no exotic psychology. The chain of reasons must consist of distinct propositions, not a loop, and must not bottom out in an obviously self-evident proposition. Then show the reasoning of a foundationalist, a coherentist, and an infinitist about the case. I want the infinitist and the coherentist to disagree—one should say the protagonist is justified, the other not—and I want you to identify the exact feature doing the work. Finally, say whether a foundationalist could salvage the case by appealing to a non-propositional or experiential justifier, and what happens if you relax the distinct-reasons constraint and allow mutual support to creep in. I cant decide whether this is a case about the regress itself or about the difference between needing a foundation and wanting one.
