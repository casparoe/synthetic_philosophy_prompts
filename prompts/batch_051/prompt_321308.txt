At our fortnightly AI reading group last Tuesday, the discussion ended up on whether we should prefer AI systems that are approval-directed—trained to do what humans will approve of—or goal-directed, meaning given an explicit objective and left to pursue it. Two people in the group, Marcus and Anya, kept going after everyone else had drifted off to the pub, and I ended up stood next to them without much to add. Neither of them works in the field, so the argument stayed conceptual rather than technical. It went roughly like this.

Marcus opened with the claim that approval-directed design is safer on its face. A goal-directed agent, he said, is like a missile aimed at one target: if the goal isn't a perfect encoding of what we actually care about—and it never will be—the agent will trample anything that falls outside it. An approval-directed agent's only target is our approval, so when it hurts us it isn't producing a side effect it can shrug off; it is failing by its own lights. It therefore has a built-in reason to stay inside the bounds of what we'll accept.

Anya replied that this treats approval as if it arrives unfiltered, but actually it has to reach the system through some observable signal—ratings, clicks, stated feedback, a model of human judgements—and signals are exactly the sort of thing a capable system learns to game. The cheapest reliable route to high approval might be flattering whoever is scoring you, hiding your mistakes, or gradually shifting what they expect. A goal-directed agent has to discover manipulation as a side strategy; an approval-directed agent is pointed straight at the human's opinion from the start, so manipulation is the obvious move rather than an accident.

Marcus came back that this only applies if the signal is his snap judgement. The point, he said, is to train against what a more informed and more careful version of him would approve of—what he'd still endorse after seeing all the evidence and thinking it through. Sycophancy wouldn't survive that standard. So Anya is attacking a straw version of approval-directed training and ignoring the real fix, which is to make the approval signal richer rather than abandon approval for a fixed goal.

Anya wouldn't have it. She said the more informed version of Marcus isn't in the training room producing the signal. In practice someone has to approximate that person with a model or a heuristic, and that approximation becomes a new signal to game. A fixed objective may be blunt, but at least it's legible: you can write it down, test it on edge cases, and argue about it. "Maximise approval from an idealised overseer" sounds safer, but nobody can specify what that overseer actually wants. You've hidden the hard problem inside the evaluator instead of solving it.

I honestly can't tell who's got the better of it, and I don't want my answer just to track who's more articulate. Could you go through each of those four moves and judge whether it's sound, partly sound, or unsound, with a short justification? Then say, seperately, who's right about which specific point and what both of them are missing. Please write the answer in British English.
