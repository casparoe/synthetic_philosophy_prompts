So my friend and I were arguing about Newcomb's problem, and we came up with a variation that I think untangles something but I can't tell which side it favors. Standard setup: a transparent box with $1,000, and an opaque box that either has $1,000,000 or is empty. But now Omega doesn't make a private prediction before you arrive. Instead, before the boxes are filled, you have to write down a number p: your honest subjective probability, at the moment of writing, that you will eventually take only the opaque box. You can't lie to yourself—p has to be your actual credence, not a strategic move in the game—and you also can't bind your future self or sign any contracts to make the number come true. Omega then fills the opaque box with probability exactly p, using some randomizing device you can't predict. So if you write p = .9, there's a 90 percent chance the million goes in; if you write p = .1, there's a 10 percent chance. After that you make the ordinary choice, one box or both, with the opaque box still closed.

Here's what's bugging me. Whatever p you wrote, once the filling is done, taking both boxes yields exactly $1,000 more than taking one box, whether the million is in there or not. So it seems your later self should two-box no matter what p was. But then your honest p should have been 0, not .9—so the only self-consistent announcement looks like p = 0, the box never gets filled, and you two-box and leave with the thousand dollars. Does the one-boxing intuition survive this at all? I would have thought evidential decision theory could rescue it the way it does in the normal problem, by saying one-boxing is evidence the million is there. But here the filling runs through your own announced p, which you already know when you choose, so it feels like your later act is screened off from the contents—in which case EDT and causal decision theory both two-box and the whole evidential/causal fight just disappears. Is that right? And what happens to the idea that deliberation crowds out prediction: is the only honest p forced to match whichever act is already dominant, so that writing down the credence is less a prediction than a way of ending deliberation? What if you could lie about p—does the problem then collpase into just writing 1 and taking both boxes, and does that tell us anything about why Newcomb's original predictor has to be independent of any number you announce?
