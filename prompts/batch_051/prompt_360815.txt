I am not a scientist or an academic, but I have spent the last year reading about proposals for solar geoengineering — deliberately putting reflective particles into the stratosphere to cool the planet — and I keep coming back to the 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo as the closest thing we have to a natural trial of the idea. From the popular accounts I have read, the basic facts are these: in mid-June 1991 the volcano injected roughly twenty million tons of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere; the resulting aerosol layer scattered a small amount of incoming sunlight back into space; and over the next couple of years global average temperatures were depressed by about half a degree Celsius. Nearly every argument about solar geoengineering has since cited Pinatubo, usually either as proof that the technique would work or as proof that a one-off natural event tells us little about deliberate, sustained deployment. I would like the episode analyzed rather than used as a slogan.

The three ideas I keep meeting in this debate strike me as the right lenses. The first is moral hazard: the worry that the promise of a cheap technical fix, or even the promise of research into one, will weaken the political will to cut emissions. The second is termination shock: the danger that if aerosol injection were ever stopped suddenly, the masking effect would disappear and the warming it had been offsetting would arrive all at once. The third is the question of who decides: a single country, or conceivably a wealthy private actor, could plausibly deploy such a technique, but the climatic consequences would fall on everyone. I would like an analysis that runs in both directions. What would each lens lead one to expect, or fear, from a Pinatubo-like event treated as a test deployment? Which features of the eruption and its actual aftermath does each lens genuinely illuminate, and which does it distort or miss? And what does the episode, looked at this way, do back to the lenses — does it strengthen or weaken the moral-hazard worry, is termination shock the right comparison for a one-off natural eruption, and what purchase does a governance question have on an event that nobody decided? Finally, I would like to know where the three lenses pull in different directions, and where this whole way of framing the issue goes silent.
