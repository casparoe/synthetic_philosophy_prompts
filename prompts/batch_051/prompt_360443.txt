W. K. Clifford writes: "It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe anything on insufficient evidence." Consider a case he did not anticipate. A loan officer at a regional credit union must decide on a mortgage application. The credit union uses a proprietary machine-learning model to predict default risk. The model has strong aggregate performance in vendor trials, but it was not validated on borrowers from the region the credit union serves, and the loan officer cannot inspect its internals or feature weights. For applicant J, the model returns a high default probability. The officer initially doubts this, knowing the validation limits, but then sets the doubt aside because the model was adopted through the credit union's official governance process. She forms the belief that J is a bad credit risk and denies the loan. In fact, J would have repaid. Does the officer violate Clifford's principle?
