I have been reading arguments about obedience and authority for years, and nearly all of them work one exhibit at a time. Milgram's subjects get produced to show that deference to a lab-coated authority is a moral bug; a surgical team or an air traffic control room gets produced to show that hierarchical deference is what keeps catastrophic error rates down; Eichmann or the Nuremberg trials get produced to show that following orders is the paradigmatic moral failure; a nurse who carries out a physician's plainly wrong dosage gets produced to show that role-occupants are not excused even by immense epistemic asymmetry. I want the full specification before the verdict.

Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of due deference: a theory of when deferring to an authority — in belief, in action, or both — is a virtue, when it is merely permissible, and when it is a vice. For each desideratum give the case or consideration that makes it binding.

In particular, the theory must not collapse the following distinctions, and you should say why each matters:

- epistemic deference to someone's word versus practical obedience to an instruction;
- whether the authority is legitimate versus whether this subordinate is justified in complying versus whether this subordinate is blameworthy for complying or refusing;
- deference as an act versus deference as a settled character trait;
- authority grounded in expertise versus authority grounded in office or role versus authority grounded in mere power.

Then give the cases and regularities the theory must get right, with the argument for why each is a requirement. These should include at least:

- the baseline Milgram result and the variations that matter (orders by telephone, a dissenting confederate, a non-institutional setting);
- the military doctrine that obedience to lawful orders is expected but manifestly unlawful orders must be refused, and why the qualifier is manifest rather than merely contrary to the subordinate's own judgment;
- whatever the right lesson is of Eichmann and of the Nuremberg-style defense, namely that role-occupancy and orders can corrupt rather than excuse;
- ordinary high-stakes hierarchy (surgery, aviation) where deference functions as a coordination technology;
- the nurse and the bad dosage, as a limit case for epistemic asymmetry;
- the parent-child case, which suggests deference may be a developmental or role-relative virtue rather than a trait good in every holder.

It also must accommodate, not explain away: that people grossly overpredict their own willingness to refuse; that polite institutional authority pulls behavior without threats or explicit incentives; that a single dissenting peer sharply reduces compliance; and that hierarchical deference solves genuine coordination and specialization problems.

Then mark each desideratum as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, and say which camp. After that, flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied without qualification — I most want to see these tensions. In particular I suspect a real one between the requirement that an authority's directive function as a preemptive reason that displaces the subordinate's own re-deliberation, and the requirement that the subordinate remain blameworthy when a legitimate-looking order is seriously wrong. Tell me whether that tension is real and how a theory could earn the right to keep both.

Finally, state which single desideratum does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and briefly run the main families of view past the list — the command/office conception, the expertise conception, a virtue-theoretic conception of due deference, and the service conception of authority as preemptive reason — one or two sentences each on where they fail. Keep the scoring brief; the criteria are the deliverable.

A table for the desiderata is fine, but the reasoning behind each entry is the point.
