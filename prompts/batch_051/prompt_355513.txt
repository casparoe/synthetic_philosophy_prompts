I have been discussing expected-value reasoning with a colleague, and she has produced an argument that seems to prove too much. We can each feel that something has gone wrong, but we cannot locate the step. The argument, as she lays it out, is this. (1) Assume that outcomes can be assigned a real-valued goodness measure with no finite upper bound: for every finite quantity of value, some possible outcome contains more. (2) Accept the standard principle that the choiceworthiness of an act is its expected value, understood as the probability-weighted sum of the values of its possible outcomes. (3) Take any mundane act—say, donating fifty dollars to a public-health charity or leaving for work an hour earlier than usual. For any such act, it is hard to assign exactly zero probability to its being a crucial link in a chain of events that averts some vast catastrophe, and even a probability of 10^-25 of an astronomically large good yields an astronomically large expected value once the two are multiplied. (4) So the mundane act appears overwhelmingly more choiceworthy than any ordinary alternative. (5) But the same construction can be run for nearly any alternative, since each choice raises the probability of some distinct chain just enough to generate a similarly enormous expected value. (6) The resulting theory either offers no stable guidance or crowns nearly every option the best. That conclusion cannot be right for an account of practical reason. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I am most reluctant to give up the probability calculus or the thought that some goods are astronomically large, but I cannot see how to keep both without introducing an arbitrary credibility threshold.
