I read a good deal of narrative history, and I keep noticing a move for which I have no name. A historian will spend a chapter arguing that an event had many independent causes — fiscal strain, popular unrest, poor decisions by particular leaders — and may even say the event was overdetermined, or that no one factor was necessary. Then, when it comes time to interpret the event or draw a lesson, the same historian quietly grants one of those factors a special status: it was the decisive cause, the trigger, what really mattered, while the others slip into the role of background conditions or preconditions. The earlier claim that the causes were all on one level is never revisited.

I have seen this in books about revolutions, wars, and the collapse of institutions, across several methodological schools. I am not asking for the general term for multiple causation, nor for overdetermination itself; I already know those. What I want is the name, if there is one, for this narrower maneuver: first flattening the causal field, and then reintroducing a privileged cause under another vocabulary. Does it have a name? If it does, where are its edges — how is it distinct from ordinary causal selection, from merely choosing one cause for narrative convenience, and from the legitimate move of concluding, after analysis, that one cause really did carry the most weight? If it has no name, how would you delimit it, and is there a reliable way, when reading a book, to tell when the maneuver is innocent exposition and when it is doing argumentative work the author never acknowledges? A bare label would not settle any of that; the boundary work is what I am after.
