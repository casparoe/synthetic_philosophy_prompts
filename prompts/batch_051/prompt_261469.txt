I keep coming back to a weird stall in the liberty literature and I want someone to diagnose it, not summarize the positions. The standard modern debate is supposedly between a liberal view and a republican view. The liberal view, in Isaiah Berlin's terms in Two Concepts of Liberty, is that negative liberty means the area within which a man can act unobstructed by others—my freedom is a matter of how many doors are open to me, and I'm unfree when someone shuts a door, regardless of whether they actually stand there blocking it. The republican view, especially in Philip Pettit's work, says that's way too thin: a slave with a kind master is still unfree, because even if nothing is actually blocking him, he depends on the master's goodwill and could be interfered with at will. So the republican says real liberty is non-domination, not just non-interference.

Here's what I can't get past. These two sides seem to agree on almost every concrete political conclusion—both oppose arbitrary government, both care about the rule of law, both would say the kind master's slave is in a bad state—but they have different words for why. And after decades of back-and-forth, neither side has moved. My working theory is that this isn't really a dispute about the meaning of 'liberty' at all: the liberal account is an account of what makes a particular situation bad for me, while the republican account is an account of what makes a social relationship bad, and the two questions just happen to share a word. But there's a competing hypothesis I can't shake: maybe the republican account is making a hidden empirical claim about how relationships of dependence shape people's behavior even when no interference occurs, in which case it would be a testable social-scientific disagreement disguised as a conceptual one.

So which is it? A verbal disagreement about the word 'liberty', a substantive disagreement about which cases count as data, a hidden empirical question, or an ill-posed question that should be split into two? And what would actually count as progress here—a new distinction, an experiment, a formal result, or just a decision to stop arguing about the word and reframe the question?
