I'm a retired physicist who's been sitting in on a philosophy reading group that's been working through Scanlon and the aggregation problem. One of the regulars, Sam, calls himself a Scanlon-style contractualist — wrongness is about principles nobody could reasonably reject, not about summing up total welfare — but I can't get his case judgments to line up behind one rule. He says switching a runaway trolley from five people to one is permissible, cutting up a healthy nonconsenting patient to save five is wrong, pushing the large man off a bridge to stop the trolley is wrong, and when you can reach only one group in a rescue you should save the five rather than the one. He also says it would be wrong to let a trapped hiker die just because saving him would make a thousand commuters late to work. Now the new case: a runaway trolley is headed toward one person who will lose a hand, and the only way to prevent it is to switch it onto a side track where five people will each lose a finger. All strangers, no special obligations, no other options. What should Sam say if he wants to be consistant with himself? Which of his earlier verdicts is the closest analog? If more than one rule fits his track record, what rule does each answer commit him to, and which answer would you bet he gives? What further case would best tell the candidate rules apart? Please answer in American English.
