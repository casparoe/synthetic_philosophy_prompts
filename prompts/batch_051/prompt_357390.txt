I sit on the investment committee of a small partnership, and a recent decision has turned my attention to the information cascade literature, where I have run into an impasse that is now almost as frustrating as the decision itself. We passed on a founder because the first reviewer gave a lukewarm note, the next two visibly discounted their own enthusiasm to track it, and by the time the memo reached the rest of us the accumulated scepticism read as information. The founder was funded elsewhere and is doing well. When I went looking for what the cascade models would say, I found two traditions that barely argue with each other. The formal rational-herding tradition treats a cascade as the rational equilibrium of a sequential decision process: each agent has a private signal and observes earlier choices, and eventually the public history is informative enough that following the crowd is the correct inference even when it overrides one's own signal. The empirical, social-psychological tradition treats cascades as conformity phenomena: fragile, frequently wrong, carried by reputation, social proof, and overconfidence, and it dismisses the formal work for assuming exactly the kind of calm Bayesian inference that real agents do not perform. The two sides seem to use rationality differently, to collect different types of evidence, and to count different cases as canonical. The hypothesis I keep returning to is that the impasse has two parts: a substantive empirical disagreement about how often real-world cascades actually run on information, and a mostly verbal disagreement about what is being claimed when a cascade is called rational, with a further difference about standards of evidence hidden underneath. But I may be flattening it. I want a committed diagnosis, not a survey. Take a definite position on the structure of this disagreement — verbal, substantive, or two different projects under one label — say what is actually doing the work in the impasse, and tell me what would count as progress, or whether the sensible conclusion is that these literatures were never answering the same question.
