I keep running into the tautology objection to natural selection, and I want to know whether the propensity interpretation actually dissolves it or only relocates the difficulty. As I understand the objection: if fitness just means differential reproductive success, then the statement that fitter organisms leave more offspring is a definition rather than an empirical claim about the world. The propensity reply says that fitness is not an organism's actual tally of offspring, but a probabilistic disposition to produce offspring in a given environment. To make this vivid, defenders of the propensity view compare fitness to the bias of a coin. The coin's bias is a real property of the coin, distinct from any particular run of flips, and it explains probabilistically why long runs tend toward the corresponding frequency of heads. Likewise, fitness is supposed to be a real property of an organism, distinct from its actual offspring count, and it explains probabilistically the actual count. The analogy is doing the main work in the conclusion that the tautology objection rests on confusing a disposition with its manifestation.

I want an audit of the analogy rather than a restatement of it. First give a table of correspondences: coin, bias, individual flip, run of flips, long-run frequency, and anything else that matters, mapped onto their biological counterparts. Then identify the disanalogies, and say explicitly which ones matter for the intended conclusion and which are harmless looseness. In particular, I want to know whether a coin's bias has a stability, trial-independence, and context-independence that organismic fitness lacks; how much damage is done by frequency-dependent selection, density-dependence, fluctuating environments, and the fact that many organisms get few reproductive trials; whether the analogy quietly imports a fixed probability distribution over interchangeable, independent trials, and whether actual reproduction has that feature; and whether the problem of measuring a propensity without falling back on the outcomes it was meant to explain is a mere practical nuisance or a sign that the tautology has been relocated rather than removed. Then give a verdict: does the propensity interpretation, as carried by this analogy, actually dissolve the tautology objection? If the coin analogy cannot carry that conclusion, say what a better analogy would be, if there is one.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
