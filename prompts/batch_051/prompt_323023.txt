I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I would like help with an asymmetry I keep noticing in negotiations over project roles, equity splits, and even things like co-founder responsibilities.

The pattern is this. In individual choice, we usually accept something like: if option A is better than option B, adding a third option C should not by itself make B better than A. But in bargaining, people often think adding a new option can change what counts as a fair agreement, even if everyone knows from the start that the new option will not actually be chosen. For example, suppose two people are dividing something, and one person has an outside option that is very good for them but not attractive enough to take. People commonly treat that outside option as a reason to give that person more in the final agreement. Yet if the option is truly not going to be taken, why should it change what is fair? It looks as though bargaining violates a principle we accept in individual choice, but many people have the strong intuition that this violation is not only realistic but fair.

I have read a little about the formal contrast in bargaining theory: Nash's solution accepts something like independence of irrelevant alternatives, while the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution rejects that property and instead requires a kind of monotonicity. Roughly, if one party's best possible payoff improves at every level of the other party's payoff, that party should not end up with less. But I cannot tell whether this formal disagreement tracks a real asymmetry in ordinary judgments, or whether one side of the ordinary asymmetry is simply confused.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher with expertise in bargaining theory and distributive fairness. Begin by brainstorming the strongest arguments on both sides. Then stress-test those arguments against nearby cases, such as a new option that improves one person's maximum payoff only slightly, or an outside option that is real but psychologically unavailable to the person who has it. Finally, give your considered judgment: is the ordinary asymmetry defensible, should it be given up, or should it be kept only under restricted conditions? I am less interested in formal proofs than in whether the underlying fairness intuition is one we should retain.
