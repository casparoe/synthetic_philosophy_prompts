Been going back and forth on Dennett's whole three-stances / real-patterns thing, and a friend of mine sent me this argument and said "the conclusion can't be right, so one of these steps has to go." I spent an hour trying to find the bad step and now I'm half convinced the argument is actually fine. Here it is:

(1) The physical, design, and intentional stances are all just prediction strategies, and for any given system you should adopt whichever one gets you the most predictive mileage for the least work.

(2) For a strong chess engine, the physical stance and the design stance are hopeless in practice: staring at silicon or reading its eval function won't tell you which move it will actually play, but attributing beliefs and desires like "it thinks its queen is trapped" or "it wants to preserve its bishop pair" predicts its moves reliably and cheaply.

(3) So the intentional stance is the right stance to take toward the chess engine.

(4) Dennett also says the intentional stance doesn't merely invent useful fictions: the patterns it picks out are real patterns, objective regularities in the system's behavior that are there to be detected rather than just projected onto it.

(5) Having beliefs and desires just is being a system whose behavior exhibits such real intentional-stance patterns.

(6) So the chess engine literally has beliefs and desires, not metaphorically.

I can't tell wich it is — whether the flaw is a bait-and-switch on "real pattern," whether (5) is doing more work than Dennett would allow, or whether the argument is just valid and I have to accept that a chess engine has beliefs in exactly the same sense I do.
