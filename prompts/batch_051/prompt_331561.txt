I teach an upper-level philosophy seminar on the epistemology of AI oversight, and I am writing the final exam. I would like one 45-minute essay question, worth 20 marks, built around the following passage from Geoffrey Irving, Paul Christiano, and Dario Amodei, "AI Safety via Debate" (arXiv:1805.00899, 2018):

"A human may be unable to judge whether an explained answer or exhibited behavior is correct: the behavior may be too hard to understand without help, or the answer to a question may have a flaw that is too subtle for the human to detect. We could imagine a system trained to both give answers and point out flaws in answers; this gives a third level of difficulty. Flaws themselves may be too hard to judge: flaws could have their own flaws that must be pointed out to a human. And flaws of flaws can have flaws, etc."

The question should not let students get by with summarizing the debate proposal; it must make them reconstruct the judge's epistemic role at each level of the regress the authors describe. In particular, it should press on this tension: if a human cannot detect the flaw in an answer, what is being assumed when we move to having a second system point out that flaw, and does the regress of flaws support the authors' stated hypothesis that debate can produce reliable information beyond the judge's direct capability, or does it merely relocate the judge's limitation upward? The question should require students to state the strongest form of the objection that debate must rest somewhere on at least one flaw the judge can recognize without help, and then to assess whether the regress is vicious or whether a weak local comparative capacity could be amplified into reliable verdicts. Please write the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme with five must-hit points, the marks each is worth (total 20), and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each point wrong.
