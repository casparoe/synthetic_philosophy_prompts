An interlocutor is pressing a master argument that seems to prove there is no coherent distinction between vicious and benign infinite regresses, since every infinite regress is vicious. I am reluctant to accept the conclusion, but I cannot yet see which step to blame. The argument is this.

(1) A regress is generated by a demand: an unexplained item calls for an explanation, an unjustified belief calls for a justifier, and so on for definition, instantiation, and similar relations.
(2) A regress is benign only if the demand that generated it is met.
(3) If the item cited to meet a demand raises the same demand in turn, then the original demand has not been met but only transferred.
(4) In an infinite regress, every item cited raises the same demand and transfers it to a successor.
(5) Therefore in an infinite regress the generating demand is met at no stage of the series.
(6) If a demand is met at no stage of the series, then the series does not meet it.
(7) Hence every infinite regress is vicious, and a benign infinite regress is a contradiction in terms.

I want to keep the working distinction between regresses that are vicious and regresses that are merely complex or unending. Which premise or step should be rejected? Why does it look true before it is inspected? And what would each of the main styles of response—a foundational or stopping-point view, an infinitist view that accepts some unending chains, and a view that treats certain regresses as harmless structural consequences—have to concede in order to resist the argument? Please write your answer in British English.
