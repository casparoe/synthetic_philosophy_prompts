I'm one of the philosophers on the curriculum committee for a required ethics course in a university with a large officer-training program, so a decent number of future military officers take it and several of the instructors are serving officers. We currently teach a standard just war block: jus ad bellum, jus in bello, and the textbook line that the rules for conduct in war apply symmetrically, so a soldier who fights for an unjust cause doesn't violate a distinctively moral rule just by being on that side — the moral equality of combatants, with the decision to go to war sealed off from the rules for fighting it.

A new colleague wants to add a unit on the revisionist critique: the view that the moral equality of combatants is false, that a combatant on the unjust side has no right to kill even when they follow every in bello rule, and that the legal symmetry is at best a convention we might keep for indirect reasons. Some of the serving officers on the committee are pushing back hard. Their worry, as I understand it, isnt that the revisionists are wrong on the merits; it's that teaching this to people who will deploy will either confuse them about what they are permitted to do in a fight, or quietly teach them that the law of armed conflict is something to set aside whenever they judge their own side's cause just. The philosophers on the committee are just as stubborn in the other direction: they think feeding students a false symmetry because its useful is the one thing a philosophy course shouldn't do.

I need to circulate a short memo before Thursday's meeting with a recommendation: include the unit, reject it, or include some modified version. The memo should have a clear bottom line the committee can actually vote on, the reasoning behind it, and an honest engagement with the strongest consideration on the losing side. Please don't dodge the hard version of the officers' objection: that even if you teach the revisionist critique as philosophy, students may walk away believing the legal symmetry is just a fiction, and that belief itself does damage regardless of what the syllabus says.
