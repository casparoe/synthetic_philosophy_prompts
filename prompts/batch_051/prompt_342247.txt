Here's a claim I keep seeing in the rationalist-ish corner of the internet, and I want it pressure-tested instead of just argued about. The claim is that modal talk is just probabilistic talk with the numbers left off: "X is possible" means P(X) > 0, "X is necessary" means P(X) = 1, and "X is impossible" means P(X) = 0. The stronger version says a coherent agent's "could" and "might" should be nothing more than a way of rounding probability to zero, one, or in-between; possibility and impossibility aren't a third ingredient, just boundary points of [0,1].

Treat this as a testable claim wherever it can be tested. Design the best concrete experiments or observations for two readings: (1) the descriptive one — do people's actual judgments of what's possible track whether they'd assign the event probability strictly greater than zero, especially in the edge cases where the mapping gets weird — and (2) the normative one — are there cases where a rational agent should genuinely distinguish probability-zero from impossible, or probability-one from necessary? Say exactly which results would count for or against each reading. Then be honest about what the tests settle and what they leave untouched: if the descriptive tests come back a particular way, does that decide the normative question, or are those seperate claims?
