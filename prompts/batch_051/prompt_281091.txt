I'm taking an epistemology course this semester and we've been going over the classic problem of justifying our beliefs. Last week the professor handed out a short summary of Albert's Münchhausen trilemma, and I've been turning it over ever since because something about it feels off but I can't locate the problem exactly. The handout says roughly this:

"Albert's Münchhausen trilemma shows that the whole project of justifying our beliefs is doomed. Whenever you try to justify a claim, you have to give reasons for it. But then those reasons need reasons, and so on. There are exactly three ways this can go. First, the chain of reasons goes on to infinity—but we are finite beings, so we could never actually complete such a regress, so it can't be what makes our beliefs justified. Second, the chain eventually circles back and a reason ends up supporting the very claim it was supposed to support—and that is just circular reasoning, which proves nothing. Third, the chain stops at some claim that is asserted without reasons—but that is dogmatism, a mere decision to stop, not a justification. Since all three horns are unacceptable and they're the only options, no belief is ever rationally justified. What we call knowledge is therefore built on an arbitrary act of faith, and in the end one position is as rational as any other."

The part that bugs me is the leap at the end: from "you can't complete an infinite regress and circles are bad and stopping is arbitrary" straight to "therefore no belief is rationally justified and every position is as good as any other." We've also read a bit of Popper, who seems to think the lesson is that you can rationally hold positions without needing ultimate justification, not that all positions collapse into being equally arbitrary. So I suspect the handout is overstating, but maybe I'm the one who's confused. Could you go through the handout more or less sentence by sentance and tell me what a careful epistemologist would accept, what they'd wince at, and what is just wrong? In particular: is it fair to treat the three horns as exhaustive, does the finitude point actually rule out the infinite regress horn, and does the "no ultimate justification" conclusion actually force the "all positions are equally arbitrary" conclusion, or is that a separate step?
