I've been drafting a short, self-contained essay on futarchy — the proposal that citizens vote on a shared welfare metric and prediction markets then select the policies expected to maximize that metric — and I would like a sharp set of comments on the argument before I expand it into a full piece. The working claim is that most criticism of futarchy focuses on whether prediction markets aggregate information well enough, but the more fundamental problem sits one stage earlier, in the choice of the welfare metric that voters are supposed to select. That stage is often described as though it were a pure expression of shared values; I want to argue that any metric precise enough for a market to forecast is already a substantive and contestable theory of what matters, because it fixes a time horizon, a distribution of weights across people, and a judgment about which downstream consequences count as consequences of the policy at all. The market's need for a legible and exogenously observable number also produces a selection effect, favoring policies whose effects are easy to quantify and attribute over policies whose effects are diffuse, delayed, or relational. I then want to add a reflexivity argument that I hope is more distinctive. When the policy under evaluation would itself alter the institutions that define, collect, or report the metric — for example, a reform that merges two agencies and thereby changes how a health outcome is recorded — the conditional market is partly trading on a prediction about its own measurement apparatus. In such cases the clean division between beliefs about outcomes and preferences over outcomes, which futarchy depends on, looks unstable, because the thing being predicted is not independent of the governance mechanism doing the predicting. My tentative conclusion is that futarchy is not a general theory of government, but at best a useful device for a narrow class of decisions with exogenous, hard-to-manipulate, near-term metrics.

I would value comments on the argument itself rather than stylistic edits. In particular: first, does the point about metric selection being value-laden genuinely cut against futarchy, or can the framework absorb it by treating metric choice as precisely the place where a democracy legitimately expresses its values? Second, is the reflexivity argument distinct from familiar objections about gaming a metric, or does it collapse into those once it is made precise? Third, is the modest conclusion — that futarchy works only for a narrow band of decisions — too weak to be interesting, or does the narrowness do the real philosophical work? Please also flag any place where a committed defender of futarchy would have an easy reply that I have not anticipated.
