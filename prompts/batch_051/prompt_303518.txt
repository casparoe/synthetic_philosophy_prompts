I am writing a term paper for a philosophy of language seminar and have been returning to David Lewis's Scorekeeping in a Language Game. As I understand the passage, Lewis proposes that when a speaker says something that requires the conversational score to be different from what it currently is, the score shifts so that the utterance counts as acceptable; something like this, he suggests, happens with presuppositions. If I begin to speak as though some proposition were already accepted, the context may adjust to accommodate me.

I have started to wonder how far this can be pushed as a practical matter. Imagine I am in a heated argument about the justice of a particular institution. If I simply speak as though its injustice were already part of the common ground, will Lewis's rule of accommodation work in my favour, quietly updating the score so that my interlocutor can no longer reject the presupposition without making the conversation awkward? Are there limits to this technique? It seems to give a speaker a way of smuggling contested claims into the background of a discussion, and I want to understand how much of that is really licensed by the model. I suspect I may be reading him too instrumentally, but I cannot quite see where the mistake lies.
