Two friends of mine got into an argument over lunch about buying factory-farmed chicken. (1) The first said that buying it is morally fine, because one purchase makes no difference: the store orders in fixed batches, and whether he buys one chicken or not, the same number of chickens will be killed. (2) The second replied that this ignores thresholds — there is some chance his purchase is the one that pushes the store's weekly order up by a case, and a small chance of causing many extra chickens to be killed can amount to a substantial expected harm. (3) The first answered that this expected-value move proves too much, since any purchase in any supply chain might be a threshold case, and in any case someone else will probably buy the chicken if he does not, making his chance of being the threshold purchase effectively zero. (4) The second admitted the threshold probabilities are hard to estimate, but said that does not rescue the first's conclusion: the someone-else point is a different argument and cuts the other way — if others will buy it, demand stays high only because of choices like his, so he cannot hold both that demand drives production and that his demand changes nothing; besides, asking whether his act made a counterfactual difference may be the wrong question, since a purchase can be part of the demand that sustains the practice without being the tipping purchase. They asked me to referee and I could not. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify each rating briefly, and say who is more right overall. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion; use items 1–4 to rate the four moves, and item 5 to state who is more right overall and which consideration would most likely change your verdict, or where your adjudication is most fragile.
