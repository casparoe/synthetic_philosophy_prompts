Over the last month I've gone down a rabbit hole reading about cliodynamics, the project of turning history into something closer to a quantitative, maybe even predictive, science, and in particular Peter Turchin's idea of secular cycles. The claim, very roughly, is that pre-industrial agrarian states have a reccuring multi-century rhythm: population growth creates surplus labor and a growing class of surplus elites; commoner wages get squeezed while elite families multiply and compete for a shrinking pie; intra-elite competition and state fiscal crisis build up; and then a wave of political instability — civil war, state breakdown, sometimes collapse — resets the system, after which the cycle starts again. Turchin and his collaborators say they find this pattern in cases like medieval and early modern England, France, and ancient Rome. A historian friend of mine thinks it is mostly curve-fitting dressed up as science, and puts his credence that the secular cycles are a real feature of history at around 0.15. I'm more sympathetic, maybe 0.4, but I honestly don't know how to decompose my own number.

So: what's your credence that something like Turchin's secular cycles — a genuinely recurring, roughly regular multi-century dynamic driven by roughly those demographic and elite-overproduction mechanisms — is a real feature of agrarian history, rather than a pattern too loose to falsify that gets imposed on noisy data after the fact? Don't just give me a number. Decompose it into the sub-questions that actually do the work. At minimum give a number with a line of justification for each of: (i) the prior that large-scale historical regularities of this kind should exist at all; (ii) the probability that the specific demographic-fiscal-elite mechanism is doing real causal work, conditional on some regularity existing; (iii) the probability that the data and case selection are good enough to distinguish a real cycle from overfitting; plus anything else you think belongs. Show how you combined them into your overall number. Then tell me which single sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to, and separately, which sub-question a person at 0.15 and a person at 0.4 most likely disagree about. And finally: what evidence over the next decade would move your number the most?
