Last evening a friend and I argued about the epistemic regress problem until neither of us could think straight. She began as a foundationalist, insisting that the chain of reasons must terminate somewhere, in beliefs that are justified but not justified by further beliefs; I kept objecting that "it simply stops" sounds altogether too much like "I will stop looking." She then turned my own sympathies against me, and I found myself defending coherentism, trying to explain that justification need not travel in a line at all, that our beliefs support one another as the strands of a net do, only to be told that a net still has to hang from something. By the end we were both uneasily impressed by infinitism, though neither of us could settle whether an endless chain of reasons is a genuine answer or merely a dignified refusal to give one. The argument has been replaying in my head all day, and I have come to suspect that the form of the argument is the thing I most want to capture: every proposed ground immediately becomes something that needs grounding in its turn.

Would you write a pantoum on this? I would like around five or six quatrains, twenty to twenty-four lines, following the pantoum's repetition scheme: the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next, and in the final stanza, once its first and third lines have been carried over in the usual way, the poem's third line returns as its second line and the poem's opening line returns as its fourth, so that the poem closes by repeating its own beginning. The repeated lines should stay word-for-word identical, though their punctuation and their surrounding context may shift, so that a line first heard as assurance can return as evasion, and a question first asked in earnest can return as a trap. Please let the carried lines do the philosophical work. A line that appears to rest the argument in one stanza should resurface in the next as the very thing for which a reason is demanded, so that the form enacts the regress rather than merely describing it. In the final stanza the closure should be offered as the coherentist's move: what presented itself as a chain is revealed to have been a mutually supporting whole all along, and the reader should feel both the relief of a system that hangs together and the suspicion, which the foundationalist will not abandon, that this is only the circle again, drawn a little more elegantly. The poem need not adjudicate among foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism; all three should remain visible, and none should have the last word.
