I keep coming back to a problem that seems more like an engineering problem than a philosophical one: any agreement made today about how the benefits and control of a future AGI will be distributed looks nearly impossible to enforce, because once some party actually has an AGI, it may have no remaining incentive to honor a deal made back when it needed other people's compute, talent, or forbearance. I am a software engineer rather than a philosopher, so I have been trying to think about whether there is a credible commitment mechanism for pre-AGI bargains, and I want the mechanism itself analyzed rather than a verdict on whether the underlying goal is noble.

One proposal I keep reconstructing is a registry of conditional escrow compacts. Companies, states, and research labs would be able to enter contracts of roughly this form: Party A places assets such as compute capacity, equity, patent rights, or cash into a multi-signature escrow today, and the contract specifies a beneficiary and a release condition tied to verifiable post-AGI developments. A contract might say that if a model scores above a threshold on an agreed battery of capability tests and can perform specified kinds of economically valuable remote work, then Party A's pledged equity transfers to a worker-ownership trust. Another might say that if Party B signs before 2030 and later refuses to share trained weights under the agreed terms, then Party B's escrowed assets are released to a humanitarian fund. The escrow is controlled by a rotating council of signatories plus a small technical secretariat that maintains the capability benchmarks. There is no treaty or court behind the registry and it has no legal status in any jurisdiction. The entire mechanism rests on physical custody of the escrowed assets and on the signatories' desire to be seen as honoring their commitments.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a moral position. What would it actually incentivize, and how would it be gamed? I can already see some failure modes: signatories will pressure the secretariat to define the triggering thresholds in their favor; a sufficiently capable AGI might simply ignore the escrow or buy out the council; smaller signatories could be coerced into releasing funds; and contracts written in natural language give enormous room for self-serving interpretation. But I want a more systematic account. How would rational parties manipulate the verification conditions and the membership of the council? What happens to the registry's credibility after the first visible default, and how does that reshape who signs? Does the existence of such a mechanism make pre-AGI cooperation more or less likely? And are the failure modes fixable through mechanisms like staking, third-party adjudication, multi-party thresholds, or hardened benchmarks, or does the whole design depend on exactly the kind of trust it is trying to replace?
