Act as a professional philosopher who works on decision-making under moral uncertainty. Think carefully before drafting anything; start by brainstorming the live pressure points in the view I describe, and only then compose the questions.

I am one of the hosts of a small philosophy podcast. Next month we are interviewing a philosopher whose central project is defending the view that when we are uncertain between rival moral theories, we should maximize expected choice-worthiness: identify the options, score each option on a cardinal scale of choice-worthiness under each moral theory in which we have positive credence, weight those scores by our credences, and choose the option with the highest probability-weighted sum. The guest presents this as a neutral higher-order procedure for acting under moral uncertainty, not as one more first-order moral theory.

The topic has recently become personal for me. I am deciding whether to leave a secure, well-paid job to work for an animal-welfare organization, and in trying to apply the guest's framework to that decision I keep hitting points where the method falls silent or quietly smuggles in moral assumptions. I want the interview to press those points from inside the project rather than dismiss it from outside.

Draft ten interview questions that go beyond restating the view. Include at least one question each on:
1. intertheoretic comparisons: how choice-worthiness scores can be meaningfully compared or summed across theories that appear to lack any common unit;
2. fanaticism: whether a tiny credence in a theory with enormous value differences is allowed to dominate the calculation;
3. the status question: whether maximizing expected choice-worthiness is itself a moral theory and, if so, whether it should also be assigned a credence and subjected to the same procedure, on pain of regress;
4. whether the framework distorts non-consequentialist theories, whose own internal account of how to act under risk may not reduce to maximizing expected value.
Two of the ten questions should use my career decision as a test case, but the focus should remain on the view, not on my biography.

For each question, give the standard reply the guest is most likely to make, and write the follow-up question that reply sets up. Do not include generic questions about what drew the guest to philosophy or to this topic.

Finally, tell me where your sense of the guest's likely replies is most precarious: which answer could pull the rug out from under your planned line of questioning, and what consideration would most change your overall assessment that the view faces serious trouble.
