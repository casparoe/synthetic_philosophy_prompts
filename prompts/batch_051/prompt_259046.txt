I've been working through Marx recently — mostly Capital volume one and the 1844 Manuscripts on estranged labor — and I keep coming back to a historical case that seems to do two things at once: fit his account of industrial capitalism almost too well, and sit awkwardly with the simpler versions of the theory. I'd like you to analyze the case through the theory, and then turn around and let the case push back on the theory.

The case is Ford's Five Dollar Day. As I understand the facts: at Highland Park in 1913 the moving assembly line cut chassis assembly time from over twelve hours to about an hour and a half, but the work became so repetitive and physically tied to the line that workers quit at astonishing rates — I've seen turnover figures around 370 percent a year. In January 1914 Ford announced that qualifying workers would be paid five dollars for an eight-hour day, roughly double the standard rate and an hour shorter than the old nine-hour shift; part of the money was described as profit-sharing and came with conditions about sobriety, thrift, and the worker's home life, enforced through the company's Sociological Department. Turnover collapsed, productivity rose, and Ford was praised for paying his workers enough to buy the cars they built.

What I want is a carefully worked Marxist analysis, not another story about Ford's generosity. Be precise about the machinery: where the surplus value comes from once the line is speeded up, what exactly is being exploited (and whether the higher wage reduces exploitation or just secures a more dependable supply of labor-power), whether the eight-hour day and the five dollars touch alienation at all or mainly intensify the real subsumption of the worker under capital — especially with the company inspecting workers' private lifes. Then run the analysis the other way. Is the Five Dollar Day a problem for Marx, a case where capital voluntarily paid above the going rate because the cost of turnover and unpredictable labor outweighed the savings from cheap labor? Or is it just an application of distinctions Marx already has, like absolute versus relative surplus value, the cost of reproducing labor-power of a particular skill and discipline, and the idea that higher wages can function as a mode of control? And where does the theory have nothing to say — for instance about the family and home life as a site of capitalist discipline? I want you to let the theory illuminate the episode and let the episode show what the theory has to stretch to explain.
