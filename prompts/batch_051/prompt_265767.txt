I'm a journalist working on a piece about why physicists still care about finding a quantum theory of gravity when the effective field theory point of view seems to say we can already compute every gravitational effect we'll ever measure. Along the way I've assembled an argument from things I've been told, and I can no longer tell wether it's a genuine problem or just a cheat.

(1) On the standard effective-field-theory view, being non-renormalizable is not a fatal flaw; it just means a theory is a low-energy approximation valid below some cutoff, and inside its domain it gives reliable predictions without you needing to know the more fundamental theory above. (2) So quantized general relativity, which is non-renormalizable, is a perfectly serviceable quantum theory of gravity up to around the Planck scale. (3) If that's right, then the finite range of an effective theory never by itself gives you a reason to demand a more fundamental theory; you would only have reason to look for one if you had access to phenomena near its cutoff, or independent signs that the low-energy theory is breaking down. (4) That logic would have applied to Fermi's theory of the weak interaction, which was also non-renormalizable and fine in its own energy range; and it applies now to the Standard Model, whose status as an effective theory is routinely described as a reason to expect new physics. (5) So the effective-field-theory picture seems to show either that the search for physics beyond the Standard Model was never well-motivated by the structure of the theory itself, or that quantum gravity is as urgent as the next particle search. (6) Yet this same framing is used to shrug off quantum gravity as good enough at accessible energies, while nobody takes the effective nature of the Standard Model to be a reason to stop looking beyond it.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I'd most like to keep (1), since it's the piece of the story the science seems to have genuinely settled. But if I reject the symmetry move somewhere in (4) through (6), I need to know what actually breaks the analogy between the weak scale and the Planck scale: is it only that we can build colliders to probe one and not the other, or is there something special about gravity that makes the demand for a completion different in kind? And whatever step I give up, I want to know what it costs the idea that scales are autonomous — does the effective field theory view actually underwrite that autonomy, or is the autonomy less than the slogan claims?
