In our philosophy of law course, we have been discussing whether it makes sense to say that a state can wrong the people subject to it, as opposed to merely harming them in ways the law later decides to remedy. A friend offered this summary of the tradition she thinks we have been reading: "Sovereign immunity is the doctrine that the state cannot be sued in its own courts without its consent. Its deeper rationale is that the state is the author of legal obligations and therefore cannot litigate against itself. On the moral side, the state is not a moral agent in the way that individual persons are, so it is more precise to speak of a failure to provide a remedy than of a wrong done by the state; that is why compensation from the state is often framed as a matter of grace rather than corrective justice." I worry that this glides from a jurisdictional doctrine to the much stronger claim that the state cannot wrong anyone, but I am not sure where the doctrine ends and her philosophical gloss begins. Is her summary fair? Please separate what is accurate from what is simplified or contentious, and indicate how a critic of sovereign immunity would push back on the moral conclusion.
