I'm a high school junior in a philosophy-of-science club, and for our unit on confirmation theory I've agreed to prepare a handout on Hempel's raven paradox. What frustrates me is that the sources I've read often talk past each other: some treat the white shoe as a harmless curiosity that officially confirms the raven hypothesis by a negligible amount, while others say the shoe is simply irrelevant, and neither side states the other's position in a way the other could sign. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher of science who works on confirmation theory. Before drafting anything, please brainstorm the strongest version of each side and the points they could jointly accept, so that neither camp comes out as a strawman, and think carefully about which disagreements are substantive rather than verbal.

The assignment is to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two named camps. Call them the Instance camp and the Sampling camp. The Instance camp accepts a refined Nicod principle: an observed positive instance — an F that is G — confirms a universal generalization of the form "all Fs are G" unless background knowledge already blocks its relevance. Because "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens," this camp holds that a non-black non-raven such as a white shoe does confirm the raven hypothesis, though to a vanishingly small degree; on its view, the air of paradox comes from quietly conditioning on the background fact that the object is a shoe and then asking whether its being white adds anything. The Sampling camp takes a roughly Bayesian line: confirmation is a three-place relation among evidence, hypothesis, and background-plus-selection procedure. It denies that a white shoe encountered in everyday life confirms either formulation, because the procedure that produced the observation could not have produced a counterexample; it also notes that under contrived selection procedures, even a black raven can fail to confirm, or can disconfirm, the generalization. Please write the document as something both camps could put their names to. Give it this format: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims, covering at least the logic of the equivalence condition and the uncontroversial ornithological cases; (2) a table of residual disagreements in which each side's position is phrased in words that side would accept; (3) a list of cruxes, meaning sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one camp; and (4) for each crux, the test, argument, or sampling scenario both camps agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction registered in advance.

Throughout, please separate what is settled in confirmation theory from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the discipline's consensus. I would also like the document to flag explicitly any place where the remaining disagreement looks verbal — for example, a difference over whether "confirms" requires a noticeable degree of support rather than a difference about what the facts are.
