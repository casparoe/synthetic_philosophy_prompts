As a retired physicist who has only lately begun reading philosophy seriously, I find myself uncertain how far Hume’s text actually licenses the positions that now carry his name. In Section IV of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Hume writes:

“All reasonings concerning matter of fact seem to be founded on the relation of Cause and Effect. By means of that relation alone we can go beyond the evidence of our memory and senses.”

I take this passage to be doing more than the modest observation that we infer unobserved facts from observed ones. But I cannot tell whether it commits Hume to the ontological view that laws of nature are nothing but regularities in the mosaic of particular facts—the usual “Humean” position—or only to the epistemological claim that our knowledge of causal connexions must come from experience, while nature itself may contain real necessary connexions that we cannot directly observe. Compare the Humean and anti-Humean readings of this passage. In particular: What exactly does the passage establish, if one grants it? Which of the two positions, if either, does it genuinely support? And does a working scientist have any professional reason to prefer one reading over the other when treating laws in physics, or is this a purely metaphysical dispute with no empirical import? My own uneasiness is that anti-Humeans often accuse Humeans of converting an epistemological limit into an ontological one, but I am not sure the text forces that reading, nor whether it matters for scientific inference.
