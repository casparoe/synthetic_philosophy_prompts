A colleague in my group keeps insisting that no current model ever makes a genuine intuitive leap — that what people call novelty is just high-dimensional interpolation between training points, and that this is an empirical claim we could test. I'm not so sure the claim is even testable. If you define the training distribution loosely, every output can be described as recombining things that were somewhere near the training data; if you define it strictly, basically every non-trivial token sequence the model emits is technically outside the support of the training distribution, so the "interpolation" story starts to lose its meaning.

I want you to design the strongest test that would actually bear on whether a model is making intuitive leaps beyond its training distribution, and be honest about what it settles. Be concrete: you'll probably need a synthetic task family where we control the generating process (something like arithmetic expressions with a difficulty parameter, or a small formal language with held-out constructions) rather than natural text. How would you set up the training and test splits so that "outside the training distribution" means something non-trivial — not just new random strings, not just paraphrases of seen strings? What would the task require for success to count as a leap rather than interpolation? What pattern of results would count for and against the claim? Then say what a positive result would leave open — for instance, whether we're measuring novelty of the outputs or novelty of the underlying competence, and whether a successful leap could still just be clever recombination of pieces that were all present in training. Don't gesture at benchmarks; give me a buildable design and say exactly where the two sides would still part ways once the data are in.
