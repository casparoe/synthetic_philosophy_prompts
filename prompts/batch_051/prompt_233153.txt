I am a high school student, and I help run a small philosophy club. Last week we debated whether an individual has any moral reason to stop buying factory-farmed meat, given that, on its own, a single person's purchases seem too small to affect how many animals the industry raises. We have had parallel debates about climate choices and voting, but the meat case is the one I keep returning to.

One member argued that this "it doesn't make a difference" objection—I think it is called the inefficacy objection—is mistaken, and he made his point with a case I had not heard before. In the case, a thousand guards each turn a dial one notch; each turn by itself makes a prisoner's pain worse by an amount too small for the prisoner to notice, so no single guard's action makes any perceptible difference to his suffering. But after all one thousand guards have turned their dials, the prisoner is in severe pain. The member then said that buying factory-farmed meat is just like being one of those guards: any one purchase is a negligible input into the industry's decisions about how many animals to raise, but the aggregation of everyone's purchases is precisely what keeps the system running. On his view, this shows that the inefficacy objection rests on a mistake, and that the individual consumer still acts wrongly.

I find the comparison intuitively persuasive, but I do not yet trust it. I would like you to audit how far the analogy actually holds. First, give the correspondence table: what in the consumer case plays the role of the prisoner, the guard, the dial, the imperceptible increment of harm, and the thousand accumulated turns. Then lay out the disanalogies. For example, the guards' actions are stipulated to add a real, imperceptible harm to a single identifiable victim, whereas a consumer's effect on production is probabilistic, runs through market thresholds and producers' expectations, and the victims are many, distributed, and outside the consumer's view. But I do not know which of these differences actually matter. Separate the disanalogies that are fatal to the argument from those that could be repaired or are merely superficial. Finally, say whether the conclusion—that the inefficacy objection fails and the individual should stop buying factory-farmed meat—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument the whole time. If it cannot carry the argument, say whether a better analogy could do the job.
