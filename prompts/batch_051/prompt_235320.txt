Here's a version of the standard argument for why we're allowed to impose small risks on each other without asking first: in typical activities, like driving or releasing trace amounts of a pollutant, the expected benefit to each person exceeds the expected harm, the risks fall more or less symmetrically across the population, and nobody can say in advance who the unlucky ones will actually be. So a rule permitting the risk is something we'd all rationally endorse from behind a veil of ignorance, and the people who do end up harmed are best seen as the losers of a fair lottery rather than the victims of a wrong.

Now suppose two things become true, as they already partly are. Personalized risk modeling (genomics, cheap chemical-exposure sensors, huge longitudinal datasets) lets us identify in advance a small identifiable subset of people for whom the so-called small average risk is actually a large conditional risk — think of a person with a gene variant who is orders of magnitude more likely to get cancer from a particular residue than the rest of the population. And the cost of warning, excluding, or charging those specific people for their extra risk has dropped close to zero.

What happens to the standard argument under these conditions? I can see it puts pressure on the symmetry premise and maybe on the ex ante/ex post distinction the argument is leaning on, but I can't tell wether the argument just dies for the identifiable high-risk group, or whether there's a repaired version (compensation, insurance, something else) that keeps doing the same work. Walk through exactly which premise is under pressure and whether the argument survives in any shape for the people it used to treat as indistinguishable from the crowd.
