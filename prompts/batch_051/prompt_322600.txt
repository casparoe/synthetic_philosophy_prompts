You are a professional philosopher who specializes in moral psychology and the ethics of interpersonal criticism, including how people hold one another to shared standards in ordinary professional life. Think carefully and do not settle for the first plausible principle that occurs to you: brainstorm several candidate justifications, pressure-test each of them against the cases below, and only then give a definite verdict.

Here is the situation that bothers me. I am a software engineer, and I have noticed that in code review the same critical comment lands differently depending on who makes it. Suppose a mid-level engineer submits a change that genuinely skips the team's agreed test coverage and leaves several edge cases undocumented. The criticism "this change needs tests for X, Y, and Z before it can merge" is simply true against the team's own written standards. If the reviewer making that point has a clean record, the comment is treated as a legitimate blocker and nobody questions it. If the reviewer is someone who, over the past year, has repeatedly merged the same kind of undertested code and waved aside the same concerns when others raised them, the team's reaction shifts. People say things like "he's the last person who should say that," "he has no room to talk," or "he has no standing to criticize." The comment may still get acted on eventually, but making it is treated as improper, and the engineer on the receiving end feels entitled to resent it.

That is the asymmetry I want you to explain. The truth of the criticism does not change with the speaker, and the criticized engineer's violation does not change with the speaker. Yet the appropriateness of making the criticism, and the target's entitlement to bristle at it, seems to depend almost entirely on the critic's own record. We do not do this with ordinary fact-stating: if a cardiologist who smokes tells me my test results show a heart problem, I do not say the diagnosis loses its force because he smokes. Technical criticism and blame seem to behave differently, and I cannot tell whether that difference is principled.

So my question is this: is there a genuine principle that justifies treating a hypocrite's criticism as less appropriate than an identical criticism from a clean-handed reviewer, or is this just a confused social reflex, a dressed-up personal attack that we should try to unlearn? I want the strongest justification you can find, and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.

Make the test meaningful by checking your proposal against nearby cases, not just the original scenario. Consider: (a) a team lead who reviews code in a system he himself wrote years ago and routinely violated the rules on; (b) someone who violated the standard in the distant past, sincerely regrets it, and now holds others to it firmly; (c) someone who never commits the violation herself only because she has had no opportunity, but plainly would if given the chance; and (d) someone who criticizes a public figure's conduct from a position of the same private vice, where no shared team standard is at stake. Does the principle you propose give stable answers across all of these, or does it start making excuses where it should not?

Throughout, keep two things carefully separated: whether the content of the criticism is true, and whether the act of making the criticism is proper. Then answer this: if the asymmetry is justified, what exactly has the hypocrite lost — the right to be believed, the right to demand change, the right to expect the target to listen without resentment, or something else? And if it is not justified, say plainly what a team should do instead when a true criticism comes from a compromised source. Work through the question in this order: candidate principles, pressure-testing against the cases, then a final verdict. Do not give a survey, and do not cite books or articles; what I care about is the structure of your reasoning and which principle survives contact with the cases.
