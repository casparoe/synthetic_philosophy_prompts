I run a small reading group out of my local library, and for the past several months two of our most dedicated members have been locked in a dispute about whether relativity turns the future into something already real. One of them defends what the group has come to call the block universe view: past, present, and future all exist equally, and the feeling that time passes is a feature of our point of view rather than of the world itself. Her main argument is the one I later learned is called the Rietdijk–Putnam argument. Because special relativity makes simultaneity depend on the observer's state of motion, a distant event that lies in our future — far enough away that no signal from here could reach it in time — can count as present, or even past, for another observer moving suitably relative to us. If being present is what makes a thing real, then that future event seems to be real already, and there is no open future left for it to be unsettled. The other member resists this conclusion: he holds that only the present exists, that the future is genuinely open, and that the Rietdijk–Putnam argument must somewhere mistake what an observer is entitled to say from within a frame for a claim about what is true outright. The rest of us have gradually divided into factions, and we have agreed not to take the topic up again until these two produce the document described below.

Please write the joint statement these two should produce if they were to carry out the collaboration in good faith. I would like a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. The agreed-claims section should include what both accept about the physics of special relativity, what they both take the Rietdijk–Putnam argument to establish and not establish, and what each side would count in advance as a serious challenge to its own view. The disagreements table should state the residual disagreement precisely rather than in caricature: each position should be formulated in words its own defender would sign, and the table should make clear exactly where the two part ways. Then identify the cruxes — the sub-questions whose resolution would actually move at least one side — and for each crux specify a case, argument, or possible result that both parties would accept as bearing on it, together with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If, as you draft, part of the dispute turns out to be verbal or a disagreement about labels rather than substance, say which part and why, and include that in the statement as something both sides can sign.
