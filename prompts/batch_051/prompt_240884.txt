I run compliance for a mid-sized infrastructure firm with operations in Southeast Asia, West Africa, and Southern Europe. My team reviews roughly thirty requests a month from country managers to give gifts, pay expediting fees, host officials, or fund local projects, and our current policy — a dollar threshold plus a ban on anything that could create an appearance of impropriety — is failing in both directions. It clears payments that are plainly corrupt and blocks small courtesies that local staff say are simply how relationships work. The recurring accusation from country managers is that we are exporting Western standards to practices that should be understood as gift-giving, reciprocity, or clientelism rather than bribery.

Write me a decision procedure my case handlers can actually run without legal training and in no more than half an hour per request. Operationally, the procedure should do the following.

First, state the operative definition of corruption the procedure will use, and take a definite position on the universality question: whether there is a core of corruption that is not culturally relative, or whether impartiality and arms-length dealing are themselves just one local standard. Do not lay out both positions and leave them standing; pick one and make the rest of the procedure follow from it.

Second, the procedure must distinguish — or explicitly and justifiably collapse — at least these four kinds of payment: buying a particular official act, paying for speed or access without changing the outcome, maintaining a personal relationship whose value lies in likely future favoritism, and discharging a social obligation whose side effect is an official's goodwill. This typology matters because our managers constantly argue that a payment is merely the fourth when we suspect it is the third.

Third, ask the questions in the order they should be asked. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer: which documents, which knowledge of local practice, which patterns over time. For each step, say what terminates the review with a definitive no or a definitive yes, and what escalates to me because the issue is one of values rather than fact.

Fourth, mark every step that is a judgment call dressed up as a rule. If a step is really an exercise of discretion, name it as discretion and say who at the firm holds it.

Fifth, say what the procedure is structurally unable to catch — the forms of corruption it will reliably miss — so I can decide where to place auditing resources.

Where the design of the procedure requires choosing between two defensible readings, make a definite choice and defend it. Then apply the procedure to these three requests:

(a) A country manager wants to give cash to a low-level public works clerk to issue a permit in three days that the statute says could take six weeks. The amount is trivial by our standards, the practice is uniform in that office, and we are legally entitled to the permit.

(b) A local agent proposes a cash gift in a red envelope for the wedding of a senior electricity utility official's daughter. The utility has no current contract with us but will run a tender next year. The agent says the amount is fixed by local norms of social reciprocity, that giving less would be read as an insult, and that no favor has been mentioned.

(c) A country manager wants the firm to fund renovation of a school in the home district of the minister who will soon decide our mining license. The manager insists it is a community-development gesture, says several other firms have done the same, and points out that the minister does not own the project.

If the correct application of the procedure would prohibit something my own instincts, or our current policy, would call normal — or would permit something our flat-dollar rule would ban — say so and defend the result rather than adjusting the procedure to match what we already do. Write this as a document I can hand to case handlers and country managers, not as a memo to me.
