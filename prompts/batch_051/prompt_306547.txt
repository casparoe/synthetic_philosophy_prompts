I work in the simulation group at a research company. Our employee handbook contains the following clause, under the heading "Use of Animals in Research":

'No employee shall carry out, or knowingly permit to be carried out, any procedure that causes pain or distress to a creature capable of experiencing pain or distress.'

The clause was written long before the company had a simulation group, when the only creatures anyone had in mind were the mice and zebrafish in the wet labs. My team has now built a high-fidelity simulation of a fruit fly's nervous system: when the simulated fly is exposed to a noxious stimulus, its model nociceptors fire, it recoils, and its later behaviour shows the sort of sensitisation you would see in a physical fly. We are about to begin a study in which we repeatedly damage the simulated fly, ablating parts of its model nervous system and exposing it to simulated heat, to watch how its escape and grooming routines degrade and recover. No physical animal is involved.

The same lab also runs an off-the-shelf hexapod robot whose controller is a thermostat-style feedback loop: it retreats from heat and from collisions, and it visibly "struggles" if held in place. For comparison, a physical fruit fly sits in a dish in the next lab; whether the clause reaches that fly turns on whether a fly counts as "a creature capable of experiencing pain or distress", a question the drafters never paused to settle, since they were thinking only of the wet-lab vertebrates.

These three cases look to me like points on a single spectrum, from a physical insect, through a detailed simulation of one, to a simple feedback loop; the clause itself is written as though there is a sharp boundary somewhere. I need to know what the clause actually requires for each of the three cases. Please run each case through the ordinary meaning of the words, the purpose the drafters evidently had, what the drafters would have said if they had been asked at the time about simulated nervous systems and damage-avoiding robots, and what a reasonable employee would now take the clause to mean. Then tell me which approach should win, and whether there is any coherent line that includes the fly, excludes the robot, and places the simulation somewhere defensible rather than merely convenient. I have no formal philosophy background, so set out the reasoning plainly.
