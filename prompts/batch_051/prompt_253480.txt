I've been reading Machiavelli's The Prince on my own over the past year, not for any course, and I keep coming back to the chapter on fortune because I suspect the usual way people summarize it flattens what is actually going on. The standard gloss is that fortune is the unpredictable part of political life and virtù is something like boldness, skill, or strength—a more or less fixed set of qualities a ruler either possesses or lacks. But the more time I spend with the text, the less stable that reading seems to me. Machiavelli repeatedly says that a ruler succeeds only when his manner of proceeding fits the character of the times, and that a man who has prospered by caution is ruined when circumstances change because he cannot bring himself to change his nature. Even his famous image of fortune as a woman who is won by the impetuous rather than the cautious strikes me not as a blanket recommendation of recklessness, but as a claim about what works under conditions of deep uncertainty: where the future cannot be reliably predicted, what matters is less any particular fixed trait than the ability to shift one's whole mode of action as the situation shifts—to be patient or violent, cautious or audacious, as the times demand, without being wedded to a single self.

I would like you to argue for that interpretation: that Machiavelli's virtù is best understood as a kind of second-order capacity, the ability to change one's own first-order style or disposition to match changing circumstances, and that fortune is best understood not as mere chance but as the pressure of changing times that punishes exactly this rigidity. Please make the strongest case you can for this reading, and in doing so take seriously the strongest objections to it—in particular, the objection that Machiavelli's closing treatment of fortune seems to favor impetuosity over caution outright, and the objection that he does, after all, praise a fairly stable list of princely qualities. I am less interested in a summary of the book than in a sustained argument that would move someone who currently holds the fixed-traits reading.
