I've been trying to pin down my actual position on police reform and I keep landing on the same cluster of views, but I'm worried it's held together by vibes more than logic. Here's where I am:

1. I'm Weberian enough to think that a state is partly defined by a succesfull claim to a monopoly on legitimate force in a territory, and I don't want to outsource that: private security, militias, corporate goons—no thanks.
2. But I also think a big chunk of what cops actually do day-to-day—wellness checks, mental health calls, noise complaints, truancy, homeless outreach—should be handled by unarmed civilians in separate agencies with their own budgets, not by people carrying guns.
3. I'm not an abolitionist, though. It seems obvious you still need a much smaller armed force for genuinely dangerous stuff—active shooters, armed robbery in progress, hostage situations. The state has to be able to use force fast, and a community meeting can't do that.
4. I think the highest-value reform is decriminalization as a way of shrinking police contact itself: decriminalize drugs, sex work, fare evasion, low-level theft. I care way more about that than about bodycams, de-escalation training, or implicit-bias workshops.
5. And I suspect most police violence is structural rather than a few-bad-apples problem: when one institution is both the 24/7 social service of last resort and the monopoly on force, it's almost inevitable that it defaults to force.

What I can't figure out is whether these five actually hang together, or whether I'm just telling myself I've found a nuanced middle position. Are there pairs here that genuinely pull against each other, and are there any tensions that dissolve on inspection? If something has to give, which is cheapest to drop and what does it cost?
