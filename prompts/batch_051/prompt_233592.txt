Here's a thought experiment. Suppose you could hand John Stuart Mill the data behind the so-called gender-equality paradox: the finding that in countries that score highest on gender equality (Sweden, Norway, etc.), sex differences in career and education choices are about as large, or even larger, than in less egalitarian countries—for instance, a smaller share of engineering graduates are women in the Nordics than in some much more conservative countries. Mill spent much of The Subjection of Women arguing that we can't know what womens innate capacities are until they're raised under truly equal conditions, and that most supposed differences are just the result of custom and training. This looks like the experiment he said he wanted to run, and it seems to have come out against him. How do you think he would actualy respond? Write it as a short reply from Mill to a modern sociologist who has just shown him the graphs: does the data refute his view, force a revision, or can he absorb it without giving anything up? Try to be faithful to his actual arguments, and flag any move he'd have to make that goes beyond what he actually wrote.
