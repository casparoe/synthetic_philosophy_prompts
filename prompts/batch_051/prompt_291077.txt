I'm setting the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of science and formal epistemology, and I want one essay question, answerable in 90 minutes, on the relationship between the no-free-lunch theorems and the traditional problem of induction. The question should make students assess the following thesis rather than merely restate it: that the no-free-lunch results give a precise, formal rendering of Hume's point — and perhaps even settle it — by showing that no inductive rule does better than any other unless we already help ourselves to substantive assumptions about the structure of the world.

Please provide three things. First, the exam question itself; it should include a brief, quotation-free paraphrase of the relevant no-free-lunch claim and of Hume's skeptical challenge, so that students who know only slogan versions of either cannot coast. Second, a model answer of roughly 700 words. Third, a marking scheme for 20 marks, organized around five must-hit points, with the marks each carries and a one-line note on the most common way students miss it. Neither the question nor the model answer should depend on students' recalling or citing primary sources; the exercise should test argument and analysis.

The question should force students beyond summary. In particular, a top answer must: (a) distinguish the no-free-lunch result in the optimization setting from the supervised-learning version and say informally what each asserts; (b) locate exactly where the analogy with induction holds and where it strains — for example, what it means to average over all possible objective functions in optimization or target functions in learning, and whether our ordinary inductive practices are well modeled by a uniform prior over arbitrary functions; (c) separate the claim that induction is reliable only given empirical presuppositions from the stronger claim that induction is arbitrary or unjustified; and (d) commit to a verdict on whether these theorems deepen Hume's problem, give it a benign formal home, or mislocate it. Write the question and the marking scheme so that an A answer is identifiable by these specific moves rather than by fluency.
