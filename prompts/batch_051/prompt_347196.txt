I've spent twenty years in epistemology and I still can't tell whether the peer disagreement debate has actually moved, or whether it just keeps finding sharper ways to describe the same stalemate. You know the lay of the land. Conciliationists think that discovering an epistemic peer disagrees with you is itself a powerful piece of higher-order evidence that your reasoning went wrong somewhere, so you ought to move your credence substantially toward theirs, maybe all the way to suspension. Steadfasters think that's a recipe for epistemic cowardice: you're allowed to hold your ground, because you have direct access to your own deliberation — the particular inferential steps, the felt obviousness of the premises — whereas the other person's verdict is just an opaque output. Conciliationists reply that appealing to your private seemings to demote the peer assumes the very reasoning whose reliability has just been called into question, so it can't break the symmetry. Steadfasters reply that conciliationism, taken seriously, would force you to defer whenever you run into an apparent peer, which makes sustained inquiry unstable and somewhat self-undermining. And around it goes.

What nags at me is that both sides seem to grant the same stock cases — you and a friend get different totals on a restaurant bill, two radiologists read the same scan differently, two philosophers read the same paper and end up worlds apart — and they just weight them differently. That makes me suspect the real fight is elsewhere. My working hypothesis is that the peer disagreement debate is a proxy war over evidential uniqueness: the thesis that a given body of evidence permits at most one fully rational doxastic attitude toward a proposition. If uniqueness is false and permissivism is true, then at least some peer disagreements are cases where both parties can be rational from the start, and the conciliationist demand to move looks like an overgeneralization from cases where the evidence really does single out one answer. If uniqueness is true, then in any genuine peer disagreement at least one party is already irrational, and the interesting question is only who should flinch and how much. But I could also see the impasse being a verbal fight over what 'peer' has to include, or a difference about the point of epistemic evaluation — aggregate social reliability versus individual intellectual responsibility — or just two camps with bedrock intuitions about when you're allowed to trust yourself.

So here's what I want, and I want a diagnosis rather than a scorecard: why has this debate stalled, and what kind of disagreement is it really? Is it substantive and about facts, about standards of evidence, about starting intuitions, about a hidden formal question, or is it two groups answering different questions under one heading? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a formal result, a distinction that dissolves the question, a shift to a better question, or an honest admission that we shouldn't expect any movement at all?
