I have been thinking about Bongard problems — the two-panel puzzles in which six boxes on the left and six on the right are separated by a hidden rule, and the solver must induce the rule from the examples alone; a typical case has the left boxes each containing one large triangle, while the right boxes never contain a large triangle even though small triangles and large shapes of other kinds appear on both sides. I have come to think the standard picture, on which the solver considers rules one at a time and improves them as they fail, is exactly backwards. I call my alternative the First Unrefuted Invariance theory. Here is the claim. After a learner has seen only part of a problem, there is never a single rule separating the examples; there is a crowd of currently unrefuted rules — I call them invariances — all of which hold for every positive example seen so far and fail for every negative example seen so far. These candidates can be partially ordered by cost, which I understand as roughly how much of the observed arrangement a rule has to take for granted in order merely to state itself. The theory says that what a thinker has at any moment is not one rule but the frontier of the cheapest unrefuted invariances: her explicit answer is whichever member of the frontier is cheapest, and her behavior on new cases is governed by where the members of the frontier agree and disagree. Learning is then nothing over and above the refutation of cheap unrefuted rules — each new example executes the cheapest current rule that it violates, and the next cheapest unrefuted rule steps forward.

I like this theory because it predicts a distinctive kind of error. A solver who has seen only part of a problem should overgeneralize down the cheapest line that still survives; in the triangle example, after two positive boxes and one negative box she should infer that the left boxes contain one triangle, and should not retreat to the rule that the left boxes contain one large triangle until a right box containing a small triangle arrives. The order of her wrong guesses should be predictable from cost alone rather than from judged similarity. The theory also predicts that someone can classify new instances correctly while being unable to state the hidden rule, because a broad frontier can agree in practice on the cases she meets even when its cheapest member is wrong. And it re-describes the flash of insight as the loud collapse of a frontier when a very cheap rule is eliminated by a single example.

I am aware that this brushes against existing notions — version spaces, Occam's razor, the minimum description length principle, perhaps elimination-by-aspects ideas from research on judgment — and I want an honest assessment of whether First Unrefuted Invariance is a distinct cognitive proposal or merely one of those wearing a dramatic name. Where does it break in principle rather than in detail? In particular, construct the clearest Bongard-style case you can in which the cheapest unrefuted rule is one that no human solver would have entertained even for a moment; then say whether that case refutes the theory or only shows that my notion of cost is impoverished. Finally, tell me what would change your mind — which consideration would lead you to conclude that the theory is not merely incomplete but false, and where in your assessment you are least confident.
