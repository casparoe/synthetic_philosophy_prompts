A few evenings ago I got into a long argument with a friend about whether Occam's razor has any genuine mathematical backing or is just a rule of thumb about convenience and taste. I took the line that simplicity is an aesthetic and practical preference: we like short theories because they are easier to test, teach, and use, not because the world is more likely to be simple. My friend disagreed, and after a good deal of back-and-forth he pulled up a passage from an article he had saved — he could not remember the author or the outlet — which he said made the mathematics precise and settled the question. The passage, as he summarized it for me, ran roughly as follows. "Solomonoff turned Occam's razor into a theorem. Among all computer programs that could have generated your observations, assign each program a prior probability of 2 raised to the negative of its length, then predict by summing over all of them, weighted by that prior. Because shorter programs receive exponentially more weight, the shortest hypothesis compatible with the data dominates the prediction. A rational agent therefore must prefer the simplest theory, since that is exactly what Solomonoff's universal prior encodes. It also follows that the shortest description of your data in plain English — the elegant theory — is the one you should believe. Solomonoff's predictor is optimal: it learns any computable regularity the world could throw at it, matching the best possible predictor. Simplicity is not a heuristic; it is a mathematically certified guide to truth. This resolves the old problem of induction and explains why science works: scientists search for short, simple laws, and Solomonoff proved they are right to do so."
