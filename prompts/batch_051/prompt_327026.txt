i keep coming back to a broadly consequentialist account of punishment — that a punishment now can only be justified by the future goods it buys, mostly deterrence and incapacitation, maybe reinforcing the norms people actually rely on. talk of desert, of someone simply deserving it, feels to me either like revenge with nicer branding or like gesturing at something nobody can cash out. so the theory is tidy: punish exactly when, and only when, the expected future benefit outweighs the harm of the punishment itself.

but i also have a conviction i cant shake, and it isnt a prejudice i half-disown. its that there is something deeply wrong with punishing a person we know to be innocent, even in cases where doing so would plainly prevent more harm — the scapegoat-to-stop-a-riot kind of case. i would refuse, and i dont think thats just squeamishness. (i suspect a mirror-image problem appears with the old offender who is genuinely harmless now, but the innocence case is the one that really unsettles me.)

the consequentialist in me wants to say the innocence rule is only a useful heuristic, because actual officials have incomplete information and a world where they can frame people whenever the sums come out right is a world with much worse consequences overall. but that feels added on afterwards to save the theory from an ugly implication, not derived from it. and if i instead keep the innocence rule as an unexplained side-constraint, i worry ive quietly stopped being a consequentialist about punishment.

so can i honestly hold both, or does one have to give? what would justify each move — debunking the intuition, dropping it, amending the theory, abandoning it, or showing the clash is only apparent? i want a negotiated verdict rather than a cheer for one side. if the way ive set this up is muddled, or the choice isnt the real choice, say so plainly rather than humouring me.
