I am preparing a podcast interview with a Kantian moral philosopher who defends the strong rigorist position that lying is never morally permissible, including in the so-called murderer at the door case. She has argued that the familiar objection—that Kant would forbid even a plain denial to a would-be murderer—is not a reductio but an implication she accepts, and that critics who recoil from it routinely conflate the duty of truthfulness with an indifference to downstream consequences. I would like eight to ten questions that move beyond having her restate the principle. Please include at least one question on each of the following pressure points, and for each, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give: whether the duty of truthfulness is owed to the murderer as a particular rational agent or instead to humanity as such, and what practical difference that makes; whether a morally relevant distinction exists between an outright lie and a misleading but literally true answer; how she responds to the claim that the murderer, as an unjust aggressor, has forfeited any right to be told the truth; whether the prohibition on lying is a duty of virtue or a duty of right, and what that distinction implies about what third parties may permissibly do or compel; how she understands Kant's remark that a person who tells the truth to the murderer remains answerable for the ensuing death, and whether this answerability is legal, moral, or something else; and how she resolves a supposed conflict between the duty to tell the truth and a perfect duty to preserve an innocent person's life. End with one question about what kind of case or consideration would lead her to qualify or abandon the absolute prohibition.
