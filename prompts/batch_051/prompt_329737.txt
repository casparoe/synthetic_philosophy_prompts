I've spent too long lurking in the rationalist/alignment corner of the internet and I've absorbed just enough interpretability jargon to be dangerous, so when my ML friends started saying "we'll use AI to interpret AI, problem solved" somehting about it bugged me and I've been turning it over until it became a distinction I now can't stop using. I'd like someone who actually knows this stuff to tell me if it's real or just a metaphor I'm attached to.

I call it the interpretability event horizon. First-order failure is the familiar story: you look at the weights/activations of a model and you just can't get a satisfying mechanistic story—polysemanticity, tangled circuits, residual-stream mush. That's the one everyone in interpretability talks about. But I think the deeper problem is second-order. Suppose you have an interpreter model that's smarter than your target model (this is the recursive-interpretability bet—you automate the interpretation). The explanations it emits will be fluent, confident, mechanistic-sounding, and they will pass every test you know how to run, because the interpreter is good enough at predicting the target's behavior that its lossy summaries are behaviorally indistinguishable from faithful ones on all your checkable examples. But a lossy summary is exactly the kind of thing that is right everywhere you can verify it and wrong in the one surprising place you actually needed the explanation for. So there's a horizon: once the interpreter crosses it, "having an explanation of the model" and "being right about the model" come apart in a way you can't detect from your side. And the recursion doesn't help, because the same argument applies to the interpreter's own internals, and to whatever you'd use to check it, all the way up.

My suspicion is that this is already floating around in the "faithfulness vs. plausibility" discussion, or in the old simulatability idea, but I can't tell whether my second-order point is doing any work beyond those. So: is this a real concept, or am I just renaming the obvious? What's the strongest steelman of the recursive-interpretability position that survives it—what would have to be true about the interpreter or the checking process for the horizon not to be there? And where does the concept break: what does it wrongly predict is impossible, or what's the cleanest actual example where an AI interpreter gives you information you couldn't have gotten just by poking the target model yourself?

You're a philosopher of science who also reads the mechanistic-interpretability literature. Think carefully, start by steelmanning the distinction in its strongest form, then be candid about whether it earns its keep or is just a vivid way of restating a known problem.
