A university adopts the following procedure for sexual-misconduct complaints grounded in deception. In advance of any sexual encounter, either party may file a confidential materiality statement: a list of the kinds of facts the filer regards as material to their consent, such as relationship status, exclusivity, recent partners, STI status, age, or intentions about the future. The statement is not shown to partners unless a complaint is filed. If it later emerges that the other party knowingly misrepresented or concealed a fact of a listed kind, the university treats the consent as vitiated in the same way it treats consent obtained by coercion. If no statement was filed, or the deception falls outside the listed categories, deception is not a ground for complaint. Trace how this arrangement would actually behave: who files, what goes into the statements, how the scheme gets gamed, what it does to norms of seduction and habits of verification, where the most serious second-order effects arise, and whether its failure modes can be fixed without gutting it. Bring out what the scheme implicitly commits the university to about the nature of valid consent, and separate what is well-established in the philosophical and legal literature on consent from what is genuinely contested; do not present a contested position as the consensus.
