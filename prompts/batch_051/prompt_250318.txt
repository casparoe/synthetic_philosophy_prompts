I'm doing a one-off guest lesson for a high-school philosophy club (bright kids, but no physics background), and I've decided to hang the whole hour on this chunk of Lucretius, De Rerum Natura Book 1, in the Leonard translation:

For without void, naught can be crushed, it seems,
Nor broken, nor severed by a cut in twain,
Nor can it take the damp, or seeping cold
Or piercing fire, those old destroyers three;
But the more void within a thing, the more
Entirely it totters at their sure assault.

I keep re-reading it and I'm not sure I can get the order of ideas right. What I'm after: he seems to slide from "solid" meaning no void inside to "solid" meaning nothing can break it, and I want the kids to see why he's allowed to make that slide instead of just nodding along. So I dont need the explanation itself — I need a plan for it. Where do I start: with the everyday sense of "solid," with the void, or with the worry that matter could be infinitely breakable? Is there a carrying example that does the work — I was going to use a billiard ball but now I'm suspicious of it — and if not that, what does the example need to have and need to lack? I'm expecting at least two wrong ideas: "so atoms are just tiny rocks" and "but a hammer crushes chalk." Where exactly in the hour do I head each one off, and what should I say or ask to do it? Also, should I keep impenetrability and indestructibility as two separate moves and then tie them, or present them as a single idea from the start? And give me one closing question that would tell me whether they actually got the argument rather than just learned the words.
