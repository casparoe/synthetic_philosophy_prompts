I keep running into the same problem with the intrinsic vs relational vs instrumental debate about the value of nature: everyone picks one favorite test case (a remote mountain, a restored wetland, a species nobody's seen) and then dismisses the other two views by how they do on that case. Before I commit, I want the full spec first. Write the desiderata for a theory of the value of nature: what does it have to deliver to count as adequate — not just for a term paper, but for actually making conservation tradeoffs?

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it and say which of the three families it's hardest for. At minimum wrestle with: the last surviving individual of a subspecies with no known ecological role and no realistic future; a wetland rebuilt from scratch that is functionally identical to the original but isnt the original; a place no human will ever see or know about; an invasive plant that raises local species counts while replacing a specialist endemic; and a landscape whose value seems tied to being unmanaged but that someone proposes to actively manage in order to keep it wild. Also cover the structural stuff: whether natural value is fungible (can a destroyed site be traded for a new one elsewhere?), what the unit of value is (individual, species, ecosystem, place, rock), and whether the theory has to be pluralist or can afford to be monist.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just camp loyalties or aesthetic preferences. Flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say what that joint failure should tell us. And tell me which single requirement does the most work eliminating candidates. Finally score the three families against the list briefly — a table's fine — but keep the weight on the criteria, not the scorecard.
