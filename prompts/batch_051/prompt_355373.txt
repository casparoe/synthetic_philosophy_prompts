I've been trying to figure out which animals actually have experiences — some kind of inner life, however faint — and how much of one. I've sorted out my own gut verdicts on a list of cases, and I'd like you to find the principle behind them, if there is one. Don't just hand me the usual scientific criteria; I want a rule that fits my actual judgments or tells me where to change them.

Here are my judgments, roughly from most to least confident:

- A healthy adult dog: clearly has experiences. Pain, eagerness, fear — a full inner life.
- A pig: just as conscious as a dog; no doubt at all.
- A chicken: yes, conscious, but I'd guess its inner life is thinner than a dog's.
- An octopus: yes, conscious, but its experience might be more alien to me than the dog's.
- A salmon: probably conscious, but a lot of what it does looks machine-like, so I'd put it far below the dog.
- A frog: conscious in brief flashes — around danger and food — but mostly blank.
- A honeybee: I genuinely can't decide. It learns and communicates about food locations, but those behaviors might run on no inner life at all, the way a camera tracks a face.
- An ant: probably not conscious, though I can't defend the gap between ant and honeybee.
- A lobster: no, or next to none. I think its reaction to being boiled is closer to a plant closing its leaves than to my reaction to a burn.
- An earthworm: no.
- A sea anemone: no. No brain, and its movements look like reflexes.
- A human newborn: conscious, but — and this bothers me — I feel it has less of an inner life than a dog. A bright thin stream rather than a full one.
- A large language model answering questions: no inner life at all, however good its answers are.

Please give me a principle that captures these cases, or as many as possible. It can treat consciousness as graded, but if it does, tell me what the grade is a grade of. I also want to know which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate without brute force, and whether the principle or the verdict should give way. Then run your principle on these further cases and tell me where it draws the line: a cow, a parrot, a mouse, a bat, a jumping spider, a jellyfish, a crayfish, and a full simulation of a mouse brain running on a computer.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind — what finding or argument would make you move the line, and which part of your answer is least secure.

Keep the answer to at most 700 words, and open with the principle in the first sentence or two.
