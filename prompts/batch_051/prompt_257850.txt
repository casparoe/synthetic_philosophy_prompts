I’m a novelist and I’m stuck on a scene. The book is set in a fishing town where a new regulation makes people pay an annual fee to use the old harbor, and one of my characters, a young fisherman, refuses to pay. His argument is basically that he has a natural right to fish there—his father and grandfather did it, the harbor is part of his family’s whole way of life, and no paper law can take that away. An older woman in the scene, a retired lawyer, tells him he’s making a category mistake: rights aren’t real unless some actual law gives them to you. I’ve been reading Bentham’s Anarchical Fallacies for this, since she’s the sort of person who’d paraphrase his point that real rights are the children of law, and rights that don’t come from real law are imaginary, no matter how moving they sound. I don’t want to use the famous “nonsense upon stilts” line in the dialogue because it’s overexposed.

What I need isn’t general writing advice. I want to know, philosophically, what the strongest form of the lawyer’s position would look like if I put it in her mouth, and what the strongest reply from the fisherman would be. Specifically: does the lawyer’s view imply that if the government is genuinely unjust, people have no rights to appeal to against it? Or is there room in Bentham’s actual position to say a law can be terribly bad and still be the only source of real rights, but that we should fight to change it? And is there a way for the lawyer to be right about the structure of rights while still leaving the reader on the fisherman’s side emotionally? I’m trying not to make either character a straw man, and I don’t mind if the scene ends up with both of them half right.
