Okay so I've been trying to pin down exactly why Berkeley's master argument feels both like a blatent sophism and also weirdly hard to shake. The passage I keep coming back to is early in the First Dialogue of the Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous, where Philonous says:

"That there is no such thing as what PHILOSOPHERS CALL MATERIAL SUBSTANCE, I am seriously persuaded: but, if I were made to see anything absurd or sceptical in this, I should then have the same reason to renounce this that I imagine I have now to reject the contrary opinion."

I want a thought experiment built to a specific spec. Two characters, A and B, are arguing about whether a particular apple continues to exist when no one is in the kitchen. A is a Berkeleyan idealist who runs something like the master argument: any attempt to conceive the unconceived apple is self-defeating because B has to conceive it in order to even refer to it. B is not allowed to say "the argument just fails"; B has to accept every step as a psychological description of what is going on in B's own mind. The twist I want is this: B still ends up with a defensible materialist conclusion, but only by drawing a distinction between two different mental acts you could perform on that apple in thought. Construct that distinction, embed it in a concrete scenario with ordinary objects, no demons and no God-talk, and then show exactly where A's version of the master argument loses its grip. Also say which feature of B's move is bearing all the weight, and whether a more careful Berkeley could patch the argument to rule that move out without making the argument circular.
