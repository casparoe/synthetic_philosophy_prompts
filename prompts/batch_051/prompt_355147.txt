I've been trying to reconstruct the old relativity-to-block-universe argument (the one where somebody walking toward Andromeda shifts what counts as "now") and I've ended up with a version that seems to prove way too much. I'd like a diagnosis: which step should I reject, and what do I loose if I reject it? Here's the argument:

1. In special relativity, for any two spacelike-seperated events, there's an inertial frame in which they're simultaneous. So there's no frame-independent fact about which of them comes first.
2. But what's real can't be frame-dependent. So if two events are simultaneous in any legitimate frame, then both events are equally real, full stop.
3. Therefore any two spacelike-separated events are equally real.
4. Now let E be me right now, and let F be me hitting send on this message an hour from now. There's some far-off event X spacelike separated from both E and F. By step 3, E and X are equally real, and X and F are equally real. Since being equally real is transitive, F is already just as real as E.
5. But if F is already real, then what I do an hour from now is fixed in advance. My deliberation between now and then isn't doing any work — it's just me finding out what was already there.
6. That conclusion is absurd. So something in steps 1–5 has to go.

I'd most like to keep step 1, since it feels like just taking the relativity of simultaneity seriously. But maybe the argument is valid and the only honest move is to accept step 5? Or maybe there's a cheaper rejection somewhere in the middle. Where exactly does this go wrong, and what does the fix cost?
