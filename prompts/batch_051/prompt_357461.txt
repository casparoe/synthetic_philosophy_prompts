I'm preparing a recorded interview with a historian who argues that the usual ban on presentism is largely a category mistake. On her account, historians cannot help describing and evaluating past societies through questions and standards supplied by their own time; what should be avoided is not present standards as such, but anachronism in a narrower sense, namely attributing to historical agents concepts they could not plausibly have possessed, or presenting such concepts as if they captured the agents' own self-understanding. She claims this lets historians judge a past practice as, say, exploitative, so long as they do not assert that the participants experienced it as exploitative. Please write a sequence of interview questions that tests whether this distinction can bear the weight she places on it. Begin by asking what makes a later concept accessible enough to a historical agent to escape the charge of anachronism, then move to a hard case such as describing early modern marriages contracted between families as lacking consent. Build in her likely replies and the follow-ups they invite, and end by asking her to identify a past practice that her position would place beyond later assessment — pressing her on what the anachronism constraint still forbids if none exists.
