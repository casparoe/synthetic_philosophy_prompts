Two friends of mine where arguing about one of those hypothetical devices that rewires your reward system so you genuinely love exercise. One said he'd get it in a heartbeat: he hates exercise now, but after the device he'd be someone who loves it, healthier and happier — so refusing is just status quo bias, letting his current lazy self veto the person he could become. The other said that's exactly the problem: the person after the device has different values, so from the current perspective the decision is bad, and the fact that a future person won't mind it doesn't make it good for the current one. First came back: that logic traps you forever — any change that changes your values would be blocked by your past self, so you could never grow, recover, or learn to like anything. Second: growth is fine when it's a byproduct of choices you already have reasons for; it's different when the change is the whole point and the reasons only exist after the change. First: but you do have a reason before — health — and choosing the person you want to become is what self-improvement is. Second: you can't want to become someone whose wants are alien to you; that's not self-improvement, it's self-replacement. They went in circles and I couldn't figure out who was right. Who's right about which specific points, and what are they both missing?
