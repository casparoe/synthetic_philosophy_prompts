In my high-school philosophy club, we recently read an introductory chapter on the philosophy of music. The chapter presents the standard view of musical works like this:

"A musical work is not any particular performance, score, or recording. It is an abstract type — a structure of sounds — which the composer creates in roughly the way one creates a new game by writing down its rules. A performance is a token of that type, just as a printed occurrence is a token of a word. Recordings are simply tokens of the work in durable physical form, which is why playing a recording is a way of hearing the work again. And from this it follows that if a machine produced exactly that sound structure on its own, with no musicians and no interpretation, that would still count as a performance of the work."

I have the persistent feeling that something is being smoothed over here, but because the chapter is by a professional and I am new to the area, I cannot locate the problem myself. Could you audit it for me? Taking it claim by claim, separate what is accurate about the type-token picture of musical works, what is a harmless simplification for an introduction, and what a philosopher of music would explicitly reject. Then, rather than laying out the options, take a definite position and defend it: does the final claim about the machine-generated sounds actually follow from the accurate parts of the summary?
