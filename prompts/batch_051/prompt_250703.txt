I teach an upper-level seminar in moral philosophy, and in the unit on complicity I want students to feel the force of a claim they otherwise only read about: that someone can be answerable for a wrong they did not cause. The group is fourteen students, and I have a single seventy-five-minute session. Design a runnable classroom activity, a structured role-play or staged institutional decision, built around a fictional case in which a joint decision produces a clearly identifiable wrong, and whose rules are arranged so that no single participant's contribution is either necessary or sufficient for that outcome. The case should be one in which the wrongness is recognizable from the inside: no one is a villain, and no specialized moral vocabulary is needed to see that something has gone wrong. Each participant should act under a narrow, apparently legitimate brief: one person's safety data are suggestive but under-powered, another's legal advice is correct but incomplete, another's financial concern is real and parochial, and so on. Information should be distributed so that the fuller picture could be reconstructed if participants pooled what each of them knows, but no one is handed the whole story. A natural vehicle would be a fictional institution's product-review board deciding to continue marketing a profitable product after a weak but credible safety signal, with an injury later traced to the product, but you may substitute any case that preserves the structure.

Give the activity a decision rule that makes each individual contribution non-pivotal: for example, arrange the role cards and agenda so that the vote is expected to be lopsided, ten to four, rather than knife-edge, so that each affirmative vote is individually redundant, with an anonymous tally that makes this redundancy visible. Any participant should be able afterwards to say, truthfully, that the outcome would have been the same had he or she done otherwise. This fact should be discoverable by the participants themselves in the debrief, from the rules and the tally, before the facilitator supplies the term for it. Each role should carry private incentives and constraints that matter within the fiction — a budget, a deadline, a constituency, a promotion, a professional obligation — so that going along is genuinely tempting even when uneasy. Include non-voting participants, such as an advisor or a minute-taker, since it matters for complicity that contribution need not be a vote. Please do not simply recreate a standard diffusion-of-responsibility or conformity exercise; the point is not that people defer to a group, but that each agent's contribution constitutes participation in wrongdoing even when it makes no causal difference.

Specify the roles, the rules, the materials, including the content of the role cards if feasible, a seventy-five-minute timeline, and a debrief. The debrief should map particular moments in the activity onto distinctions that do real work in the philosophy of complicity: causing a wrong versus enabling, facilitating, or legitimating it; what one does versus what one is party to; and a causal versus a participatory basis of responsibility. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run — students treating it as a game and voting for the worst outcome, others breaking role to play the hero and thereby dissolving the structure they were meant to experience, or the group's afterwards shifting all blame onto the chair or the instructor — and say how to handle each failure without ruining the exercise. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce, presumably the real stakes and the standing pressure of institutions, and say what the facilitator must therefore supply in the debrief. Above all, the takeaway should be that two descriptions are simultaneously true: the group did wrong, and no member's contribution changed the outcome, and that this is not a contradiction but the very shape of complicity. Finally, separate what is settled among philosophers who work on complicity from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
