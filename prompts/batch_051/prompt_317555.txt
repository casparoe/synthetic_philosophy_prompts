I'm running a reading group on the four classic theories of humour — superiority (we laugh at someone else's flaw or misfortune), incongruity (we laugh at a violated expectation or a clash of frames), relief (laughter as the discharge of built-up tension), and benign violation (the thing has to feel wrong and okay at the same time) — and a friend of mine keeps insisting that superiority theory is really just incongruity in disguise, because there's always some mismatch between a person and what they ought to be or between their self-presentation and what happens to them. I want to prove him wrong with a case built to order.

Build me one ordinary-life scenario where superiority theory and incongruity theory come apart in the hard direction: the thing is funny on superiority theory but not on incongruity theory. No setup–punchline jokes, no puns, no tickling, no pratfalls, no actual injury, no cartoonish humiliation — the laugh has to be at a person's defect or status, in something quotidian like an office, a family gathering, a classroom, or public transport. The mismatch incongruity theorists always go looking for (tone vs. content, expectation vs. outcome, self-image vs. reality) must not be doing the explanatory work; if the target talks themselves up and then fails, that's cheating because you've handed the incongruity theorist a gift. The point is to isolate the jolt of felt superiority, so the scene itself has to be arranged so that no surprising or clashing element is what triggers the laugh. This is for a paper I'm writing, so a case that merely stipulates "there's no incongruity here" with out making the scene starve the incongruity reading will be useless to me.

Then do the check. State the target, the audience, and the exact moment the laughter fires; give the superiority verdict and why it comes out as laughter rather than just a private thrill of self-congratulation; give the incongruity verdict and why a careful incongruity theorist, even after re-describing the scene, would still say that's not the sort of case their theory is built to explain — or where exactly in the re-description they'd dig in. Say where relief theory and benign violation theory land on the same case, and why. Finish with the honest note: which single feature carries the whole design, what happens to the laugh if you remove just that feature, and how an incongruity or benign-violation theorist would attack the case by re-describing the audience's state rather than the scene.
