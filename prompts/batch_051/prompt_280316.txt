Last month I watched two people argue about a congestion charge, and both of them made what looked to me like the same strange move. Each said the question is not what drivers actually want but what they would want if they were fully informed, unbiased, and thinking clearly. Each then began describing that idealized version of us — and each one's idealized version turned out, with suspicious reliability, to want the policy its describer already wanted. The first speaker idealizes away impatience, status quo bias, and the wish not to be inconvenienced, but insists that the desire to drive one's own car is a genuine value and survives the idealization. The second idealizes away the desire to drive as a preference formed by cheap gasoline and bad transit but keeps the desire to get somewhere on time. By the end of the evening, two idealized citizens existed, both supposedly produced by the same procedure, and they disagreed with each other exactly where the two actual arguers disagreed.

I have since noticed the pattern everywhere people argue about what we collectively should do, especially when the argument is about what a long-lived institution, a constitution, or a future technical system should be built to serve. The move seems to be: replace the question of what people actually want with the question of what they would reflectively endorse, and then fill in the content of that reflective endorsement by sorting actual traits into two piles — defects to be idealized away and values to be retained. The sorting is never neutral, and it always lands where the sorter already stood. When someone objects, the reply tends to be that the objector is not yet the idealized self, so the disagreement is evidence of the objector's remaining defects rather than of any problem with the idealization. The construction is somehow doing all of the argumentative work and none of it.

Is there a name for this? I am not after the generic charge that an argument is circular or question-begging, and I am not after the legitimate project of working out what people would want under better conditions; those are nearby, but I want the specific failure in which the idealizing step serves as a neutral-looking device that quietly converts the constructor's antecedent commitments into outputs. If it sits close to an established concept, tell me what the nearest concepts are, how this differs from each, and which fits best. If no established concept fits, coin a term, define it precisely, and mark its edges: a case where idealization genuinely corrects rather than launders, and where the boundary falls between this pattern and a defensible appeal to what people would accept under improved conditions. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether the pattern has an established name or needs a new one — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Write in American English.
