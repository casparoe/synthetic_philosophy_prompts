I'm retired now—I spent most of my career in natural science, mostly ecology, and lately I've been reading a lot of philosophy to keep the old mind going. The other night at dinner a couple of former colleagues got into it about a chatbot one of them had used to put together a class handout, which had confidently invented a bunch of sources about the history of forestry and handed them over as if they were real. (1) The first said the program lied: a lie is a false statement aimed at making someone believe it, and even if there's no little person in the box deciding to deceive, the whole apparatus is trained to produce outputs people will accept as true, so the deceit is in the design. (2) The second called that a category mistake: lying needs someone who believes the falsehood and intends to induce belief in it, and a language model neither believes nor intends; 'error' is the only label that fits. (3) The first came back with Frankfurt: the liar actually cares about the truth enough to hide it, whereas the bullshitter is simply indifferent to whether what he says is true; by that test the chatbot is bullshit through and through, because it's optimized for fluent, plausible text and not for tracking how things are. (4) The second said even bullshit presupposes an agent with some agenda—for Frankfurt the bullshitter is a person trying to get away with something in a social situation—while the model is closer to a badly calibrated instrument; saying it bullshits is as strange as saying a seismograph lies when it records a phantom quake. They both left thinking they'd won, and I honestly could'nt tell. Could you go through the four moves in order and grade each one, say which specific points survive and which don't, and then tell me what both of them were missing?
