For the past few months I have been in a reading group on the structure of moral categories, and I have ended up with a live disagreement I cannot settle on my own. The position I keep being drawn to is a clean threefold scheme: every act is exactly one of required, permitted, or forbidden, where permitted means only not forbidden. Its virtues are real. It is exhaustive and mutually exclusive; it yields a straightforward decision procedure, since one first asks whether the act is forbidden, then whether it is required, and if the answer to both is no, the act is permitted and deliberation is over; and it stops duty from colonizing every corner of a life, which I take to be a theoretical advantage rather than a mere convenience. I also feel the pull of stopping there: any further category seems to risk either arbitrariness at the boundary or the reintroduction of unspoken duties under a new name.

Against this, I have an intuition I cannot dismiss, and the usual debunking moves have not dissolved it. Consider a person who donates a kidney to a stranger, or runs into a burning building to save someone else's child. I do not think the person who declines the risk has done anything wrong, and I would not blame them; but I also cannot accept that the two acts stand on exactly the same moral footing, as if the hero had merely done something not forbidden. The difference is not only that I admire the hero while declining to condemn the other person; it feels like a difference in the acts themselves, which the flat scheme can register only as identical permissibility. The category we reach for — what philosophers call the supererogatory, acts it would be good but not wrong to omit — keeps presenting itself as more than a sentimental residue that a serious moral theory should discard.

What I want is a negotiated verdict, not a choice of sides. Present each position at its strongest and tell me what a move in either direction would cost. If I should drop the intuition, what would justify treating it as a confusion — of act-evaluation with agent-evaluation, or of the good with the obligatory — rather than as data the theory is failing to capture? If I should amend the theory, what stable logic can a fourth category have: if omitting the act is not wrong, what exactly is the normative status of that omission, and what keeps the category from collapsing back into bare permission or inflating into duty? Or is there a reading on which the threefold scheme and the intuition are compatible because the supererogatory marks a distinction within the permitted, drawn in terms of what is better or more admirable rather than what is obligatory — and if so, does that reading preserve the decision procedure that attracted me in the first place, or does it quietly give that procedure up?
