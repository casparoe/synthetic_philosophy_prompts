Write a sestina that does the philosophical work of Wittgenstein's remarks on family resemblance without ever mentioning Wittgenstein, family resemblance, or games. The six recurring end-words should be ordinary English words whose meanings seem obvious when the poem opens. As the sestina rotates them through new contexts, each recurrence should shift a word's sense slightly, so that by the time the envoi gathers all six, the reader cannot locate a single defining feature that has accompanied any one of them through the whole poem. The form should carry the argument: the end-words are the same tokens in every stanza, yet what each token is turns out to be not one thing but a crowd of related uses — the rope whose strength is supplied not by one fiber running its full length but by many overlapping ones. Do not let the poem conclude that the words are meaningless, that meanings are arbitrary, or that definition is impossible; the closing should leave the thought that the words work because their uses overlap just enough, and no more. A specialist should recognize the view without it being named, and a reader who has never heard of it should still feel the slippage as the poem's real subject.
