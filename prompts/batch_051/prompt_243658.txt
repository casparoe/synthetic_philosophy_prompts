I am designing a unit on concepts and cognition for an advanced undergraduate seminar, and I keep returning to a pairing that seems almost too apt. Douglas Hofstadter and Emmanuel Sander's *Surfaces and Essences* argues that analogy-making is not one cognitive faculty among others but the very engine of thought: a concept is a widening family of analogies, and understanding anything means seeing it as essentially the same as something encountered before. Borges's story *Funes the Memorious* (also translated as *Funes, His Memory*) describes a young man who, after a riding accident, is unable to forget anything. He retains every perceptual detail of every moment, and the narrator suggests that this very fullness of memory leaves him incapable of general thought: he cannot form the abstractions that ordinary minds use, because abstraction seems to require the forgetting of differences. I would like to use the story as a stress test for the Hofstadter-Sander thesis.

But I suspect the pairing is less tidy than it first appears, and I want a reading that is scrupulous in both directions. Where exactly does Funes dramatize the theory — for instance, in his discomfort at giving the same name to a dog seen at two different moments, or in the strange private number system he invents? And where does the story resist the theory or rest on a picture of abstraction that *Surfaces and Essences* explicitly rejects? Hofstadter and Sander insist that categorization does not mean discarding detail; Borges's narrator seems to treat abstraction as a loss of information. Is that a genuine disagreement, and who gets the better of it? Finally, what does the story contribute to the philosophical argument that a bare exposition of the theory does not — does it merely illustrate the claim that thought is analogy-making, or does it complicate that claim in ways a seminar should take seriously?
