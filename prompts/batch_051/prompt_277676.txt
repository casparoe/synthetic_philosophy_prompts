Two people in my reading group got locked into an argument a while back and I'd like it settled. (1) The first said: suppose a demon threatens to torture you unless you admire him for his cruelty. You'd obviously have *some* reason to make yourself admire him — if there were a pill, you'd rationally take it. But that looks like a counterexample to the idea that 'admirable' just means 'there is reason to admire': here you have a reason, and no admirability whatever. (2) The other called this a wrong-kind reason: it's a reason to bring about an attitude, not a reason that makes the attitude correct, and the analysis should be restricted to reasons of the right kind. (3) The first shot back that 'right kind' is just a label until you say what makes a kind right, and the obvious answer — reasons about the object's features — borrows the very notion of admirability the view was trying to analyze. Besides, the demon's threat really does figure in deliberation about wether to admire him, so it's a reason to admire, not just a reason to do something else. (4) The second said there's a non-circular test: right-kind reasons are object-given, wrong-kind ones are state-given. The threat is a reason to be in the admiring state (that's good for you), not a reason that concerns the demon, and you can tell them apart by asking whether the consideration still applies once you know you'll never actually form the attitude. They went in circles like this and neither budged. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, then give an overall verdict on who's right and what both are missing — as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion.
