I'm a high school junior who co-leads my school's after-school philosophy club. We meet for one hour, usually a dozen of us, mostly juniors and seniors. I'd like a structured activity — a game, a role-play, or a staged task — that makes the members feel the pull of the extended mind thesis before they are told its name. The idea I have in mind is the claim, usually associated with Andy Clark and David Chalmers, that some outside things we call tools (a notebook, a phone, another person's memory) can be so tightly coupled to our reasoning that they partly constitute the cognitive process, rather than merely causally assisting a process that stays inside the head. Please design the activity so that participants end up depending on outside props or on each other in a way that matters to the task, and only later realize they were treating those props as more than tools; the point should come from what happens, not from an opening lecture. Include concrete rules or roles, a list of materials or space requirements, a timeline for the hour, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the activity onto the parity principle and the competing interpretation that the props are simply causally useful tools. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle that, and be honest about which feature of the thesis the activity cannot reproduce (for example, it may demonstrate tight coupling and dependence but not literal constitution). In the facilitator notes, please separate what is settled — the basic structure of the original argument and the claims its defenders are committed to — from what is genuinely contested among philosophers working on the idea, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Please write the answer in American English.
