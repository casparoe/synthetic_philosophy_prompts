I am a PhD student in philosophy of science, and while preparing a unit on the demarcation problem I keep returning to two statements by Popper about the status of Darwinism that appear to point in opposite directions. In his intellectual autobiography, Unended Quest, Popper concludes that Darwinism is not a testable scientific theory but a metaphysical research programme: a fruitful framework for generating particular testable hypotheses, yet one whose central principle is so accommodating that it can absorb almost any biological observation. A few years later, in his Darwin Lecture, published as Natural Selection and the Emergence of Mind, he announces that he has changed his mind and that the theory of natural selection is after all testable — hard to test, perhaps, but capable of being refuted by empirical findings. On the face of it, the second passage affirms exactly what the first denies.

I am less interested in the biographical question of what Popper happened to believe at each date than in the conceptual one: can these two positions be reconciled without special pleading? What are the strongest candidate reconciliations — a shift in the meaning of testability between the two passages, a distinction between natural selection as a narrow causal mechanism and Darwinism as a sweeping historical programme, a real but rationally motivated change of mind, or some difference in what each passage is trying to establish — and what does each reconciliation cost? I would be grateful for a careful weighing of these options, a verdict on which reading is most honest to the texts and which strains them, and a closing remark on what this episode reveals about the demand that a genuinely scientific theory be falsifiable.
