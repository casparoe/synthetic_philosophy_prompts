I have been reading about proposals to replace or supplement elected legislatures with sortition—selecting representatives by lot from the citizenry—and I keep noticing what looks like a structural parallel with a set of practices in another field entirely. The central claim of sortition advocates, stated roughly, is that a lottery is blind in the ways that deliberate selection is not: it cannot be lobbied, curated, or crowded out by the well-resourced and well-connected, and it does not systematically favor the articulate, the incumbent, or the already-powerful. In experimental science, randomization is defended in what sounds like the same register. Random assignment of subjects to conditions is supposed to prevent exactly this kind of systematic selection effect, ensuring that whatever characteristics might affect the outcome are not unevenly loaded into one group by the choices of investigators. Probability sampling in surveys seems to make the same move as well: random selection yields a small portrait of the population rather than a portrait of whoever happened to show up.

The tempting conclusion is that these are all applications of one principle—deliberately give up control over who is selected, so that no selectable trait can bias what results. But I cannot tell whether 'random' is doing real work or whether it is a shared word covering different goals. In a randomized trial, the point of randomization is to make groups comparable so that an effect can be estimated. In sortition, the point is to constitute a body that will deliberate and decide, and it seems to matter that the body be a miniature of the citizenry, not merely that two groups be balanced against each other. In a survey, the point is again different: to estimate a distribution in a population. I would like to understand whether sortition genuinely instantiates the same principle as random assignment, merely looser or applied to agents, and what results, if any, would carry over from the experimental setting to the political one. In particular, I would like to know what the analogy would have to be true for it to be deep, what it would predict, and where it breaks down—for example, whether the fact that random subjects in an experiment do not govern themselves is a minor disanalogy or a decisive one.
