I'm a software engineer, not a philosophy person, so maybe this is dumb. I've been toying with an essay about whether a corperation can be morally blamed, and I want to use this bit from Hobbes's *Leviathan*, the introduction:

For what is the Heart, but a Spring; and the Nerves, but so many Strings; and the Joynts, but so many Wheeles, giving motion to the whole Body, such as was intended by the Artificer?

I want to say: companies are like Hobbes's artificial animals. They have parts (departments, employees, processes) but move as a whole. Legally we blame them all the time with fines and sanctions, but lots of people say that's just make-believe, a company doesn't have a mind to be guilty. My essay idea is that we can make sense of corporate moral blame if we treat the company as an artificial person whose intentions live in its governance structure — like its compensation schemes, sign-off rules, policies — rather than in any one employee's head. Then blaming the company isn't blaming the employees, but it isn't just blaming a fictional entity either; it's blaming the pattern that moves the parts.

But the passage cuts the other way too. If a company is just a machine designed by people, then blaming the company seems like blaming a watch for being wrong; the real fault is in the artificer. So maybe all corporate blame reduces to blaming the people who designed the organization. I'm stuck on whether Hobbes's picture helps or actually undermines corporate blame.

I'd like comments on this as an essay idea: does it misuse Hobbes, is the argument interesting, and where should I expect a philosopher to push back first? Also, is there a better next move after the watch objection?
