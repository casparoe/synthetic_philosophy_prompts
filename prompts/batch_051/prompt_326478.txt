Write a short story that dramatizes Heidegger's distinction between readiness-to-hand (Zuhandenheit) and presence-at-hand (Vorhandenheit) by centering on a moment of equipmental breakdown. The story should show, through concrete action and perception rather than exposition, how a piece of equipment in smooth use withdraws into the background of a practiced activity, and how a sudden malfunction makes the whole network of purposes, materials, and other equipment surface all at once as something noticed and unmanageable. Do not have the characters mention Heidegger or use philosophical vocabulary; the narrative itself should do the conceptual work, and the breakdown should have an ordinary mechanical cause — a cracked part, a failed valve, a tripped sensor, a jammed mechanism — not a mystical or portentous event.

Choose one of the following settings, or invent a comparable one: a commercial bakery's deck oven failing an hour before opening; an infusion pump alarming on a night hospital ward; a small fishing boat's engine stalling in fog. The protagonist should be someone with real tacit expertise who has used the equipment hundreds of times, so the failure registers first as a difference in feel, sound, or rhythm before it becomes a thought. Keep the story between 900 and 1,200 words.

After the story, append a single section titled "Commentary on the choices", 250 to 300 words, explaining the main decisions you made to stage readiness-to-hand, the shift to presence-at-hand, and the surfacing of the equipmental context, and note any ways in which your story deliberately departs from Heidegger's own workshop examples.
