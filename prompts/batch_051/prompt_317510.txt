I've been tinkering with a scoring rule I call the should-have-known score. Standard scoring rules — Brier, log, spherical — score a credence only against the actual truth: if it doesn't rain, then your 0.9 in rain is worse than your 0.6 would have been, full stop. I think that's wrong, or at least incomplete. Epistemic rationality is about responding to evidence, not about guessing how things happen to go. A forecaster whose 0.9 was exactly what the evidence supported and who then got unlucky shouldn't take the same hit as someone who was 0.9 from pure overconfidence.

So I score a credence c against two numbers: t, the actual truth-value (1 or 0), and e, the evidential probability — the credence an ideal agent would have held given exactly your evidence. The score is the sum of squared errors: S(c) = (c − t)² + (c − e)².

I get that this makes the rule improper: by your own lights youd in general expect some other credence to score better, since the two targets can pull apart. I also know about one consequence that sounds bad but that I've started to like: if e = 0.8 and t = 0, the best-scoring credence after the fact is (t + e)/2 = 0.4, which matches neither the evidence nor the truth. And it gets weirder: if a proposition is true and your evidence only supported 0.8, then c = 1 scores 0.04 while c = 0.95 scores 0.025, so the rule scores being perfectly right as worse than being slightly less right but closer to your evidence. It isn't even truth-directed in the usual sense.

In its strongest form, is it a real competitor to Brier scoring or just Brier plus a guilt term? Does the fact that it's improper and not truth-directed automatically destroy the accuracy-dominance argument for probabilism, or can you rebuild something like that argument using the should-have-known score?
