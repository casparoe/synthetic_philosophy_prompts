Ok so I've been trying to pin down what Bentham's argument against natural rights actually is. Everyone knows he makes fun of them, but the actual mechanics of the argument are less often spelled out. I think the best version is something like this, partly based on the less-quoted bits where he says a right has to be the product of law, and that wanting something to be a right is not the same as there being a right, hunger not being bread.

(1) To have a right is to have a legal guarantee: a law imposes on other people a duty to act or refrain from acting in the relevant way.
(2) A natural right is supposed to be a right that exists prior to, and independently of, any legal system.
(3) So a natural right would be a legal guarantee that exists before there is any law.
(4) That is a plain contradiction, since a legal guarantee is necessarily the product of law.
(5) Therefore natural rights do not exist.
(6) If natural rights do not exist, appeals to natural rights cannot justify or condemn anything.
(7) So when people appeal to natural rights against a bad government, they are either making meaningless noises or covertly expressing their own desires or interests.

I suspect step (1) is doing all the work, but I can't quite say why without just stipulating a different definition of rights. Maybe the real force of Bentham's argument is not that natural rights are logically impossible, but that without determinate legal content they are empty? Tell me where the argument goes wrong, and whether it can be repaired. If we reject (1), what is the strongest version of the argument that remains, and what does it actually show?
