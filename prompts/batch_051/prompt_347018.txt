At work we were arguing about whether we should lean on AI more for writing code review comments and architecture decision records, and when I said the risk is that we slowly stop understanding the system we own, a senior dev dismissed it with the calculator story. His version: pocket calculators made most people worse at long division and noone thinks that was a loss, and GPS made people worse at map reading but the skill that mattered just moved up a level to route planning under traffic. So he thinks LLMs are the same thing again: we lose the mental long division of drafting boilerplate and first-pass analysis, and what we gain is the higher-level judgment about whether the output actually solves the right problem. He ended with the claim that worrying about eroded understanding is just nostalgia for skills the world no longer needs. I've heard the same move made about doctors using AI for differential diagnosis and students using ChatGPT for essays, so it seems worth taking seriously.

Give me the explicit mapping first: what plays the role of the calculator, the arithmetic skill, the GPS, the map-reading skill, and the higher-level skill we supposedly gain in the LLM case. Then go through the places the mapping breaks. In particular, I want to know whether there's a relevant difference between offloading a skill whose outputs you can verify cheaply and independently (check the sum, see that you arrived on time) and offloading a skill where the ability to evaluate the output is part of the very skill being offloaded—like catching that an architecture decision record quietly sidesteps a constraint, or noticing that a differential diagnosis list is missing a rare but dangerous option. And one more angle: does reliance on a tool like a calculator leave your sense of what you know and don't know intact, whereas fluent AI prose might not?
