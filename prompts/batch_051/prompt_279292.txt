I run the intake unit of a small organization that resettles people who refuse compulsory military service on grounds of conscience. My caseworkers are not philosophers; they process roughly twenty cases a month under a two-week deadline and forward only a minority to me for final judgment. Our charter says we assist principled refusers of military service, but that phrase no longer does the work: we see applicants who are absolute pacifists, selective objectors to one war, opponents of a particular weapon system, and people whose objection reads as political rather than moral. Write me a decision procedure my staff could actually apply — the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the case to me because the residue is a value judgment rather than a factual or conceptual one. Ground the procedure in the philosophical debate over pacifism and conscientious refusal, but express it for people without that training. Then run it on three plausibly hard applicants: a physician willing to serve in uniform in a noncombatant medical role but refusing to bear arms; a conscript who served without complaint until a particular war began and now calls that war unjust; and an applicant who says all killing is wrong but concedes they would use lethal force against an intruder threatening their child. Identify the step or rule you expect me to find hardest to accept, and say whether the fault is in the procedure or in my intuition.
