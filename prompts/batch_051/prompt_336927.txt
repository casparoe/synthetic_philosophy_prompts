In the opening of the Life of Alexander, Plutarch makes a methodological remark that I will paraphrase from memory. He says, roughly, that he is writing Lives rather than Histories, and that the most conspicuous deeds do not always reveal virtue or vice; sometimes a throwaway line, a joke, or a small private act reveals character more reliably than battles in which thousands die. He also compares the biographer to a painter who works from the face and its expressions rather than from the whole figure.

I want to use this passage as a design principle for a project I am imagining: a handbook of biographies explicitly optimized for the moral improvement of the reader, something in the long tradition of hagiography and exemplary lives. As someone used to thinking about data, I would like you to help me decide how much weight each “data source” should get.

Consider these five components that a biography could emphasize:

A. Explicit moral commentary by the biographer (praise, blame, general maxims drawn from the life);
B. Large-scale public actions and achievements (victories, legislation, martyrdoms);
C. Small character-revealing episodes (private habits, spontaneous speech, jokes, reported gestures);
D. A paired comparison with another life, as in Plutarch’s Parallel Lives;
E. Hagiographic idealization: presenting the subject as an exemplar by minimizing flaws and heightening virtues.

Please rank these five from 1 (most weight) to 5 (least weight) for a biography intended to make the reader better, and give each a percentage weight such that the five percentages sum to 100. Then say in one sentence for each why it lands there, using Plutarch’s stated principle in the Life of Alexander as your standard. In doing so, please distinguish whether that principle says merely that small signs are evidence the biographer should use for diagnosis, or that small signs are themselves the more powerful instrument for moral education in the reader. Finally, if Plutarch is right, tell me whether component E is structurally in tension with the project and, if so, whether that tension is enough to rank it last.

Before giving your final ranking, take a few minutes of reasoning (hidden or shown as you prefer) to brainstorm what moral improvement would have to look like—changed judgment, imitation, emotional response, or something else—so your weighting does not smuggle in one account of moral learning without saying so.
