In science education there is a familiar move: students' systematic errors are often described as though they were scientific theories. A student who says a thrown object keeps moving because it carries a supply of motion inside it—an "impetus" that is gradually used up—applies that idea consistently to balls, pucks, and projectiles, makes predictions with it, and resists abandoning it even after Newtonian instruction. From this, educators draw a practical conclusion: teaching should imitate a scientific revolution. You first elicit the student's "theory," create dissatisfaction by confronting it with anomalies, and then help the student replace it wholesale with the accepted theory, rather than repairing isolated mistakes. Audit the analogy that a student's misconception is a scientific theory. Give the correspondence table: what plays the role of principles, auxiliary assumptions, predictions, anomalies, and ontological commitments? Then identify where the mapping breaks—coherence, stability across contexts, whether predictions are genuinely generated or only post hoc redescriptions, whether there is a community that tests and revises—and separate the disanalogies that matter for the instructional conclusion from those that don't. Does the conclusion survive? Should instruction aim at wholesale theory replacement, or at something more piecemeal?
