I am a novelist, not a professional philosopher, and I am writing to ask you to draft a two-page research proposal for a one-semester fellowship at an interdisciplinary humanities institute. The fellowship funds non-empirical research, and the proposal must state a question, a method, and a sense of what would count as success. I will handle the scholarly citations later, so please do not include a bibliography or refer to specific articles.

The question I want to fund comes out of my practice. Across three novels I have come to depend on what I will call scene-imagery: a stable, felt, spatial sense of a place and its inhabitants that I can return to, revise, and re-enter from different points of view. It is not exactly like a picture — I cannot count the stripes on a remembered awning unless I have already decided what they are — yet it is also not like a description in words. Reading the mental imagery debate, I find two positions. Kosslyn treats images as depictive representations in a spatial medium, scanned, rotated, and inspected much as pictures are. Pylyshyn replies that the underlying format is propositional or language-like and that the pictorial feel is an accompaniment to, not evidence about, the format. My suspicion is that both positions were shaped by a narrow diet of experiments — rotate this shape, scan between two landmarks — and that neither has been forced to say what representational work is being done when a novelist holds whole scenes in mind across a hundred pages of drafting and revision.

Please turn this into a proposal. It should include a precise statement of the question — perhaps something like whether sustained scene-imagery is a counterexample to the depictive/propositional dichotomy, a phenomenon both sides can absorb, or evidence that a third representational role is needed — an explanation of why the existing positions leave that question open, a month-by-month method for a single semester (what I would actually do as a novelist without a laboratory), an argument for that method over the obvious alternatives such as surveying writers' self-reports or merely reviewing the literature, a description of what a positive result would look like and what would constitute the project's failure, and an honest assessment of whether the project is the right size for one semester.
