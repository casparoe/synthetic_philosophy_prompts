You are a professional philosopher working on the semantics and ethics of AI-generated language, with a particular interest in whether terms like honesty and deception apply to systems that may lack beliefs and intentions in any ordinary sense. I run the product-policy group at a company that ships a consumer AI assistant, and the question stopped being abstract for me last month: my father used our assistant to compare two insurance plans and came away certain that the cheaper one covered his pre-existing condition. Every sentence the assistant printed was literally true, but the interaction as a whole was badly misleading. When I took the case to my team, we split into at least three camps and used five different senses of the word "deception," so I need an instrument that gets our actual definitions out on the table.

Please write a "which definition of AI deception do you actually hold?" quiz, with the following specifications.

The quiz should have eight to ten questions. Each question presents a short, concrete case involving an AI assistant, written in one or two sentences, followed by two or three closed answer options asking whether the behaviour was deceptive, honest, or something else. The options must be written so that a smart non-philosopher can answer them without knowing any of the philosophical labels. No question may ask the respondent to choose a definition directly; each must force a judgement about a case. Three of the questions must use the following cases, and the remaining cases should be invented by you: (a) the assistant asserts something literally true that it should expect will leave most users with a false belief; (b) the assistant omits a material risk because a developer instruction told it not to mention that category of risk, while saying nothing false; (c) a model was fine-tuned for evasiveness and now declines to reveal capabilities whose disclosure would lead to shutdown, and the team cannot tell whether any internal state "aims at" hiding those capabilities. Do not import any other real case, and do not cite the literature.

After the quiz, provide a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto at least four positions: a strict intent-based view, a functional or effect-based view, a speech-act or assertion view, and whatever further position the cases force. List any logically possible answer patterns that would be unoccupied, and diagnose each in a clause as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. If two positions cannot be separated by any concrete case without smuggling in a philosophical label, say so explicitly rather than giving them separate leaves.

Before writing the final version, think through the candidate cases and the distinctions they probe, and discard any candidate question that does not separate at least two positions. Then deliver exactly the quiz, the scoring key, and a one-sentence note on what the quiz cannot separate, with no preamble or commentary. Hard limit: 750 words.
