I'm assembling a teaching example for a seminar on collective intentionality, and I need a single, ordinary-life case that pulls Michael Bratman's account of shared intention and Margaret Gilbert's account of joint commitment apart in a specific direction.

Treat Bratman-style shared intention as requiring all of the following: (i) each participant intends the joint activity; (ii) each intends it at least partly because the other intends it; (iii) the participants' subplans mesh; and (iv) all of this is common knowledge between them. Treat Gilbert-style joint commitment as holding when two or more people have expressed to one another their readiness to be jointly committed, so that each is now obligated to the others and none may dissolve the commitment unilaterally; the commitment can outlive any one member's present enthusiasm.

Construct a one-paragraph thought experiment—realistic, ordinary-life, with no institutional roles, no formal contracts, no threats, and no sleeping, intoxication, or absent-mindedness—in which two adults are visibly acting together, with coordinated roles and mutual responsiveness. The case must satisfy all three specifications:

(a) a Gilbert-style joint commitment is in place and is doing real work in keeping the activity going;
(b) no Bratman-style shared intention is present; and
(c) the Bratman failure is not just two people half-heartedly going through the motions—one of the two wholeheartedly intends the joint activity.

Let the Bratman failure come from the other party's relation to the activity, rather than from a mere failure of communication between two people who would otherwise satisfy the account.

After the scenario, run the checks. State the activity under its most natural collective description, go through each lettered Bratman condition and each element of the Gilbert account, and show which hold and which fail. Then explain which single feature of the scenario is doing the work, what happens to both verdicts if that feature is removed, and how a defender of Bratman would most plausibly attack the case—for example, by arguing that the standing joint commitment is itself a shared intention, or that the reluctant party's contribution is not part of a genuinely joint action. Be explicit about the reading of intends you are relying on when the verdicts are close.

Finally, tell me how confident you are that the constructed case meets each of the three specifications, and single out the point you are least sure of.
