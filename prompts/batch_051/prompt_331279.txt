I'm a novelist, and over the last few years I've ended up talked into something like the Hofstadter view of the self — the idea that a self is a pattern, a strange loop or whatever, and that the same pattern can run at lower resolution in other people's brains. So when my father died, a rough copy of him keeps running in me: I know how he'd sigh at certain headlines, I can hear the timing of one of his jokes. The "I" isn't just the one physical brain, it's smeared across all the brains that hold a degraded copy of the loop. I've actually started writing characters this way, as patterns that leak between heads.

I basically believe this now. But I want to know what would count against it. Not the obvious stuff — yes, skulls are seperate, yes, I don't literally feel your toothache. I mean: what would have to be true, or what findings or arguments or possible cases, would make this position false or force me to give it up? Which defeaters are fatal, and which are just awkward? And which ones would a committed Hofstadter-type actually concede count against them, as opposed to the things they'd dismiss as a category mistake? I'd also like a sense of whether the view is even risky — maybe I've worded it so vaguely it can't be wrong, and I don't want that. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward and tell me which one would most likely change my mind personally.
