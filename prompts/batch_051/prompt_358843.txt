In Henry David Thoreau's essay "On the Duty of Civil Disobedience" (also known as "Civil Disobedience"; first published as "Resistance to Civil Government"), he writes:

"The mass of men serve the State thus, not as men mainly, but as machines, with their bodies. They are the standing army, and the militia, jailers, constables, posse comitatus, &c. In most cases there is no free exercise whatever of the judgment or of the moral sense; but they put themselves on a level with wood and earth and stones; and wooden men can perhaps be manufactured that will serve the purpose as well."

A standard reading of this passage takes Thoreau's target to be the abdication of conscience: the state projects force only because people such as soldiers, jailers, and constables surrender their judgment and moral sense, and his remedy is for individuals to withdraw from this mechanical service and act as moral agents. Consider a specific development. A growing share of the state's coercive and administrative apparatus is now automated or delegated to systems that never had a conscience to surrender—algorithmic bail, sentencing, and welfare decisions replacing judicial and administrative offices; automated license-plate readers and predictive patrol systems standing in for constables; drones and weapons that select and engage targets without a human deciding in the moment. The "wooden man" is no longer a human degraded into wood and stone but a literal artifact. Does Thoreau's argument survive this development, or does it lose its target? Identify the premise of the argument that the development bears on, and say whether the argument can be restated so that it still does its critical work against the automated state.
