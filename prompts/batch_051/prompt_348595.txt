My family is Scottish and Catalan, and I keep noticing that people talk past each other about secession by appealing to the same democratic principle to opposite ends. A friend summarized the core issue like this: "In a democracy, the people have the right to decide their own future. So if a majority in Catalonia votes to leave Spain, that majority has a democratic right to secede, and Spain's refusal to recognize it is just an attempt to override democracy with courts and police. But the same people say Scotland can't leave the UK unilaterally, because the UK is itself a democracy, so one region's majority doesn't get to impose its will on the whole country. So there's no stable principle: 'the people' just gets defined as whichever unit produces the result you already like." I think there's more to it, but I'm not sure how to answer. Is this summary fair to how serious defenders of unilateral secession actually argue, or does it conflate different claims about who "the people" is, what a majority may do, and whether democratic self-determination is a right to exit or a right to be heard within a larger demos?

Also, I was reading the Supreme Court of Canada's Reference re Secession of Quebec and got stuck on this passage, which seems to me to be the most interesting but also most ambiguous bit:

"The democratic principle, however, would demand no less. The right of a people to self-determination is normally fulfilled through internal self-determination—a people's pursuit of its political, economic, social and cultural development within the framework of an existing state. A right to external self-determination (which in this case potentially takes the form of the assertion of a right to unilateral secession) arises in only the most extreme of cases and, even then, under carefully defined circumstances."

Does the Court mean that democracy itself, and not just law, limits the circumstances where unilateral secession is legitimate? And when it says the democratic majority's clear vote on a clear question would give the rest of Canada an obligation to negotiate, is that supposed to be a legal consequence, a political obligation, or a democratic principle that applies even without a written constitutional right to secede? I'd like an answer in American English, please.
