I just came home from a long argument with a friend about the common prior assumption — roughly, the idea that rational agents with the same information ought to hold the same prior probabilities, so that any residual disagreement must trace to a difference in information, a mistake in reasoning, or bounded rationality. She gave verdicts on a series of cases, and I wrote them down right after:

1. Two radiologists with identical training, the same scan, and the same test results disagree about the chance of a malignancy. It later emerges that one had failed to notice a shadow on the film. She said both were rational throughout; the disagreement merely reflected an unnoticed information difference.

2. Two traders hold the same prior, observe the same public announcement, and then one buys while the other sells. She said this cannot happen with two genuinely rational traders under common knowledge of rationality; at least one must have private information or has failed to update correctly.

3. Two philosophers, equally intelligent and with access to the same arguments about a metaphysical question, reach different credences. She said this can be perfectly rational, because their priors were shaped by different lifetimes of evidence, and in practice people never have literally identical total information.

4. Two forecasters use the same public data to estimate the chance of an election outcome but choose different statistical models. She said they can rationally disagree, because model uncertainty means the choice of prior over models is not fixed by the data, and neither model is demonstrably better.

5. In the formal setting of Aumann's agreement theorem — two Bayesians with a common prior and different partitions of private information, who announce their posteriors back and forth — she said convergence is forced; if they still disagreed after their posteriors became common knowledge, at least one would have violated Bayesian updating.

6. Two people with identical evidence and identical priors reach different credences because one has a financial stake in the belief. She said stakes can affect what it is rational to do, but not what it is rational to believe, so the stakeholder's belief is irrational.

Next week I want to raise the case we never resolved: two thoughtful, mathematically trained people who share all their evidence about the chance of a civilization-scale catastrophe in the next century — one says 30 percent, the other 2 percent — and after a long exchange, neither can identify any private information the other lacks; each regards the other's prior weighting of historical analogies as unreasonable. What should I predict she will say? I am less interested in the number than in the rule she seems to be running. Which candidate principles fit her record — the strict common prior doctrine, a permissivist view on which equal evidence can rationally permit a range of priors, a modeling-idealization view on which the common prior assumption applies only inside a fixed formal model, or something else — and what would a further case have to look like to settle it? Please separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
