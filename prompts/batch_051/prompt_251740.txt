An economist put the following argument to me, and I cannot find the exit. Set aside trades with a liquidity, hedging, tax, or rebalancing motive; consider a purely speculative trade, in which each side trades only because they assess the asset's prospects differently.

(1) A trade is rational only if each party expects to benefit conditional on everything they know — including the fact that the other party is willing to trade at the proposed price.

(2) Willingness to trade is itself information. An offer to sell at $10 is evidence that the seller's information puts the asset's value at or below $10; a bid at $10 is evidence that the buyer's information puts it at or above $10.

(3) Rational traders update on such evidence.

(4) Once each side has updated on the other's willingness, the trade cannot look good to both. The buyer must worry that the seller knows something adverse; the seller must worry that the buyer knows something favorable. Each side's willingness to trade at a given price is evidence that makes the other side less willing, and there is no price at which this mutual suspicion settles: the only price the buyer would accept after revising is one the seller, after the same revision, would not offer.

(5) Therefore purely speculative trade should never occur.

(6) Yet financial markets see enormous purely speculative volume every day, much of it between rational, well-informed parties who simply disagree about what the same public information means.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, can one still hold both that market prices aggregate dispersed information and that people do not knowingly enter trades they expect to lose, or must one of those claims go?
