An introductory ethics textbook I'm using summarizes what it calls the "expectation view" of promising like this:

"After we see what is wrong with breaking a promise, there is no mystery left. When I promise, I cause you to believe I will perform and to arrange your plans around that belief. If I then fail to perform, the wrong I do has the same structure as any other injury: I have harmed you, but through your expectations rather than through force. The duty to keep a promise is therefore only a special case of the duty not to harm people who have reasonably relied on us. This explains why a promise to someone who says 'I don't trust you and won't count on you' creates no obligation at all, and why the stringency of a promise tracks how much the promisee actually loses by relying. It also refutes the older view that promising is a social practice whose wrongness consists in breaking the practice's rules: what does the moral work is reliance, not rules."

Something about this leaves me uneasy: it seems to make promise-breaking just another way of damaging someone, and I doubt an obligation could vanish the moment the promisee announces distrust.
