For my philosophy of fiction seminar I want a working definition of imaginative resistance, not the one-sentence gloss everyone recites but one that actually classifies hard cases. The going gloss is something like: it's the difficulty we have taking on board what a fiction says when the content is morally or evaluatively upside-down. But the moment I walk students through cases, the agreement evaporates, and I'm starting to suspect the phrase covers two different phenomena — a refusal (I won't imagine that) and a blockage (I can't make sense of it). So here's the task. Propose a definition, run it through all eight cases below, say which verdicts come out wrong or surprising, and then either revise the definition or defend the verdict. Also tell me whether the term picks out one thing or two.

Cases:
1. A serious literary novel, with no hint of irony, asks us to take it as fictionally true that the protagonist's torture and murder of a child was morally right.
2. A story explicitly stipulates that in it's world every bachelor in the village is married.
3. A fantasy novel stipulates that, in its world, sacrificing your first child to the volcano is genuinely, objectively morally obligatory — and it announces this the same way it announces that dragons exist, not as a piece of local custom or a character's opinion.
4. A short story gives the full text of a dreadful poem — clichéd, inert, metrically inept — and asks us to take it as fictionally true that this is the most beautiful poem ever written.
5. A sitcom has a character tell a lame joke, and every other character responds as though it is the funniest thing ever said.
6. A first-person narrator who is clearly established elsewhere in the novel as a self-deceiving villain says that he did the right thing; the story as a whole only asks us to accept that he believes this.
7. An alternate-history novel in which the Axis powers won the war; readers find it grim but have no trouble taking it as true in the fiction.
8. A novel describes, without any endorsement, a society that believes burning widows is deeply obligatory.

My own hunches, for what they're worth: 1, 4, and 5 are the same underlying phenomenon, 2 is something different, and I honestly don't know weather 3 belongs with 1 or with 7. Dont let my hunches drive you too hard.
