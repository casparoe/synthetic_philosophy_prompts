I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind working on perception and the language we use to report it, and as a student member of my university's neuroethics review board I've been asked to draft the written opinion for an application we'll vote on next Thursday. The proposal comes from a neuroengineering group and asks to implant—temporarily, in three sighted volunteers—a microelectrode array in visual cortex and deliver patterned stimulation intended to elicit chromatic experiences that fall outside standard colour space: first the classic "forbidden" opponent combinations such as reddish-green and bluish-yellow, and then, if those are achieved, candidate novel hues that are not recombinations of anything in the normal trichromatic gamut. The investigators frame the study as a direct probe of whether perceptual space is fixed by our neural architecture or can be expanded, of what, if anything, a colour scientist would learn by undergoing such an experience for the first time, and of whether a subject can have a genuinely novel quale rather than a recombination of familiar ones. They also concede the central difficulty: ex hypothesi they cannot tell prospective participants what the experience will be like, since by definition it is incommunicable to anyone who has not had it; consent would have to proceed by analogy to known colours and to non-verbal animal work that, by its nature, contains no introspective report. Half the board reads this as exciting basic science whose consent problems can be honestly bracketed; the other half thinks the consent is fatally circular and that the unknown risk of lasting perceptual distortion—persistent reddish-green afterimages, colour rivalry invading ordinary vision, changes in aesthetic response—is not offset by any therapeutic benefit. I need a formal decision memo I can circulate: a clear recommendation—approve, reject, or approve with conditions and a concrete time frame—with reasons the investigators and the rest of the committee can actually respond to. Approach this as an expert in philosophy of mind and neuroethics. Think through the argumentative structure carefully before committing to the bottom line, and make the memo engage the strongest consideration against whichever recommendation you adopt rather than merely refuting a weak version of it. Be explicit about how much work the metaphysics of qualia can and cannot do in settling the ethics, and about where the committee must simply make a judgment under uncertainty rather than deducing an answer from the concept of incommunicability.
