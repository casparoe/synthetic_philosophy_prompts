I am a graduate student TAing an undergraduate course on aesthetic judgment, and I am also taking a seminar on Kant's third Critique. I'm grading an exam and would appreciate a careful second opinion on one answer. The exam question I wrote asked students to work through section 33 of Kant's Critique of the Power of Judgment, where he considers whether our judgments of taste can be based on the judgments of others. Since I did not want to privilege a particular translation, I gave the class a paraphrase of the passage: Kant says that another person's judgment is not a valid reason on which our own judgment of taste can rest; if we let our approval track the approval of others, our judgment becomes heteronomous. He allows that examples of correct taste may serve as models for training the power of taste, but only insofar as they prompt us to exercise our own feeling, and they cannot supply the determining ground of a judgment of taste. The question then was: What exactly is Kant rejecting in this section, and what role, if any, does he allow to the testimony of other people in aesthetic judgment?

The student wrote:

The target of Kant's argument is not the causal influence of other people's judgments. He is willing to admit that we learn to judge by exposure to good examples. What he rejects is the idea that another person's judgment can serve as a reason or ground for one's own judgment of taste. If I predicate beauty of an object because a critic predicates it, my judgment is heteronomous and not properly mine. Kant says taste is autonomous, which means the determining ground of the judgment must be the subject's own feeling of pleasure in the object. Nevertheless, I think he allows a fairly broad role for testimony. As long as I eventually arrive at my own feeling, there is no difference in principle between letting a critic direct my attention to the details of a work and taking the critic's verdict as a provisional aesthetic judgment that I later confirm. In both cases the determining ground of my final judgment is my own feeling. So a novice could, on Kant's view, defer extensively to a reliable critic early on, provided that the deference is cashed out later in autonomous felt response. Kant might want to forbid that, but I do not see how he can do so consistently, since any influence from others must operate through attention in some way.
