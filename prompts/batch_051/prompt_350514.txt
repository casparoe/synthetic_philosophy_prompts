It started with a news piece saying a large study had found educational attainment is about 40% heritable. Mira, who teaches history, said: 'So for an individual child, roughly 40% of their school success is genetic, and the rest is environment.' Dev, who does data work for a research unit, said: 'No, heritability is a population-level statistic. It's the proportion of variance in a trait across a sample that's statistically associated with genetic differences. You can't carve up one child's outcome into 40% genetic and 60% environmental.' Mira: 'But social scientists do that all the time. If a teaching intervention raises average test scores by 0.3 standard deviations, no one thinks every pupil gains exactly that. The group-level estimate is still the best evidence about any individual. Why is it different here?' Dev: 'Because the intervention involves an actual manipulation, while heritability is passive. It's estimated within whatever range of environments the sample happened to experience. It doesn't tell you what happens when you change that range.'

Then Mira said: 'Fine, take it as a purely group-level claim. If 40% of the spread is genetic, then the most any amount of equalising the environment can do is wipe out the other 60%. So the number does give you a floor: even perfect environmental reform can't close the gap beyond that. That's a real policy conclusion.' Dev: 'That floor doesn't follow. The 40% isn't fixed. Make environments more uniform and the environmental variance shrinks, so heritability can go up even while the trait gap closes. And a genuinely new environment, something not represented in the current range, can change the genetic variance too.' Mira: 'But now you're saying the number is so context-bound that it can't license any policy claim, yet you also say if the environment changed the number would change. Which is it: is the number informative or not? If it can't be applied to individuals and it can't tell us what a new environment would do, what exactly is it good for?' Dev: 'It's a description of the sources of variation in a particular population under the range of environments they actually lived in. That's not nothing: it tells you there is genuine environmental variation in that range, so room for intervention in principle. It just doesn't tell you which lever to pull, or how much a brand-new environment would move the trait.' Mira: 'But "there's some environmental variation in the current range" is a thing you could know without a giant heritability study. The number is doing no real work for anyone outside population genetics, and it's actively misleading everyone else.' Dev: 'That's a claim about how the statistic is used, not about what it means. Those are different questions.' Mira: 'They aren't cleanly different when a statistic is predictably misread. If a number has a technical meaning that almost no layperson recovers, then reporting it as a headline is shipping a falsehood, even if the falsehood lives in the reader rather than the paper.'

Then Dev tried one more angle: 'Look, the same logic works in reverse. People want to say high heritability means an outcome is fixed. It doesn't. Phenylketonuria is a classic genetic condition, and a dietary change nearly eliminates the severe phenotype. So high heritability doesn't license fatalism.' Mira: 'That's a single-gene metabolic disorder, not a complex behavioural trait like educational attainment. Its a bad analogy.' Dev: 'The principle doesn't depend on it being single-gene. Complex traits have reaction norms too. The point is simply that a trait can be strongly genetic and still highly responsive to a specific environmental change.' Mira: 'Fine for that narrow point, but you still haven't said what the number should make us believe about complex social outcomes. If the answer is "only that genes and environments both vary in the current population", then I stand by my charge: scientifically legitimate, practically worthless.'
