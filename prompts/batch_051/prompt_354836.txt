I have been trying to extract the rule behind a single state appellate court's defamation decisions about ratings, rankings, and forecasts, and I would like you to work through it with me. The court has held that a television critic's line that a sitcom was "unwatchable" and that its writers "could not recognize a joke" was protected opinion; that a municipal-bond rating firm's note that a city "missed three consecutive debt-service payments" stated a fact and could go to a jury even though it appeared inside an evaluative rating; that an analyst's note, "we forecast Meridian's revenue to fall 12%; we rate the stock Underweight," was protected opinion because a projection is not provably false at the time it is made; that a consumer magazine's statement "we cannot recommend the Halcyon vacuum" was opinion but its further statement "in our drop test, the handle cracked" would be actionable if false; and that a newspaper's selection of a restaurant as "Worst New Restaurant" with zero stars was protected opinion, on the ground that the context marked it as a critic's subjective shorthand rather than a measurement.

A new case now reaches the same court. A website named RateRight publishes, for a moving company, the single line "Reliability Score: Anderson & Sons Moving — 2.1/10." The score is produced by an algorithm from past customer reports, but the site displays none of those reports, no methodology, no sample size, and no margin of error. Anderson & Sons sues for defamation, arguing that the numerical score asserts as fact that the company is unreliable. I want a ruling: what should the court decide if it is to cohere with its own precedents, and what is it most likely to decide? More than the answer, I want the implicit principle. Set out the candidate rules that fit the earlier decisions, say what each predicts for the reliability score, identify the closest analog among the precedents, and state what rule the court would be committing itself to under each available outcome. Then describe one further hypothetical case — a score with its data and methodology fully disclosed would be one obvious candidate — that would cleanly separate the rules that remain.
