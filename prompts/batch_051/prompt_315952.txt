I am a staff software engineer at a midsized company, with no formal training in economics or philosophy. I sit on a monthly Resource Review Board that evaluates requests from project teams for additional staffing, schedule extensions, or rescoping. Over the past year, a large share of the requests we have approved are for projects that have already exceeded their original cost or schedule, and the dominant rationale in the room is some version of "we are too far in to stop" or "cancelling now would waste the work already done." I suspect we are systematically letting past investment drive future investment, and that our review process contains no test that would catch this.

I recently read about the dollar auction. As I understand it: an auctioneer sells a dollar bill to the highest bidder, but the second-highest bidder must also pay their final bid and receives nothing. Once two people have placed bids, each can reason that raising the bid is better than dropping out, because dropping out means paying a bid for nothing while the other person wins. The result is that two rational parties can drive the price far past the value of the dollar, with both ending up worse off. This resembles enough of our extension decisions that I want to build a review procedure around it, but I cannot currently put it into a form the board can actually run.

Please write a reusable decision procedure for our board, grounded in the structure of the dollar auction rather than in generic advice like "ignore sunk costs." It must be runnable in roughly thirty minutes per case by engineers and product managers with no economics background, and it must terminate in one of three ways: approve, reject, or refer to a named human decision-maker with a specific question that cannot be resolved by the procedure. I want, in order, the questions to ask; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a no; and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

The procedure needs to separate two situations I keep conflating: cases that merely involve sunk cost (where the only thing favoring continuation is the amount already spent) and cases that genuinely have the dollar-auction structure (two parties locked in against one another, each new increment small compared with what is already at risk, no stopping rule agreed in advance, losing framed as paying for nothing). The procedure should also say what should be in place before a project starts so that later escalation is harder, even though our current backlog will not have those protections.

Then run the procedure on three requests, showing each step and the final recommendation:

1. A data-warehouse migration that is sixty percent over its original estimate, with no competitor and no hard deadline; the team argues that stopping would strand the work already completed.
2. Two internal teams building competing replacements for the same legacy tool, each asking for an additional two engineers to keep pace with the other.
3. A machine-learning feature the board's chair sponsored personally, which has missed three successive milestones and whose projected remaining value is now below the projected cost to finish; the team's strongest argument for continuing is that cancellation would be visible as an admission of failure.

At least one of these should produce a recommendation the board will not like; show that the procedure gives it anyway. Finally, say explicitly what the procedure cannot do and the kinds of cases where it will misfire, so we do not overread its outputs.
