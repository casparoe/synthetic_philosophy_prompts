A friend and I ended up in a minor argument last weekend that has been nagging at me. She is trying to spend less, and a few months ago she asked her partner to require a second signature for withdrawals above a certain amount from their shared savings account. Last week she discovered that he now treats that arrangement as his own — he blocked a withdrawal for a holiday because he judged it exactly the sort of impulsive purchase the rule exists to prevent, and he would not back down. I surprised myself by siding with him: she had, after all, deliberately built a machine to frustrate her own future impulses. Then I remembered that I keep a similar machine in my own life. I have set up my banking app so that any transfer above a certain amount is delayed by forty-eight hours, because I know the person I am at two in the morning is a worse judge of what I need than the person I am over Sunday coffee. Sometimes my present self is thwarted by an arrangement made by my past self, and I am not resentful; I am, if anything, proud of it. I sometimes picture it as having myself tied to the mast so I can hear the singing without steering onto the rocks. Yet if a friend or partner imposed the same restriction on me without my asking, and then refused to lift it when I objected, I would find it outrageous, paternalistic, an assault on my freedom.

The question I cannot shake is whether this asymmetry is defensible. In both cases a person is constrained by an arrangement they did not, at the moment of constraint, choose; in both cases someone has concluded that they know better than the person whose hand is reaching for the money. The only obvious difference is that in one case the constraining party is my own earlier self. Is that difference enough? Is it grounded in something real — identity, consent, authority over one’s future, privileged knowledge of one’s own weaknesses — or is it only the comforting fact that the jailer wears my own face? I have also noticed that entire countries seem to run on the same logic, with constitutions that deliberately make it hard for later majorities to change their minds; I admire that when I agree with the constitution and resent it when I do not, and I worry that my private preference has the same shape.

I would be grateful if you would think this through as a philosopher working on autonomy, promising, and practical reason. Please begin by brainstorming the strongest candidate justifications for allowing my past self to bind my present self while refusing that right to another person; then test those candidates against nearby variations of the case, such as a commitment made by a much younger self, a commitment never consciously renewed, or a friend who restricts me in a way I would clearly have consented to in advance. Conclude by saying whether I should keep the asymmetry, narrow it, or give it up. Please write your answer in British English.
