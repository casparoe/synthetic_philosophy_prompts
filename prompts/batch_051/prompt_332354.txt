Question: Explain the paradox of phenomenal judgment for epiphenomenalism about consciousness. Make clear why it is called a paradox rather than just an objection, then discuss whether the standard epiphenomenalist reply — that the same physical state causes both the experience and the judgment about it — defuses the worry. End by saying what the paradox does and does not show about the truth of epiphenomenalism.

Student's answer:

Epiphenomenalism is the view that conscious experiences, or qualia, are real but causally inert: they are produced by physical states in the brain, but they themselves produce nothing — no behavior, no further thoughts, no reports. It is distinct from identity theory, since the experience is not identical to the physical state, and from eliminativism, since the experience is not denied. The paradox of phenomenal judgment comes from applying this picture to our own judgments about consciousness. Take my judgment that I am in pain. The judgment itself is a physical event, and on the physicalist side of the view every physical event has a complete physical cause. The felt pain therefore drops out of the causal chain leading to the judgment: the judgment is caused by nociceptor firing and downstream neural processing, not by the pain as felt.

The usual epiphenomenalist response is that the very same physical state that causes the pain also causes the judgment about the pain. The judgment and the pain share a cause, so the judgment can still be reliably correlated with the pain and can carry information about it. That, the reply says, is enough for the judgment to count as being about the pain.

I don't think the response works. If the belief would be exactly the same whether or not the felt pain accompanied the physical state, then the judgment is insensitive to the quale itself. And if the quale does not cause the judgment, then the judgment is not justified. So the epiphenomenalist has to admit that all of our introspective beliefs about qualia — including the belief that epiphenomenalism is true — are unjustified. The view is self-defeating, and that shows epiphenomenalism cannot be true.
