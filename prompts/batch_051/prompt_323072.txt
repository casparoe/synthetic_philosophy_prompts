I work as a software engineer on a consumer mobile app, and I have never studied philosophy, but I have a running confusion about my own product that I hope someone can untangle. Our app has, on one side, a set of engagement features: a daily streak between friends that resets if either misses a day; a "nudge" notification system whose wording and timing we tune by A/B testing to maximize opens; autoplay of the next item; and a leaderboard among your contacts. On the other side, we ship well-being features: users can mute notifications, set a daily time cap that locks them out after a limit, and turn on a "take a break" reminder. The company's main success metrics are daily active users and total session length. One strange finding from our data is that users who set the time cap tend to open the app on more days, not fewer, so the engagement team has stopped objecting to the cap and now quietly treats it as part of the growth strategy.

I have trouble saying what theory of the user our product actually commits us to. When a user opts into streak reminders, are we treating them as a rational chooser whose preferences we are fulfilling, or as a bundle of habits to be steered? Does the presence of an off-switch make the steering unproblematic, or does it just make the design easier to defend publicly? I suspect a philosopher would say our two feature sets presuppose different and maybe inconsistent views of human agency, but I do not have the vocabulary to make that precise. I am not looking for a reading list or citations; I want the analysis applied to the case exactly as I have described it.

I would like you to reconstruct the implicit view of the user, and of our responsibility toward them, that the product as a whole seems to presuppose; say whether its parts presuppose consistent things; and tell me what would have to change in our design process or success metrics if we replaced that view with a different one.
