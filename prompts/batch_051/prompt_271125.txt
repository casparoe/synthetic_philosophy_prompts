I chair the Coastal Resilience Board of a small city-region, and we must settle a forty-year flood-protection strategy before our current planning consent lapses. Our climate modellers accept three sea-level-rise scenarios for 2070 — roughly 0.4 m, 0.8 m and 1.4 m — but they will not assign probabilities to them: the relevant processes are non-stationary, and past frequencies are, they insist, a poor guide. Three proposals are before us. Option A builds a hard barrier around the harbour and central business district. It produces the largest avoided monetary losses in the low and moderate scenarios, and would be the unambiguous cost-benefit winner in those worlds; but in the high scenario it is overtopped and the low-lying northern residential wards, where most of the city's poorest households live, flood badly. Option B raises and extends defences around exactly those northern wards. It protects the most vulnerable residents in the most severe scenario and leaves no household catastrophically exposed, but it is never the best performer in aggregate cost-benefit terms, it underperforms A sharply in the low and moderate scenarios, and it leaves the harbour and city centre with only modest protection in the high scenario. Option C is phased managed retreat from the most exposed wards, with buyouts and a restored coastal wetland. It is adaptable, has the narrowest spread between its best and worst monetary outcomes, and requires less capital up front; but it is never the cost-benefit leader in any scenario, it is the worst option for the harbour economy, and it imposes relocation on the very communities B would protect in place.

The board is split. One faction argues for a maximin rule: when probabilities cannot be assigned, choose so that the worst outcome for the least advantaged is as good as it can be (a Rawlsian line). A second faction argues that maximin is pathologically timid and favours minimising maximum regret: we should choose the option that would leave us with the least regret once we learn which scenario obtained. A third, Harsanyi-style line holds that impartiality requires weighing every affected person's welfare equally and maximising aggregate expected benefit; since the modellers will not supply probabilities, this faction says we should treat the three scenarios as equiprobable rather than letting an absence of probabilities force a maximin conclusion.

Draft the short written opinion I shall circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend one option, with a concrete timetable and any conditions, and it should treat the strongest competing position seriously rather than restating a favoured rule: say honestly why the most plausible losing option is losing. Please also say which single consideration, if it were established, would most likely change your recommendation, and where your own reasoning is most fragile. Use British English throughout. Keep the JSON fields concise.
