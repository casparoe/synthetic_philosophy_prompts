I’m sketching a section of a paper on where moral status should come from: the computation a system is doing, or the way that computation is implemented. I want a thought experiment that forces those two views apart. Build me a pair of systems, A and B, that compute the same input-output function over a large but finite set of inputs — behaviorally indistinguishable on every input you’d ever actually test them on — but where an implementation-sensitive theorist says A is sentient and B is not, while a pure computationalist is force to say they have the exact same moral status (both sentient or both not, your call, but they can’t split them). Constraints: no exotic physics, no souls, and no stipulating that B is a zombie while A isn’t. Both are ordinary physical devices, even if one of them is a bit contrived. The sentience verdict has to fall out of a structural feature you actually build into A and leave out of B — something like recurrent feedback, global broadcast, or the right biological substrate — and you should defend why that feature, rather than the I/O mapping, is carrying the moral weight. Keep the scenario itself under about 200 words, then do the check. State which structural feature is doing the work, walk through the verdict each theory gives about A and B and why, and say what happens if you add that feature to B or strip it from A — does the case collapse? Finish with the strongest attack a computationalist would make on the case (they’ll likely say B isn’t really running the same computation in the relevant sense), and say what you’d have to change to block that attack. The point is to actually use this in an argument, so if the case only works because I’ve quietly changed the computation between A and B, it fails.
