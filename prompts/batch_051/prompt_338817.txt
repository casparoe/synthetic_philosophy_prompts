I keep flip-flopping on whether there's a real moral difference between lying and misleading, and I cant get the two sides to sit still. The view I'm most attracted to on paper is roughly this: lying is asserting something you believe is false, and assertion is a special kind of act. When you assert, you hand your hearer a kind of assurance; you put yourself on the line and invite them to rely on your say-so. Merely misleading someone—saying something true while knowing full well they'll draw a false conclusion—still aims at the false belief, but it works by exploiting the hearer's own background assumptions. On this view the hearer is partly responsible for the false belief they end up with, and that changes the wrong. The view has virtues I like: it explains why "well, technically I never lied" can be a legitimate moral defense in some contexts, why being lied to feels like a more intimate betrayal than being allowed to fool yourself, and it gives a clean-ish rule: don't assert what you don't believe.

But then I imagine a case and the whole distinction looks like a loophole. Suppose I've been messaging an ex behind my partner's back. My partner asks me point blank, have you been in touch with her? I answer, "I haven't seen her since we broke up." That's true—we've only talked by phone and text. I'm inviting the exact false conclusion I want them to draw. Compared to just saying "no," the view I just defended says the first answer is a lesser wrong because no false assertion was made. My intuition says the first is if anything worse: it not only deceives, it does so while building in an alibi, and it treats the other person as someone to be managed rather than answered. The lie at least risks something; the misleading answer keeps my hands clean while I push them into the mud.

So which yields? Is my intuition tracking a real feature of the case—something about deniability, contempt, or premeditation—that the assertion-norm view should be amended to capture, or is it just the residue of being embarrassed that someone got me to deceive them with the truth? Give me the strongest version of both positions, then say which move is justified and what would justify each alternative: drop the intuition, amend the theory, or show me they're actually compatible. If the answer is "compatible," tell me exactly how the view distinguishes the two acts without ranking them in a way my intuition rejects.
