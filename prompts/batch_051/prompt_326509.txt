I am a novelist rather than a philosopher, and I am midway through a book whose central character helps to run a philanthropic foundation of the kind one finds in the effective altruism movement. While researching the movement's public materials I have been struck by two of its habits, which I want both to understand and to get right for the book. The first is the career advice often given to earnest young people: if one is choosing between, say, teaching in a state school and an uncongenial but well-paid job in finance, one is told not to begin by asking which work would be more meaningful, but to ask which choice lets one do the most good, because the salary difference, donated to cost-effective charities, could pay for several teachers or health workers in places where a marginal pound buys far more than it does at home. The second is the way funding decisions are made: interventions are ranked by quantifiable cost-effectiveness — lives saved, disability-adjusted life-years averted, income generated per pound — while goods that resist that treatment, such as dignity, belonging, political voice, or the patient's sense of being cared for, are treated as soft, unproven, or not yet evidence-based, and in practice go unfunded.

I want to know what theory of value, and what view of the moral agent, that pair of practices presupposes. Does the career advice commit one to thinking that the agent's own direct contribution is morally fungible, and that the right choice is whatever maximises the expected total good according to some single commensurable currency? Does the funding practice commit one to thinking that only the measurable matters, or merely that the measurable is all we can responsibly act on? Most of all, do these two practices presuppose the same theory, or two theories that merely happen to share an office? I would ask whoever answers to distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely sit well with, to identify which rival theories of value the practices quietly rule out, to say where the two practices are philosophically underdetermined, and to describe what a foundation's funding rule, or a career conversation, would have to look like if the presupposed theory were replaced by a pluralist view of value or by a view on which one's own relationship to one's work matters for its own sake.
