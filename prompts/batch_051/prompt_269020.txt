I've been puzzling over something from William James's *Pragmatism* and I'd like you to help me work out what a certain scientific practice is tacitly committed to. In Lecture VI ("Pragmatism's Conception of Truth") James is attacking the idea that truth is a matter of copying reality. He writes:

"The popular notion is that a true idea must copy its reality. Like other popular views, this one follows the analogy of the most usual experience. Our true ideas of sensible things do indeed copy them. Shut your eyes and think of yonder clock on the wall, and you get just such a true picture or copy of its dial. But your idea of its 'works' (unless you are a clockmaker) is much less of a copy, yet it passes muster, for it in no way clashes with the reality. Even though it should shrink to the mere word 'works,' that word still serves you truly; and when you speak of the 'time-keeping function' of the clock, or of its spring's 'elasticity,' it is hard to see exactly what your ideas can copy."

Here's the practice I'm thinking about. In several experimental fields—psychology, biomedicine, even parts of particle physics—there's a kind of two-tier epistemology. The people who actually collected the data may have direct verification of the result, but the rest of the field treats the result as established after peer review and maybe one or two replications. Review articles, textbooks, and clinicians will then say "we know X" where "we" includes lots of people who couldn't vouch for the original apparatus, the statistical choices, or the raw data if their life depended on it. So the practice seems to treat a claim as true on the strength of a process (peer review, publication, replication talk) rather than on each believer's private copying of reality.

My question: what theory of truth does that practice presuppose—really presuppose, not just loosely fit? Does it presuppose James's view that truth happens to an idea when it gets assimilated, validated, corroborated, and verified by its consequences? Or does it presuppose Peirce's view that truth is the opinion the community of investigators would converge on in the long run, so that individual unverifiability doesn't matter? Or does it shift between the two, and if so, is that incoherent? I'd also like to know what would have to change in the practice if we replaced the view it actually presupposes with the other pragmatist view. And if you think the practice is philosophically underdetermined—that several theories of truth would license it—please say where exactly the underdetermination lives.
