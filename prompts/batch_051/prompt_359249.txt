I've been reading around divine impassibility for a philosophy of religion seminar — the claim that God can't literally suffer or be moved by creatures — and I keep going in circles on how the tradition handles the obvious pressure points: the Bible having God grieve and repent, the crucifixion, and the worry that a God who can't be hurt can't really love. The stock replies (biblical language is just accommodation to human idiom, the incarnate Son suffers only in his human nature while the divine nature stays untouched, love is an act of the will rather than a felt passion) all feel a little too tidy to me, and I'd like a piece of writing that sits inside the strain and lets the strain show. Could you write a deadpan, HR-style pastoral care training memo from a fictional denominational office that is training ministers to console grieving people while holding the line on impassibility? Aim for around 650 words.

It should read like a passive-agressive administrator's all-staff email, but the humor has to come from the actual theological content rather than from inventing silly doctrine. For instance: rules for how to say "God is with you in your suffering" without ever saying God suffers, a table of approved vs. banned phrasings, a script for what to do when a mourner asks "is God sad right now?", and a section on why God's compassion is "real" but not "affective." Maybe throw in a genuinely plausible bit of jargon like "covenantal non-reciprocity" and an official-looking footnote. Keep the whole thing deadpan and don't wink; a theologian should recognize the moves being skewered — accommodation, the two-natures move, impassible love-as-will — and find the satire fair, not dismissive. And please don't actually quote anyone.
