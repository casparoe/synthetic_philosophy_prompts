I keep getting confused about why the strategy-stealing argument for Hex doesn't just carry over to chess. In Hex the idea is that if the second player had a winning strategy, the first player could make an arbitrary opening move and then play as if she were the second player, treating her extra stone as harmless. Since Hex has no draws, that rules out a second-player win, so the first player must have a winning strategy. But when I try to run the same argument on chess, everyone says it fails because of zugzwang—yet I've never heard exactly how that blocks the argument. I'd like you to spell out, as precisely as you can without falling into a full technical proof, which specific features of Hex do the real work in the argument: symmetry, the fact that an extra move can't hurt, the absence of draws, or something else. Then walk through what happens to the argument in chess if you drop each of those features one at a time. In particular, could a version of the argument still show that White can force at least a draw, and if not, which step is the first one that breaks? I'm after the conceptual joints of the argument, not just the slogan that zugzwang kills it.
