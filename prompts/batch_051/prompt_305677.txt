An opinion piece I read defends the following line. Blame, it says, is a moral attitude toward a wrongdoer, and it presupposes a mind: the wrongdoer must be able to form intentions, recognize that an act is wrong, and feel something like guilt or remorse for it. A corporation has no such mind. It is a legal construct, and when we say "the company knew" or "the company decided" we are using shorthand for knowledge or decisions held by particular managers or board members. So when a company systematically falsifies safety data or conceals a dangerous product defect, the author concludes, the proper reaction is twofold: identify and punish the specific individuals who made the decisions, and impose civil fines or compensation requirements on the firm to shift costs and deter future misconduct. But to say that the company itself is blameworthy is, in the author's view, either loose talk or a category mistake. The final verdict is that corporate blame adds nothing except moral confusion to individual blame plus corporate liability.

I find the argument superficially convincing, but I suspect it slides from a true point about feelings to a much stronger conclusion about blame. I want an accuracy audit: which steps should someone who is careful about corporate wrongdoing accept, which steps are contestable or subtly loaded, and does the conclusion follow from the accurate parts? In particular, I would like to know whether blame really requires the capacity for guilt or remorse; whether a structured group can have intentions or decisions in a sense that is not reducible to the intentions or decisions of any single member; and what the difference is between holding a company liable for harm and blaming the company for it. Please separate what is accurate, what is a simplification, and what is false, and then say how much of the article's practical advice about punishing individuals and fining the firm survives if its central claim is rejected.
