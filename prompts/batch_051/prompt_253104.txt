I have been returning to Hofstadter's "Ant Fugue"—the dialogue with Aunt Hillary in which ants exchange signals and the colony as a whole is supposed to support talk of a mind. A friend and I translated a toy version of it into a simulation, hoping the group-level patterns would settle the argument; they did not, and the disagreement has only sharpened.

The conviction I cannot refute is functionalism about minds: a mind is an organization of signals, not a substance. A neuron is a unit that fires or stays silent; an ant is a somewhat richer unit that lays and follows pheromone trails, touches antennae, shifts roles, and responds to local concentrations of nestmates. If what makes a mind is the abstract causal architecture—the way excitation spreads, is damped, pooled, routed, and fed back—then there is no principled reason that architecture must be realized in membrane potentials rather than in ants moving over a trail network. To insist otherwise looks like chauvinism about the substrate, and the Ant Fugue's lesson, on my reading, is exactly that many individually mindless units can cross a threshold beyond which the whole is doing genuine symbol manipulation rather than merely suggesting it to a human observer.

The intuition I cannot dislodge is that an anthill is not any kind of subject at all. My resistance is not that the system is too slow or too foreign. It is that the colony seems already to be a society of agents, each with its own sensory point of view and its own local agenda, and a mind composed of many minds feels like a different kind of claim from a mind composed of non-minded cells—less like an engineering gap and more like a category error. When we say the market is panicking or the traffic is flowing well, we do not think the market or the traffic is thereby conscious; we think only that the aggregate behaves usefully as if it were a single agent. I cannot see why the colony's pheromone patterns would be more than an elaborate case of the same thing.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a reading of "has a mind" on which both survive—for example, a reading on which the colony's mind is real without being an additional subject over and above the ants, or on which the composition of subjects is ruled out in principle. Give me the strongest version of each side, then a negotiated verdict: which should yield, or how the question should be reframed so that it does not have to.
