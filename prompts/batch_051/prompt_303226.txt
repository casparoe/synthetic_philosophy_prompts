In a recent graduate seminar on the structure of moral and legal rights, a student interrupted an argument about a proposed public-health measure by announcing that rights are trumps, as if this settled whether the state could permissibly restrict movement or association. The slogan was doing a specific job there: it was meant to rule out, in advance, any weighing of an individual liberty against aggregate benefit. When I pressed her afterward, it emerged that the same form of words could stand for several different claims—among them, that rights carry extra justificatory weight, that rights enjoy lexical priority over non-rights considerations, and that rights serve as exclusionary reasons that remove certain trade-offs from first-order balancing. I have also encountered the slogan in disputes about whether rights are best understood on the model of side constraints, understood as constraints on what may be done to a person rather than goals to be promoted, or on the model of instruments for protecting interests.

I would like you to disambiguate the slogan that rights are trumps for me. Please lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise theses a person might be asserting when they use that slogan. Include weak readings (for example, that rights sometimes outweigh considerations of policy or aggregate welfare), strong readings (lexical priority, or exclusion from first-order balancing), and a specificationist reading on which a right is absolute only once its exceptions have been built into its content. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or false, and explain how it relates to the side-constraint and instrument pictures of rights. Finally, tell me which of these readings, if any, would actually support the student's move of closing off the balancing argument about the public-health measure, and which readings would merely change the subject.
