Art critics and some philosophers of art have long held that an undetectable forgery is still a different work from the original, and a lesser one, because an object's history—who made it, in what circumstances, under what constraints, and with what intention—is not merely evidence about the object but part of the object itself. On this view the original has something the molecule-for-molecule identical copy lacks, even if no expert and no instrument could ever tell them apart. I cover science and ideas for a living, and I want to know whether that is a testable claim or a refined way of talking. Please design the strongest concrete test you can of whether the value people assign to originals tracks anything beyond the label "original" and the narrative attached to it. Specify what you would manipulate, what you would measure, in whom, and the comparison that separates a genuine history effect from a mere labeling effect; state what pattern of results would count in favor of the claim and what would count against it. Then say exactly what a clean positive result would and would not establish—in particular, whether it would show that history is truly constitutive of the work, or only that people are moved by stories about the work, and which part of the original claim no experiment of this sort could settle.
