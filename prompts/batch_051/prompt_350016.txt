I am a novelist constructing a secondary world, and I want to think through an institution before I put it in the book. The setting is an archipelago of city-states that has, by reputation, survived far longer than its mainland neighbours. Their founding institution is called the Office of Reversibility, and its rule is this: no community may adopt a new practice — a crop, a craft technique, a trade arrangement, a ritual obligation, a building method, a tool — unless the community can demonstrate to an inspector that the practice can be set back down within one generation: that is, that the community could return to its prior way of life without famine, mass relocation, or the loss of an irreplaceable skill. Practices judged irreversible are not simply banned; they may be run as bounded experiments by no more than one household in twenty, with the experimental households bearing legal responsibility for the costs of reversal. Inspectors are paid a tenth of the community's agricultural surplus, must live in the community they inspect for a full season, and rotate every seven years.

I want you to treat this as a machine rather than as a utopia or a parable. Act as an expert in institutional design and the comparative study of societal collapse. Think carefully, and do not settle for the first failure mode that suggests itself. Begin by brainstorming the interested parties — farming households, experimental households, inspectors, local rulers, priests, merchants, and the neighbouring states outside the archipelago — and the incentives the rule gives each. Then work through how the demonstration of reversibility would be gamed; what happens to innovation, craft knowledge, and trade; what second-order effects appear over two or three generations; and where the system breaks first.

I am especially interested in a structural question. The Office treats collapse as a failure of reversibility, one irreversible practice at a time. Is that the right diagnosis of why societies in the familiar debates — the Bronze Age, the Classic Maya, Easter Island — become brittle, or does it miss the way practices that look reversible in isolation couple together into a system nobody can abandon? Finally, say which failure modes could be patched by rule changes, such as long-after-the-fact audits or reversibility plans for bundles of practices rather than single ones, and which failure modes are baked into the idea itself. I plan to dramatize three of the most important failure modes in the novel, so I need to know precisely how this machine would break.
