so a friend and i went back and forth for like two hours last night about street names vs statues. his take was that a street name is just navigational infrastructure, so renaming it is trivial, but a statue is a historical object and removing it erases history. i kept saying a statue in front of a courthouse is the city pointing at someone and saying this is a person we honor, so if the city stops wanting to say that, taking it down is not erasing anything. then i went and looked at how my actual city handles this and its got me way more confused.

concrete case: theres a local guy from the 1920s who gave a ton of money for parks and schools but also helped write eugenics laws. his name is on a street, was on a school, and is on a big bronze statue in the main park. the city quietly renamed the school a few years ago, and the street renaming is going through now. for names, the rule they use is basically that a name can be removed when the person no longer represents what the city wants to honor. but the parks department refuses to take down the statue, and their stated rule is different: a statue stays unless it lacks historical or artistic significance, and they explicitly deny that keeping the statue means the city honors him. they say the statue is a historical artifact, not a present endorsement.

im trying to reconstruct the theory of commemoration this two-track practice presupposes. is the city committed to the view that naming is an honorific act but erecting or keeping a statue is not? is that distinction stable, or does it break down once you remember the inscription on the statue, or the fact that many statues were commissioned precisely as honors? could a coherent view instead treat names and statues the same way, either as honors or as historical records, and if so which would it be? and concretely, under each replacement, what changes more: would statue removal become as easy as school renaming, or would school renaming become as hard as statue removal, or something else?
