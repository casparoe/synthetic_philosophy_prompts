I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. I work on the orchestration layer for what amounts to whole-brain emulation infrastructure: checkpointing, migrating a running mind-simulation between substrates, rollback, forking, and scheduling under different time factors. In my work the phrase "the same mind" does real work — it decides whether a migration is lossless in any meaningful sense, whether a rollback destroys something ethically relevant, and whether two forked instances are two continuations of one history or two minds. I want a working definition or criterion of "the same mind" for emulation scenarios. By "the same mind" I mean numerical identity, not similarity: the relation that makes "this is the same individual as before" true, not merely "this is a very similar mind." If you think the better-posed unit is "same person" or "survival" rather than "same mind," say so briefly and define your preferred unit instead; otherwise stay with the mind.

Propose the definition, then run it through the following cases in the order given. For each case, give the verdict the definition yields, say how confident you are in that verdict, and flag any place the definition gives no answer or the wrong answer. If it breaks on the cases, revise it or defend the surprising verdict; I want the definition that survives the cases, not a doctrine that ignores them.

The cases:
1. Anesthesia. A person undergoes ninety minutes of general anesthesia and wakes with memories, temperament, and capacities intact. I take this as clearly the same mind before and after.
2. Non-destructive upload. A sleeping person's brain is scanned completely and an emulation is instantiated from the scan while the biological original continues to live. I am confident the emulation is not the same mind as the original — a new mind that starts out very similar, not the original — but I want the definition to explain why.
3. Destructive upload. The same scan, but the original brain is destroyed in the process and the emulation begins from the scan state. I am genuinely torn: my engineering intuition says destruction during scanning should not matter, but another intuition says this one is the original and case 2's emulation is not.
4. Gradual replacement. Over several years a patient's neurons are replaced, one at a time, by functionally equivalent artificial units while consciousness and behavior continue uninterrupted; the patient reports seamless continuity. I want this to come out the same mind.
5. Live migration. An already-running emulation is moved from one substrate to another via continuous copy-on-write migration, never fully paused. I expect this to be the same mind; if the definition says otherwise I need to hear it directly, because much of my infrastructure assumes it.
6. Rollback and re-run. A running emulation is snapshotted Friday; it runs through Saturday, forming new memories; it is then stopped, rolled back to the Friday state, and re-run. Was the Saturday-run mind the same mind as the re-run mind? Was either the same mind as the Friday mind? I have no settled view here.
7. Forking. From one Thursday snapshot, two instances are launched and then diverge. Can both be the same mind as the Thursday mind? I suspect the answer should be no, but I cannot give the reason without ruling out something I might want to keep.
8. Time dilation. The emulation runs a thousand times slower than biological time for one real-time month, then returns to normal speed. I would like this to be the same mind, but the notion of continuity starts to feel slippery to me here.

I am most unsure about cases 6, 7, and 8. It seems to me that how those come out will force a choice between a continuity-style account and a pattern/copy-style account, and I want to know whether that is right.

Throughout, keep two things separate: what is settled or close to settled among people who work on this topic, and what is genuinely contested. Do not present a contested position as if it were the obvious consensus.

Hard cap: 850 words. Lead with the proposed definition in one or two sentences, run the cases in the order above, then close with a short note on where the definition is weakest and which parts of the account are settled versus contested.
