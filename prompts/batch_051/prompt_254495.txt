I am a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I keep seeing the gene's-eye view associated with Dawkins and Williams defended through a particular picture: the gene is treated as a strategic agent whose goal is to maximize copies of itself in future generations, and the organism is treated as the vehicle or survival machine that genes construct to serve that goal. In my reading group this picture is not just exposition; it is used to settle substantive questions. The sterile worker ant is presented as no deep puzzle, because on this picture the ant is the vehicle and the genes inside it are really acting on copies of themselves in the queen's offspring. And group selection is set aside on the grounds that a group is not the sort of thing that could literally be an agent with a goal, so talk of group-level adaptation is at best loose shorthand.

I would like an audit of this agent-and-vehicle analogy, aimed specifically at how much of that substantive work it can carry. Please give me: a careful correspondence table between the source (strategic agency, interests, goals, designing and using a machine) and the target (genes, organisms, replication); an account of where the mapping breaks; a distinction between the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusions and those that are merely cosmetic; and a statement of which features of agency language the analogy quietly imports into the gene-level story even though natural selection at the gene level does not require them. Finally, say whether the two conclusions the picture is being used to support — that organism-level altruism such as worker sterility reduces to gene-level self-interest, and that group selection is thereby defused — survive once the disanalogies are on the table. If there is a better analogy, or a cleaner non-agential way of stating the gene's-eye claim, describe it and say what, if anything, it loses.

Because I want to know how much to lean on this audit, please indicate your confidence in each main claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
