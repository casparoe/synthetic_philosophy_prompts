So I've been chewing on the problem of old evidence and I want an honest verdict on my solution, because I'm probably being clever in someone else's territory. The problem, as I understand it, is that Bayesian confirmation says E confirms H when P(H|E) > P(H). But if E was already known before H was formulated, then P(E)=1, so conditioning on E doesn't move anything, and the math says old evidence can't confirm anything. That's clearly wrong: the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion was known since the 1850s, and it's one of the strongest pieces of evidence for general relativity even though it predates the theory by decades.

Here's my idea, which I call the Two-Channel Theory of Confirmation. Confirmation isn't one thing. There's updating: what happens to your credence when you first learn E. Then there's retrodiction: the support E gives H when H shows up later and explains E. For retrodiction, you can't just set P(E)=1 and be done with it. Instead you use what I'm calling a de-knowledged prior: you temporarily pretend you never learned E, ask how surprising E would have been given the theories that were around before H, and then ask how much more likely H would have made E compared to that baseline. So for Mercury, you pretend it's 1859, you take the Newtonian picture plus the live auxiliary fixes (Vulcan, a dust ring, whatever), assign them some prior, and compute how much more probable GR would have made the observed precession relative to that competing baseline. Whatever quantitiy falls out is the retrodictive support of E for H.

I also think this captures something real about scientific practice: scientists seem to keep a running ledger of open anomalies, and when a new theory finally closes an anomaly that's been sitting there unexplained for 50 years, they treat it as much stronger evidence than a theory that just re-explains data that was never puzzling in the first place. The longer something sat unexplained, the bigger the retrodictive bump.
