I'm TAing for an undergrad philosophy of mathematics course, and I've been grading the short-answer section of the midterm. The unit was on Benacerraf's dilemma for mathematical platonism. I've been going back and forth on one student's answer long enough that I can't tell whether I'm being two harsh or two generous, and I need to hand these back with comments tomorrow.

The question I set was: "Explain Benacerraf's dilemma. Which two commitments about mathematical language and mathematical knowledge come into tension, and why can't they both be held comfortably at once? Then briefly describe one response and say which commitment the response gives up."

The student wrote: "Benacerraf's dilemma is that mathematics forces us to accept two things that are hard to reconcile. On the semantical side, we want to take statements like 'there are infinitely many primes' at face value: they are objectively true or false, and the quantifier 'there are' commits us to a domain of objects, numbers, just as much as 'there are two apples' commits us to apples. On the epistemological side, we want our mathematical knowledge to work like knowledge generally does: for a belief to count as knowledge, there has to be some causal or at least reliable connection between the believer and the fact the belief is about. The problem is that numbers, as the platonist understands them, are abstract, causally inert objects outside space and time, so no such connection seems possible. Therefore platonism makes mathematical knowledge impossible. A response is Gödel's: we have a special faculty of mathematical intuition that apprehends abstract objects directly, roughly the way perception apprehends physical objects. This solves the problem without giving anything up, because the semantical side is left untouched and intuition supplies the missing epistemic link."

Can you grade this for me? I'd like a score out of 10 and, more importantly, point-by-point comments I could actually put in the margins: which parts of the answer are clearly correct, where the reasoning slips or states more than the premises support, and whether the student's proposed response actually identifies which of the original commitments gets given up. Also flag any place where you'd be lenient because the claim could be charitably read.
