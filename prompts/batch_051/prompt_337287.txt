Construct a single-utterance case engineered so that two positions on the Gricean line between saying and implicating deliver conflicting verdicts about whether a speaker made a false statement. Then run the case through Grice's diagnostics and report where the line ends up.

Work with the following two positions about the saying/implicating line. Both positions accept Grice's wider programme: sentence meaning underdetermines what a speaker conveys, and hearers recover further content by inference from the utterance and the context.

Position M — minimal what-is-said. What is said by an utterance is fixed by the standing conventional meaning of the expression plus the assignment of values to overt context-sensitive elements (pronouns, demonstratives, tense) and the resolution of overt ambiguities. Anything further conveyed is an implicature, not part of what is said.

Position E — enriched what-is-said. At least some contextually recovered content that answers to no overt element of the sentence can belong to what is said, because the asserted proposition just is the enriched proposition the speaker commits herself to. On Position E a speaker can say something false even when the sentence's minimal content is true.

You may use Grice's intention-based analysis of speaker meaning where it helps: a speaker means that p only if she intends (i) to produce a certain response in the hearer, (ii) that the hearer recognize intention (i), and (iii) that this recognition be part of the hearer's reason for the response.

Specifications for the case:

1. Exactly one declarative English sentence, uttered by a competent adult in an ordinary setting in which the difference between asserting a falsehood and merely misleading has an official consequence (a signed declaration, a disclosure duty, a warranty, or similar). No science fiction, no private language, and no special legal definition that stipulates the verdict.

2. The verdict split: under Position M the speaker says something true and only implicates something false; under Position E the speaker says something false. The two positions therefore disagree about whether the speaker made a false statement.

3. The false delivered content must be a contextually supplied restriction or completion — of time, location, domain, comparison class, or the like — not the resolution of a lexical ambiguity and not the "drink" meaning "alcoholic drink" pattern.

4. Avoid the stock examples from the implicature literature: no "I haven't eaten" used for "today"; no "Robin has three children"; no "the ham sandwich left without paying"; no restricting "everyone" to a class as in "everyone went to Paris"; no scalar "some" for "not all". The case should be unfamiliar enough that a competent reader has to reason it through.

5. The speaker must be in a position to know that the enriched content is false and must intend the hearer to record or act on that content. The minimal content's truth must rest on a specific fact of the situation, not on a logical triviality or a "there exists somewhere in the universe" dodge.

The case is for testing whether Grice's diagnostics (calculability and cancellability) reliably classify default pragmatic content as implicature rather than as part of what is said, in a setting where the lie-versus-mislead difference carries real stakes.
