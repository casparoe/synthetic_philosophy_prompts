I work as a software engineer, and I keep finding myself in arguments about whether planning is overrated in which it becomes clear, ten minutes in, that the participants are using the word "planning" in at least two different ways: sometimes to mean producing an artifact such as a roadmap, a design document, or a release schedule, and sometimes to mean the intellectual activity of thinking a problem through before committing to a course of action. I would like a working definition of planning that lets me separate planning from merely foreseeing, from predicting, and from merely intending, and that gives a definite verdict on each of the following cases: (1) a chess engine running minimax search over millions of positions before selecting the move it will actually play; (2) a person catching a falling mug out of the corner of her eye; (3) a developer writing a thirty-page design document before touching the codebase; (4) a large language model generating a chain-of-thought trace before outputting its answer; (5) a jazz musician who, during a particular performance, decides on the spot to quote a melody she has practiced for years; (6) a compiler reordering instructions so that they exploit a processor's pipeline more efficiently; (7) a person who forms an intention to call her mother tomorrow but forms no view at all about when, where, or how she will do it; and (8) a startup's five-year revenue projection.

My rough intuition is that planning requires representing some future state of affairs and then using that representation to guide or constrain present action, but that would make the chess engine count as a planner even though it has no commitment to the rest of the line it evaluates, and it leaves me unsure about the startup projection and the bare intention. I suspect the ordinary word is doing two jobs at once: one concerning an internal cognitive process, and one concerning the documents or public commitments that process may or may not leave behind. Give me the best definition you can, run it through all eight cases, tell me which verdicts you are least confident in, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. If you conclude the concept really is doing two jobs, give me both definitions and say which of the eight cases move between them.
