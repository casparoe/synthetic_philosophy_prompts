As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am working on a longform piece about restitution disputes, and I keep coming back to a case whose outcome turns on the reading of a museum's founding document. I have been following a dispute at a museum I'll call the Ridgemont Municipal Museum, which holds three Benin bronze plaques acquired in 1904. Its 1903 deed of settlement directs the trustees to keep the collection together in the city for the public's instruction in perpetuity, and it includes this clause: "The objects now or hereafter vested in the Trustees shall be kept together in this city and open to the public; and the Trustees shall not sell, exchange, give away, lend, or otherwise part with the possession of any such object, save only for public exhibition elsewhere for a limited season, upon the written resolution of the Trustees." The museum has now proposed to acknowledge Nigeria's claim by transferring the legal title to the three plaques to the Nigerian state, while the plaques themselves remain exactly where they are, conserved and displayed by the museum under a fifteen-year renewable loan-back agreement. The city attorney calls the plan a prohibited gift, since title would pass for nothing; the museum's director replies that the clause is about possession and public access, not bare title, and that the collection remains together, in the city, and on view. I would like to know how this clause applies on each of the usual approaches: the ordinary meaning of the wording, the evident purpose, what the 1903 settlors would have said if asked about such an arrangement, and what a reasonable reader today would expect the clause to require. Which approach should decide the matter, and why?
