I am a novelist, and for the past year I have used a large language model as a research assistant for period detail: the layout of a Victorian workhouse, the etiquette of a dinner party in Bombay in the 1920s, the likely weather in Trieste in November 1907. It has often been genuinely useful, but I have also caught it delivering, in flawless and confident prose, elaborate answers that were simply not true. One writer-friend dismisses the machine as a liar. Another says it is a bullshitter. A third, the only one of us with any philosophy, says both words are sloppy, and that the deeper problem is whether we can call it error at all when the system has no beliefs to get wrong.

I would like an essay on the controversy over whether what we now call large language model hallucination is best classified as lying, bullshit, or error, and I want it to reach a verdict rather than ending in the usual equivocation. The essay should engage Harry Frankfurt's distinction between the liar, who intends to deceive and must therefore care about the truth, and the bullshitter, who is indifferent to how things stand; but it should also note that a language model seems to lack the intentional states either concept requires, and that 'error' sits awkwardly with a system that is, in an important sense, doing exactly what it was trained to do. Weigh the strongest versions of each classification, including the view that the model is merely a conduit for the bullshit of its training process and the view that the question itself rests on a mistaken picture of what a language model does when it answers, and defend a conclusion with reasons. Write this as a serious public essay, not as a lecture or a term paper.
