Put on your philosopher-of-cognitive-science hat — someone who actually knows the skill aquisition and transfer-of-training research — and start by brainstorming the commitments in a few bullets before giving the cleaned-up version. I want a reconstruction, not a reading list.

I've been in rationalist forums and meetups for years, and there's this whole practice of treating rationality like a martial art you drill: weekly calibration games where you give 80% confidence intervals on trivia and check your hit rate, Fermi estimate contests, exercises for catching your own motivated reasoning, precommittment and Murphyjitsu-style planning drills where you pre-imagine your plan failing and patch it. All of it happens on safe, low-stakes toy problems, under the stated assumption that it makes you better at the real stuff later — career calls, relationships, AI-risk reasoning. The vibe is that the katas transfer across domains.

What does that practice actually presuppose about the mind and rationality? Some candidate commitments: rationality is a trainable skill rather than a fixed trait; the bottleneck is habits and techniques rather than domain knowledge; the skill is domain-general enough that calibrating on trivia transfers to deciding whether to quit a job; explicit procedures can be repeated until they become automatic. Which of these are genuinely load-bearing — the practice would be incoherent without them — and which are just decorative? Is the practice compatible with a mostly domain-specific view of expertise, or does it secretly need strong near-to-far transfer? And if the transfer effects turned out to bottom out at the drills themselves (you get better at the toy tasks but not at real decisions), what would have to change about the whole rationality-training project — would it collapse into entertainment, or is there a defensible version that survives?
