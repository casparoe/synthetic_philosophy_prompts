I work on a machine translation product, and a colleague has an argument that our quality-evaluation work is conceptually confused. It runs:

(1) A translation is faithful only if it preserves everything that matters in the source: denotation, connotation, register, rhythm, ambiguity, idiom, allusion.
(2) No translation between two natural languages can preserve all of that at once; something is always lost or shifted.
(3) Therefore no translation is faithful.
(4) But if no translation is faithful, then there is no distinction in kind between a careful translation and a loose paraphrase: both are unfaithful, and they differ only in which losses they hide and how much they hide.
(5) So a "standard of fidelity" is not a real standard, just a summary of one reader's preferences; training or evaluating our model for faithfulness is misguided.

I don't believe the conclusion, but I can't find the bad step, and several of the premises look right to me. Where exactly does this go wrong, if it does? Which premise or inference should I reject, and what do I commit to if I reject it? Be committed: pick a definite diagnosis and defend it, don't just lay out possibilities.
