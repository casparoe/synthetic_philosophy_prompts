I have spent the last decade as a planning commissioner in a mid-sized city, and I have noticed a division of labor that no one on the commission seems willing to defend but that everyone nevertheless enforces. When a zoning amendment, a bus-route change, or the siting of public housing comes before us, we treat the decision as political through and through: we hold hearings, we ask who gains and who loses, and we regard the distribution of costs and benefits as the whole point of the exercise. But when a decision reaches us in the costume of a technical specification, we stop asking those questions. A highway overpass is approved with a clearance height that will exclude municipal buses — and therefore, in practice, many of the people who depend on them — from that corridor. A benefits-application portal is adopted with a default path that steers claimants toward the fastest disposition rather than the one most likely to result in an award. A school-assignment algorithm is selected because it optimizes a quantity that no one on the commission has actually chosen as the goal. We scrutinize the first kind of decision as an exercise of power and the second as a neutral choice of means.

I want to know whether anything actually justifies that asymmetry. Give me the strongest principle you can construct that either vindicates treating technical specifications as apolitical while subjecting laws and policies to distributive scrutiny, or shows that the distinction should be given up. Test the principle against the overpass, the benefits portal, and one further case of your own choosing, preferably one that strains it. Then tell me whether the principle survives, where it breaks, and what an honest planning process would do differently once the verdict is in.
