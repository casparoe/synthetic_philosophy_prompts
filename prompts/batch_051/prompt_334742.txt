I keep getting into arguments with a colleague about Chesterton's fence, and I want to check whether I'm misreading it. The way I present it to undergrads, the principle is roughly: if you don't see the point of some existing rule or institution, you have no business tearing it down until you can say what it was for. That makes it sound like a snappy digest of Burke — prejudice as inherited wisdom, institutions as storehouses of unspoken reasons, all that. But heres what bugs me. Surely the principle has no stopping point. For any institution whatever, I can dream up some possible hidden function it might be serving, some reason it might once have made sense. So a clever defender of the status quo can always answer a reformer with "you just haven't found the fence's purpose yet," no matter how much evidence piles up that the thing is pointless or harmful. Doesn't Chesterton's principle therefore prove too much, and turn into an unfalsifiable argument against reform? Or is the principle supposed to be weaker than my colleague and I have been taking it to be?
