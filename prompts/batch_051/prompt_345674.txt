Historians of interwar Germany have long disputed how much weight to place on Hitler himself as against the structural conditions in which he operated, a dispute often cast as one between 'Great Man' accounts and structural explanations that stress impersonal forces. I should like you to treat the period from roughly 1929 to the outbreak of the war in 1939 as a case study in that dispute, but not as a survey of causes. Take the counterfactual seriously: construct the nearest possible world in which Adolf Hitler does not exist, while the Treaty of Versailles, the hyperinflation of the early 1920s, the Great Depression, the Weimar constitutional order, the radical nationalist and antisemitic movements, and the conservative elites' fear of the organised left are all held fixed. Does something recognisably like Nazi Germany still come about — a far-right dictatorship, a revisionist and expansionist foreign policy, a general European war, and a policy of systematic mass killing — or do the outcomes narrow, delay, or differ in kind once Hitler is removed? Be explicit about the replacement scenario you are envisaging, since 'no Hitler' could mean no comparable leader, a rival Nazi leader, or a stronger conservative-authoritarian alternative, and the verdict may shift accordingly.

Then run the analysis in both directions. Say precisely what claim the Great Man theorist must be making if it is to be non-trivial — not the banal claim that individuals matter at all, but something like the claim that Hitler was a necessary or disproportionately decisive cause of the distinctive outcome. It may help to distinguish three theses: that Hitler was a but-for necessary cause of Nazi Germany; that he was an INUS condition, an insufficient but necessary part of an unnecessary but sufficient set of conditions; and that the structural conditions were themselves sufficient, making Hitler causally redundant. Work through the stages at which individual agency might have made the difference — the Nazi electoral breakthrough, Hindenburg's decision to appoint Hitler, the dismantling of legal constraints in 1933–34, rearmament, the decision for war, and the turn to genocide — and at each stage say what the structural account explains, what it leaves out, and where the Great Man account has to fall back on structural enabling conditions. Finally, assess what the episode shows about the two theories: does it vindicate structural explanation, support a limited Great Man claim, or expose a problem with the way the debate itself is framed? I am less interested in a balanced 'it was a bit of both' conclusion than in a disciplined weighting of the contributions, and in an honest statement of where the counterfactual method itself runs out of evidence.
