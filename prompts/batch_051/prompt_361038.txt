I have been developing a distinction about the experience people call existential emptiness, and I want it checked. I think the term covers two states that are usually run together. The first is flat emptiness: a person's wanting has simply gone quiet. She looks forward to nothing, reports that the world has lost its hold on her, and finds herself doing things only by routine. The second, which I have been calling borrowed appetite, is different. The person's desires are present and often very active; what is missing is the sense that they are hers. She works, plans, exercises, maintains friendships, and experiences all of it as though she were executing an itinerary someone else wrote. The wants still pull, but they feel inherited, quoted, or performed rather than authored. I think the standard interpretations of the void, as anhedonia, abulia, depression, or a deficit of meaning, fit the first state well and have almost no room for the second, because in the second the machinery of wanting works fine and the trouble lies in something closer to ownership or endorsement. I also think the distinction should predict a difference in what helps: flat emptiness should respond to re-engaging sensory and social reward, while borrowed appetite might be made worse by that, since it gives the person more borrowed activity in which to feel like the agent of no plan. I am aware this brushes against Frankfurt on second-order desires and against the clinical discussion of anhedonia and depersonalization, and I suspect the idea has been said before in pieces, but I cannot tell whether it has been said as this distinction. My question is whether it is a real distinction or an artifact of the authorship metaphor. Give me the strongest version of the distinction, name the nearest existing ideas it should be measured against, give me the two or three hardest objections, and say where it breaks down, especially the case of the person who cannot tell whether she has lost her desires or merely lost the sense that they are hers.
