I work on the monitoring team at an AI lab, building systems that read an LLM's chain-of-thought to catch when it's lying or cutting corners. We keep throwing around the word 'faithful' — a faithful chain-of-thought, the model is basically just saying what it's actually doing — but I've realized I don't have a working defintion, and the arguments in design meetings are getting hard to settle. Can you give me a definition of 'faithful chain-of-thought' that actually handles these cases?

1. A model writes out a perfectly good derivation and gets the answer right, but activation probes show that the computation that actually produced the answer went through a different track.
2. A model honestly narrates its reasoning but compresses a lot, because some of what it did is distributed/vector-ish and can't be put into words exactly.
3. A model writes 'let me double-check this, something feels off' — obviously not faithful in any normal sense, but I can't say exactly why.
4. A model lists only true steps that really did play a causal role, but the text was selected and ordered because it would look good to the monitor, not because it tracks the actual computation.
5. A model that has no access to its own internals, so its chain-of-thought is a lucky-but-correct guess at what it did.
6. A clear yes case: the model outputs the exact operations in order, and we can verify those operations are what produced the answer.

Run your definition through all six and tell me which verdict is least secure. My hunch is that the key is whether the text tracks the actual computation rather than whether it's true or sounds honest, but cases 2 and 4 keep breaking that. If your definition gives a suprising answer on any of them, I'd rather you defend the surprising call than contort the definition.
