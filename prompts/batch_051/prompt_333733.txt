I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm writing a book set around the early Society for Psychical Research — the Sidgwick era, séances with sealed questions, mediums in trance states, the works. I keep getting stuck on what those investigators were assuming.

The practice, as I understand it: they were not rubes. A sitter would write a question and seal it so the medium never saw it; sometimes the sitter wasn't even in the room. They kept careful records, they knew how cold reading worked, and they threw out anything an ordinary explanation could cover. From what I can tell, Sidgwick ran it as a serious empirical question — do we survive? — and had a philosophical stake in the answer, since a yes would change what ethics is allowed to promise. And as far as I can tell he went to his death without ever calling the survival evidence settled. Fine.

Here's what nags me. When a medium in a trance produced a string of private facts — the dead sister's nickname, the wallpaper in a room only the sitter knew — these exacting people treated it as evidence that a particular dead person was communicating. Not proof, but evidence. And what made it better evidence was: the facts were known only to the sitter, the medium had no normal way to learn them, and the message sounded like how the dead person talked. So my question is what this practice presupposes about what a person is, and about what would show that a person has survived.

Specifically: does it presuppose memory-plus-character as the criterion of identity — that if you get the private memories and the verbal tics, it's the same person rather than a copy or something reading a cosmic filing cabinet? Does it presuppose that first-person testimony, a trance voice claiming to be someone, can count as evidence for a metaphysical claim at all? Or is the method two different epistemologies bolted together — hard-nosed controls for fraud but a much looser standard for identity? I can also see a reading where its actually consistent: the controls show that information arrived through some unknown channel, and the identity claim is a separate, theory-laden step the controls never touched. What I want is a clear account of which theory of evidence and which theory of surviving death the method commits them to, if any — and where the protocols are underdetermined, so more than one theory would license the same séance rules. I care less whether any medium was genuine than what the investigators would have to believe for their own methods to make sense.
