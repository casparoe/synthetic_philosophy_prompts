I am a novelist, and I am at work on a novel whose two plotlines turn on the same moral difficulty. In the first, a translator acquires an unpublished typescript, finished but never submitted, from the estate of a writer who left no will and no discoverable heirs. In the second, a programmer discovers that a small piece of software she has long relied upon has been abandoned: its sole author disappeared years ago, no license was attached to the code, and every effort to trace him has failed.

In the course of researching these situations, a friend with a background in property law offered me the following argument. Step by step it strikes me as reasonable, and yet I cannot accept its conclusion:

1. To own something is, among other things, to hold a claim over it that does not lapse merely because the owner neglects it or because another person could make better use of it.
2. A literary manuscript and a body of source code are not disqualified from being owned in this sense merely because they are intangible or reproducible.
3. It follows that an author's or programmer's death or disappearance, when it leaves no one who can be found to speak for the work, does not by itself put the work outside all ownership: the work remains claimed property.
4. If the work remains claimed property, then publishing the manuscript, or redistributing and modifying the code, without the owner's permission runs contrary to the owner's claim, regardless of how much good it does.
5. A bicycle left for decades in a locked yard by an owner who has gone abroad and cannot be found is not up for grabs; to take it would be theft, even though the owner is absent and the bicycle is wasting away.
6. By parity of reasoning, the translator who publishes the manuscript and the programmer who repairs and re-releases the code are doing the moral equivalent of taking the bicycle.
7. But that conclusion seems to me plainly false. Rescuing a lost work from oblivion is not morally on a par with stealing a bicycle.

Where does this reasoning go wrong, if it does? If forced to choose, I would give up a narrower version of the bicycle analogy before I gave up the last step, but I do not see how to do that without conceding that an owner's claim evaporates exactly when its absence would be most useful to everyone else. I would be grateful for a precise diagnosis and for an honest account of what each available repair would cost. Please answer in American English.
