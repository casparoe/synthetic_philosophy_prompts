I'm a retired physicist, and for the last few years I've been tutoring first-year university students in mechanics. Before that I spent my whole career in a lab, so I never thought much about how people learn the subject; now I'm seeing it up close. The thing that keeps striking me is that my students' errors are not random. A student who insists that a ball thrown straight up has a "force from the throw" acting on it the whole way up will, a month later, tell me that a ball rolling off the edge of a table should drop straight down, beucase nothing is pushing it sideways anymore. When I show the demonstration, she doesn't abandon the idea; she patches it over — "well, maybe it keeps a little of the original force for a while." The same underlying commitment keeps generating new wrong predictions, and she uses it to explain things, not just to answer test items.

I've been reading philosophy of science lately (Kuhn, Lakatos, the usual), and I can't shake the feeling that these errors look like little research programmes: a hard core, a belt of auxiliary patches, resistance to refutation, the works. My question is whether the education or learning-sciences people have an established name for this specific phenomenon — not for "a wrong answer," but for the fact that many students' wrong answers hang together as stable, generative, quasi-theoretical systems. I've heard the word "misconception," but that sounds like a one-off false fact, whereas I'm pointing at something more organised. Is there a term that picks out exactly the theory-like kind of misconception, and how is it distinguished from a mere error, a slip, or a gap in knowledge? If the established terms don't quite fit what I've described, tell me which ones are closest, where the edges are, and what would count as a borderline case. Please keep the reply under 500 words.
