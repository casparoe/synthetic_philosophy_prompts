I am a professor of legal philosophy, and for several years I have been moderating a jurisprudence workshop in which two colleagues argue past one another about the Hart–Fuller debate between legal positivism and natural law. One holds a roughly Hartian position: the existence and content of law are fixed by social facts about the practices and rules officials actually accept, so a norm's legal validity is one question and its moral merits are another, and a wicked or procedurally defective enactment may be valid law even if it ought not to be obeyed. The other holds a roughly Fullerian position: law is the purposive enterprise of subjecting human conduct to the governance of rules, and it is answerable to an internal morality of legality — its rules should be general, public, prospective, clear, mutually consistent, possible to comply with, stable over time, and genuinely congruent with official conduct. On this view, a regime whose norms systematically fail those conditions does not merely produce bad law; it ceases to govern through law at all, and its edicts cannot claim the fidelity that law properly demands.

The two have agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and I would like you to draft the document they should sign. The focal question is this: what is the status of a norm that passes a system's accepted criteria of validity while flouting several of the procedural conditions just rehearsed? Structure the deliverable in three parts. First, a numbered list of claims both can sign, covering at least the social character of law, law's guidance function, the difference between procedural and substantive defect, and the point at which governing by rules shades into arbitrary command. Second, a disagreements table, each row stating a residual disagreement with each camp's view phrased in wording that camp would accept; include at least one row concerning a secret retroactive decree and one concerning a procedurally impeccable but morally wicked statute. Third, a section on cruxes: the sub-questions whose settlement would move at least one side. I suspect the genuine cruxes include whether the internal morality of legality is truly a morality rather than a set of mere efficacy conditions, and whether 'law' in jurisprudence picks out a descriptive kind or a concept whose focal sense includes an ideal. For each crux, supply a concrete hypothetical or argument that both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction about what it shows. Finish by saying which parts of the dispute are merely verbal and which are substantive, and what each side would need to see to revise its own view.
