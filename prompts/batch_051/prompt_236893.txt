Augustine's position on lying is usually summarized as an absolute prohibition: every lie is a sin, and no benefit of the outcome cancels the sin. Yet he was not a leveller—he recognized that some lies are far graver than others and ordered them chiefly by the harm they do and the good they aim at, with lies about matters of faith at one extreme and, near the other, lies told to spare someone from harm. Suppose Augustine were asked to evaluate three modern practices that lie outside his own historical frame: (1) a physician who gives a patient an inert placebo while describing it as an established drug, on the theory that the deception is part of the treatment; (2) an undercover investigator who maintains an elaborate false identity over many months in order to win the trust of a dangerous organization; and (3) a caregiver who tells a woman with advanced dementia that her husband, who in fact died years ago, is simply at work, because each truthful correction would force her to grieve the death anew. For each practice, supply the most defensible Augustinian judgment. Does it count as a lie in his sense at all, or might he class it as something short of lying? If it is a lie, where would it fall in his ordering of graver and lesser lies, and what exactly determines that placement? Finally, given his absolute prohibition, does he still have the resources to treat any of these as a sin that charity might excuse, as distinct from a sin that the agent should have refused? Where his stated principles do not settle the case, say precisely which further Augustinian commitments would have to be fixed in order to settle it.
