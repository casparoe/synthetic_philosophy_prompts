I'm in a course on how moral practices change, and the instructor walked us through a popular essay that offers a sweeping explanation of why institutions like chattel slavery and dueling collapsed. The essay's thesis, as it came through in lecture, is that these practices did not end because better moral arguments finally persuaded anyone. They ended when the people whose cooperation sustained them found it in their interests, or demanded by their honor, to abandon them. On the British abolition of slavery, the essay invokes what it calls the economic thesis: abolition succeeded not because abolitionists finally made the moral case stick, but because the Caribbean sugar colonies had lost much of their profitability, and the humanitarian campaign was essentially the public-relations face of a shift that economic self-interest had already made likely. On dueling, the essay claims it disappeared not because anyone demonstrated that it was irrational or unjust, but because the rising commercial and professional classes redefined honor as creditworthiness and self-command, so that a man who fought a duel lost more standing than one who declined. The essay's overall conclusion is that in moral revolutions, moral argument is largely downstream of interests and honor: people first embrace the new norm for non-moral reasons, and only afterward do moral arguments arrive to make the outcome look principled.

This strikes me as illuminating but suspiciously cynical, and I cannot tell which parts are well supported and which are overstatements. I would like an accuracy audit of this summary. Specifically, which elements would a careful defender of the interests-and-honor account accept? Which would they reject or heavily qualify? And does the conclusion that moral argument is essentially a post hoc story actually follow from the more careful version of the view? I am especially unsure about three points: (a) whether the economic thesis is intended to show that the abolitionists were insincere, or only that their sincerity was not the decisive causal factor; (b) whether honor is best understood as an alternative to moral argument, or as the channel through which moral argument can move people; and (c) whether saying that arguments are downstream is a fair rendering of the view, or a caricature of it. Please write your answer in American English.
