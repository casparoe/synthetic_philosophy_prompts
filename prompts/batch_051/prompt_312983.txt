Last seminar we read Mill's The Subjection of Women and one guy kept answering every one of Mill's examples with "that's just natural"—women doing most of the childcare is natural, the old division of labor is natural, men making the laws for most of history was natural. He used it as if it just settled things, and when I said Mill spends most of the first chapter trying to untangle exactly this move he shrugged and said the phrase still mean something. So I want to bring a one-page handout next week that dissects the slogan "it's natural" (and its relatives "unnatural", "against nature", "you can't fight nature" when they show up in this debate). For each distinct claim the slogan could be making, I want: the precise thesis, whether it's trivial, substantive and plausible, substantive but false, or mostly a rhetorical smokescreen, and whether it actually gets you from "this is how things have always been / this is rooted in biology / this happens in most societies" to "this arrangement should be kept" or "Mill's criticism of it fails." Flag which readings are the ones Mill seems to be attacking in chapter one—where custom gets mistaken for nature and "unnatural" mostly means unfamiliar or uncustomary—and which readings he'd probably accept but say don't do the work people want them to. Aim for six or seven sharp distinctions rather than an exhaustive list, prioritized by what people actually lean on in the gendered-division-of-labor argument, and if two readings can't be told apart in practice say so.
