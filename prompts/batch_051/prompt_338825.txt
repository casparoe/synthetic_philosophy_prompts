After a working life in the physical sciences, where a procedure that could not be written down was usually a suspicion rather than a result, I have recently begun to read moral philosophy with some care. I chair the awards panel of a small charitable trust in our town. The founding deed instructs us to give public recognition and a modest sum to acts that go beyond the call of duty, but it does not define the phrase. We meet four times a year for a single afternoon; the other members are thoughtful lay people, not philosophers, and our recommendations are in practice decisive, since the trustees defer to the panel's reasoning. We have discovered that we do not share a stable test. Some of us hear the phrase as meaning very good but not obligatory; some as meaning involving real sacrifice or risk; some as meaning the sort of act one would praise but never demand. These standards come apart, and we have spent entire meetings unable to agree on whether a case deserves a civic commendation, is merely admirable, or is something we should think twice about publicly encouraging.

I have been reading the literature on supererogation — acts that are morally good without being morally required — and I would like to turn that idea into a decision procedure the panel can run in under an hour per case. Please write it as a sequence of questions we ask in order, with a note on what counts as a sufficient answer to each; it should say where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than classification. I want the procedure to keep the philosophical content — the distinctions the concept actually needs — while remaining usable by someone who has not read the theory. It needs to handle at least these hard cases: an act that is required by someone's role but would be supererogatory for a stranger; an act that is genuinely beyond duty but that we ought not to hold up for public imitation because of the risk it normalises; an act performed from mixed or self-regarding motives; and the difference between judging the act itself and judging the public act of praising it.

Run the procedure on three cases: a stranger who donates a kidney to a non-relative; a teacher who works through the summer without pay to prepare a new curriculum; and a passer-by who enters a burning building to rescue a child. I expect at least one of these to come out differently from the way most of the panel would instinctively vote, and I would rather see that than be flattered. End by saying what the procedure cannot settle, and where two reasonable panels with different background values would diverge.
