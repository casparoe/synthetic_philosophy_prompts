Something I've been chewing on as part of my dissertation (law and philosophy) and would love a careful take on. Under US-style corporate criminal liability, a firm can be convicted on the strength of what a fairly junior employee did, as long as the person was acting within their job and at least partly aiming to benefit the company. The fine comes out of the corporate treasury, so it mostly lands on shareholders, while the human beings who actually made the decision often get nothing, or a much smaller penatly. Meanwhile, in parts of civil law we seem to treat the organization as a single epistemic agent: different employees' bits of knowledge get pooled together and attributed to "the company," and the firm can be found to have intended something that no individual intended. I'm trying to reconstruct what theory of corporate agency and blameworthiness this pattern actually presupposses, if any. Does the criminal side commit us to saying the corporation is a genuinely blameworthy agent over and above the people inside it, or is it just cost-shifting dressed up as blame? Do the criminal and civil sides presuppose the same picture, or two imcompatible ones? And if you took a hard individualist line — only flesh-and-blood people are blameworthy agents — what exactly would have to change in the practice, and what would we have to say the surviving bits are really doing instead? Push back if I've got the law wrong or if "what does this practice presuppose" is the wrong question to ask here; I'd rather you say the question is ill-posed than pad out a tidy answer. And keep it under 450 words, please — verdict first.
