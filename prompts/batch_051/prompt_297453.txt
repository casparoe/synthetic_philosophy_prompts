I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, and I mostly get this from blog posts, so I might have Kuhn wrong. The way I understand his argument about scientific revolutions is something like:

(1) During normal science, a shared paradigm includes shared exemplars and standards, so observations can settle disputes more or less cleanly.

(2) During a revolution, the two competing paradigms don't fully share standards or even observations: what counts as a decisive result, and how much simplicity or accuracy matters, is partly paradigm-specific.

(3) So at the revolutionary moment there is no neutral, shared decision procedure—no algorithm—that both sides are obliged to accept for choosing between paradigms.

(4) Therefore revolutionary theory choice isn't rational in the clean Popperian way where the data simply falsifies the old theory.

Now here's the development I can't stop thinking about. In my day job, lots of empirical work is settled by fixed held-out benchmarks. Two teams with very different approaches can submit to the same eval: same test set, same metric, same labels, agreed in advance. The teams don't need to share a theory, and in principal neither side can just wave off the benchmark as theory-laden; if they do, they have to point to exactly how. But that's the thing Kuhn said was missing during revolutions—a neutral arbiter both sides have to accept.

So does Kuhn's argument survive this? Which premise is actually under pressure: the theory-ladenness of observation, or the claim that standards are paradigm-relative? Or does a repaired version say something weaker but still true—like, the neutral part only covers predictive accuracy on benchmarks, and everything else still goes the way Kuhn described? If the argument survives, show why benchmarks are really just paradigm-internal tools wearing a neutral costume; if it doesn't, say what the remaining problem should be called.

Please seperate what's settled in Kuhn scholarship from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
