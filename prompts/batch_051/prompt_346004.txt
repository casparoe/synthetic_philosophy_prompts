For the past year I have been trying to consolidate my views about cognitive outsourcing — calculators, maps, search, language models — and I keep finding myself committed to claims that look individually plausible but that I suspect may not form a coherent package. I am circulating a short statement of them to a workshop on epistemic agency, and I would like a consistency audit before I send it around. Here are the commitments as I currently hold them:

(a) Using an external cognitive artifact is not in itself a failure of understanding. A student who uses a calculator can still understand square roots, and a navigator who consults charts and instruments can still know the coastline.

(b) Understanding a body of material differs from merely knowing truths about it: it requires grasping how the elements depend on one another — for example, why a conclusion fails when a premise is weakened, or why a model breaks down when a parameter moves outside its range.

(c) Testimony transmits knowledge but not understanding. If a colleague tells me the answer and vouches for it, I can come to know the answer without thereby becoming able to see why it is the answer.

(d) When I use a language model in my own work — for instance, to reconstruct the steps of a difficult argument or to propose an interpretation of a text — I usually cannot articulate the internal basis on which it produced the output. I do not think this matters, because I subject the output to local checks: validity, consistency with the text, robustness under small variations. What survives those checks I treat as the product of an extended cognitive mechanism rather than as testimony from an informant.

(e) Even so, I would not say that a student who asks such a system for an explanation of, say, Quine's critique of the analytic-synthetic distinction, and who can fluently restate that explanation without having worked through any of the moves, understands the dialectic. The student has a reliable report, not a grasp of the argument.

(f) I also do not require that every link in an intellectual chain be executed consciously by the person who is said to understand. Much of our own cognition is subpersonal and opaque to us, and I take it that I can understand a theorem without introspecting every neural event that produced my grasp of it.

My question is whether all of this hangs together. I would like an audit rather than a lecture: identify which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension and which only look that way, order the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what the least costly revision would be, which commitment or reading of a commitment would have to give, and what collateral damage the revision would do.
