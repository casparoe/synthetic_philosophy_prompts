I'm a control systems engineer by training, not a philosopher: I spend my time with plant models, actuators, sensors, state-space and transfer-function representations, feedback loops, exogenous reference and dither signals, closed-loop identification, and the usual worries about biased estimates when your excitation is correlated with the dynamics you're trying to identify. I've been reading James Woodward's interventionist account of causation, and it keeps looking to me like a restatement of what I already do when I decide where the "system" ends and the "test input" begins—but I suspect that may be exactly the kind of flattening that misses the point. I'd like a translation and an honest residue report.

Here's the position as I understand it. X causes Y, in the relevant sense, iff there is a possible intervention on X—an idealized, surgical manipulation that sets X's value independently of X's normal causes, does not affect Y except through X, and is not correlated with other causes of Y except through X—under which Y would change; and the relation is a serious causal/explanatory one to the extent that it remains invariant across a range of such interventions. The familiar circularity worry is that "intervention" is itself spelled out in causal terms (the intervention must not be caused by the system's other variables, must not influence Y through off-path routes, and so on), so the account cannot be a reductive definition of "cause." Woodward's reply, as I've understood it secondhand, is that the circularity is benign because interventions are being used as a methodological or explanatory notion, not as the bottom-floor definition of causation.

Restate this—the intervention condition, the invariance requirement, the circularity objection, and the standard reply—in the vocabulary I actually use: plant and model, actuator, sensor, noise and disturbances, open-loop versus closed-loop, excitation and dither, identifiability, exogeneity of a test input, and wherever one draws the boundary between "the system" and "the probe." Then give me the residue report: which parts translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which parts of the philosophical debate have no counterpart in how control engineers think, or which control-theoretic concerns have no counterpart in the philosophy. In particular, I want to know whether the circularity worry is tracking something real about causal inference, or whether it dissolves once "intervention" is treated the way an engineer treats an exogenous test input—as a modeling convention rather than something that itself needs a non-circular definition. Is my instinct that the whole issue is just where you draw the system boundary right, or is it missing something?
