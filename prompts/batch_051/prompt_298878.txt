I am a retired biochemist, recently turned to the philosophy of medicine, and I now convene an evening seminar for continuing-education students. I am marking the seminar's first assessed exercise and would be grateful for an independent assessment of one candidate's answer. I am less interested in an overall impression than in a precise, point-by-point account of what the answer gets right and where it goes wrong; please award a mark out of 100 and supply the comments that justify it.

Exercise

Scenario. The AMETHYST trial would randomise adults with a progressive autoimmune kidney condition between two treatments. Arm A is an inexpensive, widely used off-label oral immunosuppressant that most specialists currently prescribe. Arm B is a recently repurposed monoclonal antibody; uncontrolled phase II studies suggest that it may be substantially more effective, but no randomised comparison of the two arms has been performed. Within the relevant specialist community, opinion is genuinely divided: roughly seven in ten practitioners would use Arm A in their own practice, while a substantial and well-credentialed minority regard Arm B as probably superior on the available evidence. Dr V, an investigator at one participating centre, is personally convinced that Arm B is superior and wishes to enrol her own eligible patients.

Question: Assess whether the AMETHYST trial satisfies the requirement of clinical equipoise, and whether Dr V may enrol her own patients. Defend your assessment.

Candidate's answer

The doctrine of clinical equipoise, as introduced by Freedman, holds that a randomised trial is ethically acceptable only when the relevant community of expert clinicians is genuinely uncertain about the comparative merits of the treatments being tested. It is meant to replace theoretical equipoise, on which a trial would be permissible only while the evidence for the two arms remained exactly balanced. Freedman's insight is that such an exact balance is too fragile to do useful work; clinical equipoise instead locates the needed uncertainty at the level of the profession, so that individual practitioners may hold preferences without thereby invalidating a trial. On that reading, Dr V's conviction that Arm B is superior would not by itself bar her participation.

Even so, I do not think AMETHYST meets the standard. Clinical equipoise requires that the evidence base be evenly balanced between the arms. Here the uncontrolled phase II results supply positive, unidirectional evidence for Arm B, so the evidence is no longer evenly balanced, and equipoise cannot be claimed. Moreover, the community is divided roughly seventy to thirty in favour of Arm A. A split that lopsided cannot count as genuine uncertainty: once a clear majority of practitioners favours one treatment, the opposing minority is best regarded as a fringe view rather than an honest controversy. I therefore conclude that AMETHYST fails the requirement of clinical equipoise; consequently, Dr V may not enrol her own patients. If the trial did satisfy equipoise, her private preference would not be decisive, but on the facts as I have described them it does not.
