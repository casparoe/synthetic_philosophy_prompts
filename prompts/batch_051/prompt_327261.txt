I spent my career as an ecologist working on invasive species—why some introduced species take over and others just fizzle out. Since retiring I've been reading political philosophy and comparative political economy, and I cant shake how much the Nordic-model export debate sounds like the transplant arguments I used to sit through: everybody fights about the species (the policy) and nobodys checking the soil, the competitors, the mutualists. Last year I got asked to join a citizens commission in a midsized American county. We review proposals that mostly amount to "let's import a piece of the Nordic model." We see maybe six or eight a year, from the county executive, from advocacy groups, from the chamber of commerce. We have one part-time policy staffer who can pull basic data, maybe two weeks per proposal, and we send a recommendation up to the county council, which does the final vote. I want something better than my hunches.

The version of the exportability question thats stuck with me is this. The Nordic outcomes—high taxes that don't produce tax revolts, generous universal benefits, pockets of trust-based regulation—are not caused by any single policy. They look more like an ecosystem: broad tax bases, universal services the middle class also uses, low corruption, relatively compressed pre-tax wages through collective bargaining, and a deep stock of generalized trust, each one holding the others up. That means transplanting one piece without the rest is like introducing a species without its mutualists: it dies, or it goes invasive. And trust is both a precondition and an output, which is the part that trips everyone up, including me.

Write me a decision procedure—a checklist in words, with branches—that the commission can actually run on any proposal that claims to import a piece of the Nordic model. It has to be usable by non-experts, including when we can't get a real number for trust or fairness, and it should be honest about where we're smuggling values in and calling it analysis.

Make sure it handles at least these things:

- A short early screen that kills clear bad fits before we burn the staffer's two weeks.
- For proposals that pass the screen, the enabling conditions that actually matter, and what counts as enough evidence for each given that we'll mostly have proxies, not measurements.
- A way to distinguish "this policy needs trust as a precondition" from "this policy could plausibly build trust as an output." That's the line our county executive likes to blur, and it's the line I most want help drawing.
- Where the procedure should stop and hand the decision to a human because the question is one of values rather than facts. I suspect means-tested vs universal is one of those, even if we all pretend it isn't.
- A note on where the procedure itself is most likely to misfire, and what to do then.

Then run it on the three proposals in front of us:

(a) Raise the county sales tax by one percentage point to fund universal pre-K and after-school care for every kid, explicitly sold as "universal, not means-tested—the Nordic way." I'd personally like this one to pass.

(b) Replace annual mandatory fire inspections for small restaurants and shops with a self-certification scheme, with random audits cut way back, on the argument that trust-based regulation is how the Nordics do it.

(c) Take over the local food bank and convert it into a universal free school lunch program in the public schools, on the argument that universal lunches reduce stigma and build public support for the schools, Nordic-style.

I'd like at least one case where the procedure tells me no even though I'd prefer a yes, and one case where the honest result is "pilot" or "this turns on a judgment call." The whole thing should be plain English and reusable—my colleagues are not philosophers, and I'll know if the branches don't actually connect.
