Many people can hold all three of the following attitudes without noticing any tension. They admire the founder who works eighty-hour weeks, sleeps at the office, and treats leisure as a weakness; they regard the 35-hour week as a hard-won advance and leisure as something a decent society protects by law; and they read about karoshi, death from overwork, as a scandal that indicts a pathological work culture. The first attitude praises a life organized around work, the second treats limits on work as a right, and the third treats the same life, when lived inside a corporation rather than a startup, as a public-health failure. The same people may invoke the Protestant work ethic to explain the first attitude and deplore its consequences in the third.

Now consider two workers whose weeks are alike in every measurable respect: hours, sleep, health risk, time away from family. The only clear difference is that the founder experiences the schedule as chosen and the employee experiences it as imposed. Is there a single principle that underwrites all three attitudes and draws the line in the right place, or are they jointly unstable? If you defend the asymmetry between admired and condemned overwork, defend it against the hard version of the case: the founder's sense of choice is itself shaped by the same work ethic that makes the employee unable to refuse, and the measurable costs are identical. I want the strongest principle you can construct, tested against nearby cases, and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned.

Where you commit to a view, say how confident you are in it, and single out the point in your argument you are least sure of.
