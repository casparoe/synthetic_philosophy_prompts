I've been going back over Searle's Chinese Room and Dennett's reply to it, the one Dennett calls the two-bitser. As I understand it, Searle says the symbols in the room only ever have derived intentionality, the way a sentence in a book does, and only brains have original intentionality. The two-bitser bites back: fine, the room's intentionality is cheap, maybe two bits' worth, but it's still real intentionality, and the kicker is that our own intentionality is also derived, from natural selection, so Searle is trying to draw a line through a middle that no longer exists.

But something struck me. The Chinese Room, as Searle sets it up, is a person executing a hand-written rulebook: symbols in, symbol lookup, symbols out. The systems that now produce fluent, relevant human text, large language models, aren't built that way at all. No one writes the rules; the system is trained by gradient descent on enormous quantities of actual human language, and the competence just falls out of the training. It seems like this development puts pressure on both sides at once. On one hand, the two-bitser looks stronger, because the model's competence isn't being borrowed from a programmer's interpretation of a rulebook; it's statistically squeezed out of the very linguistic output of creatures who do seem to have original intentionality. On the other hand, the two-bitser is committed to saying the trained system has at least a little bit of genuine intentionality, and these models are so good at producing fluent, confident nonsense that the mimicry charge can feel more, not less, plausible than in 1980.

So my question is whether Dennett's two-bitser actually survives the shift from hand-coded symbol-manipulation to gradient-trained language models. Which premise of the two-bitser is the new development really pressuring, and is there a repaired version that still does the deflationary work Dennett wanted, or does the original-vs-derived distinction sneak back in once the systems are no longer hand-programmed? Please write your answer in British English.
