Ok so I just got back from a long argument with a friend and I need somebody to help me figure out which of us was actually making the stronger point.

The argument was about whether a finite mind would eventually run out of experiences. My friend kept pushing this line: the brain only has finitely many neurons and finitely many ways to arrange them (or at least finitely many relevantly different arrangements), so there are only finitely many possible brain states. Each distinct experience just is a distinct brain state. So there are finitely many possible experiences. Live forever, and eventually you have had every experience at least once, after which you're stuck in reruns. Nothing actually new could ever happen to you again.

I kept resisting him. My move was: the world is not the brain. Even if your internal states are finite, the world can feed you an endless sequence of new inputs, so the stream of experiences could stay novel forever. The same internal state paired with a different external fact or a different history is not "the same experience." I compared it to a finite-state machine reading an infinite tape: states repeat, but the sequence of state-plus-input pairs doesn't have to. He answered that this is cheating, because an input only matters once it has changed your state; the world can surprise you only by putting you in a state you've already been in, so over an infinite life the novelty still runs out. We went in circles for hours.

I'm a systems programmer, so the vocabulary I actually think in is states, transition tables, state spaces, inputs, outputs, determinism vs nondeterminism, reachable states, eventual periodicity, observability. It's not just an analogy to me, its the native way I carve up systems. So here's what I want. Restate the argument my friend was making, plus the strongest version of the counterargument I was reaching for, in that vocabulary. Be literal about which claim maps to which machine property. Then give me a residue report: where the translation goes cleanly, where you had to bend things, and which parts of the disagreement have no counterpart in state-machine talk at all. In particular I want to know whether the whole argument reduces to whether you model the mind as a closed system or as an open system hooked to an input tape, and whether the residue means my vocabulary is too crude, or whether his argument was smuggling in assumptions about what makes two experiences "the same."
