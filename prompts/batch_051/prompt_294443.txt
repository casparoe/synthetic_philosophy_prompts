Last night my friend and I had a long, winding argument about whether the sheer amount of ambiguity in natural language is a defect or a quiet engineering triumph, and after he left I wrote down the small theory I had been gesturing at. I would like an honest assessment of it, because it feels simultaneously like a real insight and like the kind of thing an undergraduate would invent.

I call the two sides of my distinction working ambiguity and deadweight ambiguity. Working ambiguity is when a single form does several jobs, but the jobs sit in non-overlapping frames, so context resolves the intended sense almost for free and misreadings are rare or harmless: 'bank' in river bank versus financial institution, or 'run' in run a marathon, run a meeting, run a bath. Deadweight ambiguity is when the senses could plausibly collide in the same frame and the cost of a misreading is high, as with 'sanction' (permit versus penalize) or 'oversight' (supervision versus omission). My central claim is that languages are ambiguity-tolerant exactly where ambiguity is cheap, and that they tend, over time, to differentiate or mark the senses where ambiguity is dear; on this view the lexicon is a kind of economy in which ambiguity is priced, and one short, frequent form is kept because it saves memory and articulation while pushing the disambiguating work onto pragmatics. I also have an unworked-out hunch that the same logic extends to prosody, and should explain why a language with lexical pitch accent distinguishes some homophones while leaving others to context. My second, more speculative claim is that some ambiguity is preserved as a social safety valve: a speaker who could have meant one of two things can retreat to the harmless reading, so languages should retain ambiguities that smooth interaction, not only ones that are efficient. My friend kept objecting that this is just a truism dressed up in economics jargon, and he threw 'sanction' and 'oversight' at me as counterexamples.

What I want is the candid version. Is the distinction coherent, or does it collapse once you notice that whether a word is working or deadweight depends on the context of use rather than on the word itself? Does the empirical claim about languages expelling dear ambiguity survive obvious counterexamples? Which parts already exist in the literature, and under roughly what names? And is the social safety valve claim a genuine addition or a just-so story? If you could answer in British English, I would appreciate it.
