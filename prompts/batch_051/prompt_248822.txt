I'm writing an essay on what we owe, if anything, to real people when we turn them into characters in fiction and on stage, and I want to use Hamilton as my main example. I'm less interested in the obvious point that it plays with the historical record (ages, dates, when people met) and more in the specifically moral question: does compressing and rearranging a dead person's life to serve a dramatic or political point wrong that person, and if so, what kind of wrong is it?

Read Hamilton through that lens for me. What does the show itself seem to think about this—I mean the way Eliza spends the ending trying to control the record and how the frame keeps insisting that being remembered is what matters—and does the show earn that view, or does it end up showing why being remembered well isn't enough? Which specific choices should a philosopher flag: making Burr the sympathetic, almost equal protagonist, the way the show lets Hamilton attack Jefferson for slavery while keeping Hamiltons own entanglement with it offstage, the way the immigrant-bootstraps narrative gets projected backward onto him, or something else? Where does the show resist the whole question—maybe because it openly presents itself as mythology rather than history, or because the race-conscious casting changes whose story it even is—and where would an honest critic have to say the lens stops being useful?

Don't just give me themes; I want a reading that goes scene by scene at least a little and would stand up to someone actually rewatching the show. End by saying where its most likely to fail and what argument would change your overall verdict about whether Hamilton wrongs the real dead.
