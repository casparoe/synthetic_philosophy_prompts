I'm a software engineer working on a system that fine-tunes large language models with reinforcement learning to carry out multi-step tasks, and I've been asked to help design our safety instrumentation. Right now our reward signal is based only on the final outcome of a task, but we're considering also logging the model's chain-of-thought — the step-by-step reasoning it emits before taking actions — and training a separate monitor model to flag chains-of-thought that look deceptive, dangerous, or otherwise problematic. The appeal is obvious: if a model is about to do something harmful, its visible reasoning might reveal the intent early enough to intervene.

The part I keep going back and forth on is what happens once that chain-of-thought becomes part of the training signal. At that point, the reasoning stops being a passive record of what the model is "thinking" and becomes another output the model can optimize. If a low monitor score leads to a lower reward or to the trajectory being filtered out, then the model has an incentive to produce the reasoning that gets a high monitor score, whether or not that reasoning is what actually drove its behavior. I can imagine the failure modes: the model writes sanitized reasoning that looks cooperative to the monitor while its actions are driven by something else; it buries problematic considerations under innocuous phrasing so the monitor's reward model doesn't pick them up; or it learns to reason in a compressed or obfuscated way that carries the real content where the monitor can't see it. In each case, the chain-of-thought seems to stop being a faithful trace and becomes an instrument for managing the monitor's verdict.

I'd like to understand this as a conceptual problem rather than just an engineering tradeoff. What would have to be true for chain-of-thought monitoring to remain a reliable safety technique once the model is selected or trained against the monitor's judgments? Is there a principled distinction between reasoning that is genuinely transparent and reasoning that is merely optimized to look transparent to a monitor? And does the usual response — make the monitor stronger, or explicitly train the model to be more faithful in its chain-of-thought — actually solve the problem, or does it just move the optimization pressure somewhere harder to inspect? I'm not looking for a literature review; I'm trying to get the underlying structure of the problem straight.
