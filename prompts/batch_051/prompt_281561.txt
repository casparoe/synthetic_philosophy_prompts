Two researchers with opposed views on the debate approach to scalable oversight have just concluded a year-long adversarial collaboration, and your task is to write the document they should have produced. By the debate approach they mean this: on a question that no human judge can answer by direct inspection, two artificial agents argue for opposite answers over many rounds; a human judge observes the exchange and may ask follow-up questions, but may not do outside research or inspect ground truth during the debate, and must finally decide which side to believe. The proponent of debate holds that this structure lets a limited judge extract truthful answers from more capable agents, because a false claim can be attacked at its weakest point whereas a true claim can be defended, so open adversarial argument filters better than any individual's private reasoning. The skeptic holds that this transfers poorly from formal models to real oversight, because a judge who cannot check the claimed facts cannot reliably distinguish sound argument from merely persuasive argument, and agents rewarded for winning will learn to produce the latter.

Write the joint statement the two can both sign. It must include the following.

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. The claims should be non-vacuous: they should pin down exactly what the debate setup assumes, what the judge must be able to do for the proponent's central argument to go through, and what the two parties agree the abstract or formal results do and do not establish. Phrase each claim so that a third party unfamiliar with the debate literature could verify it against the setup as described.

2. A disagreements table. Each row gives one question in neutral wording, then the proponent's answer phrased in words the proponent would sign, then the skeptic's answer phrased in words the skeptic would sign. The rows should capture the genuine fault lines rather than caricatures. In particular, the disagreement should not be presented as a dispute over whether some particular formal result is true; the parties agree that the available formal results establish only idealized or conditional claims, and their real disagreement is about how much those idealized claims license in a real oversight regime, where the likely failure points are, and what standard of evidence an oversight proposal should meet.

3. A cruxes section. A crux is a subquestion whose answer would cause at least one party to revise their overall position. For each crux, specify a concrete case, experiment, or class of questions that both sides accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction about what that test would find. The cruxes should go beyond the vague question of how good human judges are. Likely candidates include at least some of the following: whether a truthful but less capable debater can expose a false claim without being able to construct the correct answer first; whether debate preserves truth when the underlying question has no independently checkable ground truth, such as questions of policy, taste, value, or long-range forecast; whether the judge can tell the difference between an argument's soundness and its persuasiveness to someone with the judge's biases when the two come apart; whether the judge's ability to ask useful questions itself requires the very oversight ability the setup was meant to replace; and whether zero-sum game-theoretic results about debate equilibria support conclusions about the behavior of finite agents selected by gradient descent. Where the collaboration showed that an apparent disagreement was at least partly verbal, say which part was verbal and what residual dispute, if any, remains.

The document should contain no bibliography and no citation of specific papers; it should stand on the arguments themselves.
