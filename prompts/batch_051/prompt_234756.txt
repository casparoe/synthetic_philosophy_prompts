I have been developing an account of vague predicates that I tentatively call the tolerance-budget view, and I would like a candid assessment of it. The proposal is this. A vague predicate never owes its vagueness to a merely unknown boundary; rather, each use of the predicate carries a finite but defeasible budget of tolerance, where tolerance means permission to treat one small incremental difference — one grain of sand, one millimetre of height, one hair — as not changing the predicate's application. In ordinary life the budget is replenished by shifts in attention or conversational purpose, which is why we can call something a heap in the morning and not be contradicted by the fact that it fails to be one over dinner. In a forced-march sorites, however, attention is deliberately held on the series, the budget is not replenished, and it eventually runs out. At that point the speaker draws a sharp line between the last heap and the first non-heap, not because a boundary has been discovered, but because the permission to keep treating the next step as insignificant has been spent. The arbitrariness is therefore genuine rather than merely epistemic, yet the tolerance that produced the sorites was genuine too.

I am aware this sounds close to contextualism and to degree theories, and perhaps to the thought that sorites reasoning exploits a tolerance principle that is false but useful. So before I write it up I want to know whether the view is more than a metaphor. Specifically: does the budget language earn its keep, or does it restate, without explaining, the fact that practical standards shift? What semantic facts, if any, would it get wrong about borderline cases, higher-order vagueness, or penumbral connections? And what would be the most awkward case for it — a forced march interrupted halfway, repeated marches with the same subject and the same stakes, or a predicate whose point is purely classificatory? Give me your honest verdict, including where the view collapses.
