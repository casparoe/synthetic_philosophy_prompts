At a family dinner last week, my uncle and his old college roommate got into an argument that I have been replaying ever since. My uncle said he had been reading about illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, the view that the felt, first-person character of experience — the redness of red, the hurt of pain — is not a real extra ingredient in the world but a trick the brain plays on itself. His roommate pushed back, and the exchange went roughly like this:

(1) My uncle: Introspection presents experience as having qualitative properties that are private, ineffable, and directly known. But introspection is just another brain process, and brain processes can systematically misrepresent. There is no redness inside my head; there are only physical states that I am wired to describe and treat as if they had redness.

(2) His roommate: That is self-defeating. An illusion is a gap between how things seem and how things are. But seeming is itself a conscious, phenomenal state. If you deny that anything ever seems any way to anyone, you have denied the very thing an illusion needs.

(3) My uncle: Not so. A conjurer does not need real magic to fool an audience, only a mechanism that makes the audience represent a false state of affairs. The brain can produce judgments like I have private, ineffable qualia without any qualia existing. The seeming is a representation, not a further private glow.

(4) His roommate: But that changes the subject. A camera can misrepresent the light without being fooled, because a camera has no point of view. If the brain merely issues false reports without any subject to be fooled, then calling it an illusion is a euphemism. What you actually hold is eliminativism, the view that consciousness does not exist at all, and you have kept the word illusion because it sounds less drastic.

They asked me to settle it, and I have gone back and forth all week. Please go through the disagreement claim by claim: for each numbered move, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, or somewhere in between, and briefly justify each verdict; then say who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both of them are missing.
