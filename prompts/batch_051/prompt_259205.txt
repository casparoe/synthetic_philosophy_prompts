In my conditionals class we've been doing Lewis's triviality result and its been bugging me all week. The theory I keep getting pushed toward is that indicative conditionals don't express propositions at all—they have no truth conditions, only assertibility conditions, and what I call "the probability of a conditional" is really just conditional credence in the consequent given the antecedent. I find this compelling because it's the cleanest way out of the proof, and it also explains why "if" sentences go weird under negation: "not: if A then C" doesn't feel like describing a state of affairs that failed to obtain.

But I also cant shake the intuition that "if it rains, the match will be cancelled" is true or false. When I say it's 90 percent likely, I don't mean I'd assert it with 90 percent confidence; I mean there's a 90 percent chance the world is the way the sentence says it is. That conviction feels non-negotiable.

Here's the proof as the prof gave it, and I can't find the slack in it. Assume conditionals are ordinary propositions and Adams's thesis holds for every rational credence P: P(A→C) = P(C|A). Assume rational credences are closed under conditionalizing, so the thesis holds for P(·|C) and P(·|¬C) too. Pick A and C with P(A∧C) > 0, P(A∧¬C) > 0, and P(C|A) ≠ P(C). Apply the thesis to P(·|C): P(A→C | C) = P(C | A∧C) = 1. Apply it to P(·|¬C): P(A→C | ¬C) = P(C | A∧¬C) = 0. Total probability gives P(A→C) = P(C). But the thesis also gives P(A→C) = P(C|A), so P(C|A) = P(C), contradiction.

I find every step unimpeachable, and the conclusion seems to be that the truth-conditional intuition is the thing that has to go. But I also find the intuition unimpeachable. I don't want someone to just pick a side. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be debunked—say, as an artifact of surface grammar or of how I imagine probabilities attaching to sentences—and what it would take for the theory to be amended instead, for instance by restricting Adams's thesis or denying that rational credences are closed under conditionalizing. Is there a reading of "probability of a conditional" on which both the proof and the intuition survive? Which of my two starting points is doing more work than I'm admitting?
