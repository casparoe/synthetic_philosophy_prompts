I teach an upper-level philosophy of mathematics course, and I'm writing the final exam. I want one essay question on the indispensibility argument and the more recent "mathematical explanation" literature — the prime-numbered life cycles of periodical cicadas, the hexagonal structure of honeycomb being optimal for minimizing wax, that kind of thing. My students can recite the basics without thinking: Quine's criterion of ontological commitment, Putnam's point that quantifying over numbers in our best science commits us to them, and the standard nominalist replies like Field's program and paraphrase strategies. What I want the question to test is whether they can seriously evaluate the *enhanced* indispensability argument: the claim that platonism gets a stronger case when mathematics does genuine explanatory work in science, not just abbreviating work. There's a live disagreement about whether the stock examples really show that, or whether the mathematics is just representing non-mathematical facts — in the cicada case, facts about predator life cycles and hybridization avoidance; in the honeycomb case, facts about physical minimization of wax and surface tension. I'd like the question to force them to take a position on whether the enhanced argument is actually an improvement over plain Quinean indispensability, and to defend that position against at least one specific objection, instead of just summarizing the literature.

Please write the question itself, a model answer of around 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that seperates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain (for example, laying out the structure of the enhanced argument, explaining why one of the examples is supposed to involve a distinctively mathematical explanation, and responding to the "the math is just descriptive" objection). It'd also be helpful if the marking scheme notes where students typically mess up — like confusing indispensability with explanation, or thinking that a mathematical claim being true and useful settles the ontological question on its own.
