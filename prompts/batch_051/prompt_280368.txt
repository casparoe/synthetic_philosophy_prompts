I'm a PhD student in philosophy of technology, working on a chapter about automation and meaningful work, and I keep coming back to a pattern I cant find a name for. I've been reading ethnographies of automated workplaces—chemical plants, long-haul driving with driver-assist, warehouses with algorithmic task allocation, glass cockpits—and the same structure keeps showing up. The automation is sold as taking over the dangerous or monotonous parts of the job, and what's left for the human is watching, monitoring, confirming. Workers describe this as boring and exhausting at the same time: they're not doing much, but they're on edge all shift. The part that puzzles me is the skill dynamic. The system absorbs all the routine cases, which were exactly the cases where people used to build and maintain an intuitive feel for the process. So the only time a human is really needed is when the system hits something it can't handle or fails outright—which is exactly when the human is most out of practice. You get a job that formally still requires high-level expertise, because in an emergency the person is the last line, but the day-to-day conditions systematically prevent them from keeping that expertise alive. Older workers describe it as the job no longer feeling like a craft; some say they stopped being operators and became supervisors of a machine, and they dont mean it as an upgrade.

My question is whether this configuration has an established name. I'm not after the broad story about automation destroying skills, and not just the claim that monitoring work is tedious. What interests me is the trap where the human is kept around for rare, high-stakes interventions that the day-to-day job no longer trains them for, and how that changes what the work means to the people doing it. If there is no exact name, I'd want a term that separates this from plain deskilling and from generic alienation, and that says where the edges are—e.g., does it only apply when failures are genuinely rare and novel, or also when the automation fails often but unpredictably? Also, if you answer, please keep clear which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on it, rather than presenting one school's view as the consensus.
