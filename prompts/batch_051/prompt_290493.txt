I'm a PhD student working on the philosophy of delusions, and I keep going back and forth on the doxasticism debate. On the one hand, I'm genuinely pulled toward the view that delusions are beliefs — just beliefs with strange contents and strange etiologies. The two-factor theory gives this view a tidy, unifying structure: factor one is an anomalous experience or a noisy prediction-error signal (say, the absence of the usual affective response to a familiar face in Capgras), and factor two is some deficit in the belief-evaluation system that would normally reject the bizarre explanation. That story preserves a lot of what I want from a theory: it explains why the person sincerely asserts the content, why it is held with conviction and resists counterevidence, and it lets me keep a single category, belief, doing the work across delusions and ordinary cognition.

But the more I read the case descriptons, the more a competing intuition digs in. The same patient who says their spouse has been replaced by an impostor will often keep living with that person, accepting meals from them, sometimes being intimate with them, and not making the downstream inferences you would expect. If the content were really a belief, shouldn't it do belief-like work — guiding action, expectation, and further reasoning? My intuition is that a state this inferentially inert is not a belief but something more like a verbal endorsement, an as-if commitment, or a circumscribed imagining. The action/inertia mismatch feels like the whole ballgame, and whenever I try to defend doxasticism someone brings it up and I feel it too.

I don't want a survey of the literature or a reading list. I want you to adjudicate. Give me the strongest version of both sides, then tell me which gives way. Could the doxasticist debunk my intuition by arguing that I am working with an overly unified picture of belief — that real beliefs are more compartmentalized, and that factor two is precisely a failure in the connections that normally let beliefs spread through action and inference? Or should the intuition push me to amend the theory, for example by treating delusions as acceptances or imaginings with a different functional role, and what exactly would that amendment cost? Is there a reading on which both survive, say by separating a state's content from its functional role? Tell me what it would actually take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, and what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes. Keep it under 700 words.
