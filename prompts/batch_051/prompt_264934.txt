I keep seeing people argue about whether death is bad for the person who dies by each picking one case and acting like it settles everything — a 20-year-old hit by a bus, an 95-year-old on a ventilator, the embryo, someone already in a PVS — and I want the full spec before the fight. So: write the desiderata, the requirements list, that any adequate theory of whether and when death is bad for the one who dies has to meet. For each requirement give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of a particular camp (Epicureans, deprivation people, desire-satisfaction types, time-relative-interest people).

Key the list to this passage, from Lucretius, De Rerum Natura, Book III, in W. E. Leonard's translation:

Look back:
Nothing to us was all fore-passed eld
Of time the eternal, ere we had a birth.
And Nature holds this like a mirror up
Of time-to-be when we are dead and gone.
And what is there so horrible appears?
Now what is there so sad about it all?
Is't not serener far than any sleep?

Use it rather than just gloss it: I want it to show up as the test for at least one desideratum (presumably the symmetry one), and I want your honest read on whether the argument there actually rules out posthumous deprivation harm or just shifts the burden of proof onto whoever says death is bad.

The list has to at least reckon with: the timing problem (when exactly does the harm obtain?), the subject problem (after death there's no subject left to bear a harm, so who or what is worse off?), the distinction between the process of dying and the state of being dead, whether killing needs a special explanation beyond "someone loses a future," why death's badness varies with age and with how good the remaining life would have been, whether death can be good for someone, and the assumption of annihilation. It also can't quietly substitute the badness of dying (pain, fear) or the badness to survivors (grief) for the thing I'm actually asking about. Add any further requirements you think belong. Flag any pairs of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — that's the part I most want. Then, briefly, score three views against the list — the Epicurean no-harm view, a flat deprivation account, and something like McMahan's time-relative interest account — and say which single requirement eliminates the most views. Keep it mostly on the criteria, not the scorecard.
