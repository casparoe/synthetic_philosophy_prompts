I'm on the committee that writes the PhD qualifying exam in philosophy of mind, and this year we want a question about illusionism about phenomenal consciousness that cannot be answered by reciting a definition or a familiar slogan. Candidates will have 90 minutes. Please draft the exam question itself, then provide a model answer of the standard you would expect from a strong candidate, and finally a marking scheme.

The question should be organized around the following crux. Illusionists hold that phenomenal consciousness — the 'what-it's-likeness' of experience, understood as a set of intrinsic, ineffable, non-physical qualitative properties — is not real, and that introspection systematically misrepresents our experiences as having such properties. A natural and serious objection is that an illusion is, by definition, a situation in which something appears to a subject to be a certain way, and appearing-a-certain-way is itself a phenomenal state. The illusionist therefore seems to presuppose the very phenomenal states she means to deny: there must be something it is like to undergo the introspective illusion, or else it is not an illusion at all. The question should require candidates to (a) develop the strongest version of this objection, (b) set out the illusionist's best reply, and (c) assess whether that reply either collapses illusionism into eliminativism about consciousness generally, or quietly concedes realism. It should push beyond slogans and force students to distinguish clearly between denying phenomenal properties and denying experience, introspection, or consciousness in every sense.

The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit points, total 20 marks, with each point assigned its marks, plus a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong. Among the things it should reward are: a precise distinction between phenomenal properties and the experiences or introspective states that illusionists accept as real; a non-phenomenal account of what introspection's 'appearing' consists in; an explanation of why a representational or functional reading of appearances does not beg the question; and an explicit ruling on the two collapse worries. No bibliographies or citations, for either the question or the model answer — everything should stand on the quality of the argument.
