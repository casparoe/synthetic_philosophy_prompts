I keep coming back to this sentence in Hayek’s The Use of Knowledge in Society: the “data” from which the economic calculus starts are never for the whole society “given” to a single mind which could work out the implications and can never be so given. I think I believe that, but I also have a batch of gut verdicts about how much trades and non-trades actually reveal, and I cant get them to hang together. Maybe you can tell me what principle, if any, captures them, and which ones I have to give up.

My verdicts:

1. If an executive sells a chunk of her own stock a week before an earnings miss, I infer she knew bad news. The sale itself does the evidential work; the price it fetched matters less.
2. If a prediction market price jumps from 40 to 80 on a candidate after a debate, I think the price now genuinely reflects a lot of dispersed information. But if the price sits at 80 on tiny volume, I mostly think two people disagreed, not that the market "knows" 80 is right.
3. If two smart traders make a trade with no public news, I infer there's private info in there somewhere, but I don't think either is irrational. I just think their models disagree, so at most one is right, and I can't tell which.
4. If a bond market freezes entirely after a stress event, no bids and no asks, I read that as information: something is wrong, even without a public announcement. But then I also think it could just be coordination failure or panicked illiquidity, not new info about fundamentals. I hold both readings at once.
5. If no one will sell insurance for some obvious risk at any reasonable price, I take that as a signal that the risk is underpriced, not as evidence there's no demand. But then the no-trade theorem worry says if both sides are rational and rationality is common knowledge, no trade should happen only when there are no gains from trade left, which sounds like the absence of trades isn't a signal at all. That can't be what I think, can it?
6. But here's one where absence feels like no signal: when my brother and his neighbor don't swap lawnmowers, I learn zero about lawnmowers. So why is the bond freeze different?
7. When two friends trade baseball cards for fun, I don't update at all about the cards. So some trades carry information and some don't, and it's not just "real market" vs not, because the low-volume prediction market feels in between.

Is there one principle, or a small set, that gets all or nearly all of these? What does the principle say about a case I keep going back and forth on: a stock where the price barely moves but volume is unusually high for days. Is that the market telling me people disagree, nobody knows anything, someone knows something, or none of the above? And which of my verdicts does the principle force me to revise or abandon? I’d rather have a clean principle that overturns two of my gut calls than an epicycle that saves all seven.
