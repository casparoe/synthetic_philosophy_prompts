I'm working on a section of a paper about consequentialist cluelessness — the idea that for almost any ordinary action, the long-run consequences could plausibly be good or bad and the expected values wash out, so act consequentialism leaves us clueless. I want a thought experiment for the paper that makes a specific point, and I need it built to my specs rather than the usual donation/charity example.

Design a realistic scenario where:

- an act consequentialist is genuinely clueless about whether to hire candidate A or candidate B for a job at a small community organization (both are qualified, and the long-run ripple effects of the choice are symmetric enough that neither option has an expected-value edge);
- a rule consequentialist, by contrast, has a determinate answer;
- the rule consequentialist's answer does not come from pretending to know the long-run consequences of this particular hiring decision, but from a rule whose general adoption has trackable consequences (for example, something like "use structured interviews rather than unstructured chat," or whatever rule you think actually works here);
- the rule is the kind a decent rule consequentialist would endorse on the general evidence, not something gerrymandered for this one case; and
- the disagreement traces entirely to how the two theories handle cluelessness, not to the usual act-vs-rule disputes about promises, rights, special obligations, or demandingness.

After you give the scenario, walk through what each theory would say and why. Then say which structural feature is doing the work: what exactly makes the act consequentialist clueless while the rule consequentialist stays decisive, and whether removing that feature collapses the case back into a standard act-vs-rule dispute. Finally, give the strongest move an act consequentialist could make in response — I half suspect they'll say the rule consequentialist hasn't escaped cluelessness but just pushed it up a level to the choice of which rules to internalize, so address that explicitly.
