You are an analytic philosopher who specializes in conceptual analysis in law and social ontology, and who is willing to defend surprising verdicts when a clear definition leads to them. Please think carefully, start by brainstorming the competing criteria and the different jobs the word does before settling on a definition, and then carry the proposal through every case below.

I want a working definition of the word marriage. The cases I need the definition to handle are these:

1. A same-sex couple who have been together for a decade, are civilly married, and are raising a child together.
2. A man and a woman in their eighties who marry for companionship; neither can have children, and both have adult children from earlier marriages.
3. Two business partners who go through a legally valid civil ceremony solely to obtain tax and inheritance advantages, with a written agreement to divorce in two years and no shared romantic or domestic life.
4. A religious wedding conducted by a community that treats the couple as married in every social and moral respect, but with no civil registration, in a jurisdiction that does not recognize common-law marriage.
5. A couple who have lived together for thirty years, merged their finances, cared for each other through serious illnesses, and are spoken of by family and friends as married, but who have never had any ceremony or civil registration.
6. A plural household of four adults who all regard themselves as married to one another; the arrangement is consensual, religiously embedded, and stable, but it is not civilly recognized.
7. Two adult siblings who share a household and finances, are jointly raising one sibling's child, and expect to care for each other until death, but who have no sexual or romantic relationship.
8. A marriage that was arranged by the couple's families and initially accepted under heavy family pressure, but that both partners now freely affirm and would choose again after twenty years.

I have rough pretheoretical verdicts, but I do not insist on them: 1 and 2 are clearly marriages; 3 is legally valid but, I want to say, not really a marriage in the full sense; 4 I would call a marriage even though a legal positivist might not; 5 leaves me genuinely torn; 6 and 7 are the cases that most unsettle my ordinary use of the word; 8 seems to me to have become a marriage even if it did not start as one. I would rather have a good definition revise these verdicts than jam them to fit.

Propose a definition, or if you conclude the word has two senses with different extensions, propose both definitions and say which cases move between them. Then run the definition through all eight cases, say clearly where it delivers the wrong answer or no determinate answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdict. I also want to know how far the definition can stay neutral about what marriage is for. Does settling it force a stand on whether the point of marriage is procreation, a special kind of exclusive union, or a freely chosen framework for mutual care and commitment? If it cannot stay neutral, say which view your definition depends on and where a defender of a rival view would most fairly push back. The deliverable is the definition itself, not a survey of debates.
