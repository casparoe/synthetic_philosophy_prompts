I'm a policy analyst in a county criminal-justice agency, and I recently sat in on a working meeting where two colleagues argued about whether we should proceed with a proposed pretrial risk-assessment instrument. The argument stayed almost entirely on what the algorithmic-fairness impossibility results imply for the project. I'm not taking a side; I'd like a careful adjudication of their argument, because each of them seems to me to score some real points.

The shared technical background they both accepted: a risk score is well-calibrated when, among everyone assigned a given score, the observed rate of the predicted outcome (say, re-arrest within two years) is roughly the same across groups. A score equalizes error rates across groups when its high-risk labels have the same false-positive rate (flagging people who would not have re-offended) and the same false-negative rate (failing to flag people who would have) across groups. The impossibility results they were arguing about hold, roughly, that a single non-trivial score cannot satisfy calibration, false-positive-rate parity, and false-negative-rate parity all at once when the groups have different base rates; forcing one property can drive the others apart.

Here is the back-and-forth as I recorded it:

1. Marcus: The impossibility results are decisive against the project. The vendor keeps saying the score can be made fair, but no single score can be calibrated and satisfy both error-rate parities at once here, because re-arrest rates differ across groups. So making the algorithm fair isn't a design requirement; it's a contradiction. Deploying an instrument that, as a matter of theorem, must violate at least one widely accepted fairness condition no matter how we set the threshold would be dishonest.

2. Priya: That's a category mistake. The impossibility results are about the joint satisfaction of a few statistical properties, not about fairness full stop. Calibration and error-rate parity are diagnostics, not moral principles. A single number cannot be expected to encode every distributive ideal at once. We don't refuse to make any allocation decision just because no single allocation rule can satisfy every desideratum simultaneously. We pick the property that matters for the decision at hand — here, arguably calibration, because a judge should know what a given risk score means — and we accept that other metrics will diverge.

3. Marcus: But that's exactly where the fairness went. Choosing calibration over error-rate parity isn't a neutral technical choice, because the trade-off determines which groups absorb the false positives and false negatives. The theorem forces some distribution of error, and if the designers make that choice unilaterally, they've simply relocated the unfairness into the choice of criterion. Saying we prioritize calibration is a policy decision wearing a technical alias.

4. Priya: You're treating the criteria as jointly non-negotiable and the architecture as fixed; neither is true. We could use the score only to offer services to higher-risk people rather than to detain anyone, in which case calibration matters and error-rate parity matters less. We could set different thresholds, fit a different kind of model, or collect better data so the predictor approaches perfect and the trade-off shrinks. The impossibility results assume one binary decision from one fixed score; real systems have more degrees of freedom than that. And not deploying isn't a neutral fallback: it leaves the existing process in place, with its own opaque and probably worse unfairness.

5. Marcus: Those are real options, but they mostly change the subject. For the instrument actually on the table — a risk score shown to a judge as one factor in a detention decision — we do have a single score and effectively a threshold decision, and the theorem applies. Better data only shrinks the tension; it doesn't remove it. Using the score only for services means buying a different tool than the one proposed. The deeper point is that for this use the trade-off is inescapable, so the agency can't go looking for a fair version that doesn't exist; it has to own the choice about which kind of error it is willing to concentrate where.

6. Priya: I can accept that the agency must own the choice, but you started by saying the results are decisive against the project, and that's much stronger. A mathematical conflict among statistical criteria doesn't show the tool is worse than the status quo, nor that no fair-enough version exists. It shows that fairness here is a matter of choosing among imperfect distributions of error with the trade-offs made transparent. The impossibility results don't say which distribution to choose, and they certainly don't say to choose the status quo over the tool.

They had to stop there, but I kept my notes. Please adjudicate this move by move.

What I'd like back, in this order:

2. Move-by-move adjudication (about 500 words): Take the six numbered moves in order and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification. Rate the moves as actually made, not the positions the speakers might have been reaching for.

3. Overall verdict (about 150 words): Say who is right about which specific point, and where each one overreaches.

4. What both sides are missing (about 100 words): Identify the most important consideration neither of them brought up.
