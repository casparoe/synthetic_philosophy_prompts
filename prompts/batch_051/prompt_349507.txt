The question concerns a passage from John Locke’s Second Treatise of Government, chapter V (Of Property). The relevant content of the passage is paraphrased in the exam question rather than quoted.

Exam question:

In chapter V of the Second Treatise of Government, John Locke argues that the earth was given by God to mankind in common, yet exclusive private property can legitimately arise. He says in effect that each person has property in his own person, and that the labor of one’s body and the work of one’s hands are one’s own; whatever one removes from the state nature left it in and joins with one’s labor thereby becomes one’s property. He immediately qualifies this: such appropriation is legitimate only at least where enough and as good is left in common for others, and where one does not take more than one can use before it spoils. Explain the argument by which Locke moves from common ownership to private property. Then explain the precise logical relation between the two qualifications in the passage: are they two independent conditions, two formulations of one condition, or something else? Finally, does the qualification continue to apply, in the same form, to acquisitions made after the invention of money and the establishment of civil society? Defend your interpretation.

Student’s answer:

Locke’s problem is that God gave the world to everyone in common, and if each person had to obtain the consent of all mankind before taking any part of it, people would starve. His solution is that a person owns his own person and therefore owns his labor. When someone mixes his labor with something unowned—picking acorns or breaking ground—he adds to it what is already his own and thereby makes it his property. This does not require the consent of the other commoners because the labor itself is the source of the new ownership. The proviso is what prevents this labor-based acquisition from being unjust to others: the appropriator must leave enough and as good for others, meaning he cannot take more than he can make use of before it spoils. The use/spoilage qualification and the enough-and-as-good qualification are really the same condition stated twice, because both are violated when someone takes so much that others are left worse off; a person who hoards acorns has both spoiled them and failed to leave enough for others. The proviso continues to operate as a distributive test for every later acquisition, including acquisitions of money. In a commercial society, this means any acquisition is valid only if it leaves for each particular other person a share of the same kind and quality as the thing acquired. Locke therefore regards monetary accumulation as a standing violation of the proviso, which explains why so much inequality is illegitimate.
