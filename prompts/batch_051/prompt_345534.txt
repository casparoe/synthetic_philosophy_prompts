Consider the following reconstruction of an argument in the tradition of Boethius. It is meant to show that divine foreknowledge does not impose the wrong kind of necessity on human actions.

(1) God is eternal, and his eternity is not endless duration spread out through time. Rather, it is the complete and simultaneous possession of the whole of his life. Consequently, God does not strictly foreknow future events from a temporal distance. He sees every event, including those that are future relative to us, as present to himself.

(2) When a human observer sees some present event, the observer’s seeing it does not introduce any necessity into the event beyond the conditional necessity of its occurring at that moment. If a man is walking at this moment, then, while he is walking, it is necessary that he is walking. But this conditional necessity is consistent with his walking having been a free action, because it does not imply that he could not have refrained from walking.

(3) Since God sees future events as present to him, the only necessity attaching to them under God’s vision is likewise conditional: if they are happening at the time God sees them, then they are happening necessarily. What they do not have merely in virtue of being seen is simple necessity, the kind that would make them unavoidable in themselves.

(4) Therefore, the fact that God knows a future human action does not remove the agent’s freedom. It no more does so than a human being’s observation of a present action removes the agent’s freedom while performing it.

Is this argument valid? If it is valid, is it strong enough to answer the fatalist who insists that an infallible past belief about tomorrow’s action makes that action unavoidable? If the argument fails, identify the exact step that fails, explain whether the problem lies in the move from God’s eternity to present-seeing, in the analogy with human observation, or elsewhere, and say what would need to be revised to salvage the argument’s conclusion.
