For an independent project at my high school, I am building a model that predicts which students are at risk of failing a course, and I want its predictions to be fair across demographic groups. In reading about this, I learned that two widely used fairness criteria — calibration, meaning that a given risk score indicates roughly the same probability of failure regardless of group, and equality of false positive rates — are mathematically incompatible whenever the groups have different base rates of failure. If the main formal definitions of fairness cannot be satisfied simultaneously under ordinary conditions, doesn't that show that the very idea of a fair algorithm is incoherent? I am considering concluding in my write-up that the algorithmic fairness literature is chasing an impossible ideal. Is that conclusion warranted?
