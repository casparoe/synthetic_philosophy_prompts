Next week I'm presenting to the philosophy reading group at my university, defending a deliberately strong version of Abelard's claim about sin and intention. The usual gloss is that Abelard is just saying the internal act is where the real moral action is — that the sin is in the state of mind, while the outer deed is merely its execution. I want to go further, and thats the part I need help with.

My thesis: on Abelard's own principles, the completed outward act adds nothing whatsoever to the sin — not merely that it adds nothing in God's eyes, but that it adds no moral badness, period. The difference between a murder and an attempted murder is, on this view, a difference in harm, in persistence or repetition of the will, in what earthly penalties we need for deterrence, and in scandal — but it is not a difference in how wicked the agent is. Once the will has consented to contempt of God, the body's cooperation is morally inert with respect to the agent's guilt.

My argument sketch, so you know what the objections will be aimed at: (1) Abelard analyzes sin as consent, and consent as resolving to do something one believes one should not do for God's sake — so its object is already complete before any deed occurs; (2) the deed cannot add anything to sin because sin is a property of the will's relation to God, not of states of affairs in the world; (3) our intuition that finishing the job makes it worse confuses moral badness with either the amount of harm or with the fact that a completed act usually takes more points of continued consent and nerve. I expect to get hammered on that last step.

The audience: mostly philosophers, no medieval specialists except one retired professor who works on Augustine. There's a virtue ethicist who keeps making points about how actions, not just choices, partly constitute character; a philosopher of action who works on attempts and recklessness and who will hate the flattening of attempt and completion; a Kantian-leaning person who is sympathetic to the will being the locus but will choke on how Abelard ties sin to contempt of God rather than to failure of duty; and a Catholic theologian who will push on the implications for penance, satisfaction, and the way the confessional treats the external act. They know the texts well enough to catch me if I fudge the stages of temptation (suggestion, delight, consent) or pretend Abelard never wavers about whether the act matters when he writes about punishment.

Write me a prep sheet for the Q&A: the objections in the order they'll actually come, phrased the way those people would phrase them, not the way Abelard scholars would. For each: should I concede it, hold the line, or concede part and hold the rest; a two-minute reply; and where the reply is weakest. Be blunt about the objection I should be most afraid of and the one I'm praying nobody asks. And give me a committed verdict: is the hard line — the act adds nothing at all to the agent's guilt — defensible as stated, or should I weaken it before I walk in, and if so to what exactly.
