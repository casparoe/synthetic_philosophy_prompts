so i started volunteering as an adult literacy tutor this year, and a student asked me why there's a g in sign, and i realised i couldn't answer. now im three weeks deep in a rabbit hole about what a writing system is even supposed to encode. the standard story is that an alphabet writes down sounds, roughly one grapheme per phoneme, but english is a deep orthography: it keeps morphemes visible even when the pronunciation changes (sign/signature), it fossilizes the great vowel shift, and it lets the same spelling do half a dozen different jobs (tough, though, through, cough, bough, thought). ive also been learning a bit of finnish on the side, where the spelling is shallow and the letters basically do what they say, and coming back to english afterwards feels like the script gave up on sound at some point and started doing something else — etymology, morphology, history. it seems less like a recording device and more like a frozen theory of the language.

for the last session of my tutor-training cohort i want to close with a poem that makes people feel that gap instead of just nodding at it. sonnet-shaped, about 14 lines, and i wont police the meter. the rhyme scheme should be shakespearean — abab cdcd efef gg — but every one of those seven rhyming pairs should be an eye rhyme, spelled like it rhymes and pronounced like it doesnt (love/move, cough/bough, come/home). so the poem rhymes on the page and refuses to rhyme in the ear. and the volta, the turn around line 9, should be the moment the poem stops treating this as english spelling being broken and starts treating it as the script encoding something else: morpheme identity, loanwords, the layers of history buried in the letters. the form should do the same work as the argument, so that by the end the reader has heard the mismatch and gotten that the mismatch is the point.
