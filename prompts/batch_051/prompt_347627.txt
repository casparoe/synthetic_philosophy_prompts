Three jurisdictions have staked out three different legal postures toward adult prostitution, and each has now been in place long enough to be treated as a stable institutional choice rather than an experiment.

The first, sometimes called the Nordic or equality model, removes penalties from the person selling sex while keeping or introducing penalties for the person buying it. In most versions it also continues to prohibit or restrict third parties—brothel-keeping, pimping, and in some cases advertising—even when the seller would prefer to work with others or from a managed indoor space. Its advocates present it not as labor policy but as a measure against men's violence toward women and as part of a wider commitment to gender equality.

The second, legalization, is exemplified by Germany and the Netherlands. Selling sex is recognized as lawful work; venues may operate subject to licensing, zoning, tax, and health regulations, and workers are in principle entitled to contracts, social insurance, and the ordinary protections of labor law—though in practice a substantial informal sector continues to operate outside the regulated one.

The third, decriminalization, is associated with New Zealand. Consensual adult sex work was removed from criminal law and is treated largely as work like any other, governed by ordinary employment, health and safety, and human rights legislation, without requiring individual sex workers to register or be specially licensed.

Each of these regimes, considered as an institution, seems to rest on a different view of what is philosophically at stake: what the harm consists in, whether and when a person can meaningfully consent to paid sex, whether money transforms sex into something degrading, and what the criminal law is for.

Reconstruct, as explicitly as you can, the theory each model presupposes or expresses about (a) the nature of the harm involved in sex work, (b) the agency and consent of the person selling sex, and (c) the moral significance of commercializing sex. Then do three further things. First, separate what each model strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and note where the policy is philosophically underdetermined—where more than one theory could license the same legal arrangement. Second, expose any internal tensions: for instance, the Nordic model relieves sellers of criminal liability while treating buyers as blameworthy for the very same transaction, and legalization treats sex as a marketable service while subjecting it to forms of state supervision not applied to most other work. Third, say what would have to change in each regime if its presupposed theory were replaced by a rival one—if, for example, one held that selling sex can be a fully consensual exercise of agency and that commercializing sex is not itself degrading, or, conversely, that no payment can make a sexual transaction fully consensual.
