I have spent years reading the anthropics threads on the rationalist blogs and forums, and I keep returning to the doomsday argument because it puts two things I trust into direct conflict.

The argument attracts me for exactly the reasons a good rationalist argument should. It is just Bayes' theorem applied to the fact of my own existence. Take two hypotheses: under the short-history hypothesis, the total number of humans who will ever live is on the order of a few hundred billion and the species ends within some centuries; under the long-history hypothesis, humanity survives for millions or billions of years and the total reaches the trillions or more. I observe that my position in the human sequence is roughly around the hundred-billion mark. That observation is hundreds of times more likely under the short-history hypothesis, where I sit near the middle of the sequence, than under the long-history hypothesis, where I sit in the first fraction of a percent. The self-sampling assumption instructs me to reason as if I were a random draw from the appropriate reference class, and it delivers a clean, mechanistic, quantitative downward update on the long future. The argument makes no assumption that humanity is especially self-destructive or sinful; it would produce the same structure of update for any observer, anywhere in the sequence. That is the kind of view I usually find compelling: small set of assumptions, definite procedure, no special pleading.

And yet I cannot actually bring myself to believe that the number of people born before me is strong evidence that the end is near. My intuition is not that a long future is guaranteed; it is that my temporal position has no business telling me the species is about to stop. The argument seems to license the same conclusion at every point in history: an ancient Roman running the same calculation with their own birth rank would have updated toward imminent doom and been wrong, and so would a medieval peasant and a nineteenth-century industrial worker. If a procedure recommends the same alarming update at every epoch, and most of those epochs turn out to be nowhere near the end, then either the procedure is being misapplied or it is not the stable source of evidence it purports to be. There is also something suspiciously throttleable about the reference class: am I a random human, a random person, a random sentient being, a random observer-moment? Each choice changes the posterior, and I do not have a principled reason to select one. That smells like a parameter that can be tuned to the desired mood.

I do not want a restatement of both sides, and I do not want to be told to bite the bullet or abandon Bayes. I want a negotiated verdict. Tell me precisely what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that gives, what it would take for the argument to be the thing that gives, and whether there is a reading on which both can survive — for example, a split between what the reasoning shows about the likely length of one's reference class and what it shows about whether I should expect to live near a discrete doomsday event. Work through the real cruxes rather than gesturing at them: the reference-class problem, the relationship between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption, whether the argument is self-undermining when applied at different historical moments, and whether any hidden assumption about prior uniformity across time is doing more work than the sampling reasoning itself.
