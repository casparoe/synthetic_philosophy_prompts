Having recently retired from a career in physical chemistry, I have begun working through the foundations of probability and decision theory, and I joined a small reading group on the subject. At our last meeting we examined the St. Petersburg game: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads, and if heads appears on the kth toss the player receives 2^k dollars. The expected monetary value is the infinite sum over k of (1/2^k)(2^k), which diverges, yet the usual empirical claim is that reasonable people will pay only a few dollars to enter. Two of the more experienced members of the group fell into a sharp disagreement about what, if anything, the game demonstrates.

Eleanor argued that the St. Petersburg game is a straightforward refutation of expected-value reasoning as a fundamental norm. A principle that directs a person to pay any finite sum, however large, for a gamble whose typical payout is two or four dollars has detached itself from real deliberation; the divergence is not a peripheral anomaly but the literal recommendation of the rule, and that recommendation is absurd. Marcus replied that the paradox reveals a defect in the set-up rather than in the rule. Actual gambling houses have finite capital, so no real counterpart of the payoff schedule can be genuinely unbounded; and once the payoffs are passed through a strongly concave utility function such as the logarithm, or a bounded utility function, the expected utility becomes a small finite quantity and the puzzle disappears. Expected utility theory, he insisted, was never a theory of expected dollars. Eleanor answered that this is a concession dressed up as a solution: if the game must first be fitted with finite resources or with a capped utility scale before the rule can be trusted, then the principle of simply maximizing expected money has been abandoned, and the surviving principle is protected only because its inputs have been sanitized. Marcus countered that excluding unbounded utility functions is not an ad hoc rescue; a utility assignment that allows St. Petersburg-type gambles to have infinite expected utility fails to provide a usable ordering, since such gambles cannot be compared. Eleanor remained unpersuaded, noting that many values—lives lost, knowledge gained—appear to have no natural ceiling, so bounding utility may solve the mathematics only by distorting the subject matter.

I stayed out of the argument at the time, and I still cannot determine how to apportion the disagreement. Please adjudicate it point by point: specify what each of them is right about, which particular claims are mistaken, and who is more right overall.
