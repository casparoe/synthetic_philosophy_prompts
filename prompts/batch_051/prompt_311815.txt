I have been thinking about Molyneux’s problem, and I want my current view pressure-tested against the original statement of it. Here is the passage from Locke’s An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1689), Book II, Chapter IX, where the problem enters the literature:

I shall here insert a problem of that very ingenious and studious promoter of real knowledge, the learned and worthy Mr. Molineux, which he was pleased to send me in a letter some months since; and it is this:—"Suppose a man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a cube and a sphere of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness, so as to tell, when he felt one and the other, which is the cube, which the sphere. Suppose then the cube and sphere placed on a table, and the blind man be made to see: quaere, whether by his sight, before he touched them, he could now distinguish and tell which is the globe, which the cube?" To which the acute and judicious proposer answers, "Not. For, though he has obtained the experience of how a globe, how a cube affects his touch, yet he has not yet obtained the experience, that what affects his touch so or so, must affect his sight so or so; or that a protuberant angle in the cube, that pressed his hand unequally, shall appear to his eye as it does in the cube."—I agree with this thinking gentleman, whom I am proud to call my friend, in his answer to this problem; and am of opinion that the blind man, at first sight, would not be able with certainty to say which was the globe, which the cube, whilst he only saw them; though he could unerringly name them by his touch, and certainly distinguish them by the difference of their figures felt.

I have been calling my position “amodal invariant access.” The core claim is that tactile and visual shape are not two separate ideas of shape that get associated through experience. They are two partial, mode-relative presentations of a single spatial invariant. A person born blind who can distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch has, I submit, already grasped the invariant itself, not merely a tactile cue. If that is right, then Locke and Molyneux’s negative answer cannot be explained simply by the absence of an arbitrary cross-modal association. The real question becomes why the invariant is not recognized when it shows up in the visual mode.

My proposal is that an invariant is only available through a sensory mode if it is encoded in a mode-appropriate format, and the blind man’s tactile invariant is bound up with active haptic exploration—pressure, contour, resistance, the way the object forces his hand. Visual shape, by contrast, requires an on-the-fly interpretation of a static two-dimensional projection, with lighting and occlusion resolved through priors he does not yet have. So he fails, but he fails because the invariant he possesses is format-locked to touch, not because the two senses have merely not been calibrated. I also think this predicts that a person born blind who could somehow be given fully informative, multi-perspective visual input all at once would do better than Locke expects, because the visual system would be given enough to construct the same invariant without the usual learned priors.

Assess this. Is the distinction between missing cross-modal mappings and format-locked invariants coherent, or is it just redescription? Does it already exist under another name? And where does it break—especially the prediction about ideal viewing conditions? Please give me a committed verdict, not a survey of possible readings.
