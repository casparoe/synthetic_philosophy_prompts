I have taught a seminar on sacrifice, guilt, and collective responsibility for years, and I have accumulated a set of verdicts I cannot yet unify. I would be grateful for an attempt to extract the principle behind them.

The cases and my reactions are these. First, in the biblical Day of Atonement rite, the high priest lays his hands on a goat, confesses the people's sins over it, and sends it into the wilderness. I am drawn to saying that the sins are genuinely carried away, that the community is discharged of something rather than merely acting out a metaphor; but I do not think the goat becomes guilty in the way a responsible agent is guilty. Second, a board makes a reckless decision, and public pressure forces out a junior manager who had no real authority. I do not think the board's guilt is transferred to the manager; the sacrifice satisfies the public, but it does not cleanse the decision-makers. The first two cases trouble me because they seem structurally alike, a community offloading something onto a substitute, yet I feel they are not alike.

Third, in purification rites where impurity is washed from a person or object, I am inclined to say the impurity is really removed; but I cannot say whether that is the same operation as the goat, or something weaker. Fourth, when one person offers to take the blame for another and is punished in that other's place, I think the wrongdoer's responsibility is not extinguished, though something relational has occurred, namely, the substitute has stood in for the wrongdoer before others. Fifth, in the doctrine of substitutionary atonement, I can make sense of a penalty or a debt being paid on another's behalf, but I cannot make sense of guilt itself, as opposed to its penalty, being transferred to an innocent party. Sixth, when a community in crisis finds a scapegoat and drives that person out, I think something real happens at the social level, the group sometimes does regain unity, but the mechanism is unjust and does not make the victim blameworthy.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts. It will probably have to distinguish among guilt, blame, liability, debt, penalty, and impurity. I would also like to know which of my verdicts the principle forces me to abandon, and what it predicts about two further cases I have not settled: a scapegoat ritual that everyone involved sincerely regards as purely symbolic, and a person who volunteers to be the scapegoat in order to hold a community together.
