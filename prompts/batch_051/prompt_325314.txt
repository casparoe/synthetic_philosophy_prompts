I'm a doctoral student in bioethics, and I've been working through the moral enhancement literature, in particular the line of argument developed by Ingmar Persson and Julian Savulescu. The position, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows. Human moral psychology—compassion, a sense of fairness, guilt, the impulse to punish cheaters—was shaped for life in small groups of kin and repeated cooperators, and it delivers its benefits unreliably, parochially, and in ways that are easily overridden by fear, distance, or ideology. Our technological capacity, by contrast, has scaled far faster: a handful of people, in principle even one, can now cause devastation that used to require an army. Ordinary moral cultivation—education, socialization, law, institutional design, public deliberation—works too slowly and too unevenly to close this gap. Therefore, the argument concludes, we have an urgent obligation to pursue moral bioenhancement: direct biomedical interventions, pharmacological or eventually genetic, that strengthen the motivational core of morality, making people more disposed to act on the moral reasons they already acknowledge and extending their concern beyond their immediate circle. I find the argument genuinely disturbing because I cannot easily locate the weak point, and I am now tentatively inclined to accept it.

Please act as a professional philosopher working in this area, but one who is honest rather than either hostile or defensive. Your job is not to tell me whether the argument is right, but to map its exposure: what would have to be true for the position to be false, or for its urgency to collapse? I am particularly interested in whether the distinction between the motivational and the cognitive side of moral agency—between wanting to do what is right and knowing what is right—can bear the weight the argument places on it. Begin by brainstorming a broad range of candidate defeaters—conceptual, empirical, normative, institutional, and anything else—then sort them by how decisive each would be if established. Distinguish evidence or arguments that would be lethal from those that would merely embarrass or complicate the view. Also address what a sophisticated defender, someone closer to Persson and Savulescu themselves, would concede counts against the position, not only what critics allege. Finally, give me an honest verdict on insulation: has the argument been, or could it be, qualified so far that no conceivable finding could refute the urgency claim—reduced, for instance, to the thought that enhancement might still be necessary one day—and if so, is that a fatal defect for a practical proposal of this sort, or a defensible feature of a precautionary argument? I am not asking for a literature survey; I want a carefully reasoned map of what the view risks and what would genuinely move someone who holds it.
