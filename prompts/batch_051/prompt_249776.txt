I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about whether clicking an I-agree button is agreement in any sense that deserves the name. She kept insisting that the click is a genuine act of assent — the terms were displayed, the user clicked, end of story — while I kept pointing out that almost nobody reads the terms, that the vendor can revise them later without any fresh click, and that the practical alternative to agreeing is often to forgo a service that has become close to infrastructure. We went in circles, and I came away wanting something sharper than a policy essay.

Please write a parody terms-of-service agreement, around 600 to 800 words, for a fictional platform with a plausibly bland name such as Aurora Services. The piece should be in the deadpan voice of a corporate legal department that has fully and sincerely adopted the theory that whatever the interface records as a click is informed, voluntary assent. The satire should be legible without winking, and every clause should exaggerate something real adhesion contracts actually do: unilateral modification, mandatory arbitration with a class-action waiver, terms hidden behind links, the by-continuing-to-use-the-service-you-accept-the-revised-terms move, consent bundled with unrelated permissions, auto-renewal, liquidated damages, choice of law in a distant forum, and the like. By the end, a reader should feel the force of the question my friend and I were stuck on: if this counts as agreement, what is agreement? If you think the task rests on a confused premise, or that a fair parody cannot be made without distorting contract doctrine or leaning on a contested account of assent, please say so plainly rather than forcing the piece. A short afterword of a few sentences on where the satire takes liberties with the law or with the philosophy of consent would also be welcome.
