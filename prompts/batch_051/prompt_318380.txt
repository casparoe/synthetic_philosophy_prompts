Okay so I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, and I keep going back and forth about algorithmic fairness. I've got two things I believe pretty strongly, and then there's an impossibility result that says I can't keep both.

The first belief: calibration is what makes a risk score honest. If the model says 7/10 people at a given score will go on to do the thing, then among the people who actually got that score it should be about 7 out of 10, and this should hold within each group. Calibration is the property that lets me say the number means the same thing no matter who its attached to.

The second belief: large differences in error rates across groups are just unfair. If people from group A who wouldn't have done the thing get flagged at twice the rate of group B, or people from group A who would have done the thing get missed at twice the rate of group B, I can't look at that and call the system fine. That one's not a math opinion; it's a gut-level one.

And the result: you can't have calibration, equal false positive rates, and equal false negative rates across groups at the same time, unless the base rates are equal between groups or the model is perfect. So at least one of my convictions has to give, or there's a reading on which they don't really clash.

Give me the strongest version of each conviction. Then tell me which one you'd give up and why, or whether the compatibility move — calibration is about what the score means, equalized odds is about how errors are distributed, so they were never answers to the same question — is a real resolution or a dodge. I also keep half-convincing myself that my gut reaction to unequal error rates is just base-rate neglect, reacting to "twice the rate" without noticing that the denominators differ. Is that debunking move actually available, or is it just a way of not listening to the gut reaction? Don't just pick a side; I want to know what would justify each move.
