Two friends of mine got into it last week over the two-envelope thing, and I honestly couldnt sort out who was right where. The setup's simple: you're shown two sealed envelopes and told one has exactly twice as much money in it as the other. You pick one, open it, and see $100. Then you're offered the chance to switch to the other envelope.

(1) Dana said switching is obviously correct. The other envelope has either $50 or $200, and as far as you know those are equally likely, so the expected value of switching is $125, which beats the $100 in your hand. Keeping the hundred is just leaving money on the table.

(2) Ben said that cant be right, because the same reasoning works no matter what amount you see. Open it and find $10, and you'd say $5 or $20; find $500, and you'd say $250 or $1,000. So the argument says you should switch whatever you find, which means you should switch before even opening. But before opening the two envelopes are perfectly symmetric, and if switching is smart then after you switch the same argument would tell you to switch back and you'd never stop. So the reasoning has to be broken somewhere.

(3) Dana pushed back that before you open, you don't have a specific amount to condition on, so there's no expected value to compute. Once you actually see $100, there is: the other envelope is worth $125 in expectation, so you switch. The symmetry point is about a different situation, where you're making a choice in the dark; once you've looked, you have more information.

(4) Ben answered that the $125 number only follows if, after seeing $100, it's genuinely 50/50 that you're holding the smaller amount from the pair (100, 200) versus the larger amount from the pair (50, 100). The puzzle never gives you that; it depends entirely on how the envelopes got filled, and there's no way to fill them so every possible amount you could see makes the odds exactly 50/50. So the 50/50 is smuggled in, and if it fails, the calculation goes with it. Dana said he was overcomplicating a puzzle that's mean to have simple odds, and then they both turned to me to referee. Could you go through it move by move and say who's right about what — rate each of the four moves, and give an overall verdict?
