In my reporting on moral criticism and public shaming, I keep running into the same philosophical impasse. Take a case everyone agrees on: a habitual liar condemns a colleague for a single lie. Both sides agree the colleague did lie and is blameworthy for it. The question is whether the accuser's own record matters to the charge. Some philosophers treat blame as fundamentally a kind of verdict on the target — an assessment of whether the target did wrong and is responsible — so the accuser's hypocrisy is irrelevant to whether the blame itself is fitting, however unseemly it makes the accuser. Others treat blame as more like a demand for accountability or a protest against wrongdoing, and on that view one can lack the standing to make the demand if one openly flouts the same standard, so the hypocrite's blame can be inappropriate even when its content is true. After decades of argument, every move on both sides is familiar and nobody changes their mind. I don't want a survey or a forced tie-breaker. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, substantive, or two different questions being run under one heading — and an honest account of what, if anything, would count as progress given that diagnosis.
