I've been arguing with a friend about whether religious experiences should count as evidence for God. I keep coming back to William James. In The Varieties of Religious Experience he thought mystical states carried a sense of contact with a deeper reality, and he spent a lot of energy fending off what he called medical materialism: the idea that if a state has a biological cause, it's automatically worthless as knowledge. His line was basically that an experience's origin doesn't settle its epistemic value. So the fact that St. Paul might have had a seizure on the road to Damascus, or that religious rapture might have some neural correlate, doesn't by itself refute the person who says God was disclosed in it.

But I'm not sure his move survives where the science has actually gone. We don't just have correlations now. Researchers can induce something that looks a lot like mystical experience pretty reliably, with psilocybin in controlled settings, and there's stuff like the so-called God helmet and temporal lobe transients. If the same core experiences—ego dissolution, a sense of oneness, an encounter with an unseen presence—can be turned on by a drug or a magnet, does the argument James was defending still have force, or is it basically dead? I'm less interested in the crude "it's just your brain" line than in whether James's specific distinction between causal origin and epistemic authority can be maintained when the cause is a known, repeatable, non-religious trigger. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating the question.
