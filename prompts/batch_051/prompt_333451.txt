You're a professional political theorist who works on Mill. Think this through carefully, and start by brainstorming the main incentives before you settle on an analysis.

A mid-size city creates a Register of Experiments in Living. Any adult can file a description of a nonstandard way of arranging their life — a group marriage, a commune that abolishes private rooms, a total-honesty society, a household that renounces clocks and calendars, a plan to live for a year without buying anything. Filing makes the person eligible for exemptions from ordinary rules that would otherwise forbid the practice (zoning, licensing, occupancy limits, and so on), on three conditions: every affected adult consents, no minors are involved, and the filer must publish a short public report once a year describing how it's going. The report is purely informational and linked to the person's name; it's not an application for renewal, and the registry staff has no power to approve or reject a filing as long as the conditions are met on paper.

Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral question. Who actually files, and what does the yearly public report do to the experiments themselves, to observers, and to the stigma around unusual lives? How does it get gamed, and where does it break first? Does it end up protecting Millian individuality or turning experiments in living into a performance? Walk through the incentives and second-order effects rather than just listing pros and cons.
