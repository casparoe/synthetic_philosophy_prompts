Retired evolutionary biologist here, recently fallen into philosophy. I've been reading Mencius and Xunzi on human nature, and I keep finding myself wanting to restate their argument in the vocabulary I actually think in. Mencius's sprouts — compassion, shame, deference, a sense of right and wrong — sound to me like developmental defaults: dispositions that reliably come online in a normal human environment unless something starves them, the way canalized traits or prepared learning do. Xunzi sounds like the opposite developmental claim: the untutored phenotype is selfish and disorderly, and morality is a kind of external technology — ritual, teaching, custom — strapped on top of a default we would otherwise express, more like a culturally transmitted adaptation than an unfolding of the organism. I'm fluent in the biology (reaction norms, phenotypic plasticity, canalization, gene-culture coevolution) but new enough to the philosophy that I dont trust my mapping. Restate the Mencius/Xunzi debate in that biological vocabulary, one side at a time, as faithfully as you can. Then give me a residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which you had to bend, and which have no counterpart in the biology at all (their claim that human nature is good or bad — a reaction norm has no moral valence). Then tell me wether the residue means the biological vocabulary is leaving something real out, or whether the original debate was partly a fight about which slice of the developmental trajectory gets to be called nature.
