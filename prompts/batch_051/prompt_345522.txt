I keep noticing a parallel between truth-value gaps in philosophy and how databases handle missing data, and I'm not sure if it's deep or superficial. In strong Kleene three-valued logic, a proposition that's neither true nor false ("unknown") propagates in a very particular way: TRUE OR unknown gives TRUE, FALSE AND unknown gives FALSE, NOT unknown gives unknown. SQL's NULL does almost the same thing when a WHERE clause evaluates it: NULL = NULL yields UNKNOWN, not TRUE, and the connectives treat UNKNOWN with exactly those Kleene rules. But then there's NaN in floating point, which looks like another "no value" thing that propagates, yet the logic comes out different: NaN == NaN is false and NaN != NaN is true, so it's not even equal to itself. Is SQL's UNKNOWN genuinely the same structure as a truth-value gap, or is it better thought of as missing data / uncertainty dressed up in logical clothes? And what does the fact that NaN breaks the analogy tell us — is NaN implementing a rival conception of what a "gap" is, or is it just not playing the same game at all? Walk me through where the mapping holds and where it comes apart.
