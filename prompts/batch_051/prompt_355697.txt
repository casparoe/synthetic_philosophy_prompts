Here's an asymmetry I keep tripping over. Two people both got fired for embezzling and are now interviewing for the same job. Asked "Were you fired from your last job?", the first says "No." The second says "I left during the restructuring" — which is true: he left during the restructuring, because that's when they caught him. The interviewer walks away from both conversations believing they quit voluntarily. Almost everyone treats the first as a much worse person than the second: the first lied, the second was just being slick. I get the grammatical difference; I don't yet see the moral one. Give me the strongest principle you can find that makes the literally-true-but-engineered answer meaningfully less wrong than the flat lie, then try to break it against: a speaker who pre-plans the misleading phrasing, a hearer who actively asks rather than merely overhears, and the same pair of answers in a life-or-death situation. I also want to know whether there's a nearby case where the misleading answer comes out worse than the lie, and what that would show. Then a verdict: keep the ordinary asymmetry, reverse it, or abandon it as a grammar-powered fig leaf? And tell me whether its actually a moral distinction or just a hangover from how much we like the word "lie."
