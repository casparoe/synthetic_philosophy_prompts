I have been developing a position I call the co-authorship account of collective harm, and I would like an honest assessment of whether it is anything more than a metaphor. The target is the inefficacy objection: because no single purchase of factory-farmed meat, no single flight, and no single car journey makes a perceptible difference to the total harm produced, it is unclear that the usual causal reasons for abstaining get any grip. My proposal is that the objection conflates making a difference to an outcome with being among the makers of that outcome.

By my definition, an agent co-authors a collective harm when (i) her action belongs to a practice or pattern that predictably produces the harm, (ii) she knows, or can reasonably be expected to know, that the practice does so, and (iii) her action is of the same kind as the actions that constitute the practice. The central claim is that a co-author bears responsibility for a share of the aggregate harm, where the share is roughly one divided by the number of relevant contributing actions in the period at issue, rather than for any marginal causal effect of her own act. My guiding example: if a hundred people each pour a liter of solvent into a river, and the combined effluent kills the fish, each person has not done almost nothing; each is an author of one hundredth of the resulting harm. Applied to meat: if I know that industrial animal agriculture produces a certain amount of suffering in a year, and I purchase from it in the ordinary way, I become a co-author of that harm in proportion to my participation, regardless of whether my particular demand would have been met by someone else had I abstained.

I think this dissolves the inefficacy objection in both its empirical and its motivational forms. The question is not whether my act would have left the outcome different, but whether I will insert myself among the makers of the outcome; and that latter question gives me a reason to abstain that does not depend on the size of my marginal causal contribution. But the view may collapse into existing complicity or shared-responsibility accounts under a better name, and it may prove too much or too little. Give me your assessment: reconstruct the position in its strongest form, say what it gets right, name the nearest existing ideas you can identify, and give the sharpest objections, indicating which one is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
