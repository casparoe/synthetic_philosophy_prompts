You're a political theorist who works on democratization and democratic erosion. I'd like you to think carefully here — maybe start by brainstorming a few candidate principles and stress-testing them against my cases before you settle. Don't just rattle off the standard indicators from the democracy-ratings world; I want something I can actually apply to a new case.

I've been following the backsliding debates about Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the US, and it's striking how the same word gets used for very different moves. So I collected my own gut calls on a bunch of cases, and I want to know whether there's one principle behind them or whether some of my calls have to go.

Here are my verdicts:

A. A government wins a genuinely free and fair referendum to remove presidential term limits — high turnout, both sides get airtime, the count isn't disputed. My gut says not backsliding. Maybe dangerous, but not itself backsliding: the rules were changed through the rules.

B. A governing party redraws district lines mid-cycle and turns 47 percent of the vote into 58 percent of the seats. My gut says backsliding, no question. That's rigging the translation of votes into power.

C. A government passes a law making any NGO that takes foreign funding register as a foreign agent and file quarterly donor lists, with criminal penalties for late filing. My gut says backsliding. It's not banning civil society, but it's strangling it.

D. A president loses re-election, spends two months claiming fraud without evidence and pressuring state officials not to certify, but ultimately leaves office on the constitutionally required date. My gut says backsliding. The norm broke even though the transfer happened.

E. A new government prosecutes a former president for embezzlement, with strong documentary evidence, after the previous government shielded him for years. My gut says not backsliding — that's just accountability.

F. The same government then prosecutes the current opposition leader for an eight-year-old minor tax violation of a kind almost nobody is ever charged with, three months before an election. My gut says backsliding — that's lawfare.

G. After a bad flood, a government declares a six-month state of emergency, governs by decree to rebuild, and then lets the emergency lapse. My gut says not backsliding.

H. A different government declares a renewable state of emergency over demographic decline and has now governed by decree for three years. My gut says backsliding.

My working principle is something like: backsliding is changing or capturing the rules and referees of the game so that alternation of power becomes less likely or less meaningful, or so that the government faces less real constraint. But I can already feel it pulling against my own verdicts. It probably makes A backsliding, and I don't want that. It also doesn't obviously handle D, where no rule changed and the guy did leave — the damage was to a norm. I need an exception for G, and exceptions have a way of eating principles. And I don't know how to separate F from E without appealing to intent, which feels like it could be applied in a purely partisan way.

So: is there a coherent principle that gets all or most of these? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And what does your principle say about a case I keep going back and forth on: a government passes, through a legally flawless process, a constitutional amendment creating a new upper chamber of appointed experts, chosen by the president, with a veto over ordinary legislation — the government says its about depoliticizing lawmaking, the opposition says it's entrenching itself. Is that backsliding?

Last thing: be honest about the who-decides problem. If the principle says backsliding is about weakening democratic contestation, doesn't that smuggle in a substantive view of what democracy is for?
