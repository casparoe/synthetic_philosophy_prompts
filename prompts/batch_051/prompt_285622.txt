Here's an asymmetry in how we talk about justice that I can't get to sit still. If a private man tortures people and then offers a full truthful account in exchange for immunity, we call the arrangement sordid, maybe corrupt. If a state torturer makes the same offer after a dictatorship falls, we invite him into a truth commission, and plenty of people describe his amnesty as justice or national healing. South Africa's transition ran on exactly that trade; Argentina first granted military amnesties and later undid them; Rwanda routed much of its accountability through community gacaca courts instead of ordinary trials. Is there a principle that makes the political version justice while the private version stays a grubby bargain, or is the only real difference how badly the new regime needs the man's testimony and how much power he still holds? Test the strongest candidate principle you can find against the nearby case of a drug gang that offers the same thing — full confessions and a neighborhood ceasefire in exchange for immunity — and say honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept or given up as an inconsistency we live with.
