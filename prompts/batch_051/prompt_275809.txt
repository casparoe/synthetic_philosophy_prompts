Take Kosovo's declaration of independence from Serbia in 2008 and the diplomatic contest over its status that has followed, and analyze it through the declaratory and constitutive theories of state recognition in international relations. I know the episode in broad outline — the declaration, the wave of bilateral recognitions, Serbia's and Russia's opposition, the blocked path to United Nations membership, and the 2010 International Court of Justice advisory opinion holding that the declaration did not violate international law while pointedly declining to say that Kosovo was a state — so do not rest the analysis on contested details of the diplomatic timeline; the salient, undisputed facts are the ones just listed.

Begin by stating precisely what each theory claims. Does the declaratory view hold that recognition merely reports an already-completed fact of statehood, while the constitutive view holds that recognition is itself an ingredient in making the fact? If so, what is to count as recognition under each theory — bilateral acknowledgment by one state, recognition by the great powers, admission to the United Nations, or something like participation in ordinary diplomatic practice? And does either theory distinguish recognition of a state from recognition of a government or recognition of a particular territorial claim?

Then run the episode through each theory. Which features of Kosovo's case does each explain cleanly, and which fit awkwardly? Address at least the following: Kosovo's functioning state-like institutions and effective control of its territory; the deliberate campaign to accumulate bilateral recognitions; the refusal of some states to recognize it; the Court's careful avoidance of the statehood question; and the fact that Kosovo has now persisted for nearly two decades in a condition of partial recognition.

Now reverse direction and let the case test the theories. Is Kosovo a confirming instance, an anomaly, or a case neither theory predicted? Does it push toward a hybrid view — statehood as something that comes in degrees, or recognition as doing several distinct jobs at once — or does it suggest that the declaratory/constitutive framing was asking the wrong question from the start? In particular, if an entity can exercise sovereignty over its population and territory indefinitely while a substantial minority of states refuse to acknowledge it, what theoretical work is recognition still doing? Be explicit about what each theory would have predicted in February 2008, and whether what followed bore those predictions out.
