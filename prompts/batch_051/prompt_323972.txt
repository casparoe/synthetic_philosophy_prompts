I have been defending a claim in value theory and I want it pressure-tested, because the arguments I keep giving for it feel suspiciously easy. The claim is what I will call the achievement-value thesis: some instances of excellent performance possess final value—value for their own sake—that does not reduce to the welfare, pleasure, or pride of any person, including the agent. I am committed to a strong form of this. Suppose a mathematician proves a beautiful theorem and the proof, along with every trace and memory of it, is then destroyed; I say the world is better for the proof having occurred than the same world minus the proof, even though every later state of the universe is observationally indistinguishable from one in which the proof never happened. I want to keep two cases apart. In the unobserved-but-discoverable case (the unknown manuscript later found), one can always fall back on potential appreciation or downstream influence. I am staking the stronger claim on the in-principle-undiscoverable case, where no one can ever know the achievement occurred. I also want to set aside the easier move that it was good for the agent to have exercised her capacities in the act itself: assume the agent is indifferent afterward and gains nothing on any welfare measure.

What would have to be true for the achievement-value thesis, in this strong form, to be false? I want more than the familiar objection that value requires a valuer or that unexperienced goodness is a suspicious posit. Give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: the possible cases that pull against it (the morally repugnant excellent performance; the achievement whose standards are themselves worthless; the solipsistic performance that is excellent only by a private or circular standard), the intuitions it has to overturn, and the collateral commitments it takes on—for instance, about whether there can be facts about value that are in principle unknowable from any perspective, and about what it could mean for one state of the universe to be better than another when no possible observer could distinguish them. I would also like to know what the thesis licenses that a welfare-only account does not (for example, that a universe containing more unobserved excellent achievements is better even when the difference can never surface in any decision procedure) and whether that implication is a feature or a bug.

Which of these considerations would a committed defender concede as counting against the thesis, and which would they dismiss as merely restating the disagreement? I want an honest assessment of whether the position is genuinely exposed or has been, or could be, qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that should bother me for a claim of this kind. I would personally be most persuaded by a deflationary account that preserves everything I want to say about why I admire certain achievements without positing extra, unexperienced goodness.
