I am drafting the middle chapter of a paper on whether describing an artificial system as 'optimising' an objective is a factual claim about the system itself or merely a convenient way of summarising its behaviour. A colleague in my group, who works on autonomous systems, has been pressing a sceptical argument on me, and I want to meet it properly rather than merely feel that it overreaches. I should be grateful if you would tell me whether the argument is valid and, if not, which step fails and what the strongest corrected version would claim.

His argument runs as follows. (1) To describe a system as optimising an objective is to explain its outputs by supposing that, within some search space of candidate outputs, it tends to produce those that score highly under that objective. (2) For any finite set of observed outputs, we can always define a post hoc objective that assigns those outputs maximal scores; the bare existence of some objective that fits the record is therefore cheap and carries little evidential weight. (3) Nor is the difficulty confined to such ad hoc constructions: the observations themselves fix only how an objective scores the observed outputs and their recorded alternatives, and every arrangement of those finite scores can be extended to unobserved cases in indefinitely many ways, including extensions that are compact and independently plausible as well as ones that swing wildly. (4) So, for any candidate objective that fits the data, there will always be some rival objective—and often a comparably simple one—that fits equally well yet ranks quite different outputs as best on untested inputs. (5) Therefore, unless one covertly helps oneself to a prior preference for certain objectives over others, observed behaviour cannot favour any one objective among these rivals. (6) Hence an optimiser's objective is not something we can infer from its behaviour even in principle; objective ascriptions are projections we impose on systems rather than features we discover in them. He adds that the same applies to the search space: a finite record of outputs is equally consistent with a small space searched nearly exhaustively and an enormous space sampled by a heavily biased proposer.

I can feel that something gives way between (5) and (6), but I cannot say exactly what, and I half-wonder whether the weak point is earlier, perhaps in (3). Is the argument valid? If not, at which step does it fail, and how much of the underdetermination claim survives a correct formulation?
