I got drafted as the de-facto tiebreaker in a work argument that's made me weirdly invested in an old social science debate, and I want a diagnosis of the argument itself rather than a ruling on our particular case. We're modelling which neighbourhoods gentrify, and the team has split in a way I now realise its just methodological individualism versus holism doing another lap. One side says the only legitimate model bottoms out in individuals—households moving, landlords pricing, developers building under constraints—and that group-level talk like "the market" or "the neighbourhood" is either shorthand or unfalsifiable. The other side says that's backwards: the structure of constraints is the causal action, and by the time you've specified individuals' options you've already smuggled in institutions, zoning, segregation, credit markets. Same fight people have been having since at least Durkheim, and nobody moves.

My working theory is that it isn't really an empirical disagreement. Both sides agree only individuals exist and act, and both would mostly make the same predictions if forced. So maybe the real dispute is about what an explanation has to do: whether explaining means decomposing things to individuals, or whether it means locating a pattern in a structure that can't be read off any one person's choice. But I could be papering over a genuine empirical question, like whether group-level variables do predictive work once you control for the individual-level ones. Which is it, and what would actually count as progress—a distinction, a result, a different question? If I've mischaracterised the debate or the premise is confused, tell me plainly instead of politely playing along with my framing.
