I have been working through the virtue ethics and situationist debate, and I keep returning to a passage in Aristotle’s *Nicomachean Ethics* that seems to me to do a great deal of philosophical work but is rarely examined in detail. In Book II, chapter 4 (in H. Rackham’s translation), Aristotle writes:

“Moreover the case of the arts is not really analogous to that of the virtues. Works of art have their merit in themselves, so that it is enough if they are produced having a certain quality of their own; but acts done in conformity with the virtues are not done justly or temperately if they themselves are of a certain sort, but only if the agent also is in a certain state of mind when he does them: first he must act with knowledge; secondly he must deliberately choose the act, and choose it for its own sake; and thirdly the act must spring from a fixed and permanent disposition of character.”

My question concerns whether the situationist critique of virtue ethics survives a specific change in its background psychological assumptions. As I understand it, the original critique claims that there is no good empirical evidence for the kind of global, cross-situationally consistent character traits that Aristotle’s account seems to require: experimental subjects behave quite differently depending on seemingly minor features of their situation, so the supposed virtues are not robust enough to do the explanatory and evaluative work assigned to them.

Suppose, however, we accept a later development in personality research. On this newer picture, people are not simply without stable dispositions; rather, their dispositions are stable only as person-by-situation regularities. Someone may be reliably honest with peers but not under institutional pressure, or reliably courageous in physical danger but not in social confrontation, and the relevant “situation” is often the agent’s own construal rather than the experimenter’s objective set-up. This replaces the old situationist slogan “there are no stable character traits” with “there are stable if-then dispositions, but few or no global ones.” Does the situationist argument against Aristotle’s account survive this changed assumption? Does Aristotle’s demand for a “fixed and permanent disposition of character” survive it, or must that demand be repaired into something like a fixed disposition indexed to a domain of practice or to a type of construed situation—and would that repair still be recognisably virtue ethics? I am less interested in the empirical verdict itself than in whether the conceptual structure of the argument survives, and which premise the new development bears on. Please answer in British English.
