I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am working on a long-form piece about population ethics and the far future. In my background reading I keep encountering Derek Parfit's repugnant conclusion, and I want to be sure I do not pass on a caricature of it. The most concrete description I have found comes from a recent popular article, which explains it roughly as follows.

Parfit asks us to compare two possible futures. In the first, there is a modest population of, say, ten billion people, all living lives of extraordinarily high quality. In the second, there is a vastly larger population whose members have lives that are only barely worth living. Because the second future contains a much greater total quantity of well-being, total utilitarianism ranks it as the better future. Parfit found this result unacceptable and took it to show that total utilitarianism should be rejected: since it would be clearly wrong to prefer the crowded world of lives barely worth living, he argued, no plausible moral theory could count that world as better. The article then says that the repugnant conclusion is best understood as a philosophical version of the familiar worry that a policy of endlessly increasing the population is bad, and that anyone who rejects the conclusion is committed to holding that a smaller number of very happy lives is worth more, all things considered, than a far larger number of lives barely worth living.

I suspect this summary overreaches in a few places, but I do not know enough to say where. I have seen conflicting suggestions about whether Parfit intended the example as a refutation of total utilitarianism or as a deeper problem that several theories must face, and I am not sure the article respects the difference between ranking one future as better and saying we are obligated to bring it about. Could you audit the summary for me: which parts would a careful population ethicist accept, which are reasonable simplifications, and which are false or misleading? I would also like to know whether the final inference is sound — if someone rejects the repugnant conclusion, does that commit them to the claim that the smaller, happier population is better? And I would appreciate a short explanation of the distinction between a future's being better and its being morally required, since the article seems to slide between the two.
