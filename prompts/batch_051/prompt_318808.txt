After a career in geology, I have lately been reading philosophy with more system than I once did, and one essay has unsettled me: William Kingdon Clifford’s “The Ethics of Belief” (1877). The passage I keep returning to is this:

“The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him.”

I find the principle clear, and as a scientist I am inclined to think it is exactly the moral defense of scrupulous inquiry that I was trained into for the whole of my working life. But a philosopher friend has constructed an argument from it that seems to prove too much, and I cannot locate the error. Here is the argument as he laid it out:

(1) If Clifford is right, then a person who holds a belief without having earned it by patient and fair inquiry is blameworthy for holding it, even when the belief happens to be true.

(2) Most of our everyday beliefs are not beliefs we have earned by patient and fair inquiry. That the earth is very old, that vaccines are generally safe, that other people have inner mental lives, that our memories report the past rather than confabulate it—these are things we acquired from testimony, schooling, habituation, and the ordinary machinery of perception, not from investigations we conducted ourselves.

(3) So if Clifford is right, most of us are blameworthy for most of our ordinary beliefs.

(4) That cannot be the correct moral verdict. A principle that convicts nearly every ordinary person of standing epistemic guilt must have gone wrong somewhere.

(5) Therefore Clifford’s principle, at least as he states it, is false.

There is a second version of the argument that troubles me more:

(6) Suppose one replies that our everyday beliefs are based on sufficient evidence even when we did not gather it ourselves, because testimony and perception are themselves evidence.

(7) But then the shipowner in Clifford’s example could also have appealed to the testimony of builders and contractors, or to his perception that the ship had weathered many storms. If that reply were adequate, Clifford would have had no case against him.

(8) Yet Clifford plainly thinks he did have a case. So the retreat to testimonial and perceptual evidence cannot save the principle while preserving the verdict.

I am tempted to reject step (2) by saying that ordinary beliefs are earned after all, because we have been inducted into a scientific culture and have thereby acquired an entitlement to trust its results. But that seems to water the principle down into nothing more than “believe what your culture tells you,” which was surely not Clifford’s point. I am also tempted to say that the shipowner’s case is distinctive because the stakes were high, and that the ethics of belief should be stakes-sensitive. But that thought sits uneasily with my scientific training: I do not think one is excused from epistemic care merely because the question is small.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid, what is the least costly way to revise Clifford’s principle, and does the result still have moral force?
