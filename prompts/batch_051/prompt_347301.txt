A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether the heterogeneity of pleasure really refutes hedonism, and by the end he had convinced me that the problem runs deeper than I originally thought — deep enough that I now suspect the argument is proving too much, but I cannot locate the bad step.

Here is the argument as I would now set it down.

(1) Hedonism is worth taking seriously only if "pleasure" names a single kind of mental state; otherwise the doctrine collapses into the claim that some disjunction of mutually unrelated states is the sole intrinsic good, and a bare disjunction is not the sort of thing that could carry a shared value-making character.

(2) The canonical pleasant experiences share no single qualitative character. The pleasure of relief when a danger passes, of being absorbed in skilled activity, of eating when hungry, of sexual arousal, of schadenfreude at a rival's failure, of scratching an itch, and of finally understanding a proof have no felt common denominator. They are pleasant in recognizably different ways, and their differences feel more salient than anything they share.

(3) Nor is there any non-qualitative candidate that obviously unifies them: they differ in their causes, in the bodily and attentional states that accompany them, and in the motivational work they do. Whatever common property a theorist might cite is, at this point, a promissory note rather than a discovered kind.

(4) Therefore "pleasure" picks out a gerrymandered disjunction rather than a real kind that could serve as the sole intrinsic good.

(5) By exactly the same reasoning, "pain" is a gerrymandered disjunction. The pain of a burn, of a migraine, of grief, of humiliation, of phantom limb, and of social exclusion likewise share no common qualitative character, and they arise through different mechanisms and play different roles in our lives.

(6) If "pain" is a gerrymandered disjunction, then the ordinary practice of treating harms as comparable — of saying that one injury hurt more than another, or that a policy causes more suffering than it prevents — is no better grounded than the hedonic calculus that the heterogeneity objection was meant to bury.

(7) But that conclusion is unacceptable: we are not ready to give up the idea that pain is a real, univocal thing, or that the difference between causing more of it and causing less of it is a difference in the world.

(8) So at least one of the premises (1)–(3) or (5)–(6) must be false, or one of the transitions between steps must be invalid.

We spent the rest of the night going in circles about which move to make. My instinct is that something slips in the passage from the heterogeneity of pleasant experience to the claim that "pleasure" has no unifying nature, and again in the move from (4) to (5). My friend insists the only real exits are to reject (1) — hedonism was never a serious contender — or to accept (7) and learn to live with a disunified ethics, which he thinks is far less absurd than I do.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey. In particular, I am not asking only whether the argument is sound; I want to know which step you would reject, whether the flaw is a false premise or a slide between two senses of a term such as "kind" or "property," and why the rejected step looked right enough to be tempting. Then I want the bill: what does rejecting that step force one to give up, and what does each of the alternative exits cost? If your diagnosis is that the argument is valid and that one premise simply has to go, say which premise is the least costly to surrender and what, if anything, survives of both hedonism and ordinary talk about harm afterwards.
