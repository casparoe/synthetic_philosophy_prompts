A friend and I spent an evening arguing about whether Malthus can be rescued as "right but early," and he left me with an argument I cannot shake. We agreed that Malthus's specific claim — population grows geometrically while food grows arithmetically — was false, and that his timetable was wrong. The fight was over whether the "core" that survives is still an empirical claim at all. Here is the argument as he gave it.

(1) A long-run prediction is empirically testable only if it specifies some possible history of the world that would count as its failure, rather than merely as its not having come due yet.
(2) The Malthusian core, as defenders now use it, specifies no such history. It says population pressure must eventually outrun the means of subsistence and produce a check — famine, disease, war, or whatever. When the check fails to arrive, the reply is always that the tendency was present but offset: by fossil-fuel energy, synthetic fertilizer, the demographic transition, or whichever auxiliary factor kept the mechanism at bay for another generation.
(3) So the Malthusian core is unfalsifiable: any finite stretch of history in which the predicted check has not arrived is compatible with it, because "eventually" is always still available.
(4) But whatever makes the Malthusian core unfalsifiable applies equally to every long-run claim with an "eventually" in it. The sun will eventually exhaust its fuel; a species will eventually go extinct; a Ponzi scheme will eventually collapse — each can likewise be rescued from any particular non-occurrence by saying the tendency was present but delayed or masked.
(5) So, by parity, every claim of the form "X will eventually happen" is unfalsifiable.
(6) Yet we plainly distinguish well-tested long-run claims from crank ones, and we do so on evidence rather than taste.

My friend concluded that either the Malthusian core is empty or our ordinary confidence in long-run predictions is a habit we cannot justify. I want to keep (6), and I am prepared to pay for it, but I want to know exactly which step goes and what the bill is. Please commit to a definite diagnosis: which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost for how we should evaluate long-run predictions generally? Do not just lay out the menu of options.
