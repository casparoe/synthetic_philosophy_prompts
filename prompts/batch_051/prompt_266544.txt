I've been rereading the Prelude to *Middlemarch* and this paragraph keeps snagging me, and I want help working out what George Eliot is actually doing with religion here:

"That Spanish woman who lived three hundred years ago, was certainly not the last of her kind. Many Theresas have been born who found for themselves no epic life wherein there was a constant unfolding of far-resonant action; perhaps only a life of mistakes, the offspring of a certain spiritual grandeur ill-matched with the meanness of opportunity; perhaps a tragic failure which found no sacred poet and sank unwept into oblivion. With dim lights and tangled circumstance they tried to shape their thought and deed in noble agreement; but after all, to common eyes their struggles seemed mere inconsistency and formlessness; for these later-born Theresas were helped by no coherent social faith and order which could perform the function of knowledge for the ardently willing soul. Their ardor alternated between a vague ideal and the common yearning of womanhood; so that the one was disapproved as extravagance, and the other condemned as a lapse."

I get the surface point—Dorothea's a kind of misfired saint with no large outlet for her ardour. But the line "helped by no coherent social faith and order which could perform the function of knowledge for the ardently willing soul" feels like it's doing the real philosophical work, and I cant quite decide what claim its making. Is she saying the loss of a shared faith is what leaves these later-born Theresas unable to organise their moral ambition, or is that just one condition among several? And is she mourning religion, or diagnosing what has to be supplied once the beliefs are gone? I know she'd broken with orthodox Christianity young and had that Feuerbach-translating phase, so I keep flip-flopping between reading the paragraph as nostalgic and reading it as a humanist diagnosis. Can you walk me through it slowly and say which reading the text supports, or whether it's genuinely ambiguous?
