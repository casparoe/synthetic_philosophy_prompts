I serve on the compensation committee of a worker-owned co-operative, and we are about to vote on pay ratios for a newly restructured set of roles. A colleague has circulated the following summary of Rawls to frame the decision:

"Rawls's central idea is that a just distribution is whatever people would agree to in an original position behind a veil of ignorance, where no one knows their future place in society, their natural talents, or their conception of the good. Because the parties cannot assign probabilities to where they will end up, Rawls thinks they become extremely risk-averse and adopt a maximin rule: they judge social arrangements entirely by how well the worst-off group fares. This, he says, is the reasoning behind the difference principle, which permits any inequality of income and wealth that leaves the least advantaged at least slightly better off than they would be under strict equality. So the principle is not really a limit on the size of gaps; it amounts to a Pareto-improvement test applied to the bottom."

Something about this feels too permissive to me, but I cannot tell whether my unease is grounded in Rawls or in my own discomfort with large pay differences. Could you go through the passage and tell me which parts accurately represent Rawls, which parts are simplifications or distortions, what a careful defender of the difference principle would reject, and how the corrected version would actually apply to a decision like ours about whether a steep pay ratio can be justified?
