I run a monthly philosophy discussion group at a public library, usually around a dozen adults, in a single two-hour session. A recent falling-out in my extended family has made Peirce's account of how beliefs get fixed feel suddenly concrete: several relatives have settled into a shared conviction that is plainly contradicted by evidence they all possess, and every attempt to introduce that evidence only tightens their loyalty to the conviction. I would like to spend one session letting the group feel, before I give them any vocabulary for it, the difference among four ways a belief can be held: by sheer tenacity, by deference to an authority, by what seems antecedently reasonable to the thinker, and by a method that deliberately submits the belief to the risk of experience. Design an activity, game, or structured simulation for this group. It should need no special equipment and no real money, and should run around a library table. The rules, roles, or incentives should make participants run into, in their own behavior, the brittleness of the first three postures and the distinctive self-correcting quality of the last, so that the contrast emerges from what happens rather than from any opening explanation. Then give me a debrief that maps specific moments in the exercise back to Peirce's four methods of fixing belief, draws out the connection to Peircean fallibilism — the idea that a settled conclusion remains answerable to future experience — and says what will predictably go wrong when I run this with intelligent but conflict-averse adults who are not studying philosophy. Be explicit about which feature of the real distinction the exercise cannot reproduce and that I would need to supply in a short closing discussion. If the exercise needs a concrete practical scenario (a village deciding whether a water source is safe, or something like it), specify the scenario and say what turns on that choice.
