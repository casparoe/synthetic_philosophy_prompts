I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether Gödel's modal version of the ontological argument should actually count as a proof that God exists. He thinks yes, I think it's more of a formal parlor trick. The criteria I was taught for a successful a priori proof of God's existence are these five: (1) the deduction is valid in a logic we have independent reason to accept, not just valid inside an ad hoc system built for the occasion; (2) each premise is either self-evident or at least more obviously true then the conclusion; (3) the proof doesn't beg the question — the conclusion isn't already smuggled into a definition or an axiom; (4) it doesn't prove too much — if the same form would also prove a maximally great island or a supreme pizza, something has gone wrong; (5) the key terms stay fixed and mean one thing throughout, no sliding between senses of 'great' or 'positive.'

The case I want you to assess is the version of Gödel's proof that goes roughly like this. Call a property positive when its negation is not positive, so exactly one of a property and its complement is positive. Positive properties are closed under necessary entailment: if F is positive and necessarily everything F is G, then G is positive too. Define being God-like as having all and only the positive properties. Assume that God-likeness is itself a positive property, and further that necessary existence — existing necessarily — is a positive property. From this you get the little lemma that every positive property is possibly instantiated: an impossible positive property would vacuously entail every property, including ones whose complements it also entails, which clashes with the closure and complement axioms. So God-likeness is possibly instantiated. Then, with S5 and an essence-based account of necessary existence (a thing exists necessarily just in case every property essential to it is necessarily instantiated), that bare possibility gets upgraded to actual and necessary existence.

So here's what I want: is that a proof by the five criteria? Go through them one by one, and where the criteria don't force a verdict, say so and say whether the fault is in the criteria or in the argument. I've also seen it claimed that Gödel's system has a side effect sometimes called modal collapse — that with a couple natural additions, every actual truth comes out neccessary. If that's right, tell me which criterion it's best filed under, or whether it's a separate sixth problem that the criteria miss. My suspicion is that everything interesting is happening in (2) and (4) and the modal machinery is mostly decoration; tell me if you disagree.
