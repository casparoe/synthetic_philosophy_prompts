I teach a decision theory class, and we've been doing Pascal's wager in its usual two-by-two form, where God either exists or doesn't and you either wager or don't, and the infinite payoff does all the heavy lifting. A student came to office hours and asked what would happen if we stopped treating God's existence as a fixed feature of the world and made it depend on how many people wager. I ended up sketching a variant that I'd like to work through properly, because the more I think about it, the less it looks like the standard one-person wager.

Here's the setup. There are N people deciding simultaneously. There's a known threshold T. If at least T of them wager, then God—let's just accept this as a stipulated feature of the scenario—becomes actual, and every person who wagered gets the infinite reward, while the non-wagerers get nothing. If fewer than T wager, no God is actualized, and wagering turns out to be a pure cost (say -c), while not wagering gets some finite worldly payoff (say +b). Everyone knows N and T. The usual dominance-style argument seems to me to break apart in this version: if you think at least T others will wager, wagering looks like the only sane move; if you think fewer than T-1 others will wager, not wagering looks better; and if you think exactly T-1 others will wager, then your choice is pivotal and wagering looks enormous. So it starts to feel like a coordination game with an infinite stake rather than a private decision under uncertainty.

How does the standard wagering analysis change in this setup? Does any version of Pascal's dominance argument survive, and what should a rational agent actually do? I'm especially interested in whether the infinite payoff short-curcuits everything, and what changes if the reward is instead huge but finite.
