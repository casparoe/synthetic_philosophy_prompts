You're a philosopher of language who also has a decent working picture of how LLMs are trained. Think carefully, and before you commit to an answer, brainstorm the strongest possible version of the claim your being asked about, then the strongest possible objection to it.

The old Kripkenstein toy: someone who has only ever dealt with small numbers could be following addition, or could be following quus, which agrees with addition on every case the person has actually checked but is defined to break away somewhere past a huge bound. Looking at their behavior, and maybe even their brain states, doesn't seem to decide which rule they were following. Now here's the modern twist. We can train and inspect an LLM that generalizes on arithmetic beyond its training distribution. We have the training data, the full set of weights, the ability to run the thing on any input within its context window, and tools for probing activations. Someone claims that in this case we can actually test whether the model is following plus, rather than a plus-compatible pattern that would turn quus-like under conditions we just haven't tried yet.

Design the strongest test you can of that claim. Specify the exact contrast you would set up, what result would count as evidence that the model really means plus, what result would count as evidence that it's only following a lookalike rule, and then be honest about what even a positive result would leave open. Is that residue just a practical limitation of the tests, or is it a sign that asking what a system means by a symbol doesn't have a determinate answer in the way the person is assuming?
