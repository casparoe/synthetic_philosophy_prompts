right, so I've been reading Keynes on probability and uncertainty, and I've sketched out my own view for a seminar. Before I present it I'd like a consistancy check, because something feels off but I can't put my finger on where. Here is what I'm committed to:

(1) Probability is evidential: it's the degree to which the available evidence rationally obliges belief in a proposition. Two people with the same evidence should assign the same degrees of belief. It's not just subjective feeling, and it's not long-run frequency.

(2) Most real probabilities aren't precise numbers. Plenty of them are only comparable pairwise (this is more probable than that, without assigning either a number), and some pairs just aren't comparable at all.

(3) The weight of evidence matters separately from probability. A 0.7 probability of rain backed by decades of records is a better basis for acting than a 0.7 cranked out by a model fitted on six data points, even though both say 0.7.

(4) Rational choice under uncertainty means maximising expected utility: multiply the probability of each outcome by its value, and pick the act with the highest total.

(5) Incompleteness of probability (from (2)) doesn't paralyse decision: we make reasonable choices under partial comparability all the time, and a theory of rational choice ought to say these are rational.

Which pairs are actually in tension, and which only look that way? Rank whatever tensions you find by severity, and for each say what has to give and what the cheapest fix is. Then tell me which single revision would restore the most consistency overall. And could you answer in British English please.
