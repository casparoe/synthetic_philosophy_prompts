I've been reading about the old Mu'tazila–Ash'arite fight over what makes acts good or bad, and I've ended up convinced of a version of the Ash'arite line that I want you to try to break. The position is this. The Mu'tazila treated moral terms as picking out real features acts have independently of God's will, so God commands the good because it is good. I think that's wrong. On my view good and bad applied to acts are just shorthand for juridical statuses: what is good is what God commands, and what is evil is what God forbids. Reason can establish that God exists and that he commands, but there are no prior moral properties for reason to find, so the so-called dilemma—does God command it because it's good, or is it good because he commands it?—is actually a bad question, not a dilemma I have to answer.

What I want is a map of what would count against this. Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether a defender of my view can honestly absorb it and what they'd have to give up to do so. In particular: does the arbitrariness objection (if God could command lying, lying would be good) actually land, or does it beg the question by assuming the objective moral properties I deny? Does my position commit me to saying God is powerful but not just in any sense we recognise? Is it a problem that revelation and ordinary moral language seem to work by appealing to an antecedent sense of fairness? And be straight with me: have I insulated the view until it's unfalsifiable by defining good that way, and if so is that a defect of this kind of view or just what taking divine command theory seriously amounts to?
