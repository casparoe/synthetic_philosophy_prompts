I am a professor of philosophy who sits on my university's academic honor council, and I have been asked to turn the council's case-by-case intuitions into a written procedure the members can actually apply. The council hears roughly forty contested cases each year—plagiarism, purchased essays, falsified data, unauthorized collaboration—and must decide, ordinarily within a single hearing, whether to impose a grade penalty, probation, or suspension, or instead to route a student toward remediation, accommodation, or medical withdrawal. The members are faculty, staff, and students, most without philosophical training, and we have noticed that two structurally similar cases can produce very different outcomes depending on how sympathetic the student seems. I want to give them a decision procedure drawn from Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes: the view that resentment, indignation, and guilt are not chiefly instruments of deterrence or retribution but partly constitute our standing with one another as mutually accountable persons, and that to hold someone responsible is, in the first instance, to be disposed to address these attitudes to them.

Please write this procedure so that it makes the following distinctions usable by non-philosophers. First, the difference between an act being excused—through ignorance, accident, coercion, or circumstances that keep the act from expressing the student's quality of will—and the student being exempted from the reactive attitudes altogether, so that the objective stance is warranted because the student is not, for now, an appropriate target of resentment and blame. Second, the difference between blameworthiness and institutional response: a grade penalty or separation may be justified on grounds of fairness or academic integrity even where blame is mitigated, and the procedure should keep those two questions apart rather than letting one settle the other. Third, the moral cost of the objective stance itself: treating a student as something to be managed, remediated, or removed rather than answered can be a way of withdrawing from a relationship rather than a discovery about the student, and it can be condescending while appearing kind. The procedure should therefore say when the council may not take the objective stance, and it should mark every step at which judgment cannot be eliminated—in particular, assessing whether a proffered excuse is true, whether ignorance was culpable, and how severe an incapacity must be before a student counts as exempt. Then apply the procedure to three cases: a final-year student who copied two sentences without attribution while caring for an ill parent and working nights; a first-year international student who reused an essay they had written for an earlier course and says no one had told them this was misconduct; and a student with an undisclosed psychiatric condition who purchased a paper and insists they are fine. Include at least one application in which the procedure yields a conclusion the council will find uncomfortable, and close by saying what the procedure cannot decide.
