There's a practice on big video platforms that bugs me. Most of them have a rule against posting terrorist propaganda or similar inciting material, full stop. But several also have an exception saying the same footage can stay up when its shared for educational, documentary, or counter-speech purposes and is clearly labelled as such. In practice the exception barely functions: the upload gets automatically matched against a database of already-banned footage and removed before the label or the commentary is even looked at, and the person has to appeal afterwards, by which time the propaganda has already spread through less careful channels. I'm not asking whether the platforms are being inconsistent or lying about their values. I want to know what account of the harm of propaganda this whole setup presupposes. Does the blanket ban presuppose that propaganda harms by exposure alone, like a pollutant, while the educational exception presupposes that harm depends on how an audience takes the material up? Can both be true in one policy, or does the automated enforcement quietly commit the platform to one of them? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change in the design—matching, review, appeals—if the harm of propaganda were treated as fully context-sensitive: a matter of audience, framing, and likely uptake, not of the bare content.
