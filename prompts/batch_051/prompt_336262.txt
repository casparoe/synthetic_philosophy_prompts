I'm drafting an essay under the working title "National Character as Equilibrium, Not Essence," and I want your comments on the argument before I commit to writing the full piece. Below are my working notes, treated as a proposal; after them I'll specify exactly what kind of comments I need.

The notes:

The essay starts from a dilemma. Public discourse about national character seems trapped between two unacceptable options. The eliminativist option says that talk of national character is disguised stereotyping and should go. The folk-realist option says that such talk tracks real cross-cultural differences and is just the layperson's version of cross-cultural psychology. Both options, I claim, share a hidden premise: that a national trait, if real, would be a disposition carried around by individuals — something like an internalized tendency to behave in a certain way across a wide range of situations. The essay's target is that premise.

My positive proposal: national traits are properties of social equilibria, not of individual minds. Claims like "the Swiss are punctual" or "Germans are procedurally rigorous" are badly parsed. The correct parsing is something like: within a given institutional setting — transport networks, appointment conventions, enforcement practices, reciprocal expectations — a behavioral regularity obtains, and that regularity persists because it is costly to deviate from and because others expect it. The trait is distributed across persons-plus-institutions; individuals figure in it only as bearers of situation-sensitive, conditional patterns (for example, treating lateness as costly under these conditions), not as carriers of a portable national essence. Thus "generalizing about a people" and "generalizing about a society" come apart: the first is usually either false or covertly essentializing, while the second can be a perfectly sober description of a set of self-reinforcing arrangements.

The essay then draws four consequences. First, on coherence: national character becomes a coherent notion only after this transformation — person-level dispositions do not aggregate into stable, cross-context national types, but equilibrium-level regularities are exactly the sort of thing that can be stable and explanatory. Second, on acceptability: a generalization about a national group is acceptable exactly when it can be expanded, without strain, into a claim that names the maintaining institutions and the situational bounds; it is unacceptable when it asserts a portable individual disposition or treats the trait as explaining an individual's behavior without situational detail. Third, on the dialectic: this vindicates neither full eliminativism nor folk realism — it is a third position, on which national-character talk survives but only in a domain-restricted form. Fourth, on practice: social scientists may use national-level averages as reduced-form descriptions of equilibria; journalists and politicians should drop dispositional idioms because those idioms systematically misrepresent where the regularities live; and using nationality as a practical proxy in individual decisions is defensible only under the constraints that govern any statistical proxy, never as a folk-psychological essence.

Those four consequences are the parts I expect a critic to doubt. My suspicion is that the second and fourth may run ahead of the first and third, and that the core disposition/equilibrium contrast may turn out to be less clean than I am presenting it.

Now, please comment on the proposal as a sharp, sympathetic-but-critical referee would. Organize your response in the following sections, in this order:

1. "The core contrast" (about 250 words): does the equilibrium/disposition distinction actually do the work I claim, or does it merely relocate the problem — since equilibria are sustained, in part, by individual dispositions and expectations?
2. "The strongest objection" (about 300 words): the most damaging criticism of the essay's thesis, stated as forcefully as I should expect from a hostile reader.
3. "Weakest step" (about 200 words): which of the four consequences is least supported by the first part of the argument, and why.
4. "Missing distinctions" (about 200 words): one or two distinctions I need to draw but have not.
5. "Verdict and next steps" (about 150 words): whether the essay is worth writing, what to strengthen first, and what to cut.

Engage only with the argument; do not suggest readings, citations, or bibliography.
