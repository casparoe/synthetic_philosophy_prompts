I've been reading about wu-wei, and I keep coming back to a parallel with something from my old life in physiology. The paradox is usually put as: you can't try to be effortless, because the trying is itself a kind of straining. But sports science knows a version of this. There's solid evidence that when a skilled performer starts attending to the internal mechanics of a practiced action — the position of the elbow, the order of the footwork — performance falls apart; the centipede's dilemma, choking under pressure. The standard fix isn't to tell the athlete to stop trying, because that's just another instruction to monitor themselves. Instead you redirect attention outward — the flight of the ball, the sound at contact, a pre-shot routine — and the automatic system takes back over. That looks to me structurally identical to the Daoist problem of how to cultivate wu-wei without turning it into one more goal to strain after: you don't suppress the deliberative mind, you give it something harmless to do. My qustion is whether this is a real structural match or a loose metaphor. If it is real, then the motor-control results should transfer: wu-wei should be trainable by attentional redirection rather than by trying harder to let go. Where does the analogy give out? I suspect it's in the fact that wu-wei is supposed to be a whole ethical stance — responding spontaneously to other people and situations — and not just fluent execution of a learned skill.
