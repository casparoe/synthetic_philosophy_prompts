I've been losing an argument with myself about what makes promises bind, and I tried to write down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases to see if there's any principle underneath them. My best shot so far is something like: a promise binds unless the promisee lets me off, or I never really made it (coercion, didn't mean it), or keeping it would make me forgo something genuinely more weighty than what the promise gives the other person. But I already see that third clause slipping around — it threatens to excuse the concert case below as long as I care about the concert enough. So treat that as a rough draft to fix or abandon. Here are the cases:

1. I agree to help Sam move on Saturday. On Thursday he texts me that his brother showed up with a truck and he doesn't need me. I don't go and feel zero guilt. I think the promise just stops binding.

2. I promise my partner I'll pick up the dry cleaning before the shop closes. Walking there I run into an old friend sobbing alone on a stoop, so I sit with her for an hour and the shop closes. I don't think I did anything wrong: what she needed was bigger than clean shirts.

3. I promise a colleague I'll get comments on his manuscript to him by Friday. Thursday night someone offers me a ticket to a band I've wanted to see for a decade, so I go, and the comments go out Monday. I think that was wrong, even though I really badly wanted to go.

4. At a bar, a drunk acquaintance corners me about his startup and says "promise me you'll put money in." I say "sure, sure" just to end it. I don't invest. I think that was never a real promise.

5. Someone with compromising photos of me says he'll send them out unless I promise to bring him $500 the next day. I promise, then I don't show. I think I'm not bound; it was extortion.

6. In his last weeks, a close friend asks me to promise to hand-deliver a letter to his estranged daughter after he dies, explaining a decision he made years ago. He dies before I do it. I think I'm bound — if anything, more bound than before. Its not just that I want to honor him; it feels like the promise still has a grip on me that noone on earth could now release.

The pair I can't make coherent is (1) and (6). If the promisee saying "you're off the hook" is enough to dissolve the obligation, why doesn't the promisee being gone altogether dissolve it, or at least freeze it? And I also can't state the line between (2) and (3) in a way that doesn't collapse back into "how much I prefer it," which would make (3) come out fine if I preferred the concert enough. So: can you give me a principle — or two or three candidate principles — that gets these verdicts as close as possible? Please say, for each principle, exactly what it predicts about a case I keep going back and forth on: I've promised to attend a friend's wedding, but a week before I'm offered a place on a two-week trip to see a total solar eclipse that I'll almost certainly never see otherwise. And be honest about which of my verdicts the principle forces me to give up, and why.
