I teach a module on group decision-making for PPE undergrads, about 24 students, one 90-minute session. I want an exercise where they actually feel the discursive dilemma rather than just hear me explain it: the thing where a committee can have majority support for premise A and majority support for premise B, and also a majority against the conclusion that follows from A and B, all at the same time, so the group view comes out different depending on whether you aggregate votes premise-by-premise or just take a vote on the final question. They've read the abstract version but it doesnt land.

Design a runnable in-class game or simulation that makes this happen to them: rules, a concrete case with two or three premises and a conclusion (not the standard judge/contract one if you can avoid it — maybe a university disciplinary panel, or a club deciding whether to fund a trip), a ballot procedure, a points or payoff scheme that doesn't need real money, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the formal structure. I want them to notice the contradiction themselves before I name it. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students spotting the trick and voting strategically, the case not producing clean majorities, someone arguing the premises aren't really independent — and how to steer around that. The aim isn't to reach the correct verdict, it's to make the aggregation problem feel unavoidable.
