I have been trying to pin down what I mean by "physical" when I say I am a physicalist, and my settled verdicts on particular cases seem to pull in incompatible directions. If a future physics were to introduce a kind of entity as different from today's ontology as electromagnetic fields were from eighteenth-century mechanics, I would count that entity as physical; physicalism should not be hostage to the present contents of physics. But I refuse to concede that physicalism is just the view that the world contains whatever an ideal completed science would contain, since that would let someone count irreducible mental forces as physical simply by writing them into the ideal theory. At the same time, I would never say that pain is non-physical merely because the word "pain" does not occur in fundamental physics; a pain realized in a nervous system is physical through and through. Is there one account of what it is to be physical that delivers all three of these verdicts? If there is, state it precisely and test it against at least one further case I have not listed; if there is not, tell me which of my verdicts should be revised and what principle should take its place. Please answer in continuous prose, as you would in a letter; no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
