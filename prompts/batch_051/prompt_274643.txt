Suppose we could replace each neuron in a person's brain one at a time with an artificial component that exactly reproduces the replaced neuron's input–output behavior, carrying out the procedure gradually enough that the patient remains conscious throughout, never notices the replacements except by being told about them, and is afterward indistinguishable from the original in behavior, memory reports, and dispositions. One philosopher holds that this is clearly a survival case: there is no moment at which the person goes out of existence, so the original person survives. Another holds that the procedure merely substitutes a duplicate over time, and that the original person is eliminated just as surely as in a scan-and-copy upload, only without an observable break. Are these philosophers disagreeing about a fact, or are they recommending different ways of using terms like "same person" and "survives"? Design the strongest test you can—using third-person observations, first-person reports, or both—that would bear on the claim that the original person survives such gradual replacement. State what results would count for and against the claim, and then say as precisely as you can what no test could settle; in particular, address whether any observation could distinguish the preservation of the original subject of experience from the mere preservation of its memories, dispositions, and outward behavior. Does the untestable residue show that the dispute is partly conceptual rather than entirely empirical?
