I'm coming at this as a TA for a jurisprudence seminar, and I need to mark a long exam answer on the Hart-Fuller debate over the postwar German grudge informer cases — courts after the war had to figure out what to do about people who'd denounced neighbours under statutes the old regime had validly enacted. The student clearly did the reading, and the answer hits a lot of the right beats, but a couple of moves feel almost-right rather than right, and I don't want to mark by gestalt, or by whether the answer matches the standard two-camps summary then by whether the argument goes through. The question was:

"Hart and Fuller disagreed about a successor court's options. Suppose an authoritarian regime, now fallen, had a published criminal statute requiring citizens to report 'disloyal remarks' to the secret police; in practice the police also followed unpublished directives that broadened the offence and ignored the statute's own limits. Explain (a) what Hart's rule of recognition implies about whether the statute was law and how the successor court should treat it, (b) what Fuller's internal morality of law implies about the same, and (c) whether either side establishes a necessary connection between law and morality. Then give your own view."

Here is the student's answer:

"Under Hart's positivism, legal validity is a social fact: in the old regime, officials had a convergent practice of treating whatever the regime's formal procedures turned out as binding, and that practice is what the rule of recognition names. So the informer statute was valid law under that rule, and saying it was 'not law' because it was wicked conflates the law as it is with the law as it ought to be. His remedy is the honest one: the successor court should punish informers, if it thinks it must, under an explicitly retrospective statute, accepting the fair-warning cost instead of pretending the old statute had always been void.

But Hart also has his minimum content of natural law. Because humans are vulnerable and resources are scarce, any legal system that is going to survive has to protect life, bodily integrity, property, and at least elementary promise-keeping; without those, persons have no reason to obey anything. That shows the separation thesis isn't as strict as people think — Hart has a natural-law floor. So with an informer statute this far outside the floor, one that turns neighbours into instruments of the secret police, Hart can't consistently maintain that it really was law; he has to accept the natural-law conclusion that it lacks the minimum content and is at most organized force rather than law.

Fuller agrees with the bottom line but not the route. For him law is the enterprise of subjecting human conduct to the governance of rules, and a legal order only merits the name if it meets the eight desiderata: generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, consistency, possibility of compliance, stability, and congruence between announced rules and official action. The old regime failed congruence, because the published informer statute was run alongside unpublished directives that the police treated as overriding, so the official action didn't match the declared rules. A system that fails the internal morality in that way isn't producing law in the full sense, so the successor court can hold the statute void without needing retrospective legislation. And that is the deeper point: the eight desiderata aren't just machinery. A state that satisfies them is, to that extent, a substantively just state, which is what makes Fuller a natural lawyer."
