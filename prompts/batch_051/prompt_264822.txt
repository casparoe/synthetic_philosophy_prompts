As a science journalist, I have spent much of the past few months on the physics and philosophy of time, and an argument I keep returning to will not let me go. Here is how I understand the Rietdijk–Putnam argument. Special relativity tells us that simultaneity is not invariant: observers in relative motion slice spacetime into different sets of simultaneous events. Now fix my present—say, the moment I raise a coffee cup here on Earth—and choose some other, spacelike-separated event that, in my frame, lies in the future. Because the separation is spacelike, there is an inertial frame in which that event is simultaneous with my raising of the cup; in that frame the event is not future but present, and it is just as real as the cup itself. If every event that is simultaneous with my present in some valid frame is real, and if reality cannot be relative to a choice of frame, then the supposedly future event is already real. And because the choice of event was arbitrary, the same reasoning reaches every event in spacetime. Past, present, and future are all equally real. The block universe is not one interpretation among many; it is forced by the physics.

So here is my question. If special relativity really establishes the block universe, why do philosophers of physics still treat presentism, the growing block view, and the idea of an open future as live positions? Why do physicists go on talking about events as though they come into being, when a century of empirical success has already settled the matter? What could the dissenters possibly be appealing to, apart from a sentimental attachment to the moving now?
