I'm not a philosophy person, so maybe I'm useing these words wrong. But I been chewing on a passage in Bertrand Russell's On Denoting. He writes:

"For example, we know that the center of mass of the solar system at a definite instant is some definite point, and we can affirm a number of propositions about it; but we have no immediate acquaintance with this point, which is only known to us by description."

He's mostly talking about how you can say things about something you're not directly in touch with because you reach it only through a description. Now I want to vary his famous puzzle about "the present King of France is bald" and see if the same machinery holds. First, swap the empty description for a fictional name: "Sherlock Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street," and suppose the sentence appears inside one of Conan Doyle's stories. Second variation: a writer puts a character in a story with the same name as a real person he knows, but everything the story says about that character is false of the real person. Can you give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, covering: what Russell's analysis would say about the original present-King-of-France case, what it says about the Sherlock-inside-the-story case, what happens in the real-name-total-falsehood case, whether the analysis changes when you go from a definite description to a proper name, and where the description-analysis starts to break down if it does.
