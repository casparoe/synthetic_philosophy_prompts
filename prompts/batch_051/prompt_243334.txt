I'm a high school student, and over the past year I have been reading online arguments about AI risk. The view I keep finding myself almost convinced by is structural: the profit motive, or more generally the competitive market in which AI labs operate, is the root cause of dangerous AI development. The argument is not that anyone in particular is malicious. It is that a lab that slows down to do safety work, runs extensive evaluations, or declines to release a capable model will simply be outcompeted by a hungrier rival. If the market rewards speed and capability, then well-intentioned people are systematically selected against or made irrelevant, and moralizing about bad executives or irresponsible engineers misses what is actually doing the work. I am drawn to this view because it is unifying (one mechanism explains many superficially different failures), it predicts that appeals to conscience will not work at scale, and it gives a tidy prescription: change incentives through regulation, auditing, or international coordination rather than asking people to be heroic at their own expense.

But I also have an intuition I cannot shake, and it contradicts the full-strength version of that theory. Even inside a competitive market, no one is literally forced. At each step where a dangerous capability gets built or shipped, a particular person at a keyboard, a particular manager signing off, or a particular CEO with a comfortable cash cushion could choose differently. Not every lab is on the edge of bankruptcy; some have enough of a technical lead or enough money to pause and still survive. An engineer could refuse a dangerous project and take a worse-paying job elsewhere; an executive could decide to be the one who slows down. My intuition is that the structural story treats a strong incentive as if it were a compulsion, and thereby dissolves individual agency in places where agency is still real.

Which of these should give way? Please present the strongest version of the structural side and the strongest version of the agency side, and then give me a negotiated verdict. Should the intuition be debunked and dropped as a storytelling bias? Should the structural theory be amended with scope conditions — for example, that it applies only in tight, symmetrical races with thin margins, and not to leading actors with slack? Should it be abandoned? Or is there a reading on which the two are compatible, such as that the market sets the baseline while individual choices move the margin, so that capitalism is an enabling condition rather than the root cause? Rather than only picking a side, say what would justify each of those moves. Finally, I want to know where your answer is fragile: which argument or consideration should make you reverse the verdict, and which step in your reasoning is most likely to fail. End with a single machine-readable line in the form \answer{...}, where the text inside the braces is a short label for your final verdict, such as debunk the intuition, amend the theory, abandon the theory, or compatible.
