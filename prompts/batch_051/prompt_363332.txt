I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think about translation, and my gut keeps handing down verdicts I can't stitch together. Maybe you can find the principle.

A novel where a character makes a pun on "bank" (river vs. money). Translated into Spanish, the translator uses "banco," which carries both meanings, so the joke survives more or less intact. Fine, obviously.

The same pun, but the target language has no comparable double meaning. The translator drops the river/money content and writes a different pun that lands the same way in the scene. I think that's fine too — better than a footnote.

A short story where a working-class character speaks in heavy dialect. The translator renders it in standard, slightly informal prose because the nearest target-language dialect would locate the character in the wrong region and class. I'm not fine with this. I'd rather have an awkward invented dialect or a few nonstandard markers than lose the fact that this character talks diffrent from the narrator.

A scene where two characters shift from polite to intimate forms of address (tu and vous, that kind of thing) and the target language is English. The translator has them switch to first names at that moment to mark the shift. Fine, I think.

A novel set in the American South where a white character uses a racial slur. The translator softens it to something like "those people." I think that's wrong — it makes the character less ugly than the author made him, and that's not the translator's call. But I also don't think you can just drop in the target language's hardest slur as an equivalent, since the histories differ. I'm okay with the translator finding something in between that preserves that it's ugly and racialized without pretending it's the same word.

A character eats "un cassoulet." The translator writes "a rich bean stew." Fine.

A poem where the translator keeps imagery and line breaks but gives up rhyme and meter entirely, and says so in a preface. I don't think that's a translation of the poem. I'd insist on calling it an adaptation or a version, no matter how good it is.

The case I keep wobbling on: a comic novel whose humor is mostly referential — characters joke about specific local celebrities and TV shows. The translator swaps in target-culture equivalents (a different comedian, a different game show) so it's just as funny but all the cultural texture is different. Half the time I think that's exactly like the pun; half the time I think it's a betrayal.

The closest I've got to a principle is: translate what the text does, not what it says — except when what it says is part of the point. But that rule doesn't decide the celebrity-reference case, and it seems to imply I should translate "un cassoulet" as "a cassoulet" or explain it, which I don't actually believe.

Is there a principle that gets all or most of my verdicts? What does it say about the celebrity-reference case, and about a religious text that relies on wordplay with names? And which of my verdicts does it force me to drop?
