After a career in atmospheric physics, where an instrument was not considered trustworthy unless its response function was known, reproducible, and documented, I have lately been reading moral philosophy and have found myself puzzled by an asymmetry in the way we evaluate human and computational moral reasoners. Consider a machine that must make ethically significant choices under uncertainty, such as triaging access to intensive care or resolving an unavoidable-accident trade-off in a vehicle. The public and many regulators appear to require that such a system implement a rule that is explicit, consistent across cases, auditable, and promulgated in advance; a learned model whose dispositions cannot be inspected is treated as unacceptable, and a system that made intuitive, case-by-case departures from its own rule would be returned as defective. Yet for human beings performing the same tasks—a triage nurse, a driver in the split second before a collision—we routinely tolerate opacity, discretion, and inconsistency, and often dignify them as experience, clinical judgment, or moral seriousness. A nurse whose implicit triage decisions have no explicit decision function and vary noticeably across cases is not recalled as a faulty instrument; she may be praised. So we appear to demand that the machine's ethical reasoning be of a kind we never demand of our own.

I would like to know whether this asymmetry is justified, and if so by what. The candidate justifications I can formulate are these: human beings can be held accountable and can answer a demand for reasons after the fact, whereas an algorithm's dispositions are final unless someone redesigns it; a human's inconsistency is local and idiosyncratic, whereas an algorithm is deployed at scale and systemizes any error it encodes; and a human being is the sort of entity whose unforced moral judgment carries at least some authority, whereas a machine is an artifact into which our values are being deliberately written. Each seems partly right and partly special pleading. Please identify the strongest candidate, or supply a better one, and test it against hard cases: a human triage officer whose emergency judgments are statistically demonstrably biased, and a machine rule deliberately written with a vague discretionary clause such as except where manifestly unjust, which its designers cannot fully specify. Should the asymmetry survive these cases? Should we accept the human and reject the machine in them, or should we begin holding one to the other's standard?
