We have just finished a unit on the philosophy of religion, and I have been trying to set out an objection to the doctrine of the Trinity as a numbered argument. As the doctrine was presented to us, the Father is God, the Son is God and the Holy Spirit is God; the persons are distinct, so the Father is not the Son, the Son is not the Spirit and the Spirit is not the Father; and yet there is only one God, not three. For brevity I have used only the Father and the Son; adding the Holy Spirit would strengthen the conclusion but not change the difficulty.

(1) The Father is God.
(2) The Son is God.
(3) The Father is not the Son.
(4) If a thing A is God, and a thing B is God, and A is not B, then there are at least two Gods.
(5) Therefore, from (1), (2), (3) and (4), there are at least two Gods.
(6) But the doctrine also says that there is exactly one God.
(7) Therefore the doctrine entails a contradiction.
(8) Therefore the doctrine of the Trinity is logically incoherent.

The argument strikes me as too quick, but I cannot say exactly where it goes wrong. If I read 'is God' in (1) and (2) as identity, then the argument is valid, but (1), (2) and (3) are already in tension, because identity is transitive; a defender would then have to reject one of them, which would mean rejecting the doctrine as stated. If instead I read 'is God' as saying what each person is, rather than which God each person is identical to, then I do not see what licenses (4), and I worry the doctrine then says there are three divine things, which sounds like tritheism. One further thought I had is that 'the same God' and 'the same person' might be two different ways of counting, so the Father and the Son could be the same God without being the same person; but I do not know whether that idea is coherent or whether it simply abandons the ordinary logic of identity.

What I want to know is this: is the argument valid as written? If it is valid, which premise fails on the strongest orthodox reading of the doctrine? If it is not valid, identify the exact step at which the inference breaks and say what principle would have to be added to make it go through. In particular, could a defender of the doctrine allow that there are three distinct persons each of whom is God while denying that there are three Gods, and if so, what account of counting makes that position consistent?
