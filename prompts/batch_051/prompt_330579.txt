I'm a journalist that covers science and ideas, so I spend a lot of time in listening-to-theologians-and-then-overthinking-it mode. At a confernece I heard a proposal for how an afterlife could avoid the proportionality problem, and I want it stress-tested as a mechanism, not as a doctrine. I don't care, for now, whether the metaphysics is true or whether the deity doing this would be good; I care how actual people would behave under it.

Here's the proposal. A new religious movement teaches that the afterlife works like this. Harm you knowingly cause is converted into a finite moral debt, measured in something like adjusted victim-hours: the duration and severity of the suffering you caused, from the victim's perspective. You pay the debt by undergoing the victim's experience at the same intensity, plus a twenty percent surcharge that goes into a general restitution pool used to reduce everyone's remaining sentence. There is no eternal torment. Because finite acts by finite beings can only produce finite debt, everyone eventually pays it off and exits; universal salvation is, in effect, structurally guaranteed. But there's a catch. At death, every person with unpaid debt gets a single, informed, irreversible choice: start the repayment process, or be annihilated painlessly on the spot. People who choose annihilation just stop existing. People who choose repayment are eventually rehabilitated.

So you get this weird mashup of annihilation and universal salvation, with finite-but-huge punishment doing the moral work. Walk me through how this plays out. I want to know what it incentivizes among ordinary selfish believers, genuine sociopaths, very anxious rule-followers, the people who run the religion's institutions, and communities trying to enforce norms around atonement and forgiveness. Where does it get gamed first? Does the annihilation escape hatch undermine the whole thing, or does it actually make the system more credible? And does the restitution pool create a market in other people's redemption or anything like that?

You're an expert in incentive design and moral psychology. Think carefully, and before you settle on an answer, brainstorm privately at least two or three ways this system gets gamed. Then give me your committed verdict, not a survey of possibilities. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
