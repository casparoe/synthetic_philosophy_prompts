I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and a recurring theme in my reporting is how people describe their own values. Across interviews about hard choices—career versus caregiving, loyalty versus honesty—I've noticed that people talk about "my values" in the plural, but when a conflict gets concrete, they usually reach for a single underlying currency. A source will insist that honesty and kindness are two separate values; press her on a case where they pull apart, and she will often re-describe the honesty as a long-run form of kindness—the painful truth was, she says, "the kind thing in the end"—so that one of the two values quietly becomes a denomination of the other. I've come to suspect that a great deal of apparent value pluralism is monism in disguise, and I've worked out a diagnostic I call the Remainder Criterion: a value is genuinely distinct from another only if choosing against it leaves a remainder—a loss that no amount of the chosen value can make up for, something with no honest exchange rate. If a person can fully account for the sacrifice in the winner's terms ("I lost the friendship, but the meaningful work was worth that cost"), then there was never a second value on the table, only a second price tag. By contrast, when someone gets the career and still finds that the betrayal sits in a category of its own—not just less of something, but a debt that nothing denominated in the chosen value could repay—then two values really were in conflict.

I'd like a candid assessment of the criterion itself, not a defense of my suspicion. Is it coherent? Is it already in the philosophical literature under a name I haven't found, and if so, is that idea normally taken to establish monism, or only something weaker such as commensurability? Most importantly, where does it break down: are there cases of genuinely distinct values that leave no remainder, or cases of a single value that leaves one? Please reply in American English.
