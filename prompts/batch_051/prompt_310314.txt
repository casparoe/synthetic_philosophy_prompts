I just spent the whole walk home after a long argument with a friend trying to figure out if he actually had a point or was just being annoying. My position was that if you have some optimizer—an RL agent, a planning system, whatever—and you watch it make enough choices in enough different situations, you can pretty much read off what it's optimizing for, the same way revealed preference theory is supposed to let economists recover preferences from choice behavior. You dont need to open the black box; the behavior basically is the objective, at least operationally. My friend kept insisting I was missing something about the search space: he said two systems could pick the same action out of the same menu, but one might have searched over thousands of possible plans while the other only ever considered two, and that the choice alone can't tell you what the system was even capable of representing, let alone what it was actually trying to do. To me that sounded like a practical measurement problem, not a deep limit—if they'd also make the same choices across all the relevant counterfactual menus, shouldn't your inferred objective just converge to the same thing, with the search space stuff washing out as noise?

So my question is: does the search space point actually undermine the behavior-to-objective inference, or is it a red herring? What exactly can you infer about an optimizer's objective from watching its choices, and where does the revealed-preference-style idea break down (or hold up) once you also need to recover the space of options it searched over?
