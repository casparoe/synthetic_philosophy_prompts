You’re an expert on ancient Greek economic thought, especially Aristotle’s Politics, and you have a good eye for the unstated principle behind someone’s verdicts. Start by brainstorming the possible rules that could fit the track record I’m about to give you, then work through what each rule predicts. Don’t just jump to the most famous Aristotle quote.

My friend Dana has gotten really into Aristotle on money and exchange, and she keeps issuing verdicts about economic practices as if she’s running his playbook. Here’s her track record so far:

- She approves of two neighboring households bartering olive oil for tools.
- She approves of a farmer selling his surplus grain at the market and using the money to buy clothing for the household.
- She approves of a craftsman borrowing money from a neighbor to buy materials, so long as he pays back exactly what he borrowed.
- She disapproves of a merchant who buys cheap and sells dear with no interest in using the goods herself, just to pile up money.
- She thinks currency exchange for profit is also bad.
- She is extremely hard on lending money at interest. Her paraphrase of Aristotle in the Politics is that interest makes money itself generate more money, which is unnatural because money was brought into being for the sake of exchange, not to be a parent to further money.
- She wavers on a farmer who stores grain after harvest and sells it later at a higher price. She ends up saying it’s okay if the extra money goes back into the farm’s upkeep, but if it just gets hoarded, then no.

Now for the new case: a community grain bank collects grain from local members after harvest, stores it, and sells it to the public later at a markup that covers only storage costs and a small emergency reserve. The reserve is used to lend grain to members at no extra charge when their crops fail. Nobody involved is trying to accumulate money, and any surplus above costs goes back into the reserve.

Given Dana’s earlier verdicts, what’s her most likely verdict on the grain bank? I’m less interested in the answer itself than in the rule she’s implicitely running, the other rules that fit her track record just as well, and which further case would best tell them apart.
