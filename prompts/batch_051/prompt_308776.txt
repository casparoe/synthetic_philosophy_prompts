My philosophy of medicine reading group has been stuck for a term on the naturalism vs. normativism debate about health and disease. One member is a naturalist in the broad Boorse tradition: health is normal biological functioning, disease is a departure from it, and 'normal' is a statistical-empirical notion with no evaluative content smuggled in. The other is a normativist who thinks you can't even pick out which biological differences count as malfunctions without a value-laden picture of what a body is for. They've agreed to stop arguing and write an adversarial-collaboration joint statement instead. Draft that statement for them.

It should have three sections.

First, a numbered list of claims both can sign. These should be stronger than truisms — include claims about specific hard cases (hypertension cutoffs, the removal of homosexuality from the DSM, a statistically normal but painful age-related decline, something like that) where both agree on the fact and only disagree about what the fact shows. Avoid 'values sometimes influence diagnostic practice in messy real life' filler.

Second, a disagreements table. For each row: the claim in dispute, the naturalist's exact formulation, the normativist's exact formulation — each worded so it's own side would say yes, that's my view, not a strawman — and a note on wether that row is a genuine substantive disagreement or mostly a fight about the word 'disease.' Concept-vs-conception rows should be flagged as such, with the residual substantive issue, if any, stated separately.

Third, a cruxes section: three or four sub-questions where a settled answer would actually move at least one of the two. For each crux, give a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides would accept bears on it, and write down in advance what each predicts about it. At least one crux should target the naturalist's notion of proper function — whether a value-free choice of reference class and environment is even possible — and at least one should target the normativist's claim that harm or disadvantage is doing the real classificatory work. Also invent one fictional diagnostic case that doesn't just collapse into either side's favorite real-world example, and have both sides state how they'd classify it and why.

The document should read like something both of them could sign at the end, not a debate scorecard, and it should make clear which parts of their year of arguing were actually about the same thing.
