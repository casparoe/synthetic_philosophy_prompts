I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I've been chewing on a distinction for a couple years and I'd like somebody who knows ethics to kick its tires. I spend a lot of my life making small promises — deadlines to an agent, notes back on a friend's manuscript "by the weekend," the three-different-answers kind of thing you say at parties about a book being nearly done — and I've noticed that when I break one, the thing I actually feel bad about isn't always the lateness. So here's my homemade taxonomy. A clock promise is one where the hurt really is the timestamp: I said I'd meet you at the cafe at three and I show up at 3:25, I said the rent would be in by the first, I said I'd be back before the panel started. The lateness just is the offense. A weather promise is different. I tell my editor the novel will be done "by autumn," or I tell a fellow writer I'll read her first sixty pages "over the weekend," and both of us at some level know I'm forecasting myself, not clocking myself. If autumn comes and the book isn't done, the wrong wasn't that October happened. The wrong, when there is one, is that I handed her a better forecast than my private evidence actually licensed and then let her plans run on that forecast long after I knew it was bad.

And the little theory part, which is the part I probably shouldn't be this confident about: I think we keep misreading weather promises as soft clock promises, and that's why small flexible deadlines create this weird low-level resentment that nobody can quite defend. My claim is that the moral content of a weather promise is mostly about the quality of the self-forecast and how early and honestly you update it — do you have any idea what your own completion rate actually is, and do you flag the change before the other person builds on your old timeline? On this picture, the person who says "by Friday" while privately knowing it's a coin flip, and waits until Thursday night to say "actually Monday," has done something worse than the person who said "by Friday" on decent evidence, missed by ten days, and flagged it on Wednesday. Being late matters as evidence of a bad forecast, not as the offense itself.

I want to know if this is coherent, if I'm reinventing something standard under a worse name (no need to send me a reading list, I'm mostly asking wether the shape of the idea survives contact), and where it actually breaks. Clock promises presumably start looking like weather promises if you zoom out far enough, and maybe all real promises are a mix; and a promise to a bereaved friend that you'll be there — "be there" isn't a forecast, or not mostly one, so is the whole forecast model just a story I'm telling myself about the tiny professional promises I'm comfortable breaking? Be as blunt as you need to.
