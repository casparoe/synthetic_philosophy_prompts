I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm trying to finish an essay on the old puzzle of whether you could have come from a different sperm and egg. Most people Ive talked to settle pretty quickly on the Kripkean line: no, your actual origin is essential to you. But I've got a draft that presses on identical twins, and I'm not sure my argument is as strong as I want it to be. Here's the core of the piece. Identical twins come from the same egg and the same sperm, yet they are two different people. So the gametes alone don't single out a unique person — that is, coming from those gametes isn't sufficent for being you. I then try to go further: suppose the zygote splits around day five. In a nearby possible world where it didn't split, who is the resulting single person, twin A or twin B? It seems like the essentialist has to say neither, because each twin's origin includes some post-fertilization event, like the split itself or the particular cell lineage they descend from. But that makes origins weirdly fine-grained, and it seems to undercut the intuitive idea that you began when one sperm met one egg. So my essay concludes that origin essentialism either collapses into a sloppy slogan or has to adopt something like a branching picture, where persons overlap in the embryo and then divide.

Before I file this, I'd love your comments on the argument. Did I actually land a blow against the Kripkean view, or am I mostly attacking a slogan nobody defends? Also, is there an obvious fix from the essentialist side about twinning that I'm missing — like the idea that each twin has its own origin in a different cell, or that twinning is just a case of personal fission and the Kripkean can absorb it? I want to steelman the other side properly.
