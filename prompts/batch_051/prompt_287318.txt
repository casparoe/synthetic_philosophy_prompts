I am presenting a paper next month to a university bioethics centre, and I want a Q&A preparation sheet. The thesis I am defending is a qualified version of Macklin's deflationism about human dignity: in practical ethical argument, appeals to dignity do no independent justificatory work, because specifying autonomy, bodily integrity, non-exploitation, and standards of respectful treatment does everything that dignity is supposedly doing; but I stop short of calling the concept useless. I argue instead that dignity is a summary concept — it gathers a list of more basic considerations under one heading, yet it neither weights those considerations nor settles conflicts among them. I test this view against three applied disputes: access to assisted dying, markets for organs, and decisions on behalf of adults with profound cognitive disability.

The audience matters here. The centre's permanent seminar includes two Kantian philosophers who work on the formula of humanity and use Würde as a technical term for incomparable, non-fungible worth; a human-rights lawyer who works on the post-1945 human rights instruments in which human dignity is the explicit foundational concept; a disability-studies scholar who regards autonomy-centrism as the central failing of bioethics and reaches for dignity precisely to protect those with impaired autonomy; and a clinician-ethicist who is sympathetic to deflation but distrusts any philosophical tidiness at the bedside.

Draft the prep sheet for me. Order the objections by how likely and how damaging they are, phrase each one the way this specific audience would actually put it — not as a generic bioethics examiner would — and for each objection give a two-minute reply, what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is softest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. End by telling me honestly whether the summary-concept qualification preserves anything worth keeping, or whether it is only the old deflationary view with more polite manners.
