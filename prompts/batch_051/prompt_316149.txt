I spent most of my career as a planetary geologist and since retiring have been reading moral philosophy with more enthusiasm than system. I've ended up on the ethics review board of a small foundation that funds early-stage proposals for permanent human settlements off Earth. We see maybe six to ten proposals a year — everything from a rotating lunar research station to a one-way Mars colony — and the stakes are about as high as a decision gets: people's lives, decades-long commitments, possibly irreversable changes to another world. The board is a mix of engineers, a physician, a lawyer, and me; almost none of them have formal philosophy, and we get about ninety minutes per proposal to arrive at a recommendation.

Could you write me a decision procedure we can actually run on one of these proposals — a checklist or a flow of questions in order — grounded in whatever moral philosophy has to say about space settlement, future generations, consent, and risk? Phrase each question so a smart non-philosopher can answer it without already knowing the theory behind it, and give clear standards for what counts as a good enough answer. The procedure should be honest about which steps are value judgments rather than factual findings, and it should mark the points where the board has to vote instead of pretending the procedure decides. In particular I want these distinctions to show up somewhere in the flow, though you can weight them as you think right: the difference between imposing risk on people who signed up and deciding the conditions under which future people will be born (the non-identity problem); whether there is a realistic right of exit, including for children born there; reversibility and what a failed settlement forecloses; planetary protection and whether an untouched world has value over and above its usefulness to us; and the difference between "we would then have a duty to rescue them" and "this proposed life was so bad it should never have been started." Keep it usable for exactly our situation: a short meeting, a mixed board, no web access, and strong disagreement among members about whether settlement is even a good idea. One or two pages is fine.

Then run the proceedure on two or three proposals: (1) a one-way Mars settlement of about a hundred volunteers, no return vehicle planned, children expected within a decade; (2) a six-person lunar research station with a return vehicle always docked, a six-month rotation, and no children; and (3) one of your own choosing if it would help. Give me at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I wont like — I'm pro-settlement, but I'd rather have an honest procedure than a rigged one. End by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do and where you'd expect it to misfire on a real proposal.
