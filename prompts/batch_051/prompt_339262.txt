I am a high school student, and I keep returning to a parallel I cannot yet judge as deep or superficial. Darwin and Turing are both often described as having performed the same inversion: accounting for apparent intelligence or design without invoking a comprehending mind. Darwin explained adapted complexity through blind variation plus natural selection, with no designer overseeing the whole; Turing explained apparently intelligent behavior as the execution of a mechanical procedure, with no thinker inside the machine who understands the whole.

It seems to me that the common law might be a third instance. Across centuries, judge-made law produces a sophisticated and adaptive order—contract, tort, property, and procedure respond to changing circumstances—without any single judge, legislature, or planner having designed the system as a whole. Each judge decides the particular dispute at hand, constrained but not mechanically determined by precedent, and the overall order is the cumulative product of all those decisions.

My question is whether this qualifies as genuinely the same kind of inversion, or whether the analogy breaks. In Darwin's case, variation is blind and selection filters; in Turing's case, the machine follows a fixed transition table and the competence lies in the sequence of states. But a judge at common law intends to do justice, gives reasons, distinguishes earlier cases, and occasionally overrules them. Does that foresight and reason-giving break the structural analogy, or is it simply one more local operation that the cumulative process can absorb? What would have to be true of all three systems for results to transfer among them—could distinguishing and overruling, for instance, be understood as the legal analogue of mutation and selection of rules? And can you identify a prediction the analogy would make about one of the three that is false, which would mark the point where the mapping gives out? I am less interested in a yes-or-no verdict than in a precise account of where the mapping holds and where it comes apart.
