I follow a general policy about skeptical hypotheses — brains in vats, worlds created five minutes ago with intact memories, and the like. I refuse to lower my confidence in any ordinary belief because of such a hypothesis unless I can specify in advance some course of experience that would be more likely under the hypothesis than under the ordinary story; where no possible experience discriminates, I do not keep a small residual doubt but round the hypothesis down to zero for purposes of my reasoning. I applied the policy last week when a friend pressed the brain-in-a-vat scenario as a reason to doubt that I know I have hands: since the vat and the ordinary story predict exactly the same experiences, I regarded the scenario as not a live possibility and left my confidence in my hands unchanged. She called this verificationism — dismissing a possibility merely because I cannot check it. I think I am only refusing to let hypotheses with no predictive content tax my beliefs. Is this policy defensible as an epistemic habit, or is it a bias that conveniently protects my beliefs from unanswerable challenge? If the policy is defensible in some form, give the most careful statement of it that keeps the benefits without committing me to the claim that unfalsifiable hypotheses are meaningless or impossible. Finally, say which consideration would most likely change your verdict, and where your reasoning is least certain.
