I'm a retired chemist who only got into philosophy a couple years ago, and I've been going around in circles on Mill's harm principle—the idea that you can restrict someone's liberty only to prevent harm to others. It gets cited constantly in free speech arguments, but nobody seems to pin down what counts as 'harm' before appealing to it. I want a working defintion of 'harm to others' that's tight enough to actually decide speech cases, and Ive got seven cases to test it against:

1. Someone stands outside a hospital at 3 a.m. blasting amplified nonsense syllables through a megaphone, purely because they enjoy being disruptive.
2. A truthful advertising campaign drives a rival business into bankruptcy.
3. A newspaper publishes true but private facts about someone's medical history; the person loses their job and becomes clinically depressed.
4. A demonstration outside a mosque features speakers calling the worshippers an existential threat to the country, causing intense fear in the community but no direct physical contact.
5. A widely shared false claim that a vaccine causes permanent injury leads parents to skip vaccination, and a child dies in the resulting outbreak.
6. A very popular account repeatedly posts that a specific journalist is a rat who ruined this country; strangers send the journalist death threats, and one person physically attacks them.
7. Fifty thousand people each send one mildly insulting message to a teenager; the teenager attempts suicide.

My instincts: 1 and 5 are harm, 2 is not harm, and 3, 4, 6, and 7 I genuinely can't settle.

Please propose the definition, run it through all seven, and tell me which verdict you're least confident in and why. If the definition gives a verdict you think is wrong, say so and revise it instead of defending it. Also, since I can't tell what's settled: keep explicit which parts of this are consensus among people who work on the harm principle and which parts are actually contested. Hard cap: 800 words.
