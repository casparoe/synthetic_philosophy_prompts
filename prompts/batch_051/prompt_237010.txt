Take the Epicurean symmetry argument against the badness of death: the long nonexistence before our birth does not disturb us, so the nonexistence after our death should not disturb us either, since in both cases there is no subject present to be deprived. Now vary the case. A person is told that, after total brain death at 80, she will be revived for exactly one fully conscious day fifty years later, with her memories and character intact, and will then die permanently. Call the first event the arrest and the second the end. In detail, how should an Epicurean and a deprivation theorist (one who holds that death is bad because it deprives a person of goods her life would otherwise have contained) each assess the arrest, the fifty-year interval, and the end? Does the prospect of the revived day make the arrest less bad for her than an ordinary irreversible death at 80 would have been, or does it only show that the arrest was never really her death? Does the symmetry argument apply to the fifty-year interval with the same force it has for pre-natal nonexistence, and if not, precisely what breaks the symmetry? Finally, what does the case force each side to say about the difference between temporary and permanent nonexistence, and which side is better positioned to explain why that difference should matter?
