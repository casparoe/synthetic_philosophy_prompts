I keep coming back to a puzzle about how we handle everyday risk, and I want you to dig out the philosophical committments behind it.

There's a unit in risk assessment called the micromort — a one-in-a-million chance of dying. People use it to compare risks across activities: in the UK numbers, a 370-mile drive comes to about one micromort, a skydive is around eight, and they'll cheerfully set those next to the risk from an MDMA tablet or a scuba dive. Transport agencies go further. When they consider widening a highway, they estimate the extra road deaths the project will cause, multiply by something called the value of a statistical life, and put that on the cost side of a cost-benefit analyis, to be weighed against hours saved for drivers. The deaths are treated as a fungible cost, and there's an accepted background level of them — tens of thousands a year — that nobody treats as a crisis.

But the same societies don't take that same attitude to risks one person imposes on someone else without their say-so. Secondhand smoke and factory air pollution get regulated hard. A driver endangering pedestrians, cyclists, or kids living next to a stroad-y arterial road (one of those wide streets that's also a highway) does not. And you'd be in trouble if you proposed an "acceptable level" of micromorts from neighborhood gun violence, the way we accept a background level for car travel.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory of risk, agency, and value that this pattern presupposes. Is the voluntary-vs-involuntary distinction doing the real work, and does it survive contact with the pedestrian who dies for someone else's convenience? Or is the practice underdetermined — compatible with several different philosophies at once because nobody thought it through? Be explicit about what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits well with. And then get concrete: what would transport policy have to change if it ran on the principle that risks imposed on nonconsenting people are presumptively wrong, weighted anything like as heavily as risks people take on voluntarily? I don't want a survey of risk perception or a reading list — I want the implicit philosophy dug out, stress-tested, and its practical consequences flipped over. Please write the answer in American English.
