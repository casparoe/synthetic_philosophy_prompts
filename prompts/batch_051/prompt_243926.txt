I've been in a political philosophy reading group for most of a year, and we've spent the whole time stuck on the compatriot partiality question: whether the fact that someone is my co-national gives me a special reason to help them that I don't have toward a similarly needy foreigner, or whether nationality should be morally inert the way sharing a birthday is. What's strange is that nobody in the room is a nationalist and nobody is a hardline cosmopolitan; we all accept the same empirical story about how national identity gets built, and we all grant that we do in fact favor compatriots. The split is between two camps. One side says co-nationality is a real associative relation — not a brute similarity, but more like a distant cousin of friendship — and relationships like this generate agent-relative reasons that don't have to be justified from an impartial standpoint. The other side says that's exactly the sleight of hand: the relation is largely imagined, and special concern for compatriots is at best a permissible accommodation to human limitation, not a source of reasons in its own right. By now we can all predict each other's replies, and no one has moved an inch in months, which makes me think something structural going on.

My working theory is that the real disagreement isn't about the facts, and maybe not even about duties, but about what counts as a moral reason in the first place — specifically, whether a relationship can *create* a reason, or only shape how we're permitted to weight reasons we already have on impartial grounds. But I can also see it being a verbal dispute about the word 'reason,' or two different questions being asked under one heading. I'd like a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on which camp is right: is it substantive, verbal, or two questions? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a kind of case, an argument form — or should we just expect the impasse to be permanent? Lead with your diagnosis in one or two sentences, then support it. Hard cap: 300 words.
