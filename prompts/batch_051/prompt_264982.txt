In Britain, wildlife rehabilitation centres spend considerable sums rescuing and nursing individual injured wild animals—an oiled seabird, a hedgehog cut by a strimmer—even when the species is common and the animal's prospects after release are doubtful. The same broad conservation sector routinely culls healthy animals, such as grey squirrels shot or trapped to protect red squirrels and regenerating woodland, and it does almost nothing about the vastly larger everyday suffering of wild animals from disease, starvation and predation, writing that off as nature. What theory of value, welfare or moral status does this pattern of practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, and separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with. In particular, does the pattern presuppose that an animal's moral significance depends on species membership, nativeness, whether a human caused its plight, or whether its suffering is visible, rather than on its individual welfare? Which rival views of wild-animal value does it quietly rule out, and where is it philosophically underdetermined? Finally, state what a policy genuinely built on wild-animal welfare would have to change and what it could retain. Begin with a one-sentence statement of the theory you take the practice to presuppose, then discuss. Limit your answer to 450 words and write in British English.
