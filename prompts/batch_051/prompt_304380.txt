So I'm a couples counselor, and I've been going back and forth with a collegue for months about whether monogamy is a moral requirement or just a convention. I've mostly landed on the convention side: sexual exclusivity isn't intrinsically required, it's a social default, and what carries moral weight is what people do with that default — explicit promises, reasonable reliance, deception, pressure. But I keep hitting cases where that view feels either too permissive or too legalistic.

I need a reusable decision procedure I can run in about fifteen minutes before a session, for deciding whether a client's complaint about broken exclusivity should be treated as (a) a moral breach, (b) a difference in preferences that has to be renegotiated, or (c) grief over a convention that was never actually agreed to. I see maybe fifteen couples a week, and these calls shape whether I validate a complaint of betrayal, name something as coercion, or frame the issue as renegotiation. The procedure has to be plain enough to explain to clients with no philosophy background, and I need you to flag every step that's actually a judgment call dressed up as a procedure. Also tell me what to do when I can't tell whether an assurance was ever given.

Give me the checklist or ordered tests, then apply it to three cases: (1) a husband who never explicitly promised fidelity but lived monogamously with his wife for nine years while she says she planned her life around it; (2) a wife who wants to unilaterally open a twelve-year marriage over her husband's objections, saying monogamy is a religious convention forced on her; (3) a couple who had an explicit agreement to be monogamous but one partner secretly did something that wasn't sex but clearly violated the spirit of the agreement. Make sure at least one case comes out in a way I won't like. And tell me what the procedure cannot do. Please write your answer in American English.
