In A Treatise on Probability, Keynes suggests that the weight of an argument is something different from its probability: probability reflects the balance of favourable and unfavourable evidence, whereas weight reflects the amount or completeness of the relevant evidence on which that probability is based. I have been using that distinction to think through my own verdicts on rational belief under uncertainty, but I am not sure they can all be captured by a single principle. Here are my cases.

1. I read one small, well-conducted trial of forty patients in which a drug reduced symptoms at p = .04, and I assign the claim that the drug is effective a probability just above .95. In a second case, I read ten large, pre-registered trials of a different drug with a pooled result also significant at p = .04, and I assign the second drug roughly the same probability. Yet I am much more willing to prescribe the second drug, and I think this difference is rational, not merely a difference in nerve.

2. I am deciding whether to carry an umbrella on the basis of a single barometric reading that I know is 70% reliable, so my probability of rain is .70. Alternatively, my probability is .70 after consulting years of local weather records, several forecast models, and current satellite images. I feel much more entitled to rely on the second .70, and I think someone who treated the two as epistemically equivalent would be making a mistake. But I also believe that rational credence obeys the probability calculus and that once two people have the same probability, their beliefs are equally well supported in any truth-directed sense that matters.

3. I know a fair coin is about to be tossed, so my probability of heads is .50. If someone then gives me a detailed engineering account of the coin's exact physical symmetry, my probability stays .50, and I do not feel that my belief has become better supported in any way relevant to action.

4. One juror has heard a single piece of compelling but easily misinterpreted forensic evidence and on that basis assigns probability .90 to guilt. A second juror in another trial has heard that same piece plus a large body of independent circumstantial evidence, and also assigns .90. I think the second juror is more justified in convicting and less likely to be wrong, even though the probabilities are equal.

Is there a single principle—Keynesian, Bayesian, or something else—that delivers all four verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and what is the cheapest revision? Please state the resulting principle precisely and apply it to a fifth case I have not listed: a scientist whose theory predicts an event with probability .80 and who then receives a very large amount of redundant evidence that points the same way without changing the .80.
