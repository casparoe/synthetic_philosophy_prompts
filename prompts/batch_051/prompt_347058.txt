I have been turning over a problem in legal philosophy and find myself split between a theory I am drawn to and an intuition I cannot shake. The theory is a formal, rule-of-law picture: a statute enacted through the proper procedures remains law until it is repealed or invalidated by a competent authority. If long non-enforcement could quietly remove a statute from the books, then legal validity would become hostage to the shifting enforcement priorities of officials and to social drift, and we would lose the virtues of legality — generality, stability, prospectivity, and a kind of separation of powers. But my intuition cuts the other way. Consider a statute that has fallen into desuetude, in the sense that it has gone unenforced for generations and everyone ordered their affairs on the assumption that it would not be applied. Imagine a nineteenth-century commercial regulation everyone in an industry has ignored for decades, with regulators inspecting and licensing the very conduct it appears to ban. It strikes me as profoundly unfair — a kind of retroactive trap — for the state suddenly to revive that statute against one defendant. If this intuition is right, a law that is valid in form has ceased to function as law; if the theory is right, refusing to enforce it is lawless. I do not want a prediction about what some court would do. I want to know which should yield. What would justify treating the intuition as a mere aversion to harsh results and dropping it? What would justify amending the theory to include something like desuetude as a real legal phenomenon? And is there a third reading on which the statute remains validly enacted but its use against a defendant who reasonably relied on its non-enforcement is nevertheless legally improper?
