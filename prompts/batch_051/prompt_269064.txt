I'm a science journalist trying to write a piece about an old argument in cognitive science that refuses to die. My editor thinks 'modularity' is too wonky, but to me the underlying question is the interesting one: is the mind one general-purpose thinking machine, or a Swiss army knife of specialist gadgets? Fodor's line, as I understand it, was that only the peripheral systems - perception, motor control, maybe some language - are modular in the strong sense: fast, mandatory, informationally encapsulated, with their own neural wiring. Central cognition, where we reason and revise beliefs, he thought was not like that; it was a general-purpose, unencapsulated system. The other side says central cognition is modular too, all the way down: dedicated circuits for things like social exchange, intuitive physics, maybe even abstract reasoning, not one general inference engine.

The thing is, I can't tell whether this is a disagreement about the mind or about the word 'module'. Some researchers insist it's an empirical claim an experiment could settle; I don't see the experiment. So: take the strongest version of the claim that central cognition is massively modular, and design the best concrete test you can of it. What behaviour or data would make you more confident in massive modularity, and what would count against it? Then tell me what a positive result would and would not settle - how much of the fight is genuinely empirical and how much is a definitional squabble running seperate from the data. If you have to adjust the definition of 'module' to make it testable, say so, and be explicit about what that adjustment leaves out.
