Consider Benjamin Libet's classic argument that the readiness potential (RP) — a slow negative shift in scalp EEG beginning several hundred milliseconds before a self-initiated movement — shows that the brain is already preparing the movement before the subject reports the time (W) at which he or she becomes aware of deciding to move, and therefore that conscious intention does not initiate voluntary action; on Libet's own view, the most the conscious will can do is veto an action already under way. Suppose we now accept a widely discussed later reinterpretation of the finding: the pre-movement RP is largely an artifact of averaging over trials, because subthreshold motor activity drifts randomly and the subject moves when that activity happens to cross a decision threshold. On this accumulator picture, the apparent pre-conscious build-up is not an unconscious decision to move now; it is the stochastic approach to a threshold, so the gap Libet found between neural preparation and reported awareness may be an artifact of the averaging procedure rather than evidence that the brain decided before the agent did. My question is not primarily historical or exegetical. I want to know whether Libet's challenge to free will survives this reinterpretation, needs repair, or is dead. In your answer, identify precisely which premise of Libet's argument the reinterpretation bears on; say whether it restores conscious initiation or merely replaces a deterministic pre-conscious process with a random one; and take a definite, argued position on what, if anything, remains of the Libet-style case against free will once the reinterpretation is accepted. If you think the challenge survives, state it in its strongest surviving form and say what evidence would now be needed; if you think it does not, say whether Libet's experiments leave any residual problem for free will worth taking seriously. Do not simply survey the options — give your considered verdict and defend it.
