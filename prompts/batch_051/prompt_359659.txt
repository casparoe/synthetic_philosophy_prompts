I'm a retired polymer chemist, and after a career of worrying about chain lengths and reaction pathways I've got it into my head to figure out wether "complexity" is a real property of things or just a word for what surprises us. I've drifted toward a set of convictions and I'd like a consistency check before I inflict them on my reading group. Here they are, roughly as I'd defend them over a beer:

(1) Complexity is objective: a ribosome is more complex than a quartz crystal or a pile of sand, full stop, independent of what anyone knows or what language they describe it in. (2) But complexity should be intrinsic to the object itself; it shouldn't shift when we change our choice of elementary building blocks, our library of reusable parts, or what else happens to be in the environment. (3) Shannon entropy is not complexity; a gas at equilibrium has enormous Shannon entropy but is in any interesting sense simple. (4) Kolmogorov complexity also misses, because it treats a random string as maximally complex, and I want to say randomness is the absence of structure, not the height of it. (5) And complexity is not a historical or etiological property: if a clever chemist makes the very same molecule in a three-step lab shortcut that a bacterium needs thirty enzymatic steps to make, the molecule is exactly as complex either way, because what matters is the object that got made, not the route by which it happened to get made. (6) The measure that survives these failures, I think, is assembly index: the length of the shortest sequence of joining operations needed to build the object from some set of elementary building blocks, with previously assembled pieces reusable as units. It gives low scores to regular crystals and to random aggregates, and high scores to a folded protein, a watch, or a genome.

To my eye this is a tidy little package, but I've learned not to trust my eye in philosophy. Could you go through the set and tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension and which only seem to be, rank the real tensions by how severe they are, and say what the cheapest repair would be and what it would cost me? I'd rather you dont dress it up: no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables — just continuous prose, the way a colleague would write me a letter.
