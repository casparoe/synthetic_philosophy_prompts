Write a deadpan corporate incident postmortem (roughly 650 words) from Fretless, a fictional last-mile autonomous-delivery company, after one of its Level-4 vans (no human driver; a remote operator monitors the fleet) struck a pedestrian in a crosswalk. The pedestrian's injuries are minor; the postmortem is the company's public "Trust & Safety" write-up, composed in the soothing idiom of a no-blame engineering retrospective.

The satire's target is a practice: the diffusion of responsibility for harms involving autonomous systems across so many designers, operators, vendors, fleet supervisors, and corporate entities that no human being and no entity is left answerable. This is the responsibility gap that opens when a learning system does something no single person predicted or intended, and the piece should show, rather than state, why that diffusion is philosophically suspicious. To do that:

- Include a "Contributing Factors" section listing many individually reasonable handoffs that jointly dissolve accountability: a perception engineer who selected a loss function, a supplier's pretrained model, a remote operator monitoring twelve vehicles at once, the municipal signal infrastructure, an edge-case reviewer, and a braking policy the text calls "emergent." Each contribution should be causally real but structured so that no one contribution is sufficient for blame.
- Work in, without using the jargon in the piece itself, the idea of a moral crumple zone (the nearest human operator absorbing more legal and moral force than their actual control can justify), the many-hands problem (blame dissolving across numerous contributors), and the gap between causal contribution and answerability.
- Include a safety statistic that compares aggregate fleet collision rates with a human-driver baseline while remaining silent about who, if anyone, would have been liable in this particular case.
- End with a metric or company line that turns the absence of a responsible party into a feature — for example, "zero single points of blame" or accountability described as "horizontally scalable."

The humor should come from getting the mechanism right and exaggerating its real features, not from cartoon villainy: at each step a reader should feel the reported practice is only slightly deformed from how blame actually gets distributed around automated systems. The piece should stay in character throughout. Someone unfamiliar with the responsibility-gap debate should find it readable and quietly unsettling; a specialist should recognize the precise philosophical moves being satirized.

After the piece, append an author's note (around 150 words) separating what is settled from what is genuinely contested here: for instance, it is widely accepted that intricate learned systems can behave in ways their designers did not concretely anticipate, but whether that produces a genuine responsibility gap rather than a correctable failure of liability law and organizational design is genuinely contested. Also flag where the piece caricatures for effect (for example, treating every handoff as motivated rather than as ordinary engineering practice). Do not present a contested position as the consensus.
