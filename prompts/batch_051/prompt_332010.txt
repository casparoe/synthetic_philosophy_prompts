Me and a friend just spent a whole evening arguing about whether comparing the Holocaust to other genocides is inherently illegitimate. I ended up writing out this argument, and I think it's solid, but he kept saying I was pulling a fast one somewhere. Here it is:

(1) Every historical event is unique, if by 'unique' you just mean it happened only once, with its own causes, actors, and details.
(2) If that kind of uniqueness made comparison illegitimate, then no two historical events could ever be compared at all.
(3) So someone who says the Holocaust's uniqueness rules out comparison must mean uniqueness in a stronger sense: that it has some feature, or combination of features, no other event has.
(4) But the only way to know that no other event has that feature is to compare the Holocaust with other events along exactly that dimension.
(5) So the uniqueness claim, when used to forbid comparison, presupposes the comparisons it is meant to forbid.
(6) Therefore uniqueness, even if true, cannot by itself make comparing the Holocaust to other genocides illegitimate.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails, and under what reading? My friend says step 3 is a strawman, and that step 4 conflates comparing in the sense of checking for a property with comparing in the sense of ranking or equating. I don't buy either objection, but I also can't tell whether my conclusion is weaker than I think.
