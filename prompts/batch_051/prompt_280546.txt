I am a novelist, not a professional philosopher, and I want to get a conceptual matter right before it shapes a book. The novel turns on a disagreement between two characters about whether one can intelligibly be grateful to luck and to the past. One character, whom I think of as the rigorist, holds that gratitude is intelligible only as a response to a benefactor: an agent who intended to bring about some good for the recipient, at least partly out of good will. From this he concludes that a person cannot literally be grateful to the storm that spared her harvest, to the chain of historical accidents that made her life possible, or to a long-dead ancestor she never knew; at most she can be glad that things turned out as they did. He further concludes that chance and the past therefore make no normative claim on us of the kind gratitude makes. The other character resists: she says what she feels toward the accidents, inheritances, and dead hands that made her possible is not mere gladness, and she suspects the rigorist has mistaken one species of gratitude for the whole genus. I keep finding the rigorist's argument persuasive, and I suspect the second character is simply committing a category mistake; but something in her resistance nags at me. So my question to you is this: if the rigorist's principle is sound, should I write the second character's reverence toward luck and the past as sentimental self-deception, and does the principle show that we owe nothing at all to chance and to the past? Please take the role of a professional philosopher who works in moral psychology, and think through this carefully. Begin by brainstorming the different things that get lumped together under gratitude and neighboring backward-looking attitudes — being grateful to a benefactor, being grateful that such-and-such happened, feeling indebted without a debtor, honoring an inheritance, owing something to the dead — and identify which conditions are doing the philosophical work, such as agency, intention, benevolence, cost to the benefactor, and the absence of obligation. Then diagnose whether the rigorist's principle really licenses his conclusions, where his reasoning overreaches or fails, and how I should best portray the second character's attitude to keep the novel philosophically honest rather than sacrificing rigor to sentiment.
