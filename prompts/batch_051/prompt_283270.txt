Can you put on an epistemologist hat for a second and help me find the exit from this? My reading group has been stuck on an argument that seems to show nobody has ever justifiably believed anything -- not even that the room we're in has a floor -- and I can't find the flaw, eventhough the conclusion is obviously insane. We wrote it out like this:

(1) A belief is justified only if the person has at least one good reason for it.
(2) For a reason to actually support a belief, the reason must be another belief that is itself justified; otherwise it's an arbitrary stopping point and calling it a reason is just window dressing.
(3) So a justified belief sits on top of a chain of justified beliefs, each one supporting the next.
(4) The chain can't terminate. If the last belief had a reason, it isn't the last belief; if it had no reason, then by (1) it's unjustified and by (2) it can't justify anything above it.
(5) The chain also can't loop back on itself, because if A supports B and eventually B supports A, the circle gets no support from outside itself; it's two people vouching for each other's honesty.
(6) The chain can't be infinite either. I'm a finite thing, and an infinite series of reasons is not something I can have before my mind. Saying "the chain is there in principle" just sounds like a polite way of admitting I can't produce it.
(7) But a justification chain has to do one of those three things: stop, loop, or go on forever. There's no fourth option.
(8) So no belief is justified.

I obviously don't believe (8). And I can see the textbook exits: weaken (1) so some beliefs are justified without being supported by further reasons, which gets you foundationalism; reject (5) and allow a web of mutually supporting beliefs, which gets you coherentism; or reject (6) and allow infinite chains, which gets you infinitism. Fine. But every time I actually try to walk through one of those exits, the argument seems to regenerate one level up. If I weaken (1), I have to say which beliefs get to be basic, and any criterion I give looks like just another reason, which starts the chain again. If I reject (5), I can't see why a tightly coherent fairy story isn't justified whenever a tightly coherent scientific theory is. If I reject (6), I've swapped actually having my reasons for a promise that reasons go on forever, which feels like relabeling the problem instead of solving it.

I keep having the feeling the real trick is somewhere around (4) or (6), maybe in what "having" a reason is supposed to mean, but I can't make it land. So: which premise should I actually reject, why did that premise look so safe, and what do I have to give up by rejecting it? Take your time and maybe brainstorm the strongest version of each exit before you commit. And please don't end with "it depends" -- pick one and tell me what it costs.
