My friend and I spent last night going in circles about whether species are a real thing or just naming conventions, and I realized I still can't state the rule she's actually applying. Everytime I thought I'd pinned it down, the next example knocked it loose.

Here's roughly her track record from the argument:

- Dogs and gray wolves: one species. She'd even lump a chihuahua and a wolf, on the grounds that they can interbreed perfectly well and dogs are just wolves that have been under artificial selection for a sliver of evolutionary time.
- Polar bears and grizzlies: two species, despite pizzly hybrids being a thing and pretty much fertile. Her line was that hybridization is real but the two forms stay distinct where they overlap, so occasional crosses don't bother her.
- Black-capped and Carolina chickadees: two species, because in the narrow zone where they meet the hybrids do worse than the parents.
- The Ensatina salamander ring around the Central Valley: the two end populations that overlap in Southern California and don't interbreed are two species, but she says there's no non-arbitrary place along the loop to draw the line, because each adjacent form does interbreed.
- E. coli strains: she gets pluralist and a little impatient. For her, 'species' there is a convenience label based on overall genome similarity, and it's a mistake to expect it to work like it does for big sexually reproducing animals.
- Humans and Neanderthals: two species, even though they interbred and most living humans carry a bit of Neanderthal DNA, because the two were on separate trajectories for hundreds of thousands of years.
- Two frogs that look identical but have different mating calls and don't interbreed in the wild: two species.

Now I have a case I want to put to her, but I'd rather reason it through before I do. Two plant populations were separated for maybe 200,000 years, and their DNA falls into two clean branches, each population on its own branch; a botanist can tell them apart without trouble. About 3,000 years ago a river changed course and brought them back into contact, and now there's a broad middle zone where basicaly every individual is a mix of the two. The intermediates are fully fertile and healthy, and the two original forms are still common at the edges. Would she call it one species, two species, or something messier if she's applying whatever rule she was using on the earlier cases?

I want to know what principle is actually doing the work in her verdicts, which other species concept fits them about as well, and what feature of this new plant case is making those principles come apart. Then give me one follow-up case that would cleanly separate the two front-runners if the plant case leaves it a coin flip.
