A friend and I have been arguing about universalizability after a seminar that mixed Kant, Hare, and Singer, and he built an argument that seems to prove way too much, but I can't locate the exact step to push back on. He lays it out like this:

(1) If it would be wrong for everyone to do X, then it's wrong for each person to do X, including me. (That's just the "what if everyone did that?" test in its most general form.)

(2) It would be wrong for everyone to become a novelist: if everyone did, nobody would grow food, staff hospitals, or keep the power on, and that would be a disaster.

(3) So it's wrong for me to become a novelist.

(4) By the same reasoning, it's wrong for me to go into academic philosophy — indeed, the test seems to rule out nearly every career, becuase for almost any occupation you can fill in X with "becomes a full-time Y and does nothing else that's socially necessary," and the world falls apart.

(5) But it's absurd that almost every career choice is wrong.

(6) Therefore premise (1), and the whole universalizability idea, has to go — which would also mean giving up the thought that "what if everyone refused to give to famine relief?" shows I should give.

I'm not fully convinced, because (1) looks almost tautological, and the verdicts in the standard cases (the false promise, the lying loan, fare evasion) still feel right, as does the famine-relief extension. If I have to choose, I'd rather keep some version of (1) than accept (5) or (6), but I don't know what I'm paying for that. So: which step should I reject, why does it look fine, and what does the fixed-up version of universalizability look like? Can it keep the Kantian and Singer-ish verdicts without ruling out every career? Answer in American English.
