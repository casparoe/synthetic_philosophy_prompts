For the past several months I have been drafting a chapter on the idea that rationality can be trained the way a martial art is trained. The picture, as I encounter it in discussions and in my own teaching, is this: one does not become more rational merely by learning about biases or the norms of good reasoning, any more than one learns to fight by reading about fighting. One does formalized, repeatable exercises — analogously to katas — deliberately practiced in a simplified and artificial setting: calibration drills against one's own past forecasts, base-rate translation problems, pre-mortems, argument-mapping, red-team/blue-team routines. The claim of rationality-as-martial-art is that such structured and partly decontextualized practice produces durable, far-transfer improvements in real-world judgment, not merely improvement on the exercises themselves or on near variants of them.

I would like your honest credence in the following proposition: structured, decontextualized practice of specific reasoning exercises produces durable improvements in ecologically representative real-world reasoning and decision-making that generalize across subject matter and format, and these improvements are attributable to trained skill rather than inert content knowledge. I will count the proposition false if the gains are real but confined to narrow variants of the drills, or if they are fully explained by learning domain-specific facts. Please do not tell me that it depends. Give a number, and then decompose it: state your probability for each of the following sub-questions and say how they combine. (a) Is rationality significantly skill-like and trainable, rather than mostly a stable trait or stored knowledge? (b) Conditional on its being trainable, does skill practiced in one format transfer broadly across domains, or does it remain bound to the training format? (c) Is the martial-arts analogy itself a reliable guide — do kata-like drills in actual martial arts substantially contribute to fight-relevant ability, or do they survive mainly for pedagogical, aesthetic, or ritual reasons? (d) Do the well-known far-transfer failures in cognitive-training research (for example, working-memory training) generalize to reasoning skills of the relevant kind? For each sub-question, give a rough probability, explain how the four combine into your overall number, and indicate which sub-question the final estimate is most sensitive to. Finally, tell me which single empirical finding or conceptual argument would move your number most, and in which direction.

End the response with a single machine-readable line of the form \answer{0.XX} containing your final credence to two decimal places.
