Me and my friend stayed up way too late arguing about whether people online ever actually engage with the other side's real position or just a cartoon of it, and he proposed this rule for a debate-focused forum that he swears would fix discourse. I'm skeptical, but I want to think through how it would actually behave. The rule: before you can post a rebuttal to a position you don't hold, you have to write a short summary of the view you're attacking. That summary goes to three randomly selected raters who self-identify as holding that view, drawn from a pool of users who themselves have a track record of their own summaries being approved by opponents. If at least two of the three raters mark your summary as fair, your rebuttal posts with a "steelman passed" tag and your summary appears above it; if not, the rebuttal is blocked and you recieve the raters' comments explaining what you got wrong, and you can revise and resubmit as many times as you want. Top-level posts stating your own view are exempt, so nobody needs a gate just to talk. What would this actually do if a large platform rolled it out? I want the mechanism analyzed, not a verdict on whether it's a good idea. What does it incentivize among ordinary users, among people who angle to become raters, and among people trying to game it? How does it get gamed—people falsely claiming to hold a view to get on a jury, raters colluding to approve weak summaries for allies or refusing to approve anything so opponents can't post, people picking weird niche views precisely because there aren't enough raters to gate them? What happens to positions that are real but have too few self-identified holders, or whose holders are so polarized they never approve anything? Does it change what gets argued and who argues, or just how people package the same attacks? And are the failure modes fixable by tweaking the thresholds or the rater pool without making the whole thing toothless?
