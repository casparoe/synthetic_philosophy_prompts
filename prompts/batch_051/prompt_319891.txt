I am currently taking a philosophy seminar on the ethics of space settlement, and in two weeks I have to present my term paper to a group made up of the seminar's regular members—mostly analytic political philosophers who work in liberal, person-centred traditions—and several planetary scientists from the astrobiology programme who audit the philosophical sessions. The paper defends the following thesis: the wrong-making feature of terrestrial colonial undertakings was not ultimately that they harmed identifiable persons, but that they instituted a unilateral relationship of extraction, exclusion, and normative imposition toward a place, and this structural kind of wrong can obtain even where the place contains no pre-existing persons. On that basis I argue that a permanent, largely self-governing Mars settlement led by a small consortium of states and private corporations would reproduce the same relationship even if it harmed no settler and even if Mars proves lifeless, and that the standard justifications for settlement—scientific discovery, resource extraction, and the creation of a backup civilisation—therefore inherit a burden their proponents have not yet discharged.

I need help preparing for the question period rather than a defence of the thesis itself. The political philosophers in the room will be suspicious of any wrong that floats free of determinate wronged individuals, and will press on who exactly is wronged, what distributive principle is violated, and whether the position collapses into an aesthetic preference for untouched landscapes; the planetary scientists will be broadly sympathetic to caution but impatient with what they will call mere framing, and will demand to know whether I am proposing prohibition, delay, or only different governance. The session is forty-five minutes, so replies must be short. Please give me a prep sheet: the objections I will actually receive, ordered by likelihood and likely damage, phrased the way this audience would phrase them, each with a compact reply and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what to concede up front, what I must not concede, and include the objection I am hoping nobody raises—and what to say if someone does.

My argument runs roughly as follows: a relational account of the colonial wrong; an application to Mars through the Outer Space Treaty's non-appropriation principle and the likely governance structure of any settlement; and the conclusion that the wrong persists in the absence of indigenous persons. The question I most hope to avoid is whether the same reasoning condemns every transformative human use of a landscape, including a terrestrial nature reserve or a city, because I have not worked out a stable line there; I would rather find out now how to answer it than be surprised in the room.
