I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I'd appreciate help thinking through a dispute that has turned into a conceptual puzzle. My small company builds software for government clients. Last year we delivered a permitting system to a state agency; they accepted the system and have been using it, but they have refused to pay the final invoice. Their lawyer's position is that the agency is immune from suit under the doctrine of sovereign immunity. A friend of mine who has read some political theory tells me this is more principled than it sounds. He says sovereign immunity descends from the old maxim that the state can do no wrong, and that this is not merely a legal doctrine: the state is not a person, it has no mind or intentions, and so it cannot literally do anything wrong. Breaching a contract is the sort of wrong only an individual official can commit, and when an official commits it, the wrong is the official's personal wrong, not the state's. I find this self-serving and implausible, but I cannot articulate exactly where his reasoning goes wrong. How should I evaluate his argument? Is there a meaningful sense in which the agency itself, rather than the officials who signed and administered the contract, has done something wrong? And is that the same question as whether we should be legally allowed to sue it?
