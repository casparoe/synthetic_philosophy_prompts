In *Language* (1921), Edward Sapir says, “Speech is a human activity that varies without assignable limit as we pass from social group to social group, because it is a purely historical heritage of the group, the product of long-continued social usage.” I’m a linguistics PhD student trying to work out what this means for how we evaluate writing systems. The standard line is that alphabets are meant to be phonemic: one letter per phoneme, and deviations are defects. But if speech itself is a historical heritage rather than a natural grid of sounds, then the whole idea of a “phonemic alphabet” seems to presuppose that phonemes are already there, waiting to be transcribed. What exactly does a script encode, and how phonemic should an alphabet really be? Please explain the conceptual issue here, giving the strongest version of both the phonemic ideal and the alternative view that a script encodes a tradition’s categorization of its language, including morphological and historical information. I’d rather you focus on the argument than on listing orthographies, though a few stock examples like English “nature”/“natural” or the different pronunciations of written -ed are fine if they clarify. The thing I keep bumping on is whether a good writing system should track the sound surface, the morphophonemic level, or something else, and whether calling a spelling “defective” is really just a way of assuming speech has a fixed phonemic structure it doesn’t actually have.
