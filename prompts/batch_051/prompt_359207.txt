I tutor high-school and early-college mathematics for a living, and I have no formal background in philosophy, but for years I have been turning over a single question: when has a student actually understood a proof, as opposed to merely being able to reproduce one? I want a working definition of "understands a proof" — not a grading rubric, but a definition precise enough that I can look at a student's behavior and say whether the term applies.

Here are the cases I want the definition to get right. I am confident about the first two and genuinely unsure about the rest.

1. A student who can write out a proof that the square root of 2 is irrational exactly as it appears in the textbook, but when asked why we assume the fraction is in lowest terms, or what would go wrong if that step were dropped, just shrugs. I say this student does not understand the proof.

2. A student who cannot reproduce the formal proof on demand, but can explain the central idea in their own words, sketch a picture that captures why the theorem has to be true, and say where a naive attempt at the proof would break down. I am inclined to say this student does understand it, but I cannot defend that against a colleague who says you cannot understand a proof you cannot actually produce.

3. A student who has gone through a proof line by line and verified that each step follows from the previous ones, and can confirm the proof is valid, but cannot state the main idea of the proof in a single sentence — like checking that every move in a chess game is legal without seeing the plan.

4. A student who cannot reproduce the proof from a blank page, but given the written proof can accurately answer "why is this step here?" for each step, and can teach the proof to a classmate who then understands it.

5. A student who can take the proof and modify it to prove a slightly different theorem — changing the hypothesis and seeing where the argument would need adjustment — but who says they do not really "get" the proof and cannot explain it without the written version in front of them.

6. A student who has never seen the proof at all, but uses the theorem fluently and correctly in unfamiliar problems, and can state precisely what the theorem says and when it applies.

I want the definition to count case 1 as not understanding and case 2 as understanding, and to give a definite yes or no for cases 3 through 6. If a natural definition gives a different answer for case 1 or case 2, I want to know whether you would revise the definition or argue that my confident judgment is wrong.

Please act as a professional philosopher who works on mathematical understanding and mathematical practice. Think carefully; if it helps, brainstorm two or three candidate definitions and test them against the cases before settling. Then lead with your proposed definition in the first sentence, with no preamble and no restating of the question. After that, run the definition through all six cases, say where it gives the wrong answer or no clean answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts.
