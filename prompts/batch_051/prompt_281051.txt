I've been teaching a unit on the value of knowledge, and I've realized I've been defending a view without ever making it face a genuinely serious challenge. The view is that knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief in a way that matters — not just a point of terminology, but a real difference in epistemic standing. What animates it is the old Meno contrast: a person with a true belief about the road to Larissa guides you exactly as well as someone who knows the way, and yet we still treat the two states as non-equivalent. I want to know how exposed this view really is. In particular, I'm disturbed by the swamping worry — the thought that whatever extra ingredient turns true belief into knowledge (justification, reliability, an anti-luck condition, or some other factor) looks like it has only instrumental value for reaching truth, so that once true belief is already in hand it adds no further value — and by cases in which mere true belief appears to serve every practical purpose just as well. I'm also unsure what to make of nearby positions: that understanding or wisdom rather than knowledge carries the distinctive value, that what matters is epistemically virtuous belief rather than knowledge, or that the intuition of a difference is just an artifact of how we use the word 'know.'

Please map out what would count against my view. For each consideration — intuitive, case-based, formal, empirical, or definitional — tell me what it would show if it succeeded, how decisive it is (fatal, major, moderate, or merely awkward), whether a committed defender of the value of knowledge would concede it as counting against them, and what the best reply is. I especially want the defeaters sorted from most to least threatening, and an honest closing judgment on whether the view can be qualified so far that it risks nothing at all — and whether, for a view of this kind, being insulated in that way is itself a problem. I don't need a literature review or citations; precise reconstruction of the arguments and cases is what matters.
