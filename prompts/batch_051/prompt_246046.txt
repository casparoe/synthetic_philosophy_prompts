Write a dialogue between two equally competent people who disagree about what should happen to orphan works and abandonware. One of them, Aria, defends a 'use it or lose it' rule: if a work hasn't been commercially exploited for, say, fifteen years and a diligent search can't locate the rights holder, it should lapse into the public domain, or at least become freely usable without fear of a later infringement suit. The other, Marcus, thinks the property right shouldn't die just because the owner is missing, and wants a clearing house instead: would-be users would pay a licence fee into escrow, and the money would be released to the owner if they ever turn up. Have them argue about a concrete case, maybe a 1994 educational CD-ROM from a publisher that went bust and whose assets were never traced. Make it a real back-and-forth where each responds to the other's actual points, not two alternating monologues. Push them into the weeds on a few questions: whether non-use is a reliable signal of abandonment when holding an intangible costs almost nothing; whether the escrow model actually solves the hold-up problem or just turns it into a tax on preservationists; whether abandoned software is importantly different from an orphaned novel because it decays without maintenance; whether the real culprit is the absence of decent records rather than the absence of owners; and whether there's a seperate question about what we should assume the vanished author would have wanted. Neither should be a strawman; both should concede the other side's best point at least once. Don't tie it up neatly at the end.
