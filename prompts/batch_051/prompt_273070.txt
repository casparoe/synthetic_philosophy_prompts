I've been messing with a bunch of old PC games lately, stuff from the 90s where the studio has been gone for twenty years, and I went down a rabbit hole about why so many of them just disappear. A lot of them aren't sold anywhere anymore; the only way to play is to download from a fan site, which legally is piracy, even though theres nobody to buy it from and nobody who seems to care. The copyright just sits there automatically for the author's life plus 70 years — or 95 years from publication when a company owns it — and if the studio folded, the rights are owned by... someone, maybe from an asset sale, maybe an estate, often nobody can even figure out who. So a game can be effectively abandoned but still illegal to copy for decades.

Meanwhile, physical property doesn't work like that. If a company goes under and leaves a warehouse full of unsold stuff, the stuff eventually gets liquidated or, if it's just left sitting somewhere, there are abandonment and unclaimed-property rules that let someone clear title to it. A song or a piece of code, though, can be orphaned and off-limits at the same time. Even libraries and archives mostly can't digitize and share this stuff without permission from an owner who may not exist. And it never lapses from non-use the way a patent expires or a trademark gets cancelled when nobody uses it.

I'm trying to figure out what theory of ownership this practice is actually assuming. It treats copyright as property that can be inherited and sold, so property; but at the same time it can't be abandoned the way property can, and non-use doesn't kill it. Is there a coherent theory of property that gives you the inheritance part without the abandonment part? Or is the system just a compromise between different theories that don't really fit together? And if we instead took seriously the most natural story — that copyright exists to incentivize making things that eventually enrich the public — what parts of the current system would that force us to give up? I'm less interested in specific policy fixes and more in the underlying assumptions and where they pull in opposite directions.
