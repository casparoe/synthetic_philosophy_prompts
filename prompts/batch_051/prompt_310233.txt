Two friends of mine recently went in circles over what had seemed like a narrow, concrete question, and neither moved an inch, so I am hoping for a point-by-point adjudication rather than a general impression.

The question was whether, setting aside veganism and assuming one eats meat, beef or chicken is the less bad choice for animal suffering.

One argued for beef on direct-death grounds: what matters is the number of farmed animals killed per unit of food, and a single steer yields hundreds of meals while a chicken yields only a few, so chicken eaters are responsible for vastly more individual deaths than beef eaters.

The other replied that this draws an arbitrary boundary at the farm gate. Both cattle and chickens are fed from cropland, and the planting and harvesting of that cropland kills wild animals — mice, voles, insects. Because cattle convert feed into meat far less efficiently than chickens, a calorie of grain-finished beef embeds several times more cropland than a calorie of chicken, and therefore several times more of these crop deaths. Once indirect deaths are counted, she said, the ranking flips, or at least the direct-count argument no longer settles the matter.

The first answered that this at most indicts grain-finished beef. Pasture-raised cattle eat grass we cannot eat, involve no crop-death burden, and may even maintain grassland as habitat; so the objection targets feedlot beef, not beef as a category. He added that even if insects in harvests matter, they presumably matter far less than a chicken, and a tiny probability of sentience spread across billions of insects might still not outweigh the certain suffering of millions of chickens.

The second answered that comparing the best-case version of beef with ordinary factory-farmed chicken rigs the comparison: pasture-finished beef is a small share of actual supply, and pasture is not morally innocent either — it displaces wild herbivores and is often managed with fencing and predator control. On the insects, she said that "matters much less" is not "matters not at all"; under expected-value reasoning, a modest probability of sentience multiplied by the sheer number of crop animals can still dominate the direct farm-animal count.

There the argument stalled; each conceded the other's points were relevant in principle but denied they changed the bottom line.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each claim in the exchange, say whether it is roughly right, clearly wrong, or depends on further facts, and briefly justify the assessment. Then say who is more right overall, and what both sides are leaving out of the comparison.
