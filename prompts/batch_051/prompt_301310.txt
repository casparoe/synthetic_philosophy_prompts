I'm an aesthetics PhD student and I keep runnning into a version of the argument that beauty can't be objective because taste varies so much across eras and cultures. The inference is usually: if beauty were a real property in the world, competent judges would eventually converge on it; but they manifestly don't, so it isn't. Hume's standard-of-taste essay gets cited for this even though he kind of tried to have it both ways. Lately I've been reading empirical aesthetics that seems to undercut the premise: people in very different cultures prefer the same sort of open, park-like landscapes with water, symmetric and average faces get high attractiveness ratings across samples, and infants supposedly look longer at symmetric faces than asymmetric ones. So there's more cross-cultural convergence than the argument pretends. Does the argument-from-disagreement survive this development, need repair, or is it just dead? I want to know which premise the new evidence bears on, whether a subjectivist can rebuild the argument in a weaker but still damaging form (say, by conceding low-level perceptual preferences and relocating the disagreement to paintings and novels), and whether what's left is really a dispute about whether that low-level convergence deserves the word 'beauty' at all.
