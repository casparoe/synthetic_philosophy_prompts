I've ended up an objective Bayesian of the Jaynes-y sort: I think that for any fixed state of background knowledge there's a uniquely rational prior, and the maximum entropy principle is the engine that finds it — from all the distributions consistent with the constraints you actually have, you pick the one with the largest Shannon entropy, since it's the one that adds the least information beyond your data, and when you don't have constraints to run maxent over, symmetry or transformation-group arguments step in and hand you something like a Jeffreys prior. I know the standard complaints, mainly that maxent priors on continuous parameters change under reparameterization and that Bertrand-style paradoxes show "least information" isn't well defined. Those don't feel fatal to me, but I want to pressure-test the view propertly. What would count against it? I'd like a ranked map of the defeaters — formal results, specific worked examples, arguments about what a state of ignorance even is, convergence results — sorted from "this actually kills the project" down to "this is just a bullet to bite." Which of these would a committed objective Bayesian concede as genuine costs, and which would they dismiss as confusions? What does my view predict that subjective Bayesianism (or imprecise probability) doesn't, such that the difference could embarrass me rather than just mark a difference in taste? And has the view, by the time it's done defending itself, become unfalsifiable — and if it has, is that a problem for a view of this kind? Be honest about how exposed it really is.
