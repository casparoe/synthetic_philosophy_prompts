I've come to accept a strongly empiricist position about fakes and originals: an object's aesthetic value is fully grounded in its perceptible properties and the experiences those properties make available, so a perfect forgery—one indistinguishable from the original by any sensory or technical means available in principle—would not be aesthetically inferior to the original, and discovering that a work is a forgery should not by itself lower one's aesthetic assessment of it once the initial surprise has passed. I want to know what would count against this position.

I'm not looking only for the obvious point that the art market pays more for documented provenance, or that people feel cheated when they learn a work is fake. I want a map of the position's exposure: which cases would put real pressure on it (a painting replaced atom-for-atom by a duplicate after a restoration, an indistinguishable copy of a family photograph, a forged musical manuscript later revealed, a rebuilt instrument, a digital work with no original file), which intuitions about achievement, authorship, and historical witness the view is forced to reject, whether empirical findings about how beliefs concerning authenticity alter perceptual experience and pleasure count as direct evidence against it, and whether the position survives extension to performances, rituals, and sacred objects. For each, say whether a committed defender should concede it as a cost or dismiss it as a confusion, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. If the view ends up insulated by qualifications of the form "aesthetic value is this, but other kinds of value are affected," say whether that insulation is itself a serious problem for the position.
