I am a welfare economist with no philosophical training in ethics. I work daily with individual utility functions, Pareto efficiency, social welfare functions, interpersonal comparability of utility, and compensation tests such as Kaldor–Hicks, and I am comfortable with both additive and non-additive forms of aggregation. I keep encountering the charge that utilitarianism fails to respect the separateness of persons: roughly, the thought that it extends the way a single person may rationally trade off gains and losses across moments of a single life to trade-offs across distinct lives, treating a group of people as if it were one person whose aggregate satisfaction is to be maximised. I gather this is meant to be a foundational objection, not merely a quibble about the chosen distributional weights.

Please restate that objection entirely in the vocabulary of welfare economics, as faithfully and as precisely as you can. Then give me a residue report: which parts translate cleanly into claims about the properties of a social welfare function or a compensation test; which parts can be captured only by bending or augmenting my framework (for instance, by imposing constraints that forbid certain inequalities, or by denying that gains to one person can compensate losses to another at all); and which parts have no counterpart in the framework. In particular, I want to know whether the objection reduces to a familiar technical point — that one may reject a strongly additive and unbounded social welfare function while remaining a consequentialist — or whether it points to something the framework cannot represent even in principle, such as a prohibition on balancing one person's claim against another's in the first place. When you report the residue, tell me whether the inexpressible remainder is best understood as a defect of my target vocabulary or as evidence that the philosophical objection is less precise than its proponents take it to be.
