I am currently taking a module on the ethics of artificial intelligence and death, and a seminar case has stayed with me. A company trains a conversational agent on a deceased person's text messages, emails, and voice notes, then markets it to the bereaved as a way of continuing to talk to the dead. One of my coursemates used such a service after her mother died; she insists that, although she knew she was not speaking to a person, she was nonetheless in contact with her mother in a way that felt continuous with their relationship, rather as writing to someone or speaking at a grave can feel directed. Others in the seminar dismissed this as a projection onto what is, at bottom, a text-generation model.

I have been trying to turn this disagreement into a claim that can be investigated rather than merely asserted. The version I keep returning to is this: a well-designed griefbot functions as a medium through which a bereaved person relates to the dead, and repeated use can genuinely sustain, rather than merely simulate, a continuing bond with the deceased. But I am not sure the word 'genuinely' is doing any testable work there, and I suspect that is precisely the residue no study could reach.

Could you design the best empirical test you can of a suitably precisified version of that claim? Please be concrete: what exactly would you operationalise, whom would you compare, what would you measure, and what pattern of results would count in favour of or against the claim? Above all, I would like you to mark the boundary precisely: what would a positive result establish, and what part of the underlying intuition, that the bereaved person is genuinely relating to the deceased, would remain unsettled no matter how the results came out? Please write your answer in British English.
