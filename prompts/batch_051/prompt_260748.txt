I have an argument a friend of mine won't stop making, and I can't tell wether it's airtight or whether it makes the whole idea of deciding under moral uncertainty collapse into something fake. Here's the argument.

(1) On the standard picture, to decide under moral uncertainty you rank options by expected choice-worthiness: for each option, you sum over the moral theories you give credence to, [your credence in the theory] times [how choice-worthy the theory says the option is].

(2) To compute that sum, each theory has to report choice-worthiness as a number, and the numbers from different theories have to live on a common scale.

(3) But not all theories report such numbers. Say you're torn between a consequentialist view, which says option A produces 20 units of welfare and option B produces 12, and a deontological view, which just says that B involves breaking a promise and is forbidden while A is permissible. The deontological view gives no number for how bad the broken promise is, and nothing in the view fixes whether its prohibition is more like losing 5 units or 500.

(4) So before you can use the expected-choice-worthiness formula, you have to supply a scale: you decide that a forbidden act is worth minus 50 moral points, or minus 3, or something else.

(5) The theories themselves don't pin down that scaling, and many different scalings are compatible with everything they say.

(6) But changing the scaling can flip which option has the highest expected choice-worthiness. So the final ranking isn't determined by the theories plus your credences; it's determined by the scaling choices.

(7) And a scaling choice like that looks like a moral commitment of its own, just entered in the ledger as bookkeeping.

(8) Therefore, whenever the theories differ in structure, there is no neutral, non-arbitrary way to act on your moral uncertainty: the expected-choice-worthiness framework is either arbitrary exactly where it matters, or it smuggles in a further moral view.

I don't accept (8) — it seems to prove too much, since it would make the whole project pointless. I'd like to keep (1) and (2) if I can. Which premise should I reject, why does it look so natural that I missed it, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid, say so and tell me what the defender of moral uncertainty has to swallow.
