I am a philosophy professor who works on the history of utilitarian moral thought, and I should be grateful for your careful, expert analysis of a parallel I have been trying to assess. Please think through the structural similarities and differences rather than giving a general answer. I am not asking for a survey of the secondary literature or for citations; I want your own reasoning about whether the parallel is genuine.

Here is the parallel. In Jeremy Bentham's long unpublished essay commonly known as Offences Against One's Self, he develops a sustained argument against punishing homosexual acts. In one of the central passages, as I read it, he takes the common charge that such acts are unnatural and asks what work the word unnatural is doing. If the ground is that the acts do not lead to procreation, then the same ground would equally condemn masturbation, intercourse during pregnancy, intercourse after menopause, and intercourse with an infertile partner, none of which his contemporaries punish. If the ground is instead that the acts excite disgust or antipathy, then Bentham says the feeling is not an independent reason; it is at most a sign that the speaker has run out of rational objections. The rhetorical effect is to show that the objection to the act rests on an inconsistent classification plus an unargued emotion.

The parallel I keep seeing is with a later debate in bioethics about the so-called wisdom of repugnance. There, one side says that a visceral feeling of revulsion, for example toward human cloning or genetic enhancement, can be a fallible but real moral signal: it may track dangers that the agent cannot yet articulate. The other side replies that disgust has repeatedly attached itself to harmless or even beneficial innovations, and that a feeling of revulsion is evidence of something about the person feeling it, not about the object. This looks to me like the same dialectical structure as Bentham's argument: an appeal to a felt aversion is met with the demand for a separately statable harm, and the refusal to treat the feeling as independently probative.

My question is whether this is a deep identity of argumentative structure or only a superficial resemblance. In particular, is Bentham's deflation of disgust embedded in a specifically utilitarian framework that makes it stronger or weaker than the modern deflation of repugnance? Does the parallel predict anything that turns out false when one side is transplanted into the other debate? And does Bentham, in the essay, ultimately treat antipathy as having no evidential value at all, or only no legal weight, in a way that breaks the parallel with the modern epistemic question? I would like your answer in continuous prose only, as you might write in a letter to a colleague: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and please use British English.
