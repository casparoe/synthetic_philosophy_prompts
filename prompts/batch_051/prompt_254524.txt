i've been sitting on a little theory for a while and i'd like a honest verdict on it, because i can't tell if it's a real distinction or just a programmers metaphor that got out of hand.

as far as i can tell there are two ways to know what a piece of code did: you can step through it live, or you can read a trace from a run that wasn't instrumented. and i've started to think my own cognition is always the first case. whenever i try to observe myself solving a problem mid-flight, the process changes: i slow down, i second-guess steps that would have just executed on their own, and sometimes i lose a thought the moment i try to hold a pointer to it. afterwards i reconstruct a clean linear story of how i solved it, but i don't trust that story either, because i know from debugging that a trace produced while the thing is being watched is a trace of the watched process, not a log of the original one.

so i've been calling this the introspection overhead principle (iop): any cognitive process you observe is a different process than the one that would have run unobserved, and the report you give afterward is a reconstruction, not a log. the stronger claim i want checked is that this explains both why thinking about thinking sometimes helps (when you're re-examining a finished, cached thought) and why it backfires (when you attach the debugger to a hot path).

is there a established name for this in philosophy or psychology, and is it actually true? and if it is true, does it put a hard limit on metacognition in general, or only on this live-monitoring kind? i'm not after a label alone, i want to know where the principle stops being true.
