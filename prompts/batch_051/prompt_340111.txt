Suppose you are advising the scientific program committee of a foundation that can fund three years of conceptual work on a problem in the foundations of the life sciences. The problem—call it mechanistic anomaly detection—is this. Researchers regularly claim that two systems (two cell lines, a model organism and a patient, a neural circuit in two individuals) share the same mechanism, or that a drug, mutation, or lesion has switched a system to a different mechanism. Yet there is no settled account of what it takes for a mechanism to remain the same across changes in parameter values, context, or network structure, or across substitution of components. The committee is considering five lines of work: (1) a criterion of mechanism identity requiring sameness of the component parts, the operations they perform, and their organization, with principled allowances for normal variation; (2) a criterion that identifies mechanisms by their causal topology or regulatory logic, so that identity survives replacement of parts; (3) a deflationary account on which sameness of mechanism is relative to the explanatory or interventional purpose at hand; (4) formal work on when a perturbation is a difference of degree within one dynamical regime versus a difference of kind across a regime boundary; and (5) the question whether identity criteria must be domain-specific—signaling cascades, metabolic cycles, homeostatic loops, developmental programs—or whether a unified criterion is feasible.

Which two or three of these lines of work should the committee prioritize, and in what order? Defend the ranking explicitly: for each selected line, say why it is upstream of or downstream from the others, how much routine biological inference would change if it were settled, how tractable it is for philosophers and conceptually minded biologists rather than purely empirical researchers, and what results over the next few years would move it up or down the list.
