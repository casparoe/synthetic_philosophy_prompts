I'm on the board of a small housing co-op, and we're currently fighting about whether to spend most of our reserve fund on reinforcing the foundation against a failure that's very unlikely but would be genuinely ruinous. I keep noticing a pattern in these arguments that I don't have a word for. People treat a 1-in-1000 chance of ruin as if it looms much larger than 1-in-1000—they'll reject an option even when the expected value strongly favors it. But the same neighbors will shrug off a 10% chance of a merely bad outcome as if it were nothing, and won't pay $40 to fix a 2% annual chance of a flooded storage room. So it isn't risk aversion in any steady, across-the-board sense; the small probability only gets magnified when the downside would be catastrophic. Does this pattern have an established name? I'm not satisfied with a bare label, though. I want to know how it differs from ordinary risk aversion and from the common idea that people overweight small probabilities generally, and whether a coherent decision theory can build this in without making choices irrational or inconsistent. You're an expert on formal decision theory—please brainstorm a few candidate concepts first, then carfully sort out which one actually fits the pattern I'm describing and where its edges are.
