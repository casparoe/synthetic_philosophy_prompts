I'm a novelist, not a philosopher or physicist, so please correct me if I misstate something. I'm working on a novel where two characters keep getting swapped in everyone's perception until the narration itself stops treating them as separate people, and I fell down a rabbit hole about what it means for two things to be distinct but indistinguishable. The classic case I keep coming back to is Black's two iron spheres: an otherwise empty universe containing only two exact duplicate spheres, some fixed distance apart. Black took that to be a counterexample to the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles — the claim that if two things share every property, they're not two things but one. The standard comeback is that the spheres don't share every property: one is at one location and the other at another, so they differ spatially or relationally.

Here's the variation I'd like you to work through for me. Keep the otherwise-empty universe, but replace the two iron spheres with two identical quantum particles of the same species — say, two electrons — described by the usual two-particle state in which swapping the particles makes no physical difference (the state is antisymmetrized, so the labels are doing no real work). In that setup, the locational fallback of "this one's here, that one's there" seems to vanish much more strongly than in Black's case: the state doesnt assign any property to "this electron" as opposed to "that electron." There just isn't a well-defined fact about which electron is at which location.

So my question is: does the quantum variation destroy the relational defense of PII, or does that defense just need to get more subtle? Work through what someone trying to save PII should say here. In particular, I've seen a move claiming the two fermions are "weakly discernible" via a symmetric but irreflexive relation like having opposite spin. Does that genuinely save the principle, or does it just redefine "distinguishing property" so the principle survives as a formality? I mostly want to know how the analysis changes from Black's classical case.
