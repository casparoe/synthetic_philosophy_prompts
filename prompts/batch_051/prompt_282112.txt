Act as a professional philosopher who works on reflective equilibrium and has experience turning it into procedures that non-philosophers can actually run. Think carefully, and before you commit to a final procedure, spend a minute on which features of reflective equilibrium are load-bearing and which can be simplified for a room of tired, non-philosophical clinicians.

I chair a small hospital ethics committee — roughly a dozen of us, mostly physicians and nurses plus a social worker, a chaplain, and one community member. We meet weekly for about an hour, cases usually arrive with 48 hours notice, and urgent cases sometimes get thirty minutes on the phone. The committee only recommends; the attending physician decides, but in practice they nearly always follow our recommendation, so our reasoning actually matters. Right now our method is that whoever argues with the most confidence wins, and if nobody feels terrible afterward we call it consensus.

Someone walked me through reflective equilibrium, and it sounds like what we need: start from your considered judgments — the ones that survive reflection rather than the ones made in panic, fear, disgust, or self-interest — form candidate principles that would make sense of them, and when principle and judgment clash, go back and forth revising whichever you can defend with least confidence, until the whole set hangs together. But I cant hand the committee a lecture, and I've watched "just use reflective equilibrium" turn into "trust your gut but say we balanced things."

Please turn reflective equilibrium into a reusable decision procedure my committee could actually run. I want: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each before moving on; how to test whether a member's initial judgment is considered enough to enter the pool; where the candidate principles come from or how they get phrased; when the procedure should stop and hand the decision to the full committee plus a patient representative because it's a values call; and the points where judgment cannot be eliminated no matter how faithfully we run it.

Also say what the procedure cannot do — the kinds of cases it will mishandle, the judgments it will smuggle back in under a new name, and where the time constraints force approximations. Then run it on these three cases:

1. A 34-year-old with severe, long-standing anorexia is refusing a nasogastric feeding tube. She says she understands she may die and accepts that risk. No advance directive. The treating team is split, and the family is begging us to recommend involuntary feeding. The committee's overwhelming pre-reflective reaction is that she is sick and we should save her.

2. A 70-year-old after cardiac arrest, no advance directive, no realistic chance of recovering consciousness. Three adult children split two-to-one on withdrawing the ventilator. The two who want to continue cite religious reasons; the one who wants to stop says their father always said he never wanted machines. The ICU is under severe bed pressure, and some committee members openly mention it.

3. A competent patient with advanced cancer is demanding a third round of treatment the oncologists call futile. Nursing staff say continuing is harming the patient's dignity, and the committee's sympathy is strongly with the staff.

For at least one of these, I expect the procedure to return an answer the committee will not like. Flag which one, and tell me why that is still what the procedure says.
