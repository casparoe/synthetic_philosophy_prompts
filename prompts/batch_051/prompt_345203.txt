I coordinate the assessment committee for our department's first-year honors mathematics sequence, which covers proof-based linear algebra and single-variable real analysis. The sequence ends not with a traditional written final but with a short oral "proof defense": each student is assigned one theorem whose proof was developed in the course, presents the proof, and then answers questions from an examiner. We run about 140 of these each term; the examiners are graduate students with uneven teaching experience and no more than a half-day of calibration beforehand; each defense lasts twenty minutes. The stakes are real: passing is required to continue in the honors track, and borderline cases are reviewed by a faculty member, but we would like to keep the fraction of cases that reach faculty review small.

The committee has converged on a working principle that we want the examiners to operationalize: a student genuinely understands a proof to the extent that they can (i) state the central idea of the proof in their own words, (ii) say why each hypothesis is there by identifying where the argument would fail without it, and (iii) adapt or modify the argument under a small change to the setup — as opposed to merely reproducing the proof correctly from memory. Handing this principle to examiners as general guidance has produced wildly inconsistent judgments, so I would like it turned into a concrete reusable procedure: an ordered list of questions or probes for the examiner to run, with a rubric for what counts as a sufficient answer to each; explicit stopping rules for "not demonstrated" and for "clearly demonstrated"; and a defined notion of "borderline" that is the only thing sent to faculty review. Please also mark the points where the procedure cannot eliminate examiner judgment, and say plainly what the procedure will reliably miss or get wrong.

Finally, run the procedure on three students to show how it behaves in practice: (a) a student who reproduces the assigned proof perfectly, notation and all, but cannot answer any question about why a hypothesis is needed or what would happen if it were weakened; (b) a student who stumbles badly over notation in the opening definitions, but when prompted describes the proof's main idea in plain terms, explains the role of one key hypothesis, and reconstructs the argument with a small gap at the end; and (c) a student who gives a correct but non-standard proof of their own devising, executes the standard techniques competently when asked, but cannot connect their proof to the one developed in the course or identify its central idea. For each, I would like to know what verdict the procedure returns, and I want to be warned now if the procedure is going to return a verdict that I or the committee will resist.
