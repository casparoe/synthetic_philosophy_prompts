I'm preparing a seminar session on the problem of prelapsarian animal suffering for students who have already absorbed the usual moves, and I want to force the discussion past them. The difficulty, as I see it, is this. The evolutionary record gives us hundreds of millions of years of predation, parasitism, disease, and mass extinction before any human being existed, and much of that animal suffering is of a kind we would find morally significant if we were considering creatures capable of suffering at all. In tension with that, the doctrine of creation holds that the world God made was good in more than a trivial sense, and the traditional reading of the Fall treats human sin as the point at which death takes hold of the created order and creation begins to display corruption. So a satisfactory account must either explain why a good creation ran for eons on the suffering of nonhuman creatures, or explain how a fall committed by humans could have consequences that reach back before the humans who would commit it existed.

The standard strategies I've collected are these. (1) Deny the natural history: before the Fall animals were herbivorous and free of suffering, and carnivory, parasitism, and disease are later corruptions. (2) Restrict the meaning of the death introduced by the Fall to spiritual death, alienation from God, so that pre-human biological death requires no special explanation. (3) Trace the corruption to an earlier angelic fall, so that animal suffering belongs to a creation already damaged before the Edenic state. (4) Make the effects of the Fall retroactive or atemporal, so that the whole temporal order, including the deep evolutionary past, is subjected to corruption by a sin committed later from the creatures' point of view. (5) Read the goodness of creation as a goodness of function, order, and fittingness rather than moral or hedonic perfection, so that predation and extinction are not defects in the world's goodness. (6) Deny that nonhuman animals suffer in the morally relevant sense, on the grounds that they lack the higher awareness that would make pain an evil for the one who undergoes it. (7) Treat animal suffering as a cost internal to God's chosen method of creation, justified by the goods the evolutionary process yields and finally answered in a cosmic redemption. (8) Restrict moral standing to creatures made in the image of God, so that animal suffering and death are not the sort of fact that calls for a theodicy in the first place.

I want one more strategy of a different kind from all eight. By "different kind" I mean a different principle of reconciliation, not a restatement, a special case, or a conjunction of items on the list; someone already committed to one of the eight should still learn something structural from the new item. If you think the list is exhaustive as it stands, you may say so, but only if you earn it: construct the strongest nearby candidate you can, say why it seems to differ from every item above, and show precisely which of the eight it collapses into. Otherwise give me the new strategy, state in one sentence what principle it uses that none of the eight uses, and give an honest verdict on whether it dissolves the tension or merely relocates it.
