I work in causal inference — structural causal models, do-calculus, counterfactuals, confounding, and mediation — and I am fluent in that vocabulary; in theology I am only an interested amateur. My rough understanding of the debate is this. Augustine and Calvin hold that God unconditionally elects some people to salvation, and that grace so renews the elect's will that it certainly, yet voluntarily, comes to faith; Arminius holds that election is conditional on God's foreknowledge of who will believe, and that the enabling grace offered to the will is genuinely resistible. I keep hearing this described as a dispute about causation: what it is for grace to cause faith, whether foreknowledge causes or merely registers the thing foreknown, what it means for grace to be resistible, and whether a divine decree determines the will or leaves it free.

Restate that debate, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use: treat the divine decree and grace as variables or operations in a structural model, and the human decision to believe or not believe as an outcome. I am not asking for an analogy, and I am not asking you to adjudicate the theology; I am asking for a systematic translation.

Then tell me what did not survive the translation. Where does the causal-model vocabulary force a choice the theologians were trying to avoid, or lack any expression for a claim they treat as central? For each such residue, give your view: is the causal vocabulary missing something, or is the original claim less clear than its defenders take it to be?
