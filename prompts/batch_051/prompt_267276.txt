Could you act as a political philosopher who works on church-state relations and theories of secularism for this one? Take your time and maybe brainstorm a few readings before you settle on one, because I keep going back and forth on it.

Here's the practice that's bugging me. In Germany there's this thing called church tax, Kirchensteuer. If you're a registered member of one of the recognized "public-law" religious communities—mostly the Catholic church or the Protestant regional churches, and in some states Jewish communities too—the state collects a surcharge on your income tax, usually around 8 or 9 per cent of your income-tax bill, and hands it straight to your church. The weird part to me is how membership is handled. You become a member for these purposes by being baptized, which for most people happens as an infant, so you're opted in by your parents. And you stay a member, and keep paying, until you go to a civil registry office or local court, file a Kirchenaustritt (a formal exit), and sometimes pay a small fee. If you don't do that, the state will keep collecting from you your whole working life. Once you do it, you stop paying, but the church treats you as having left, with consequences for things like church weddings or being a godparent. Germany is supposed to be a secular, neutral state under the Basic Law, and yet the state is effectively maintaining and enforcing the membership rosters of religious bodies and collecting money for them.

I know the surrounding systems differ alot: England has an established church with bishops in the House of Lords and the monarch as its head, but no church tax; the US has no establishment and no state-collected church dues, with churches as tax-exempt voluntary associations; France has laïcité and, outside Alsace-Moselle, doesn't fund churches out of tax receipts either. Germany looks like some fourth thing.

What I want to know is: what philosophical commitments does this practice actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of religious membership and of church-state relations that makes sense of it. In particular: does it presuppose that church membership is more like a civil status that you inherit at birth and terminate by a state-recognized declaration, rather than an ongoing voluntary commitment? Does it presuppose a particular conception of state neutrality—maybe one where the state is neutral among recognized organized bodies rather than neutral between belief and non-belief? Is the exit requirement doing the main justificatory work, and if so, what view of "choice" does that rest on? Also say which parts the practice strictly presupposes versus which parts it merely happens to be compatible with, and what would have to change in practice if you swapped the underlying view for a more voluntarist, US-style picture of religious membership.
