I run the weekly model-review meeting for a visual perception lab that compares computational models of how timing illusions arise, and I need a reusable procedure for classifying any model's account of such an illusion as Orwellian, Stalinesque, or neither. The users are graduate students and postdocs who know the experimental paradigms well but have not read the philosophical literature on the timing of experience, so the procedure must be usable in about fifteen minutes from the model paper's timing story and the stimulus/timing diagram alone, with no background reading. The stakes are moderate and recurring: each week we assign one model to a category in our shared model registry, these labels later make their way into literature reviews and grant narratives, and a misclassification that survives into a submitted manuscript is professionally embarrassing even though nothing safety-critical hangs on it. I make the final call, but I want the procedure to do the real sorting.

Ground the procedure in the following distinction. An Orwellian account says the nervous system produces an initial conscious content that is then revised or overwritten before or as it is reported, so the subject never enjoys the uncorrected first version. A Stalinesque account says the content is held back and edited before it first enters consciousness, so the subject's first conscious content is already the corrected version. A model that denies any single moment of entry into consciousness — for example, a Dennettian multiple-drafts model — should fall into a third bucket rather than being forced into one of the first two. The procedure should therefore test early whether the model posits a discrete first presentation to awareness at all, before asking when any revision happens.

Write the procedure as an ordered checklist or decision flow. Its inputs are (a) a model's account of one experimental paradigm and (b) the stimulus/timing diagram for that paradigm. Its output is exactly one of these labels with a one-line justification: Orwellian-revision, Stalinesque-delay, underdetermined-between-the-two, or no-single-entry. Include the worksheet questions the reviewer must answer about the model's timing structure, phrased so that someone who does not know the labels can still answer them.

Then apply the procedure to three worked examples: the classic color phi display (two spatially separated flashes of different colors reported as a single moving dot that changes color in mid-path), a flash-lag display (a static flash aligned with a moving object is seen as trailing behind it), and one further paradigm of your choosing from backward masking, metacontrast masking, or a Libet-style timing judgment. For each, show which questions in the procedure do the classifying, what the output is, and the exact point where a reasonable reviewer could reach a different result depending on how the model treats the interval between stimulus and report. Include at least one worked case in which the procedure returns an answer the lab will not like — for instance, it classifies the lab's own preferred model as Stalinesque, or it declares two models we treat as rivals indistinguishable under the procedure — and do not soften that result.

End by stating plainly what the procedure cannot settle: cases where Orwellian and Stalinesque stories are empirically equivalent given the data, cases where a "revision" can be redescribed as a "delay" without changing any prediction, and the points where the reviewer must still judge by hand, such as when a model attributes the timing effect to response bias or motor-side shifts rather than to anything about experience itself. Finally, say what the procedure deliberately ignores — for example, whether the relevant contents are representational or whether the timing judgments are veridical — so that users do not lean on it for questions it was not built to answer.
