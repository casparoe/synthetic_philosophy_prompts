I'm a novelist, not an academic, and I've got myself into a spot where I'm giving a lunchtime talk to a pretty sharp crowd at a cognitive neuroscience program — mostly PhD students and postdocs who do fMRI/neuroimaging and computational modeling, plus one or two older faculty who treat philosophy as soft. The talk is basically the epiphenomenalist chapter of my next essay collection. My line: conscious experience is real, it has qualitative character, but it doesn't do any causal work. The brain is a closed causal system; the feeling of a decision or a thought is more like the heat coming off a laptop than a cog in the machine. I actually do defend that in the book.

The part I'm nervous about is the Q&A, because I'm not trained in philosophy and I know this position has a famous problem — sometimes called the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If consciousness never causes anything, how did I write a book arguing that consciousness never causes anything? My judgments about my own experience are themselves the evidence I'm relying on, but if epiphenomenalism is true, those judgments are caused by something else and the experience itself is inert. I can feel that objection coming from the audience in a more science-y vocabulary.
