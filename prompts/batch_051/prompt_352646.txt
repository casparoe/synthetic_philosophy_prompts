Take the old rule that ignorance of the law is no excuse. People usually defend it with something like three moves: first, the law is presumed to be knowable by everyone (or at least the courts pretend it is); second, if ignorance excused, people would have a strong incentive to stay ignorant rather than find out what the law says; third, the criminal law mostly tracks common morality, so an ordinary decent person already knows what is genuinely off-limits. Now think about a modern administrative state where a huge share of offenses are malum prohibitum, which just means conduct that is wrong only because a rule makes it wrong, and where the relevant rules are scattered across thousands of pages of agency text that change faster than any nonlawyer could follow and often have no discernible connection to moral intuition. In that setting it's not just that a few people are ignorant; virtually everyone is unavoidably ignorant about some chunk of what the law requires.

So: does the standard argument for that old rule survive this development, or does the development kill it by undercutting it's knowability and common-morality premises? If it survives, say which premise is left doing the real work and whether that was always the load-bearing part of the argument. If it doesn't survive, say what should replace it — maybe an excuse for rules that weren't reasonably accessible, or just not treating as crimes those malum prohibitum offenses with no moral content. Be specific about which premise the development bears on.
