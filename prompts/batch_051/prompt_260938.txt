In a philosophy of mind seminar I have been working through the multiple drafts model of consciousness that Daniel Dennett develops as an alternative to the Cartesian theater. As I understand the view, it denies that the brain contains any place or moment at which contents are handed to consciousness and thereby become conscious. Instead, the brain continuously produces many parallel streams of content, none of them conscious in and of themselves; a stream becomes a conscious content only through its downstream effects, such as its availability to reports, to memory, and to the guidance of behavior. The model calls the processes that draw on a stream in these ways probes, and it treats the content a probe reveals as relative to that particular probe.

The argument that troubles me runs as follows. (1) If no stream of content is conscious in itself, then whether any content is conscious is fixed only relative to a probe. (2) A subject contains indefinitely many possible probes, and probes can occur at different times and in different contexts. (3) Different probes can assay different contents: a speeded forced-choice report, a delayed considered report, and a motor response may reflect different stages of processing of a briefly presented stimulus, and they can yield different answers about its content or timing. (4) On the model, there is no probe-independent fact of the matter that could arbitrate which of these probe-elicited contents is the one the subject really was conscious of. (5) So in such cases there is no determinate fact of the matter about what the subject was conscious of at all — there are only facts about which probes yield which responses. (6) But there is a determinate fact of the matter about what I am conscious of right here and now: my current experience has a definite character, and in attending to it I encounter something that seems fixed independently of how I might later report it. (7) Therefore the multiple drafts model is committed to something false, unless it is prepared to deny (6) and lapse into eliminativism.

I would most like to keep (6), and I also find (1) through (5) natural; so I cannot tell whether the argument is valid and I must surrender one of the model's central commitments, or whether one of the steps above contains a subtle mistake. Tell me which premise or inference to reject, why that step initially looked compelling, and what rejecting it costs — both for the multiple drafts model and for the idea that there is a fixed fact about what I am conscious of at a moment. Please answer in American English.
