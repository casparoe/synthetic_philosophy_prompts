Here's the essay:

When people want to show the difference between mechanical behaviour and real flexibility, they sometimes bring up the sphex wasp. The version I heard is that the wasp has a very rigid nesting routine. It digs a burrow, stings a cricket to paralyse it, drags the cricket to the mouth of the burrow, leaves it there, goes inside to check the burrow, comes back out, and then drags the cricket in. But if, while the wasp is inside checking, someone nudges the cricket a few centimetres away from the entrance, the wasp comes back out, drags the cricket back, and then goes inside to check the burrow again. Do this enough times and the wasp just loops forever: drag, check, drag, check. It never seems to notice that the cricket has already been handled or that something weird is going on. and that's meant to be the difference between it and us. The wasp is a little machine running one program; we step back, notice the program, and change what we're doing.

I want to push back on that picture a bit. First, humans do sphexish-looking things all the time. When I lose my keys I check the same pocket four times. When the wifi drops I keep tapping the same button even though it didn't work a second ago. That's not exactly the wasp, because I eventually stop, but it makes me think rigidity and flexibility aren't two separate kinds of behaviour. They're more like two ends of one scale.

Second, and this is the part I actually care about: maybe our flexibility is just rigidity one level up. What if humans aren't non-programmed, but are running a much bigger program that has a special subroutine which activates when our predictions fail? When our routine stops working, we don't escape the program; we switch to the 'try something new' routine. That switch is itself completely predictable. On that picture, being flexible is simply a fancier kind of sphexishness, not an escape from it.

But then I argue with myself, because I think there still might be a real difference hiding in the example. The wasp's routine can't be revised by the wasp; mine can. Even if my 'try something new' response is itself a routine, it's a routine that revises my other routines, which lets me fail less badly over time. The wasp is stuck with a fixed policy; I can change my policy about my policies. That sounds like a difference in kind, not just degree, and maybe that's all free will needs to be: not being uncaused, but being able to climb up a level and edit yourself.

That leaves me with a question: is the sphex example even an argument about free will, or is it really an argument about what kind of flexibility something needs before we treat it as free and responsible? I get that a creature could be sphexish about being non-sphexish. I just don't know how much that matters.
