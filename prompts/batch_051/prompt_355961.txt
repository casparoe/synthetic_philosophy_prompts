I'm in a decision-theory seminar and after the professor walked us through the Allais problem, two classmates, Che and Mara, got into an argument I keep going back and forth on. The choices, with payoffs in millions, were these. Problem 1: option A is $1M for sure; option B is 89% chance of $1M, 10% chance of $5M, and 1% chance of $0. Problem 2: option C is 11% chance of $1M and 89% chance of $0; option D is 10% chance of $5M and 90% chance of $0. Che said he'd take A in problem 1 and D in problem 2. Mara said the independence axiom rules that pair out: A and B share a common 89% chance of $1M, and C and D replace that with a common 89% chance of $0, so the only thing left to compare is the same two residual gambles—a sure $1M versus a 10/11 shot at $5M with a 1/11 shot at $0. Someone who prefers A to B should therefore prefer C to D, and Che has both without being bothered by it. Che said he knows his pattern violates the axiom, but the axiom is just a condition a preference has to satisfy for expected-utility theory to represent it, not a proof that picking D over C is a mistake. In problem 1 the certainty of a million is worth a lot to him; in problem 2 nothing is certain anyway, so he'd rather have the 10% shot at five million than the extra 1% shot at one million. Rewriting the options as two-stage lotteries, he said, doesn't change what it's like to face the actual choice. Mara came back that if he really ranks D over C while ranking A over B, he's open to a money pump: he'd pay something to swap B for A and C for D, and someone could string those trades together. She didn't finish spelling out the exact sequence, though, and I couldn't reconstruct it, so I'm not sure if that part is right. Che replied that even if some clever sequence of trades grabbed his money, he doesn't have to accept the package—he can keep choosing A and D one at a time—and anyway his pattern is perfectly representable by rank-dependent or prospect-style theories, so his choices aren't incoherent, they're just not expected-utility choices. Mara said representable by some model isn't the same as defensible, and the Allais paradox is a fact about how people systematically choose, not a spare axiom of rational choice.

Go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about which specific point, including whether the money-pump claim actually goes through and whether Che's certainty argument does any real work.
