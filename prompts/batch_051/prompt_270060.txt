Say you buy the Bentham line that a lot of natural-rights talk is dressed-up noise: it names a right without saying who owes what to whom, how much they owe, or what happens when they come up short. Fine. Now say a constitutional convention that shares the worry proposes a permanent rule: whenever a legislature or court invokes a natural right (or human right, or fundamental right, or any right not already spelled out in enacted law) as a ground for requiring or forbidding someone's conduct, that claim has no legal effect unless, within 180 days, the body files a concretization naming the duty-bearers, the specific conduct required or forbidden, and an actual remedy. If no concretization is filed, the right-language is struck from the opinion or statute as inoperative while the rest of the instrument stays in force.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a way of scoring points for legal positivism. Walk me through how it would actually behave. What does it incentivize in legislators, judges, and litigants? How does it get gamed—do people just move the natural-right talk into preambles, relabel it dignity or social order, file deliberately vague concretizations, or something else? Where would it fail first? And does the rule end up having to smuggle in a theory of what makes a right a right in order to police its own boundary?
