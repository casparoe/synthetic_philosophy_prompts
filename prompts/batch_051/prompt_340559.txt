As a novelist, I have spent years making a version of the familiar humanistic case for public support of literature: that literary fiction does a distinctive kind of work because it trains readers to inhabit perspectives unlike their own, to hold conflicting inner lives in mind at once, and to recognize that other people are the authors of their own stories; that these capacities are public goods in a democracy; and that the market alone will under-supply such work because its benefits are diffuse, delayed, and hard to price. I have made this argument to arts councils, to students, and to myself, but I no longer know whether it survives two developments that now shape how fiction is actually produced and encountered: large language models that can generate competent and occasionally moving fiction on demand, and recommender systems that increasingly decide what a reader sees next. Does it matter for the argument whether the text on the other side of the page was written by a human consciousness that lived, intended, and selected the words? If it does not, then the argument may still go through in a world of machine-generated fiction; if it does, then the argument may be dead, not merely in need of repair.

Please treat this as a professional philosopher of art and public policy would. I want you to first identify the argument's load-bearing premises — I suspect at least three: an empirical premise about what reading narrative fiction does to readers, an evaluative premise about why that effect constitutes a public good rather than a private gratification, and an economic premise about why public subsidy is the appropriate remedy for its underprovision. Then tell me which of these premises the two developments genuinely press on, and whether the argument as a whole collapses, survives, or can be repaired into something weaker but still worth asserting. If you think it survives, say exactly why machine authorship and algorithmic curation are not fatal to the premises; if you think it is undercut, say what a novelist or a funding body should now argue instead. I am also prepared to learn that the argument was always weaker than its defenders, myself included, admitted, and that these developments only make that visible. Do not settle for the general observation that artificial intelligence is changing culture; I want to know precisely where the argument gives way, or why it does not.
