I sit on the external safety board of a mid-sized AI lab, and by Thursday I need to circulate a short written opinion on a decision that has split the lab's leadership. The question is whether the lab should sign a proposed reciprocal disclosure agreement with its two main competitors, decline it, or propose a specific amendment. I am the board member who tends to push the bargaining-theoretic lens in these discussions, the one shaped by years of reading rationalist blogs and international-relations work on conflict, and I want the memo to stay close to Fearon's account of why rational actors nonetheless end up in costly fights: private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and stakes that cannot be cleanly divided. I would rather the memo apply that framework than drift into general AI-safety commonsense.

Here is the case. The lab's interpretability team has developed an unpublished method that flags a class of deceptive instrumental reasoning in large models at an unusually low false-positive rate. The board regards the method as one of the lab's only genuine sources of leverage over two larger rivals. Through an industry working group, those rivals have proposed an accord with three main obligations. Signatories would agree to refrain from any training run above a specified compute threshold unless they file a public safety case at least ninety days in advance; to deposit any safety-relevant technique, including our method, into a shared repository within thirty days of signing; and to expose their safety cases to reciprocal audit by the other signatories. In return, the lab would gain access to the rivals' safety cases and audit rights, and the accord would make it harder for any signatory to race past the threshold without warning.

The lab's chief executive recommends declining. Her argument is that the accord asks us to give away our one piece of leverage before the larger labs have handed over anything enforceable; their promises to reciprocate are cheap talk, and they will use our method and our safety cases to calibrate their own sprint while we lose the ability to trade access later for much stronger concessions on training pauses and external review. The head of interpretability answers that this is the very bargain-failure dynamic the rivals are counting on: if every lab withholds its best safety information for leverage, the three labs converge on an unchecked costly race, an outcome all of them would pay to avoid. She concedes the commitment problem is real but thinks the audit mechanism is a partially credible third-party device, whereas the policy of withholding and trading later assumes a moment of leverage that may never arrive. A third board member has floated an amendment under which we would place our method with a neutral escrow agent, to be released to each rival only after that rival verifiably deposits an item of comparable safety value, so that disclosure becomes conditional and matched rather than unilateral. The chief executive replies that this merely relocates the commitment problem to the escrow agent instead of solving it; the interpretability head worries it would stall the accord for months.

Please draft the short memo I would circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action — sign, decline, or sign with the escrow amendment or some variant of it — and give the board reasons it can act on. Engage the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously rather than dismissing it; say which of the three mechanisms is doing the most work in this decision; and give a considered view on whether the escrow amendment solves the commitment problem or only moves it up a level. End by telling me where your reasoning is most likely to fail, that is, which argument or factual development would lead you to change your recommendation to a different course of action. Write it as continuous prose, the way a circulated memo would read, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
