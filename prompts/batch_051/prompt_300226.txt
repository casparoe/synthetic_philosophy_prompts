I am drafting a short essay in moral philosophy. Its planned thesis is that prioritarianism does not escape the leveling-down objection as cleanly as is usually assumed, and that the objection is better understood as a diagnostic than as a knockdown argument. Below are the notes for the argument. I would like you to critique them before I write the full essay.

The setup. Telic egalitarianism holds that distributive inequality is non-instrumentally bad: a distribution can be worse simply in virtue of some people being worse off than others through no relevant difference. Prioritarianism holds that only absolute welfare levels matter, but gains to the worse off count for more: a benefit has greater moral weight the lower the recipient's absolute level. The leveling-down objection is usually aimed at the telic egalitarian. If equality itself had value, then making the better off worse off while helping no one would be in one respect good, because it reduces inequality; but a change that harms someone and helps no one cannot be good in any respect. Prioritarianism avoids this implication, at least in the fixed-population, deterministic cases in which the objection is usually run, because leveling down always lowers the weighted sum of well-being.

My tentative moves, in order:

First, the objection only gets traction against a monistic telic egalitarian who treats equality as a final value that always counts for something. A pluralist telic egalitarian can concede that leveling down is in one respect better, while all-things-considered worse. The objection then needs a further argument: an explanation of why this particular respect should be inadmissible as a reason alongside absolute levels, rather than merely outweighed. So the real debate shifts to whether relational facts about distributions can count as outcome goods.

Second, prioritarianism avoids the implication only by treating its weighting function as primitive and non-relational. That buys it immunity from the objection, but it owes an account of why a lower absolute level makes a benefit matter more. The most natural candidate accounts are either relational — fairness, equal consideration, or the idea that the worse off have stronger claims — in which case a leveled-down analogue can be reconstructed and the escape is only apparent; or individualistic — something like diminishing marginal moral importance of well-being — in which case prioritarianism risks collapsing into welfarism with a concave transformation and loses its claim to be a distinct view about priority to the worse off.

Third, the disagreement that actually matters is not whether leveling down is all-things-considered good, but what kind of normative property equality and priority are: properties of outcomes, requirements on acts, or constraints on how distributions may be justified. Framed this way, the leveling-down objection is a diagnostic for what a theory's evaluative vocabulary must include, not a standalone argument for prioritarianism.

Please give me exactly five comments on these notes, each one or two sentences, as a numbered list with no introduction or conclusion. Allocate them as follows: (1) an accuracy comment on the first move, in particular whether the objection is fairly described as needing a further argument once pluralism is on the table; (2) an accuracy comment on the second move, in particular whether it is fair to describe prioritarianism's weighting as primitive and unexplained; (3) the strongest objection a committed prioritarian would press against the argument as a whole; (4) the place where the essay is most likely to go wrong for a reader, and the fix; (5) a verdict on whether the central thesis is worth writing up, with one suggestion for raising the stakes.
