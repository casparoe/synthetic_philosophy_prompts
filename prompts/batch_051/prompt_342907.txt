I have been re-reading F. P. Ramsey's 1927 paper "Facts and Propositions", and one passage has been bothering me. He is arguing against Russell's view that a judgment has a fact as its object, and writes:

"So also 'that the knife is to the left of the book', whether it is true or false, cannot be the name of a fact."

Here is my confusion. I understand this as a point about 'that'-clauses: they are not names of special objects, so the analysis of judgment need not posit "facts" as relata. But I often see people in rationalist circles move from something like this to a full deflationary theory of truth: that truth is not a substantive property at all, that 'is true' is just a device for assertion, disquotation, or prosentence formation, and that correspondence theories are reifying a metaphor. Those seem like two different claims — one about the grammar of 'that p', and one about the nature of truth — but I am not sure how deep the difference really is.

What would a sophisticated correspondence theorist say in response?
