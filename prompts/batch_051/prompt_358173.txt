For a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, construct a decision tree that sorts students into positions on how rational credence should defer to objective chance. Begin from whether learning a proposition's objective chance obliges a rational agent to set her credence equal to that chance, and make every internal node a question a student can answer before knowing the names of the standard views. The nodes should include whether objective chances can be nonzero in a deterministic world; whether facts about chance reduce to the total arrangement of local matters of fact or are primitive propensities; whether a reliable crystal ball reporting the outcome of a genuinely chancy process is admissible evidence or a case in which chance no longer constrains credence; and whether an undermining future, one whose occurrence would falsify the very chance law that assigned its probability, forces a departure from the simple equality. The leaves must include at least a non-Humean propensity view that keeps the deference principle unamended, Lewis's best-system analysis of chance paired with Hall's New Principle, an actual frequentism on which deference is really direct inference from actual frequencies, a compatibilist view on which deterministic worlds can support nonzero objective chances, and a subjectivist view on which there is no objective chance to defer to. Build the tree from the space of answers rather than from the list of names, diagnose each unoccupied combination as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and state which pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate.
