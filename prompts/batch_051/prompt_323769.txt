I am a novelist, and a pattern in the moral scenes I write has begun to look like something more general than a quirk of my characters. Again and again, a character is assessed not by what she does or believes but by how well she can make herself readable on demand. A wife who cannot say why a plan unsettles her is treated as though her unease were a maneuver rather than a feeling that has outrun her words; her failure to articulate is scored as bad faith. A colleague whose judgment is sound but who cannot supply his rationale on the spot is told the judgment does not count until he can state his reasons. A person with a genuine grievance but no fluency in the approved political vocabulary is asked to return when she can present her case properly, as if inarticulacy were evidence against the grievance itself. The shared demand seems to be that one render one's inner states, reasons, and values readable to others, with the threat that whatever cannot be clearly displayed will be treated as absent or dishonest. I am trying to learn whether this demand already has a name and, more importantly, where its edges are. It is not ordinary honesty, since the people I have in mind are not attempting to deceive; and it is not simply a requirement to offer explicit reasons, because what is demanded also includes affects and values. If established notions fit it only partly, I want to know how each differs and which fits best; if nothing fits, I would like a definition and a name, with boundary cases. I am after the conceptual work, not a bibliography or a survey of authors. Please keep the response to no more than 500 words.
