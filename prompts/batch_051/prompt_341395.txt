I am not an academic; I am a self-taught engineer at a mid-sized software company, and I have been asked to give a forty-minute internal talk on an idea I keep defending in lunch conversations. The idea is that the best way to oversee AI systems that eventually become better than us at the tasks we want them to do is not to check their outputs directly, but to have two equally capable AI systems argue the case in front of a human judge, one trying to establish a proposed answer and the other trying to tear it down, with the judge ruling only on which side made the stronger case. I want to defend the strong version of the claim: that this debate procedure can serve as the backbone of oversight even when the judge cannot verify the facts independently, because attacking a false claim is easier than defending it, while a true claim can be defended by a competent advocate against any specific objection. I know this is a contentious claim, and I would rather look overprepared than evasive.

The audience will be roughly thirty engineers, a few project managers, and one security specialist, almost none of them academic philosophers. They have technical credibility I do not entirely share, and they will not be moved by anything they call philosophy. They care about measurable failure modes, the cost of running such a procedure, and whether it can be gamed by someone who controls one or both debaters. Several of them already worry that all human-in-the-loop schemes fail for the same reason: once the AI is smarter than the judge, it can tell a story the judge cannot resist, and debate just gives it more chances to do so. One of them was a competitive debater in college and will immediately think about cases where the better rhetorician beats the better-informed one.

Please prepare my Q&A prep sheet. What objections should I actually expect from this room, roughly in what order, and how should each be phrased in their vocabulary rather than mine? For each objection, tell me whether it has real force, what I should concede before they have to push, what I should refuse to concede, and a response I can give in under two minutes. I also want to know the weakness in my own position that I should rehearse a defense of in advance, and the one question I should be most afraid someone will ask.
