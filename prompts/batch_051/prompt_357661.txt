Suppose a government sets up a licensing scheme for decisions it classifies as transformative—having a child, permanent emigration, entering a closed religious order, irreversible medical procedures. Before you can finalize one of these decisions, you have to complete a few days in a "first-person preview": a high-fidelity simulation of the post-choice state, deliberately tuned to make the hardest parts salient (a simulated infant that won't stop crying, a simulated monastery with long silences, and so on). The previews are run by for-profit providers paid per completion; each provider writes a "fit report" but is not shown the participant's own logged reactions, and the participant can ignore the report while the clinic or court that requires the preview cannot. Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral position: what does it incentivize for participants, providers, and the institutions that enforce the preview; how would people game it or get captured by it; would it actually track whether the future self would endorse the choice, or track something else; and which failure modes can be fixed without gutting the scheme?
