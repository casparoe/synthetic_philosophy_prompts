I am a retired physical chemist, and since leaving the laboratory I have been reading political philosophy, particularly recent work on populism, and I keep tripping over the phrase “the will of the people.” Politicians claim to express it, critics call it a fiction, and I find I have no settled view. I have therefore begun collecting my own verdicts on cases to see whether any principle governs them. In a high-turnout constitutional referendum in which a single clear question is debated for a year, no significant misinformation drives the result, and the measure passes with sixty-two percent, I find it natural to say the people willed the change. In a low-turnout election won with fifty-point-three percent after a false rumor about an opponent’s health moves the decisive votes, I do not want to say the winner expresses the people’s will. In a mass uprising that topples a regime without any ballot, where subsequent assemblies and polls show broad and durable support for the overthrow, I do want to say the people willed it. But when a legislature enacts a policy that about fifty-five percent support in every poll, and yet each supporter holds a different and mutually incompatible belief about what the policy will actually do, I am inclined to say there is no determinate will behind it. And when an electorate’s preferences cycle—a majority prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A—I cannot say it wills any one of the options, even though each would win if paired against the right rival.

These verdicts already pull in different directions. I also judge that a policy favored by a bare majority in a snap poll but abandoned by a citizens’ assembly after hearing the evidence is not yet the people’s will, because no stable will had formed. Yet a different case troubles me: a stable, informed majority favors a measure that would strip a religious minority of its schools, and I want to say both that this is the people’s will and that it should nevertheless be blocked; I am unsure whether I can keep both claims. Finally, when a governing coalition enacts a package that no one ranked first but that most citizens come to accept as fair after deliberation, I lean toward saying the people willed the outcome, though no survey would have detected it beforehand. Is there a single principle—or a small family of principles—about information, stability, majority support, and the coherence of a collective choice that captures most of these verdicts? Which of my verdicts must be given up, if any? And what does your preferred account say about the case I have deliberately left open: a leader who loses the popular vote but takes office under institutional rules, then claims to embody the people’s will against a corrupt elite?
