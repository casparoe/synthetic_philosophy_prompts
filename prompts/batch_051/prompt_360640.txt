Consider the following content-governance scheme, proposed for a large platform on which user-generated video, images, and text circulate. It has two linked rules. First, any post that criticizes a piece of user content as propaganda, disinformation, or manipulation must reproduce the target in full and unmodified within the same post — the entire clip, image, or text, presented before the critique rather than merely linked, screenshotted, or briefly quoted. Second, the platform's automated systems deprioritize content scored as likely propaganda, but a reproduction that appears inside a post satisfying the first rule is exempt from that deprioritization. The stated rationale is that the rule makes criticism transparent and prevents propaganda labels from functioning as disguised censorship: critics cannot ask readers to shun what the critics are unwilling to show, and readers can judge the original material for themselves. Analyze how this scheme would actually operate, rather than whether it is admirable in principle. Cover at least: what it does to the volume and character of criticism that survives; how the exemption interacts with recommendation and demotion to alter the reach of the original material; what the first, easiest forms of gaming are and who is positioned to use them; the second-order incentives the scheme creates for critics, propagandists, and the platform's own moderation staff; and which failure modes might be patched with further rules and which seem intrinsic to making reproduction the price of critique.
