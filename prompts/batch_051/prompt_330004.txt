I'm a software engineer who got dragged into the interpretability side of ML, and I keep butting heads with people over wether the stuff we find inside a network is 'really there' or just a useful way of looking at it. The argument I hear for the real side goes like this: if you find a unit or a direction in activation space that tracks some concept across a bunch of inputs, and if you intervene on it—ablate it, boost it, clamp it—and the model's behavior changes in the way you'd expect if that thing actually carried the concept, then it's fair to call it a representation. The intervention is supposed to do the work that correlation can't, because a causally load-bearing component isn't just a coincidental pattern.

But that argument got comfortable back when models were small enough that a unit could be 'the edge detector' or 'the sentiment neuron.' In a big transformer there are more concepts than dimensions, so everything is smeared across units: individual neurons fire for an unrelated mix of inputs, features overlap, and the content is distributed across thousands of dimensions. People now train seperate decoders—sparse autoencoders and such—on the activations to recover candidate features out of that soup. The catch is that the recovery isn't unique: change the decoder size, the sparsity penalty, the training seed, whatever, and you get a different set of features that all fit the network's activations about equally well. So when you 'intervene on a feature,' you're partly intervening on something the decoder constructed, not something you found lying around in the network.

So my question is whether the intervention argument survives that shift. I can see three answers: it survives more or less intact because interventions still give causal evidence even when the object is reconstructed; it needs repair, so the realism should attach to the whole distributed state (or the network-plus-decoder system) rather than to individual features; or it's in trouble because the argument required a discrete carrier for each concept and modern networks just don't have those. Which premise of the old argument does the new setup actually break, and is there a weaker version that's still worth holding onto? And if it does survive, what exactly is it claiming is real now?
