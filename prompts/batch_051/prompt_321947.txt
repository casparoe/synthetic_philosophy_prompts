My roommate has gotten himself into Quine's "gavagai" stuff and built an argument from it that I can't find the exit from. He's not claiming it's Quine's own argument, just that it's where the reasoning seems to go. It runs:

(1) The only evidence we can ever have about what a speaker means by a word is behavioral: which sentences they assent to, dissent from, or how they react in observable situations.

(2) For a field linguist translating a wholly unfamiliar language, that behavioral evidence underdetermines the translation. A native's "Gavagai!" on seeing a rabbit is comaptible with "Rabbit!", "Undetached rabbit part!", "Temporal rabbit stage!", and endlessly many other renderings; no amount of pointing, question-and-answer, or testing seems to pick one out as the right one.

(3) Since all the possible evidence is behavioral, and the behavioral evidence underdetermines the translation, there is no fact of the matter about which translation is correct. That's the indeterminacy of translation.

(4) But the same thing goes on at home. If I treated my roommate's word "rabbit" as a foreign expression, his whole behavioral record would fit rival manuals just the same — one translating it as "rabbit," another as "undetached rabbit part," and so on. Nothing in his observable speech dispositions rules the alternatives out, any more than it did for the field linguist.

(5) So by parity of reasoning, there is no fact of the matter whether my roommate's word "rabbit" means rabbit or undetached rabbit part.

(6) But that's absurd. My roommate knows what he means by "rabbit," and he means the whole floppy-eared animal, not a bunch of undetached rabbit parts. First-person knowledge of meaning has to settle something.

(7) So Quine-style reasoning proves too much. It either makes our own home language just as indeterminate, or it owes us a prinicpled reason the home case is different. And I can't see how to give that reason without quietly giving up the claim that translation is indeterminate.

Which step do you reject, if any, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd like to keep (6) if I can, but I want to know what I'm paying. Also, please separate what's genuinely settled about Quine's view from what's contested among people who work on this, rather than presenting one reading as the consensus.
