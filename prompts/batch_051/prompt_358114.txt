A friend in my philosophy of mind group has this argument he says is a cleaned-up version of Dennett's Chase and Sanborn stuff, but he's pushed it further than I think Dennett would, and now I can't find the step to reject. It goes:

(1) If qualia exist in the usual sense — private, intrinsic, directly introspectible properties of experience — then a subject can in principle tell whether two of their experiences have the same quale or different ones.
(2) But the only way a subject can tell anything about their experiences is by forming some judgment or making some report, and any such judgment or report is just another functional state of the system.
(3) The coffee taster cases show the problem: Chase's ability to discriminate coffees has shifted over time, and Sanborn's standards for what he likes have shifted over time, but if you ask them what changed — the experience itself or something about how they react to it — they can't say from the inside.
(4) If a subject can't tell from the inside whether the experience changed or only their standards changed, then there is no fact of the matter about which changed.
(5) So for Chase and Sanborn there is no fact about whether their qualia have changed or stayed the same.
(6) If qualia exist, there should be such a fact.
(7) So qualia, in the usual sense, don't exist.

The part that bothers me is that the same reasoning seems to prove too much. Step (4) looks like a general principle: if you can't tell from the inside whether A changed or B changed, then there's no fact about which changed. But that principle would wipe out plenty of ordinary introspective knowledge too — people can be uncertain whether their mood changed or their appetite, whether they're tired or bored, whether they still believe their teenage political views or just don't want to argue about them. And we don't normally conclude there are no facts about moods, beliefs, or appetites.

I want to keep the idea that I do have some kind of direct knowledge of my own experience, at least in favorable cases. So which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the flaw is in (4), what replaces it as a principle connecting first-person ignorance to lack of fact?
