I've been trying to figure out whether the scaling behavior of large language models changes the old dispute between reductionism and emergence, and I keep going in circles. The reductionist line, as I understand it, is that a system's higher-level behavior is in principle nothing over and above its parts and their arrangement: given a complete description of the parts and the rules, everything the whole does would follow. The emergentist line is that some higher-level features resist that derivation, at least in practice and maybe in principle. Now machine learning people keep reporting emergent abilities: a model of one size cant do a task at all, while a bigger model with the same architecture and the same training, just scaled up, suddenly does it, and nobody can predict from the smaller model which abilities will appear. Suppose some of those reports survive the usual criticisms (bad evaluation metrics, insufficient prompting, cherry-picked tasks). Does that actually hand the emergentist a real argument, or does it just show we dont yet have the right lower-level story, so the reductionist's in-principle claim is untouched? What I'm really asking is which premise of the reductionist position the scaling results would need to falsify in order to matter, and whether they can do it.
