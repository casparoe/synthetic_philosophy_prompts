I found myself going back through the older debates about definite descriptions after a recent forum discussion about how much ontological weight one may pin on surface grammar, and I would like a careful assessment of whether Bertrand Russell's eliminative analysis still holds up against the work on presupposition and anaphora that came after it. The original argument was a striking instance of dissolving an ontological puzzle by logical paraphrase. On Russell's view, a sentence of the form "The F is G" is not a subject–predicate statement picking out an object; it is a quantified claim to the effect that exactly one thing is F and that this thing is G. One payoff was that sentences about things that do not exist need not be counted as truth-valueless or as being about shadowy objects: the Russellian treatment makes the whole quantified claim simply false when there is no F. Another payoff was that so-called negative existentials, which had looked like paradoxical references to nonexistent objects, came out as straightforwardly true negations of an existential claim.

What I am unsure about is how well this survives the later work on presupposition and anaphora. Definite descriptions in natural language do not always behave like the quantificational phrases Russell assimilated them to. In a sequence like "A letter arrived this morning; the letter was from the tax office," the second sentence does not obviously assert, all over again, that exactly one thing is a letter; it picks up an entity the discourse has already introduced. And the implication that there is an F shows the signature behavior of a presupposition rather than a plain entailment: it survives under negation and in the antecedents of conditionals in ways that the Russellian truth conditions, on their most natural reading, do not predict. The stock examples from the literature (a bald present King of France, and similar cases) test exactly this, but they are usually treated as one-off puzzles, and I want to know whether the accumulated evidence about projection and anaphora amounts to a genuine refutation of something central to Russell's program, or merely to a set of pragmatic complications around a largely intact core.

The question, then, is not whether natural language is full of context sensitivity, but whether the Russellian argument itself is refuted, repairable, or essentially superseded. Which premise of the original proposal is actually under pressure: the claim that descriptions contribute existence and uniqueness as part of what is asserted, the claim that their logical form can be extracted by paraphrase into first-order quantifier notation, or the background assumption that a sentence's content can be assessed apart from the discourse context it updates? Is there a defensible neo-Russellian position that keeps the quantificational core while absorbing the presupposition and anaphora data, and if so, what exactly does it concede? Or does a dynamic semantics, which treats descriptions as context-updating devices rather than as a species of quantified phrase, do the real explanatory work and leave Russell only the historical point that ontology need not be read off surface grammar?
