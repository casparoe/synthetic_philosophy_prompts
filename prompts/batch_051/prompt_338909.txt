I co-run an undergrad reading group — mostly second and third years with a intro logic course and an intro ethics course between them, no metaethics at all — and next session I'm doing the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism in about seventy-five minutes. I don't need you to explain the problem to me; I need you to plan how I explain it. The students will arrive having read just enough Ayer to think the boo/hurray view is the obviously finished answer to all of metaethics, which is exactly what I'm trying to unsettle. I want them to leave seeing why "moral talk expresses feelings" is a research program, not a sentence. So what's the actual order of ideas? I assume we need the expressivist starting claim (moral judgments are conative, not representational, so they don't report moral facts); then the fact that moral sentences show up unasserted inside conditionals, questions, and negations; then the demand that an argument like modus ponens isn't an equivocation when the same sentence is asserted in one premise and embedded in another. But maybe one of the pieces has to come before another, or maybe I'm missing the piece that makes the problem bite.

For the one carrying example, the obvious candidate is the little-brother argument — stealing is wrong; if stealing is wrong then getting your little brother to steal is wrong; so getting your little brother to steal is wrong. But I'm not sure it still lands for students who dont care about little brothers and already find stealing-wrong intuitive. What exactly must the carrying example have and what must it lack? Should I swap in something closer to their lives (cheating, ghosting, grading), or is there a structural reason the little-brother version works that I'd lose? The misconceptions I expect, in roughly the order they'll come up: "expressivists deny moral sentences are true or false, so of course logic doesn't apply, why is that a problem"; "the conditional just expresses a second-order attitude toward the combination of the two attitudes, and that fixes it"; "this is all just the problem of moral disagreement dressed up." Where in the sequence should I head each off, and how do I do it without letting the session turn into a fight about relativism? Also tell me what to leave out at this level and say so (hybrid expressivism? the negation problem? the attitude-revision machinery?), and give me one question for the last ten minutes that would show they understand the problem itself rather than just that expressivism is in trouble.
