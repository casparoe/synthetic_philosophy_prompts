I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology and I've been going around in circles on an argument I made for a seminar, because the conclusion is obviously mad but every step still looks fine to me. Heres the argument.

(1) If I know φ, and I know that φ entails ψ, then I know ψ.
(2) I know the axioms and inference rules of Peano arithmetic; in particular I know that modus ponens is truth-preserving, i.e. for any sentences P and Q, if P is true and 'if P then Q' is true, then Q is true.
(3) Now take any theorem T of PA and fix a proof of it, with lines φ1,...,φn = T. φ1 is an axiom, so I know φ1, by (2). Suppose I know all lines up to φi. The step to φi+1 applies modus ponens to two earlier lines, say φj and 'if φj then φi+1'; both are among φ1,...,φi, so I know them. Since I know modus ponens is truth-preserving, I know that their truth entails the truth of φi+1. So by (1), I know φi+1. Hence I know all lines up to φi+1.
(4) By induction along the proof, I know φn, i.e. I know T.
(5) So I know every theorem of PA.
(6) But I don't know every theorem of PA. There are plenty I've never laid eyes on, and presumably some whose shortest proofs are longer than anything anyone has actually checked.
(7) Contradiction. So either the closure principle goes, my knowledge of the axioms/rules goes, or I have to accept that I already know every theorem.

Where does this actually break? I'd like to keep (6) and some version of (1) if I can, but I want to know which step I'm rejecting and what it costs. Give me the diagnosis, not just a premise number.
