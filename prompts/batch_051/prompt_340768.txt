Two people I'd invited for dinner got into a long argument I've been unable to resolve, and I'd like you to act as a referee. The county historical commission, which one of them sits on, is weighing whether to fund a project to document and teach a language that still has a couple of dozen fluent speakers in our area, all of them elderly, and that is no longer being passed on to children. The conversation quickly became a more general one about whether a language, tradition, or art form can still be worth preserving once almost no one participates in it.

Marcus argued that the project is sentimentality. The only reason to fund it, he said, is that the remaining speakers care about it, and when they die no one will be worse off, because future residents will not have learned the language and will not miss it. Value, he insisted, has to be value for someone. Elena replied that a language is not just a tool for communicating; it is a distinct way of carving up experience, carrying jokes, stories, plant names, kinship terms, and ways of being polite, and losing it shrinks what human beings are able to be, whether or not later people notice the loss. If a library burns down and no one had borrowed its books, she said, it is still a loss. Marcus answered that the library analogy misleads: if no one ever read the books or knew what was in them, there is no bearer of the harm, and you cannot say what the world has actually lost. Elena then said that his standard would make every unmissed destruction harmless — quietly destroy an old painting and also the records of it, and by his lights nothing bad has happened. Marcus replied that he was not saying only felt losses count; he was saying a loss must be a loss in someone's life, now or later, and cases like the painting have future viewers who are wronged, whereas the language has no next generation of possible valuers. Elena said that the remaining speakers are not asking for their own comfort; they are asking that the language be recorded and taught so it has a life after them, which only makes sense if the language has a good beyond their satisfaction. Marcus said that a dozen people's wish cannot outweigh the concrete needs of the living, and that she was sliding between two claims: that the language would be useful to some future person, which is a cost-benefit question, and that it is good independently of any person at all, which he cannot make sense of. Elena answered that we protect old-growth forest that no one may walk in, not because we have calculated future visitors but because we think some things should not be ended through our inaction; being the last generation that could keep something going, she said, changes our obligations.

I genuinely could not tell who was right and neither moved. Please go through the exchange move by move and adjudicate it: say which of each person's points are right, which need refining, and where each is mischaracterizing the other, and say what both of them are missing. End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase saying who is more right overall.
