After a long career in evolutionary biology, I have recently retired and turned to philosophy, and I have been working through the metaethical literature on evolutionary debunking. The arguments are closer to my old field than most philosophy, but they are often compressed, so I have tried to reconstruct what I take to be the strongest debunking argument as a sequence of steps whose transitions can be checked one at a time. I would be grateful for a philosopher's assessment of whether it is valid, and, if not, where exactly it fails.

(1) The proximate mechanisms that produce our basic moral judgments, the spontaneous reactions of approval, disapproval, and felt obligation, were shaped by natural selection over human history. (2) Natural selection shapes mechanisms for inclusive-fitness maximization, and it is blind to whether a mechanism's outputs correspond to stance-independent facts about what is right or wrong. (3) A mechanism shaped for fitness rather than for truth will not be reliable about a domain unless fitness and truth in that domain happen to covary. (4) We have no independent evidence that fitness and stance-independent moral truth covary, and from the inside we cannot check this without first trusting our moral judgments. (5) Therefore, we should assign no better than a coin flip to the proposition that our moral mechanisms are reliable about stance-independent moral facts. (6) A person who should assign no better than a coin flip to a mechanism's reliability is not justified in trusting that mechanism's outputs. (7) Hence we are not justified in our basic moral judgments, and since every more considered moral judgment rests on those, none of our moral judgments is justified.

My own suspicion is that the inference is valid and that the real dispute must concern one of the premises, perhaps (4) or (6), but I have no settled view about which is the most promising point of resistance, nor about whether the conclusion is best read as global moral skepticism or as pressure against realist construals of moral truth in particular. Could you say where a moral realist could legitimately refuse to follow, and which premise a critic should attack under its most charitable reading?
