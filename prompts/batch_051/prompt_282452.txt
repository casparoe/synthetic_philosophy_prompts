I'm in a course on play, work, and leisure and we've spent weeks on Huizinga and Pieper. Both of them, in pretty different ways, argue that play or leisure is not just a break from real life but something valuable in itself—Huizinga sees culture as emerging out of play, and Pieper sees genuine leisure as the thing that makes culture possible in a world that measures everything by work. But I cant get past how our ordinary judgments seem to say the exact opposite, and I want someone to force the issue instead of hand-waving.

Take the same four hours: a seven-year-old building an elaborate block castle that gets knocked down at the end of the afternoon gets praised for being a kid, while a 22-year-old down the hall building a huge cardboard model of our campus (not for a class, not to post anywhere, not for anything) gets asked if he's okay. A cat with a puzzle feeder counts as "enrichment"; an adult who spends her evening on a jigsaw puzzle is "wasting time." The closer the adult's activity is to pure play—absorbing, self-contained, not productive—the more people treat it as a symptom. I'm suspicious that we only trust play when the player is a child, an animal, or a retiree with decades of work behind them, and that this is exactly the work-centered deformation of leisure the readings diagnose.

Act as an expert philosopher of play and leisure. Start by brainstorming the best candidate justifications for treating adult play as suspicious while child play is obviously valuable (development, adult obligations, dependency, something about capacity to choose, whatever else). Then build the strongest version of the one you think does the most work, and test it against hard cases: a retiree, an unemployed person who does both job applications and model-train building, a child pushed into competitive chess, and an adult's physically risky weekend sport treated as respectable because it's "exercise." Give me an honest verdict: is there any principle that actually justifies the asymmetry, or should we give the asymmetry up? And does the Huizinga/Pieper line of thought force a particular answer, or do they leave our ordinary judgments untouched?
