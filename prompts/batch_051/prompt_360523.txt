The actual Mediterranean is a nearly tideless sea, and many features of its maritime history—harbours built at water level, beached galleys, lagoon settlements—presuppose a coast that stays put. Now consider a counterfactual in which, from roughly 3000 BCE onward, the whole Mediterranean basin has the tidal rhythm of the open Atlantic: semidiurnal tides with a range of two to four metres along most coasts, strong tidal currents through the Strait of Gibraltar, the Strait of Messina, and other narrows, and everything else (coasts, islands, winds, river mouths, climate, available technology) held fixed. Predict, with reasoning, how maritime life develops differently across the ancient and medieval periods. In the spirit of longue-durée history, the aim is not to narrate an alternative timeline but to isolate the causal weight of one slow physical structure. Address at least these dimensions: how coastal trade, navigation, and the siting and engineering of ports adapt to a shore that advances and retreats twice daily; how naval warfare and the projection of sea power change; what happens to lagoons and shallow-water livelihoods such as fishing, salt-making, and the early Venetian-type settlements; and whether consequences propagate inland—in the provisioning and taxation of cities, in state control of coasts, in the relative advantage of maritime versus landed powers—or remain mostly confined to the shoreline. Make the chain of reasoning explicit and assign confidence to each link: which effects follow robustly from the physics and the economics, which depend on assumptions about how societies adapt, and what changes would have to be added for the predictions to fail.
