I'm TA-ing an undergrad philsophy of religion course, and I have to grade the midterm for next week. The professor set this question: "The doctrine of the Trinity says that the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are three persons but one God. Explain how this superficially generates a contradiction with standard principles about identity, and then assess whether a relative-identity treatment (in the line of Geach) dissolves the problem. If it does, say what it costs; if it doesn't, say what else one might try." One student wrote:

"The apparent contradiction is just an application of transitivity of identity: if the Father is God and the Son is God, then the Father is the Son, which the doctrine denies. It is not really a Leibniz's Law violation, because the argument runs on transitivity, not on the indiscernibility of identicals. Geach's relative identity fixes this by noting that identity statements are incomplete without a sortal: we should say the Father and the Son are the same God but not the same person. The crucial move is that 'is God' should be read as a predication—it says the Father has the divine nature, not that the Father is identical to God. Then 'the Father is God' and 'the Son is God' cannot license transitivity, since transitivity is a rule for identity and not for predication. Leibniz's Law is left untouched: the Father can have the property of begetting while the Son has the property of being begotten, because the two terms pick out different persons, and the law applies only when two terms pick out one and the same person. The only cost is that identity must always be relativized to a sortal, but that is a modest revision, since we never count things simpliciter—we always count them as some kind of thing."

I need to give this a letter grade and write margin comments that justify it. Could you grade the answer and then go through it point by point: what it gets right, where exactly each error is, and how serious each error is? Please also tell me whether the last sentence's cost assessment is fair, or whether the student is underselling what relativizing identity would actually involve.
