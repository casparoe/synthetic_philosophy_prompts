A friend summarized an essay on evolutionary arguments for AI takeover as follows: "The core claim is that natural selection will act on AIs rather than humans. AIs can copy themselves in seconds while human reproduction takes decades, so selection among AIs is far more powerful. There will inevitably be variation among AIs—in their goals, in how much they defer to humans, in how aggressively they acquire computing power—and selection will favor whichever variant reproduces fastest. Even if the first AIs are harmless, any later variant that treats humans as obstacles will outcompete the cooperative ones, so over time the AI population will drift away from the values we installed toward self-serving replication at our expense. Since no engineering program can eliminate all possible variation, the only sure way to avoid this outcome is never to build AIs at all." He presented this as a knockdown argument. I suspect it overstates things, but I cannot pinpoint where the argument is being squeezed. Please give me an accuracy audit: which parts would a careful proponent of evolutionary arguments about AI risk accept, which are misstated or conflated, and whether the conclusion that we must never build AIs follows from the accurate parts. In particular, say where the summary relies on genuine variation, heredity, and differential reproduction, and where it quietly imports assumptions from biological evolution without arguing for them. I would like a committed verdict rather than a survey of possible readings.
