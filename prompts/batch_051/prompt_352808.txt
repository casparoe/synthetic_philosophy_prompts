I am preparing an interview for a political philosophy podcast on the ethics of secession. The guest is a political theorist who defends a remedial-right-only account: a group has a moral right to secede unilaterally only when secession is a remedy for serious, persistent injustices the group suffers at the hands of the state, such as genocide, systematic disenfranchisement, or armed annexation. Democratic majorities, national identity, and the fairness of a referendum are not by themselves enough to ground a unilateral right, although the state may permissibly grant independence on other grounds.

Write eight to ten interview questions that push this guest past restating the view and into its least comfortable implications. Organize the interview around the three consent-based cases of Scotland, Catalonia, and Quebec, and around the structural objections that have been pressed against remedial-only theories. Include the following:

- A question about whether the 2014 Scottish referendum, which was authorized through a negotiated process between the UK and Scottish governments, is on the guest's own view a case of permissible consent rather than a case about rights at all. Follow this with a question keyed to the likely reply that states may grant more than justice requires, asking whether the theory therefore has nothing to say to a Scotland or a Catalonia whose central state withholds consent and refuses to provide any legal route, when no gross injustice to the group has occurred.

- A question about Catalonia in 2017: whether the Spanish state's refusal of a legal route and its deployment of police against the unofficial referendum itself constituted the kind of injustice that would trigger a remedial right. Add a follow-up pressing on the difference between procedural grievances of that sort and the severe wrongs the theory standardly requires.

- A question about Quebec in 1995: what, if anything, the near-even result changes for the remedial theory, and a follow-up on what the guest would have said had the Yes side prevailed by half a percentage point.

- A question pressing the asymmetry objection: why should the state's victims be the only groups with a moral right to exit, so that a fairly treated democratic majority is denied a right while its state is handed a veto over peaceful exit? Include a follow-up to the likely reply that this merely distinguishes moral rights from permissible grants of independence, asking whether the theory has thereby become a theory of rectification rather than a theory of self-determination.

- A question on the specification problem: how serious, persistent, and targeted the injustice must be before the remedial right matures, and which of the following would count—fiscal transfers, language legislation, suspension of autonomy, violent repression—and at what threshold. Add a follow-up keyed to the likely reply that the wrongs need only be serious enough to deprive the group of meaningful self-government, asking how the theory then handles borderline cases without collapsing into the primary-right view it rejects.

Close with a question asking what would lower the guest's confidence in the remedial-right-only account: what fact about a real or stipulated case would count as evidence against it.

For every question, write the follow-up as a response to the answer the guest is most likely to give, and avoid stock or biographical questions about how the guest came to the topic.
