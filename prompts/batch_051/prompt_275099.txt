I'm a high school student, and I have been trying to work out what I actually think about how anything in a purely physical world could be about something. I would like to know what theory my judgments add up to. Here are my verdicts on cases.

A toad sits still until a small dark object moves across its visual field, and then it snaps; it does the same for a lead pellet dragged on a string. I want to say the toad's snap response means something like fly or food, and that the pellet case is a misfire, not merely a trigger. A thermostat's bimetallic strip bends as the room's temperature changes and closes the circuit that turns on the heater. I do not think the strip represents the temperature; it simply responds to it. A honeybee returns to the hive and performs a waggle dance whose angle and duration correspond to the direction and distance of a nectar source. I think the dance means that location, even though the bee presumably cannot think about it. A tree's rings are wider in wet years and narrower in dry ones. I think the rings carry information about past climate but do not represent it. A smoke detector's alarm sounds when smoke enters its chamber, and also when I burn toast. I think the alarm represents smoke, and the toast case is a false alarm. A laptop shows a low-battery warning when the charge falls below five percent. I think the warning represents the battery's charge. A mouse has cells in its hippocampus that fire when it is in a particular spot in its enclosure, even in the dark. I think those firings represent the mouse's location. A person's immune system produces antibodies shaped to bind a particular virus. I do not think the antibodies represent the virus; they are just a chemical match.

I have been using a rough rule: a state represents something when it has the job of indicating that thing and can get it wrong. But that rule seems to break on the thermostat, which has the job of indicating temperature and can get stuck, and on the antibodies, which have the job of binding the virus and can fail. So either my rule needs repairing, or some of my verdicts have to go.

Is there one account of representation that delivers all eight verdicts, or close? If not, which verdicts should I overturn, and why? Please state the principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot keep, and tell me what it predicts about two further cases: a plant's stem bending toward light, and a car's fuel gauge that is miscalibrated so it always reads a quarter-tank high.
