I am a retired geologist, and philosophy has become my retirement project. At a dinner I hosted last month, two friends fell into an argument that I have been unable to settle to my own satisfaction; I mostly listened, and I have been turning it over ever since. I would like a careful adjudication.

At issue is a mutual acquaintance, a retired surgeon, who has spent four years and more money than he can comfortably afford restoring a derelict wooden sailboat. A marine surveyor recently told him that, once finished, the boat will be worth roughly a third of what he has already put into it, and that completing the work properly would cost more than buying a comparable boat already in good condition. The surgeon accepted the figures and said, "All the same, after what I have put into it, I cannot walk away now." The argument concerned whether his decision to continue is irrational.

Here is the exchange as I remember it.

(1) M: "The surgeon is committing the sunk cost fallacy. The money and hours already spent are gone whatever he does next. The only rational question is whether the next hour and the next dollar are better spent on the boat than on their alternatives, and on the surveyor's own figures they are not. The past expenditure is doing all the work, and that is exactly the mistake."

(2) D: "That is too quick. He is not deciding whether to buy a boat; he is deciding what kind of person he is. If he abandons the project now, he teaches himself that large commitments may be dropped when they become inconvenient. A standing policy of finishing what one starts, even when the marginal balance looks poor, can be worth a great deal over a lifetime. Spending to keep that policy alive in himself is not automatically irrational."

(3) M: "That turns the fallacy into an unfalsifiable alibi. If a policy of persistence has value, that value has to be priced like anything else: how much is it worth, and is this the cheapest way to buy it? He could abandon the boat and practice follow-through on a project whose completion is actually worth something. Invoking 'character' without a number attached is how people stay trapped in bad investments."

(4) D: "You are treating the value of finishing as exhausted by the finished boat. But a half-restored boat on which one has worked for years is not the same object in a person's life as an equivalent boat one could buy next week. The accumulated labor has changed what the project is for him. The question is not whether that history has resale value; it is whether it can rationally matter to him."

(5) M: "That is the same move again, applied to the project's identity. If every attachment produced by past effort is redescribed as a new project with a new value, then no amount of good money after bad can ever be called irrational. A consideration that can be used to justify every continuation justifies none."

(6) D: "I am not claiming continuation is always rational. I am claiming it is not always irrational, and that current market value is the wrong test. Some commitments are like promises: part of their value consists in being kept even when the immediate balance turns against them. He made a private commitment to this boat. The rationality of honoring a commitment cannot be read off a balance sheet at the moment of temptation."

(7) M: "A rational commitment is not a suicide pact. When the original estimates turn out to be wrong by a factor of three, a rational agent reopens the decision. A blanket 'never abandon' policy is not character; it is a machine for converting forecasting errors into ruin. If the commitment was made on the assumption of costs that no longer hold, its rationale has already been voided."

I would like a move-by-move adjudication: for each numbered statement, say how much of it you accept, where it goes wrong if it does, and rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right. Then give me your committed answer to the question behind the argument: is honoring sunk costs always irrational, and if not, what principle separates the rational exceptions from mere rationalization? Please do not lay out a menu of positions; take a definite position, defend it, and indicate how confident you are and what would change your mind.
