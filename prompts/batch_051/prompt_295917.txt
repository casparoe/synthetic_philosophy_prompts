I'm a novelist trying to get inside the head of a character who used to be an actuary and now runs a small-time confidence scheme. I want him to have a private, almost theological attachment to the idea of consistency, so I've been reading Frank Ramsey's 1926 paper Truth and Probability. One move in there has me a little unmoored. As I understand him, Ramsey argues that if someone's degrees of belief violate the probability laws, then you could offer that person a series of bets, each one looking fair or better to them individually, but arranged so that they lose no matter what happens. He then says something to the effect that this dependence of choice on the exact way the options are framed would be absurd. So far so good, I think: incoherent credences are exploitable.

But then I start writing scenes and realize I've maybe been reading the argument wrong. The setup seems to require a person who is prepared to accept bets at their stated degrees of belief, and then a bookie with unlimited time and no transaction costs. Why does that become a norm for belief itself rather than just a practical warning against being a certain kind of gambler? And once my character's credences are coherent, the argument appears to be completely silent about whether they are reasonable, supported, or in any way connected to the world. He could be probabilistically perferct about utterly daft things. So is the Dutch book argument really an argument about rationality in belief, or is it closer to a piece of accounting advice: don't put your quotes where an adversary can arbitrage you? I suspect I'm smuggling in a false assumption, because philosophers treat Ramsey as doing something much more ambitious. What exactly is the argument licensed to show, and what would it be a mistake to think it shows?
