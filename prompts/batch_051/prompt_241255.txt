I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so go easy but dont dumb it down. I'm writing a book in which Aristotle is brought back to life in the present day and, by some mix-up, ends up sitting in on a philosophy of biology seminar about whether functions are still teleological. The hinge of the scene is him reacting, and I need it to feel like his actual view, not a Wikipedia caricature.

Here's what I'm working with. Aristotle had four causes, and the final cause did real work: the acorn grows into an oak because that's what it's for, the heart exists for pumping blood. Modern biology supposedly killed all that — Darwin gave us design without a designer, and ever since, teleological explanation has either been shown the door or quietly let back in through function talk. People still say the function of the heart is to pump blood, and there's a live argument about whether that's honest-to-god teleology or just shorthand. One camp says a trait's function is whatever effect it was selected for. Another says function is just what a part contributes to the system currently running. A third says the whole thing is leftover folk-Aristotelianism we havent managed to shake.

What I'd like from you: predict what the resurrected Aristotle would actually say. Not "oh, he'd be intrigued." Get specific. Would he treat the selected-effects account (function = what it evolved to do) as a genuine recovery of final causes, or as a parody of what he meant by the "for the sake of which"? Would he think Darwin really threw teleology out of nature, or just relocated the intending from organisms to natural selection, or to nobody? Where would he think modern biology still smuggles in final causes it claims to have dropped, and where would he think they're truly gone? And would he say the current-role talk alone is enough for philosophy, or does the selected-effects story have to stay in the picture?

Write his likely position partly in his own vocabulary — actuality, potency, substance, form — but translated enough that an editor would stomach it. I want to know where he digs in, where he concedes, and what would actually stop him cold. American English, please.
