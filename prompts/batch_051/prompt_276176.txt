After a long argument with a friend about discounting the future, I am left with a tension I cannot settle. My friend defended the view that it is rationally permissible, perhaps even rationally required, to give greater weight to benefits and burdens simply because they are nearer in time. He kept insisting that a cost in ten years really does matter less to a person now than the same cost next month, even when the difference is not due to uncertainty, growth, changed preferences, or a fading sense of identity. I resisted this, partly because I had been rereading Henry Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics. In the section where he asks whether we ought to care about our future selves as much as our present self, Sidgwick argues that the bare difference between earlier and later positions in time cannot by itself be a ground for unequal concern. His thought is that a moment's temporal position is no more relevant to how much it matters than its spatial position would be. A rational agent, on his view, should be impartial across all parts of his own life, just as he should be impartial across persons; to favour the present merely because it is present is a kind of arbitrary bias. This account attracts me because it is simple, unified, and extends the same impartiality that already seems right in ethics to one's relation to one's own future.

But I also have an intuition I cannot shake. Suppose I must choose between a painful operation next week and a slightly more painful operation in a year, where I can stipulate that the delay would not make the second one easier, shorter, less certain, or different in its aftereffects. I find that I would still prefer the one next week to the slightly worse one in a year, and the preference seems to be driven purely by the fact that the second is further off. If I imagine the two operations placed at the same distance from me, the intuition disappears, which makes me think this is really about time itself and nothing else.

So I am caught. On one side is Sidgwick's theory of temporal neutrality, which has systematic virtues and seems to follow from the same impartiality I accept elsewhere. On the other side is a firm first-person preference that pushes against it. Should I treat my intuition as a debunkable product of how we imagine future selves, and give it up? Should I amend or abandon the theory because it overcorrects a real difference between present and future? Or is there a reading on which the two are compatible, such as granting that motivation and affect may be future-discounting while normative reasons are not? Give me the strongest version of each resolution and say which you would adopt, and what would justify it.
