I'm a novelist writing a book about a wildlife photographer who realizes her award-winning shot wasn't exactly taken — the camera stitched a dozen frames, grabbed the eagle's eye from an instant when she never pressed the shutter, and replaced the sky. I want the book to think through an old philosophical idea about photographs, and I'm stuck.

The idea is that photographs are transparent: when you look at one, you literally see the thing photographed, the way you see through a telescope or mirror. Photos supposedly do this because light from the object reaches the film or sensor through a mechanical optical process, with no person's beliefs or intentions shaping the image. A painting, even a photorealistic one, only represents the thing — you don't see through it, because a painter's mind stands in the causal chain.

Now the problem. Modern cameras don't just register light; they merge exposures, align them, denoise, relight, and fill in detail the sensor never resolved. Some of the final image is causally downstream of the scene, and some is selected, warped, or synthesized by software. I keep going back and forth on wether the transparency idea survives this. Either it's fine, because there's still an unbroken optical chain somewhere in the pipeline; or it needs repair, so transparency comes in degrees depending on how much was registered versus generated; or the new tech just exposes that the old view was confused from the start. Which premise of the transparency argument does computational photography actually break, and can it be salvaged? I'm after the argument, not the plot.
