A long-standing argument in programming-language design holds that programs are written primarily for human readers—for maintainers, reviewers, and one's future self—and only secondarily for machines. On this view, source code is a durable medium for human reasoning and communication, and that is what justifies care in language design. Suppose we are entering a regime in which most code is generated by an LLM from a natural-language specification, and can be regenerated, translated into another language, or summarized on demand: source code starts to look less like a durable artifact and more like a disposable intermediate between a specification and a behavior. Does the original argument survive this development? Identify which premise is under pressure and whether a weaker version can be salvaged. Focus on the argument, not on the labor market for programmers. Answer in no more than 250 words.
