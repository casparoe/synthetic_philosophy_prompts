I am a student in a seminar on Spinoza’s Ethics, and in our discussion of Part V I keep stumbling over what looks like a conflict between two of Spinoza’s central claims about God’s affective life. On one side, Part V, Proposition 17 and its corollary assert that God is without passions and is not affected by any emotion of pleasure or pain; from this Spinoza concludes that, strictly speaking, God neither loves nor hates. That earlier account of love and hate as passive affects tied to external causes seems to rule out any genuine love in God. On the other side, Part V, Proposition 35, and the propositions that follow, attribute to God an infinite intellectual love of himself, and identify our mind’s highest blessedness with its intellectual love of God, which is said to be part of the very love with which God loves himself.

I am not asking for a survey of the literature, but for a careful analytic answer. Are these two stretches of text genuinely in tension, or can they be reconciled without forcing one of them into a marginal reading? If they can be reconciled, what precisely is the sense of love in each context, and does Spinoza give us enough to hold that the intellectual love attributed to God is not the kind of love God is denied in Proposition 17? If they cannot be reconciled, which strand has the better claim to be Spinoza’s considered position? Please lay out candidate reconciliations—different senses of a term, different contexts, a doctrinal shift, or a distinction Spinoza never explicitly draws—and then offer a verdict on which reading is honest and which is special pleading.
