I am a core maintainer of a widely used open-source library with about six hundred accounts holding commit access, and we process roughly eighty self-merges a month. Our current rule is that any committer may merge their own pull request immediately, without review; we adopted it to keep contribution latency low. In the past eight months we have had two serious incidents: a first-time contributor merged a change to login-token validation, believing it trivial, and it silently introduced a security bug; and a senior committer unilaterally merged a performance rewrite of the storage layer that broke several downstream projects and consumed our entire next release cycle. The steering committee has asked me to replace our "any committer may merge" rule with a written, reusable merge procedure that every maintainer applies before merging their own work.

Please write that procedure, in a form we can paste into CONTRIBUTING.md. It should be a checklist or ordered decision procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a passing answer to each, where the procedure ends in "merge now," where it ends in "stop — do not merge," where it requires review and by whom, and where it must hand the decision to a person or committee because the question is one of judgment or values rather than facts. It has to be usable by someone with no background in decision theory, runnable in under two minutes for a typical change, and no longer than about one printed page. We have exactly two people qualified to review security-sensitive code, so the procedure cannot route every change to them.

Ground the procedure in what is known about the unilateralist's curse: the problem that when any one member of a large group can act on their own private judgment about whether an action is good, and those judgments are noisy, the group's effective decision rule becomes the most optimistic member's, so the chance that someone will unilaterally take an action the group as a whole would not have endorsed grows with the size of the group. Do not simply replace that with the most pessimistic member's rule and block everything; instead, distinguish changes by reversibility, blast radius, and the cost of being wrong, and route each change to the lightest form of review that is still strong enough.

Then run the procedure on three proposed self-merges and show exactly where each one lands:

1. A first-time contributor fixes a broken link in the README and corrects a wording error in an error message.
2. A senior committer wants to merge a change that replaces our sorting routine with a faster algorithm, with no new tests, on the grounds that they have benchmarked it and found the behavior identical.
3. A mid-level committer wants to add a small third-party parser dependency, with a one-line lockfile update, to support a new config format.

Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer the asker would rather not apply, and show why the procedure still says that. End by stating plainly what the procedure cannot settle and the predictable ways it will miscarry.

Begin your response with the procedure itself, at its first rule or question, with no preface, no commentary, and no restatement of the request; give the reasoning and the worked applications after the procedure.
