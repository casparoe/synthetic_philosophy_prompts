I am a high school senior in a semester-long philosophy elective, and next week I have to lead our seminar in defense of a reading of Mill. My thesis is that, in Chapter IV of Utilitarianism, Mill gets the place of virtue and duty within consequentialism right. A trait or precept earns the name of virtue or duty only because it tends to promote the general happiness, so both are derivative in origin and neither is an independent moral requirement; but Mill is also right that a virtue can come to be loved for itself, and when it is, it is no longer merely a disposable instrument, heuristic, or rule of thumb. The discussion will turn on one sentence, which I plan to put on the board:

"Virtue, according to the utilitarian doctrine, is not naturally and originally part of the end, but it is capable of becoming so; and in those who love it disinterestedly it has become so, and is desired and cherished, not as a means to happiness, but as a part of their happiness." — John Stuart Mill, Utilitarianism, Chapter IV.

My argument, briefly, is this. The principle of utility tests actions and dispositions by their tendency toward the general happiness, but happiness is not one uniform sensation: it includes as parts whatever has come to be desired for itself through association, as Mill's later analogy with the love of money tries to show. So Mill is able to say consistently both that virtue is originally only a means and that it has become part of the end in someone who loves it disinterestedly, without abandoning the happiness principle.

The seminar is small and argumentative. One classmate is reading Kant and will say that rooting morality in happiness corrupts the only thing that gives virtue its worth, namely doing one's duty for its own sake. Another is working through Aristotle and will object that Mill turns virtue into a private feeling rather than excellent activity over a complete life, and that "part of happiness" is weaker than eudaimonia. A third is a confident act-utilitarian who thinks the whole idea of loving virtue for itself is self-deception that consequentialists should debunk rather than dignify. And our teacher will probably press on whether the associationist psychology Mill borrows can explain how we come to value virtue without debunking that valuation. Please prepare me a Q&A sheet for the question period: the objections I am actually likely to get from this audience, in roughly the order they will come, phrased as these people would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, where each reply is softest, and the question I should most hope nobody asks.
