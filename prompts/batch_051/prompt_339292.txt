I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am working on a piece about why originals still matter in an age of perfect copies. In the course of reporting it, I have collected my own reactions to a series of cases, and they do not fit any single slogan I know. I would like your help finding whatever principle, or small set of principles, best captures them—and I am asking you to be committed rather than merely to lay out the options.

Here are the cases with my verdicts.

1. The invented-scene forgery. A painter produces a new work in the manner of a 17th-century Dutch master—period oak panel, hand-ground pigments, plausibly aged craquelure—not copying any surviving painting but inventing a scene the master never painted. Sold as a lost work, it is admired for a decade and then exposed. My verdict: the picture did not become a worse picture on the day it was exposed; its composition and handling are what they always were. Yet I do not think the collapse in its standing was mere snobbery. It presented itself as standing in a certain relation to the past and to a painter's achievement, and that relation was fraudulent.

2. The honest copy. A copyist produces an exact, visually indistinguishable copy of one particular painting and labels it as a copy. My verdict: the copy can serve many of the same visual purposes, but it is not a major achievement and it does not possess whatever it is that gives the original its central interest. I feel no strong moral objection to it, unlike the forgery.

3. The natural double. Imagine a geological process produced a rock formation molecule-for-molecule identical to a small carved sculpture, every pore and chisel mark in place. My verdict: the natural object is not a work of art and lacks the value of the carved one, even if no instrument could tell them apart.

4. The reattributed workshop picture. A canvas long attributed to a famous master is reassigned, on technical grounds, to an unknown assistant from the same workshop. My verdict: the picture has not changed visually, and I would not remove it from the gallery, but a real part of our understanding of it has changed. This feels different from case 1, but I cannot yet say exactly how.

5. The deleted original file. A photographer's only digital file is copied bit-for-bit, the copy is verified, and the original storage is destroyed. My verdict: nothing of the artwork has been lost. The work simply is the pattern, and the copy is the work.

6. The marked-up manuscript. An author's heavily revised draft, full of crossings-out and false starts, sits next to a clean typescript of the published novel. My verdict: the manuscript has a value the clean typescript lacks, though the published text is the work for ordinary reading. The manuscript is a record of decisions.

7. The posthumous casts. A sculptor leaves a plaster model. The estate has one bronze cast made from it. Later, an outside foundry makes an unlimited run of casts from one of the earlier bronzes. The shape is identical at each step. My verdict: there is a real slide in standing from the plaster to the authorized first bronze to the later run of recasts. But in case 5 I thought no such slide occurred in exact copying, so I need an account of why material lineage matters in bronze and not in a digital file.

My tentative hypothesis is that two kinds of value are doing the work. Call them presentational value, which depends only on what is perceptually there now, and achievement value, which depends on how the thing came to be—on the decisions, discoveries, skill, or fraud that the object embodies. On this hypothesis, a perfect copy can fully retain presentational value while lacking the original's achievement value; a forgery is a special sort of fraud because it pretends to an achievement value it does not have; and the natural double has presentational value but no achievement value at all. But I can see the strain: the hypothesis does not by itself explain the difference between the workshop reattribution and the forgery, since both involve a mistaken belief about whose achievement the object embodies; and it does not yet say when gradual replacement destroys the original rather than preserving it.

So my questions are these. Is there one principle, or a small set of principles, that captures my verdicts with less strain than this two-value hunch? State it as precisely as you can, and say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, if any. Then apply it to two cases I keep going back and forth on. First, the Theseus restoration: over several decades, conservators replace every flake of pigment in a medieval panel, and at some point every bit of original material is gone, though the image is unchanged and the panel itself was replaced in the early 1900s; is this the same painting as the one that came down to us? Second, the fire and the facsimile: a painting is destroyed in a fire, but a visually perfect facsimile of it had been made beforehand by a restorer of great skill; should the facsimile inherit anything like the original's place, or is it merely a record of what was lost? I would rather have a definite, arguable position than a menu of possibilities.
