I'm puting together a small philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a moral philosopher who defends a strict deontological prohibition on interrogational torture — the sort of person who says the ticking time bomb shouldn't move us at all. I'd like eight to ten interview questions that go past just having him restate the view, and I'd like each question to come with a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give. Could you write them out as a numbered list? And no generic "what drew you to philosophy" or "which authors influenced you" stuff; the pressure should be internal to the position itself.

Make sure the set includes:
- a question pressing the defensive-force asymmetry: killing in self-defence is sometimes permissible, so why is torture never permissible even when it would save more lives, and if he says torture attacks a defenceless person's agency or dignity in a way that killing an attacker does not, a follow-up asking whether the prohibition is really tracking the victim's dignity or the torturer's relationship to the victim;
- a question about the institutionalisation worry: even if a one-off act could be justified (or at least excused), what goes wrong when security services build a practice of interrogational torture around that thought, and does his view target individual acts, the practice, or both;
- a question about the epistemics of ticking-bomb examples: they stipulate certainty about the plot, the detainee's knowledge and the deadline, and real cases almost never have any of that, so what intellectual work is the thought experiment doing, and what should it and shouldn't it be allowed to show about actual policy;
- a question pushing on the dirty-hands/threshold reply, where someone says "it's wrong but a decent person would still do it to save thousands of children and then carry the guilt", including a follow-up for if he insists that wrongness is all that matters morally;
- a question about whether the view has any threshold at all — a million lives, a city, a country — and if not, how he'd respond to the charge that this makes deontology look fanatical rather than principled;
- and a final question about what kind of case, institutional design, or empirical finding would most shake his confidence in the absolute prohibition, even if he doesn't think anything could overturn it.

Oh, and could you write the questions themselves in British English please — our listeners complain about American spelling.
