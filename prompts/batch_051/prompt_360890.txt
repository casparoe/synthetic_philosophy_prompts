I teach a small seminar on Rousseau's political philosophy, and last week we spent the whole hour on the passage where he argues that whoever refuses to obey the general will will be compelled to do so by the whole body, and that this compulsion is what makes the person free. The students split roughly in half. One side thinks the paradox dissolves once you understand the general will as the output of a properly constituted deliberative procedure, one whose background conditions are precisely what enable individual autonomy, so enforcement is not really overriding freedom but protecting the conditions of it. The other side thinks that reading sanitizes Rousseau, who on their view has a substantive conception of the common good and is prepared to override private judgment in its name. Write a dialogue between two of these students after the seminar in which they try to pin down what exactly the state is licensed to do on each interpretation, whether their dispute is really about the metaphysics of the general will or about the value of non-interference, and whether the substantive reading necessarily collapses into authoritarianism. Each should respond to the other's strongest formulation of the position, not a caricature of it, and neither should win outright.
