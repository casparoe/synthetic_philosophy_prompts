Courts in common-law jurisdictions routinely insist that no sum of money can truly compensate for the loss of a limb or the loss of a loved one, and then proceed, in personal-injury and wrongful-death litigation, to award a precise monetary amount for exactly such losses. A jury may award one plaintiff a modest sum for an injury and another plaintiff a far larger sum for what looks like nearly the same injury; an appellate court may later reduce an award as disproportionate to the harm. The practice, in effect, treats one person's pain, grief, or disfigurement as commensurable with another's, and with money.

What does this practice presuppose about the measurability and interpersonal comparability of well-being? In particular, what kind of scale must well-being have if these comparisons are to be more than arbitrary convention? Does the practice strictly presuppose cardinal, interpersonally comparable utilities, or is it compatible with weaker views? Do the elements of the practice presuppose different things—does a jury's initial valuation, for example, presuppose more than appellate review for excessiveness? And if interpersonal comparisons of utility turn out not to be well defined, what should change in how courts handle noneconomic damages?
