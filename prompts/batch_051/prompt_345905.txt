After a close call on the highway a few months ago — and now that I have a toddler — the question of whether any given drive is "worth it" has stopped being abstract for me. My city is considering replacing its flat annual vehicle registration fee with a per-trip road-pricing scheme built on micromorts, a unit of risk meaning a one-in-a-million chance of death. Each trip would carry a charge equal to a base rate per mile multiplied by a route-and-time risk factor: a vehicle-class multiplier, a road-type multiplier (freeway, arterial, rural two-lane), and a time-of-day and weather multiplier. The multipliers would be published and updated by the city's transportation agency, and half of all revenue would go to a road-safety fund administered by that same agency, which by statute would have to spend it first on the street segments the agency itself ranks as riskiest; the other half would be returned as an equal annual dividend to every licensed adult, whether or not they own a car. I want the proposal analyzed as a machine rather than judged as a slogan. What does it incentivize in when people drive, which routes they choose, which vehicles they buy, and how they chain trips together? How would drivers and the agency game it — odometer and GPS manipulation, arbitrage through pricing-map errors and gaps, vehicle-class misreporting, the agency's incentive to overstate risk to grow its own fund, and its incentive to understate risk on routes the city favors? What second-order effects should I expect on land use, on night-shift and rural residents who cannot easily reduce their micromorts, and on whether the scheme actually forces the comparison it is meant to force — a drive's risk weighed against its value, rather than merely against its price? Which of these failure modes can be patched from within the formula, the dividend, or the auditing rules, and which are inherent in pricing micromorts per trip?
