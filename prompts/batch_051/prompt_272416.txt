I have been trying to pin down what it means to say that a message has meaning, using Douglas Hofstadter's three-level picture in Gödel, Escher, Bach. On that picture, the outer message is the physical carrier: the marks, the sounds, the voltages. The inner message is the content the sender intends to transmit. The frame message is the implicit meta-message, carried by context, format, and genre, that tells a decoder that there is something to decode and how to decode it; without it, even an intact outer message is just marks or noise. What I find slippery is saying where the meaning actually lives: in the outer message, in the sender's intention, in the decoder's act of interpretation, in the frame that enables the transaction, or in some relation among them that cannot be assigned to any single location.

I want a working definition of the meaning of a message — a criterion for when a physical pattern carries meaning and where that meaning is located — that classifies the following cases. Take the stop sign at an intersection and a dog's bark at an intruder as cases that clearly carry meaning. Take the mirror reflecting a room and a chance arrangement of beach stones that happens to spell out the word HELP as cases that clearly do not, despite carrying information or resembling a message. The hard cases are: a stop sign used as a prop in a film; a tablet inscribed in an undeciphered script by a human scribe; an intercepted ciphertext whose key has been destroyed; a joke told in a language you do not speak but whose genre you recognize; a gene's sequence of codons; and a facial tic that reliably betrays that someone is lying without any intention to signal. I find myself genuinely unsure about the undeciphered tablet and the codon sequence, and I doubt the eavesdropper's ciphertext is meaningfully a message even though it was deliberately produced. Propose the definition, run it through every case, and tell me where the definition surprises you, where it gives no answer, and how you would revise it.
