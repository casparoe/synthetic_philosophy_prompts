I've somehow landed myself runing a two-hour Saturday session for a local adult-education centre on John Dewey's idea that democracy is not basically a set of institutions—voting, legislatures, accountability—but a way of treating shared problems experimentally: you form a public around the knock-on effects of people's actions, you frame responses as hypotheses, and you revise them in the light of what actually happened. The people who've signed up are mostly retired professionals who spend their week on parish councils, residents' associations, allotment committees and the like. They already know the machinery of local democracy better than I do, and I suspect a lot of them will arrive thinking Dewey is going to be a fuzzy sermon about listening to each other. I also want to avoid leading with the word 'experimentalism', because to them that's lab coats, or trying things at random never mind the cost, and 'inquiry' will just sound like 'do a questionnaire before you decide.'

What I need from you is a plan for the session, not an explanation of Dewey for me. What has to be in place in their heads before the main idea can even make sense, and in what order; what single ordinary running example should carry the whole two hours—I'm imagining something like a dispute over a road-calming scheme or library opening hours, but tell me why the example you pick and not a big constitutional crisis—; what misconceptions they'll bring or form as we go (I can already hear 'democracy is majority rule' and 'if every policy is just a guess, then any guess is as good as another'), and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off rather than trying to mop them all up at the end; and one question I can put to them at the end that will show whether they've got the experimentalist point rather than just been polite about it.
