I’ve been trying to get clear on what I actually think about free will and blame, because my gut calls don’t line up with the simple thing I learned in undergrad. Here are the cases where I’m trying to find the pattern.

1. Jones wants to shoot Smith. Black, a neuroscientist, has planted a device that can detect what Jones is about to decide and, if Jones looks like he’ll choose not to shoot, will force him to shoot anyway. Black wants Smith dead. But Jones decides on his own, without Black doing anything, and shoots Smith. I say Jones is fully blameworthy. The fact that something in the background guaranteed he couldn’t do otherwise doesn’t matter to me at all.

2. Same setup, but this time Jones is only starting to lean toward shooting Smith when Black, worried Jones might lose his nerve, triggers the device and makes the decision for him. Jones then shoots. I still blame Jones some, but a lot less than in case 1. I’d want to call it something like reckless or negligent, not full murder. But in both cases it seems he lacked alternative possibilities, so that can’t be the whole story.

3. A man is sitting in a locked room, looking out the window at a shallow pool where a child is drowning. He decides to keep reading. He later learns the door was locked and he couldn’t have gotten out anyway. I still blame him for not even trying or forming the intention to help. It feels like his bad will did the work, even though he couldn’t have acted on it.

4. A woman has an undetectable chip implanted by a mad scientist. The chip produces an overpowering urge to insult her best friend at a party. She has just enough awareness to think “this is wrong” but goes along with it. She afterward says she couldn’t help herself. I think she’s somewhat blameworthy, but much less than if the urge had come from her own personality.

5. A man is threatened with losing his job if he doesn’t falsify a safety report. He hates it, wrestles with it, and signs. I blame him, though less than if he’d done it for money. Here he could have refused, so alternative possibilities were open, but his blame is still reduced.

6. A woman drives drunk with no one forcing her. She doesn’t crash. I blame her, and it doesn’t even occur to me to ask whether she could have done otherwise before blaming her.

So my gut seems to say: alternative possibilities matter in some cases but not others; what matters more is whether the action came from the person in the right way. But I can’t articulate what “the right way” is. Is there a principle here, maybe something like “what matters is whether the action issued from your own will in the right way, not whether you could have done otherwise,” that actually gets all these cases? What would that principle say about someone who was brainwashed years earlier into having a character from which a later bad act flows? And which of my verdicts, if any, do I have to give up?
