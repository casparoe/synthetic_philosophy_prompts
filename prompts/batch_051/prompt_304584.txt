I'm in a little ethics reading group — six of us over video, mostly grad students and one guy who did a philosophy BA a while back — and for the last couple months we've been stuck on how to read a single value: frugality. We all agree frugality is a real value and not just a taste or a lifestyle preference; the fight is over what it actually asks of a person. One camp reads it deontically: being frugal is something you do in your own life, a kind of self-imposed constraint on waste, consumption, display, and excess, whatever the aggregate consequences turn out to be. The other camp reads it consequentially: frugality is a state of the world to be promoted — fewer resources wasted in total, more people using less — and if that requires preaching, nudging, policy, shaming, or setting a conspicuous example, then that's what the value demands; your own private thrift matters only derivatively, through its effect on the total. We've run the same cases maybe six times now: the rich environmentalist who flies private while funding a minimalist foundation; the scrooge whose personal frugality is just hoarding and never does anyone any good; the self-appointed prophet of frugality who writes books and sells branded jars; the person who quietly wastes nothing but influences no one. Nobody has moved, and the arguments all feel like reruns.

For the next session I want to come in with a diagnosis of why we're stuck, rather than yet another argument for one side. My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement is about the formal structure of the value: is frugality an agent-relative requirement (a rule about how I live) or an agent-neutral end (a goal to be promoted), and if so, maybe the two camps aren't actually answering the same question — they're just both using the word "frugality" and talking past each other. But I'm not confident. It might also be a hidden empirical disagreement about what actually reduces waste in the long run, or a bedrock difference about whether morality is fundamentally a matter of constraints on conduct or of good outcomes, or something about demandingness and which reading lets each side off the hook. Act as a professional philosopher who works on the structure of moral reasons and deontic notions like agent-relativity. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming three or four possible diagnoses and then narrowing down, so I can see the reasoning. Give me a diagnosis with reasons, engaging concretely with what each side in our group actually says, and then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a distinction, a better-posed question, a case that could separate the readings, or an argument that we should stop expecting a single answer.
