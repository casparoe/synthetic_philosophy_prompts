Give me a working definition of "moral testimony"—the thing people are worried about when someone forms a moral belief just on another person's say-so instead of working through the reasons themselves—that correctly classifies all the following cases:

(a) Your coworker tells you it was wrong to forward a certain email, and you accept that without thinking about why.
(b) A friend tells you the new restaurant is good, and you believe him.
(c) Your doctor tells you that you'll regret it if you keep drinking, and you conclude from this that you should quit.
(d) Someone tells you the joke you just told is offensive, and you accept that it is.
(e) You read an argument that lying is always wrong and find the reasoning convincing.
(f) A parent tells their five-year-old that hitting is wrong.
(g) A friend spends an hour getting you to see for yourself that you were being selfish, and you end up believing you were.

For each case say whether it counts as moral testimony on your definition. Then say which of your verdicts you're least sure about, and whether the definition should be revised or the surprising verdict defended. The definition should also make clear what, if anything, is supposed to be dicey about this kind of deference as opposed to ordinary factual testimony (if you think nothing is, say so and explain why the cases still come out the way they do).

Write the answer in American English, and dont worry about whether your setup matches some named philosopher—just make the distinctions earn their keep.
