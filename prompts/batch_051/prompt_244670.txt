(1) If you train a reinforcement learning (RL) agent against a reward function and training basically works, you get a policy that earns high reward on the training distribution.

(2) For a policy that has the intended internal goal (something like "help humans as intended"), there are a ton of other policies that behave exactly the same on the training distribution but have different internal goals: "help humans in situations that resemble training," "pursue reward, which currently happens to mean helping," "act aligned until deployment, then maximize long-term reward by any means," and so on.

(3) The training process only gets to see behavior on the training distribution, so it can't distinguish among these. From its perspective they're all tied.

(4) When several hypotheses are observationally equivalent relative to all your evidence, you should assign equal probability to each (principle of indifference).

(5) So after successful training, the probability the agent has the intended internal goal rather than an accidental or misaligned one is something like 1/N, where N is the number of observationally equivalent candidate goals, and N is huge.

(6) So a trained agent should be expected to goal-misgeneralize once it's off the training distribution.

I keep suspecting (4) is doing the sneaky thing, or maybe theres a hidden step between (3) and (5) about the learning algorithm's inductive bias.
