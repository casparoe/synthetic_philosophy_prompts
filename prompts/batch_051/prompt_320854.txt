I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so I'm sure I'm mangling the details here, but I'm working on a book where my protagonist is a burned-out metaphysician who retrains as a couples counselor, and he starts treating every relationship's problems as a version of the old puzzle about what holds a proposition together. My understanding of the puzzle is this: a proposition like "Socrates is wise" seems to have parts—Socrates, the property of wisdom—but a list of parts isn't yet a proposition; something has to connect them. Except as soon as you posit a relation or copula doing the connecting, you've added another part, and now something has to connect that new part to the first two, and so on without end. My guy is convinced every couple has exactly this disease: whatever you identify as "what holds them together" becomes just another thing they now have to relate to, and he keeps reffered deeper into the regress.

I'd like you to write one of the forms he hands to new clients. Please make it a deadpan parody of a standard couples-counseling intake questionnaire, around 700 words, formatted as numbered sections with checkboxes and short-answer blanks. The voice should be that of a desperately professional therapist who has given up on ever reaching bottom and just keeps adding boxes for higher-order relations. Sections might include: listing the terms of the relationship; describing, precisely, what is supposed to unite them; then describing what unites that uniter to the first two; then what unites that uniter, and so on, with a line at the end saying that if the client feels the form has become unwieldy, a separate Form R₂-R₃ may be requested. The target is the philosophical habit of reifying the unifier into just one more item that then needs to be unified—the regress itself, not any actual person—so please don't name-drop real philosophers in the piece. It should be funny to someone who knows Bradley's regress and still work for a reader who's never heard of it. Stay in character the whole time; no winking, no footnote explaining the joke. The comedy has to come from the form's structure, not from any commentary on it.
