I've been going back and forth about this for weeks now and my grad students are tired of hearing about it. In a seminar I've found myself defending a version of psychological egoism that I used to dismiss as the kind of thing you'd roll your eyes at in a undergraduate paper. The view is this: every action is the agent's strongest desire winning out, and—here's the controversial bit—what it is for something to be in a person's self-interest just is for it to satisfy their desires, all things considered. Put those together and psychological egoism drops out for free: you always do what you most want, all things considered, and what you most want, all things considered, is your good by definition. Not your pleasure or your narrow advantage, necessarily—your good as your settled desires fix it. The appeal is that it's one mechanism for all action explanation, it dissolves weakness of will (what looked like acting against your strongest desire is just a lower-order desire losing to a higher-order one, or a change in what you want on reflection), and it makes normative ethical egoism look less like a crazy revision of morality and more like a cleaned-up description of what we already do. It even survives the Butler-style point that I can desire your welfare for its own sake: it just says that my having that desire and acting to satisfy it is my self-interest, since self-interest is defined by my desires, whatever their objects happen to be.

I've run the standard cases—the soldier on the grenade, the anonymous donor—and the theory handles them in a way I'm almost comfortable with. But then there's a case I keep stumbling over. A colleague spent a decade caring for a parent who was, by his own account, manipulative and cruel, at enormous cost to his health and his marriage. When I asked him why, he said—without defensiveness, not like someone confessing a compulsion—that he didn't do it because it made him feel better or because he couldn't live with the guilt; he did it because the old woman was owed care from him, full stop. I know exactly what the theory says: the consideration "this person is owed care from me" was his strongest desire all things considered, so the action was self-interested by definition. And my intuition is firm that at that point the theory has stopped saying anything. It has no independent notion of a person's own good left; it's just renaming whatever motivation won out as "self-interest," and a theory that can't lose can't explain. So which one should give? I want the negotiated verdict, not just a side. What would it take—what would I have to believe about self-deception or post hoc rationalization—to debunk the intuition as a flattering self-description? If the intuition wins, what would an amended theory look like, and is there anything left of the egoist program? And is there a reading on which the theory and the case are compatible without the theory becoming a tautology?
