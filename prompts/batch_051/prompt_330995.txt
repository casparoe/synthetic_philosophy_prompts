You are grading an exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of language, with a unit on translation. Below are the exam question and the student's answer.

Exam question (20 points; part (a) is worth 8 points, part (b) is worth 12 points):

Distinguish the claim that some expression is untranslatable from the Quinean thesis of the indeterminacy of translation. Then discuss whether either claim supports the normative conclusion that every act of translation does violence to the source text and therefore obliges the translator to preserve the foreignness of the original rather than paraphrase it. Use at least one example.

Student's answer:

Untranslatability and indeterminacy are often run together, but they are different. A word like the Portuguese saudade is called untranslatable because no English expression carries exactly the same combination of longing, melancholy, and tenderness; one can give a paragraph explaining it, but the compact semantic and affective content is lost. This is untranslatability relative to a standard of exact equivalence.

Quine's indeterminacy thesis is stronger. It says that the totality of behavioral evidence available to a radical translator, who is learning a language from scratch by observing how speakers respond to their environment, is compatible with many different translation manuals that are not mere stylistic variants of one another. For example, the same expression could be rendered as a term for a whole animal or as a term for that animal's undetached parts, and the available behavioral evidence cannot decide between them. Quine concludes that there is no fact of the matter about which manual is correct; translation is not merely lossy but fundamentally indeterminate. Since there is no fact of the matter about what an expression means, no translation of it can be said to be correct. In that sense every sentence is untranslatable: for any sentence there is always a rival rendering that the evidence equally supports, so no single rendering is the right one.

The ethical upshot follows from both points. Because translation always involves loss, whether it is the affective content of saudade or the underdetermination of every sentence by the evidence, every act of translation imposes the translator's own interpretive choices on the reader. There is no neutral way to render a text. Every choice therefore does violence to the source, replacing what the author wrote with something else. The translator's ethical obligation is accordingly to minimize interpretation: to keep the original's foreign constructions and terms visible rather than naturalizing them into the target language, since only foreignizing translation can avoid adding the translator's own interpretation. Paraphrase is a betrayal; preserving foreignness is the only faithful option.
