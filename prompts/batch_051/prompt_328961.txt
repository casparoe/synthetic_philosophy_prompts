Last year I started restoring a 1978 Honda motorcycle. I've put in a couple hundred hours and about $3,500, and there's still a lot left. The truth is I don't enjoy it anymore and I probably won't ride it enough to justify the remaining work. The forward-looking theory of sunk costs says none of that past investment matters now: those hours and dollars are gone no matter what I choose, so the only question is whether the *remaining* work is worth the *future* payoff. I'm drawn to that theory. It's simple, unified, and gives a crisp decision procedure — ask yourself whether you'd start this project today from a blank slate, knowing what you now know, and if the answer is no, stop. Applying it has saved me from throwing good money after bad before.

But I can't shake the conviction that quitting now wastes the past year in a way that never having started wouldn't have. If I finish, the past effort gets absorbed into something finished; if I quit, it's retroactively all for nothing. That doesn't feel like a mere emotional illusion I can just price in — it feels like a real feature of what my past effort amounts to. I can see two ways out: maybe the intuition is just anticipated regret dressed up as a reason, and it should be treated as a cost of quitting rather than a reason to continue. Or maybe the value of completing something can depend on how much you already invested — the way the value of a marathon medal depends on the training — in which case sunk costs aren't always irrelevent.
