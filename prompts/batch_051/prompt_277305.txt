I hold the view that randomness is fundamentally a property of infinite sequences—or of the processes that generate them—and that no finite string, considered on its own, can be called random or nonrandom in anything more than an instrumental sense. On this view, saying that a particular finite sequence like 0100110101 is random is really a compressed claim about its plausible origin, or the claim that it is initial-segment-like enough to be a prefix of some Martin-Löf random infinite sequence; the finite string itself has no autonomous randomness status.

What would count against this view? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward. Include both formal considerations—finite randomness tests, Kolmogorov complexity, the fact that finite frequencies seem, as an empirical matter, to distinguish strings—and intuitive or conceptual defeaters, such as the stubborn conviction that a thousand-bit string with no apparent order is random in a way that a thousand alternating bits is not. Which of these would a defender of the infinite-sequence-only view rightly concede as genuinely counting against the position, and which would they dismiss as conflating randomness with disorder, incompressibility, or unpredictability? Does the view risk anything at all, or has it been insulated by qualifications? And if it is insulated, is that insulation a defect of the view or a principled feature of distinguishing randomness from mere apparently stochastic finite data?
