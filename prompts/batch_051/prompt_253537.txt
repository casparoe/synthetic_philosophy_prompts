Metaculus-style forecasting platforms are basically an accidental philosophy-of-probability lab, and I want someone to reverse-engineer what the practice commits them to. The concrete setup: thousands of people put a number from 0 to 1 on whether some real-world event happens. The platform scores each forecast with a Brier score — squared error between the number and what actually happened — and over time publishes calibration curves showing whether people who say 70% are right about 70% of the time. Users get reputation for low Brier scores and good calibration, and the platform also aggregates the individual forecasts into one community prediction, often by taking the mean and then pushing it away from 0.5 because extremized averages score better.

On its face this whole apparatus seems to pressupose something like the Cox/Jaynes view: plausibility is a real-valued quantity obeying the probability axioms, and for a fixed body of evidence there's a right number, with scoring rules as the enforcement mechanism. But I doubt the parts actually add up. Leaderboards treat a probability as a personal property or a skill. Calibration treats it as a frequency fact about a person. Aggregation treats probabilities as if you can average them across people with different evidence and different priors — which is strange if you really think probability is a logical relation between evidence and proposition rather than a fact about brains. Even the resolution process quietly assumes there's a determinate fact about whether a messy event 'happened' or didn't.

So: what does this practice strictly presuppose, what is it merely compatible with, and where is it underdetermined? And if we really did take probability to be the logic of plausible reasoning, rather than propensities or bare subjective degrees of belief, what would a forecasting platform have to change to be consistent with that — in particular, would 'well-calibrated' still be a virtue? A good answer should distinguish 'the scoring rule rewards honesty' from 'the scoring rule presupposes frequentism,' and should say what a Cox-consistent platform would put on the leaderboard, if anything.
