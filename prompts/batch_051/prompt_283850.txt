I'm a PhD student in legal theory, and I've been going back and forth on a case. The definition I was given for a court is roughly: an institution authorized by law to resolve legal disputes, with an impartial decision-maker, adversarial hearings, decisions that apply legal rules and bind the parties, and some avenue of review. Now picture this. A large arbitration provider handles nearly all disputes between a big company and its customers or employees. It has a permanent roster of arbitrators with conflict-of-interest rules, live hearings, a little discovery, written findings of fact and law, and even an internal appellate panel. But it operates entirely through pre-dispute mandatory clauses (use the product or take the job and you waive your right to sue), the company pays the provider's fees, the proceedings and the award are confidential, its so-called precedent only governs by the provider's own policy if at all, and a normal court can only vacate an award for fraud or bias, not because the law was misapplied. Is the provider a court in every sense that matters? Go criterion by criterion. Then tell me weather the case shows my criteria are too thin and need something like public reason-giving, finality under official state appeal, or state-provided funding, or whether that would just stack the deck.
