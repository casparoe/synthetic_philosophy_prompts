I'm a retired chemist who's gotten into reading philosophy for fun, and I keep running into the same puzzle in arguments about "Western values," and I can't tell wether the puzzle has a name. It goes like this. Someone says free speech (or secularism, or tolerance) is a Western value, and that this counts as a reason for Western societies to protect it. So far so good. Then if I point out that Western countries have censored, persecuted, and excluded people throughout their history, the reply is that those episodes were betrayals of Western values — the values are what the tradition was aiming at, not everything it actually did. So counterexamples from history never seem to land: the phrase gets silently defined as the good parts, and the bad parts get reclassified as failures to live up to itself. But then when I ask what makes these values Western at all, if they're just the good ideals the tradition sometimes happened to express, the same person will go right back to history and say they're the distinctive threads that grew out of the Western tradition. And if I ask whether that descriptive fact, by itself, gives anyone a reason to keep holding those values, the answer is no, of course not — the ideals are what matter, but the ideals are still Western because that's where they developed.

So the phrase seems to work only because it shifts between a descriptive reading (what these societies' traditions actually produced) and a normative reading (what they ought to stand for — a sanitized selection from all that history). And whichever challenge I press, the speaker's answer is planted in the other reading. It's not plain equivocation, exactly, because the two meanings aren't accidental near-homonyms; they're linked, one a polished version of the other, and maybe the argumentative force comes from the link itself.

Here's my question: does this specific thing — a concept that in argument flips between a descriptive-historical sense and a normative-idealized sense, each sense being used to shield the other from objections — have an established name? If it has several near-misses, which one fits best and what exactly separates it from the others? And if it has no name, I'd like you to coin one, define it crisply, and give a couple of boundary cases where the phenomenon is or isn't happening. I'm after the carving, not the bare label.
