I'm giving a talk next month at a service academy ethics colloquium, where half the audience is active-duty officers who teach just war theory and the law of armed conflict, and the other half is civilian philosophers — two of them diehard Walzerians. The paper argues that the moral equality of combatants is false: I defend the revisionist view that whether an individual act of killing in war is permissible depends partly on whether the war itself is just, so a soldier fighting for an unjust cause don't stand in the same moral position as a soldier fighting for a just one, even when both follow the same in bello rules. My quick argument in the talk is: (1) in ordinary interpersonal morality you can kill in self-defense only against a wrongful threat; (2) a just-side soldier is relevantly like someone resisting wrongful aggression, while an unjust-side soldier is relevantly like the aggressor, uniform or not; (3) the traditional separation between jus ad bellum and jus in bello is a legal and institutional compromise, not a claim that survives as individual morality; and (4) duress, indoctrination, and the just-following-orders excuse mitigate but do not make the killing permissible.

The people I'm most nervous about are the Walzerians, who think the war convention is a kind of mutual contract that protects soldiers and civilians precisely because it treats both sides' combatants as morally equal, and the law-of-war folks, who will think my view is politically dangerous because asymmetry would unravel the whole rule-based system. So, act as a professional philosopher who works on the ethics of defensive force and knows both the Walzerian and revisionist literatures cold. Start by brainstorming the strongest counterarguments to my view, then produce a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this audience — not the ones I'd get from an online forum — ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way these people would word it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is softest, and end with the question I should be praying nobody asks. Also flag the one concession I should make in my opening to keep the room from turning on me.
