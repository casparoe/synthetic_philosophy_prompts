Consider the following argument, in the spirit of Douglas Hofstadter's account of the self as a pattern, for the conclusion that an "I" can be distributed across brains.

(1) A person, at a given time, is not an immaterial substance or a bare lump of tissue; a self is a pattern—a configuration of memories, beliefs, habits, mannerisms, and characteristic ways of speaking and reacting.

(2) A pattern, unlike a physical object, can be instantiated in multiple substrates at once and can survive in partial or degraded forms.

(3) Over years of close association, other minds internalize parts of your pattern: they can finish your sentences, reproduce your judgments, and carry around a mental model of you that disposes them to think and act in your characteristic ways.

(4) When your brain is destroyed, the full pattern is no longer instantiated in its original substrate, but the partial instantiations in those other brains remain.

(5) If a sufficiently rich part of your pattern remains instantiated in another mind, that is not merely a static record of you, like a photograph or a diary; it is a low-resolution continuation of your self.

(6) Therefore, to the extent that such partial patterns survive in other people, your self survives your biological death; and if the surviving fragments are distributed across many minds, your "I" is distributed across many brains.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step first fails, and why? In particular, scrutinize whether the word "self" (or "you") keeps a single meaning across steps (1) through (6), and whether any step assumes precisely what is at issue. If the argument can be repaired by clarifying a term or tightening a premise, give the repair and say whether the conclusion remains recognizably the same. End your answer with a line of the form \answer{valid}, \answer{step 1}, ..., or \answer{step 6}: give \answer{valid} only if the argument is valid on its strongest reading; otherwise give the earliest step at which the argument as stated fails.
