I have no academic training, but I have spent a long time thinking on my own about a puzzle that keeps showing up in ordinary life: sometimes a crowd is clearly wiser than any single person in it, and sometimes a crowd walks off a cliff that no individual member would have chosen alone. I have seen the words "information cascade" and "rational herding" in passing, but I do not know the technical differences, and I am not asking for a literature review. Before I look at the more formal explanations people have offered, I want the full specification of what any adequate account of this would have to get right.

Please write me the desiderata for a theory of when following the group's lead is reasonable and when it is a mistake, where "adequate" means: good enough for an ordinary person making decisions in three kinds of settings — everyday choices (restaurants, products, online reviews), high-stakes group decisions (jurors, committees, evacuation decisions), and financial decisions (markets, investments).

For each requirement, give the concrete case or observation that makes it a requirement, and say whether it is non-negotiable for a serious account or merely a bonus. Make sure the list does at least the following, though you should not limit yourself to it:

- It must explain how many independently judging people can beat any one of them, and also how a group can converge on a bad answer even when most members would privately have gotten it right.
- The cases it must handle should include things like: a long line outside an unfamiliar restaurant in a city full of tourists; a treatment spreading through a hospital without anyone pointing to a study; a market where prices rise mostly because others are buying; a jury whose early speakers lean heavily one way; a panic in a theater when several exits are available; and a product with thousands of five-star reviews written by people who bought it after reading other reviews.
- It must respect the distinctions that ordinary experience forces on us, such as the difference between a crowd being informative and a crowd being merely influential, and between group accuracy and individual rationality.
- It must not contradict the fact that these outcomes are all stable parts of life: some large groups consistently outpredict their own members, while some equally large groups are systematically wrong for long stretches, and neither fact reliably announces itself in advance.

Then, flag any pair or group of requirements that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; say for each whether the conflict is a real one a theory must live with or a sign that one requirement should be demoted. After that, name the single requirement that does the most work to eliminate the simple folk theories people carry around, and explain why.

If it helps, you may close with a brief table scoring three rough views against your list: roughly, that following the crowd is usually reasonable because others know things I do not; that following the crowd is usually a failure of independent thinking; and that everything depends on the details of who knew what and when — but do not let the scoring take over; at least three quarters of the answer should be the requirements and their cases.
