I am drafting a novel whose central figure is a political strategist who defects from the party of his youth to its rival, and I have been trying to determine what, if anything, he is defecting from. In the course of that reading I keep running into a dispute that has been going on for at least a century and that does not seem to have moved: whether left and right name a real axis of political thought, a real social structure, or neither. One side holds that the distinction tracks a stable underlying dimension — a stance toward what ought to be preserved and what ought to be equalized, or something of that sort — and that this is why a person's view on the estate tax carries information about her views on education, borders, censorship, and the family, even when the particular issues change with the age. The other side holds that this is mostly an illusion of packaging. They note that the contents of the left and right bundles have been renegotiated many times, that there is no internal affinity between free markets and social conservatism, or between labor politics and cultural liberalism, and that ordinary voters, when they are actually surveyed, turn out to be considerably less ideologically consistent than the axis image requires. On this second view, the distinction is real only as a team marker: left and right do genuine psychological work as identities and coalitional labels, but they are not underwritten by any single dimension of principle. The two sides seem to agree on the polling data and on the historical record; they disagree about what the data are data about.

My working hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than assumed, is that the impasse comes from a conflation of three questions: what would order the commitments of a fully coherent person, what actually orders the attitudes of ordinary voters, and what the two words do as badges of group membership. If I am right, then the question whether the left–right distinction is real is really three questions, and the interesting task is to say which of them, if any, is the one people are actually asking when they ask it. If I am wrong, I would like to know what kind of disagreement this actually is — factual, verbal, or a difference over which cases count as data — and what, if anything, could count as progress: a dataset, a formal result, a distinction, or the abandonment of the question.
