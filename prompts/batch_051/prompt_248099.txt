I have been reading Peirce and James on truth, and I have developed a principle that I would like you to evaluate as candidly as possible. I call it the Filtrate View of Truth. Its core claim is: a proposition is true if and only if it would remain reliable, or at least harmless, under every possible practical aim that any inquirer could have, no matter how unfamiliar or distant; a proposition is false if there is at least one possible practical aim under which relying on it would systematically mislead someone, and no broader practical aim restores its reliability. This is not the same as saying that truth is whatever is useful right now, because I am not allowing the standards to be set by a single person's present purposes. It is also not the same as Peirce's view that truth is what all investigators would eventually agree upon, because I do not require actual convergence; I require only that the belief would pass every possible practical test. I think my view keeps James's idea that truth has practical consequences while avoiding the charge that James makes truth subjective, since my standard includes all possible purposes, not just those of the believer. For example, the belief that a fever is caused by an evil spirit might have been useful for maintaining social cohesion in a particular society, but on my view it is false because there are aims, such as lowering a disease rate, under which it reliably misleads. By contrast, the belief that the earth is round is true because for any practical aim I can imagine, such as navigation, astronomy, or construction, relying on it does not systematically mislead. I would like to know three things. First, is the Filtrate View a disguised version of Peirce or James, or is it genuinely distinct? Second, what is the strongest objection to it, and is the objection fatal rather than merely uncomfortable? Third, please separate what is settled in the scholarship from what remains genuinely contested among people who know this topic, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
