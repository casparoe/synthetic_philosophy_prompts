It has become common, in my department and in the wider debating community, to describe argumentation as a sport: there are opponents, rules of engagement, referees, scoring, training regimes, and spectators. The analogy is not idle; people lean on it to support the claim that treating intellectual argument as a competitive sport, with formalized scoring and winners, would improve the quality of reasoning in roughly the way competition improves athletic performance. I would like you to audit that analogy. Provide a detailed mapping: what corresponds to the athlete, the opponent, the rulebook, the referee, the foul, the training, and the victory. Then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular, say whether the features that make sport improve performance — fixed scoring criteria, zero-sum opposition, spectators who reward spectacle, and an incentive to win rather than to be right — transfer to argumentation, and which of these disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion rather than being merely cosmetic. Finally, tell me whether the claim that competitive argumentation would improve reasoning survives the audit. If it does not, say whether some constrained version of competition, or a better analogy, would do the work that the sporting analogy is being asked to do.
