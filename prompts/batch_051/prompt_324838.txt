I am drafting a paper on distributive justice and would like a consistency check before I circulate it. I find myself inclined to hold all of the following:

1. Inequalities that arise from unchosen circumstances—native capacities, childhood environment, unforeseeable market shifts—are unjust in themselves and ground claims to compensation.
2. Inequalities that arise from a person's responsible choices, including the outcomes of knowingly undertaken gambles, are not unjust and ground no such claims.
3. What justice requires us to equalise is not resources but real freedom, understood as access to advantage: two people with identical resources can face very different ranges of worthwhile options, and the one restricted by unchosen disadvantage is wronged unless that restriction is neutralised.
4. It does not matter whether compensation is delivered as cash, goods, vouchers, or social insurance; what matters is only that the unchosen disadvantage is neutralised.
5. A person whose expensive taste is unchosen and unavoidable—say, a preference that requires champagne to reach the enjoyment others get from water—has no claim of justice to the extra resources that taste requires; such tastes belong to her ambitions, not her endowments.

Which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, and how severe is each tension? Which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection, and why? What is the least costly revision that would make the package consistent, and what would it give up? Please answer in British English.
