I’ve been following the aesthetic testimony debate on rationalist-adjacent forums for years, and it seems to have settled into a pattern where both sides already know the moves before the argument starts. The anti-testimony side says aesthetic judgment requires first-hand acquaintance with the work; otherwise you are merely parroting a review. The pro-testimony side says this is mysterian about aesthetic concepts: if critics really have reliable discriminatory capacities, then something their reports transmit should be able to enter into my reasons. The odd thing is that both camps agree on the standard examples — we can defer to someone about whether a film was shot on location, but not about whether it earns its irony — and each side can predict exactly which cases the other will deploy. The debate is frozen, but not for lack of arguments.

I recently returned to Hume’s essay Of the Standard of Taste and noticed this passage, which seems to name the pin:

“It is natural for us to seek a Standard of Taste; a rule, by which the various sentiments of men may be reconciled; at least, a decision, afforded, confirming one sentiment, and condemning another.”

My hypothesis is that the aesthetic testimony dispute is really a disagreement about what this “standard” would have to be. On one reading, a standard of taste is a decision procedure: a rule that could be stated, handed to another person, and used to confirm or condemn particular aesthetic judgments — in which case testimony about what the standard says ought to do real epistemic work. On another reading, the standard is a norm of cultivated response that can be exhibited by trained judges but not converted into a transferable rule — in which case the anti-testimony side is right that testimony cannot replace acquaintance, but wrong if it concludes that aesthetic judgment is merely private. If that is the hinge, then the debate is not fundamentally about whether testimony transmits knowledge, nor even about whether aesthetic experience matters; it is about whether a standard of taste is the kind of thing that can be encapsulated in a decidable rule or the kind of thing that must be acquired as a skill.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey. Is this a substantive disagreement about the nature of aesthetic standards, a verbal disagreement about what “standard” means, or two camps answering different questions under one heading? What would count as progress given the diagnosis? Could a distinction — or an experiment, or a formal result about judgments of the same work made with and without deference — actually move this, or should I expect the impasse to persist because the competing readings of “standard” are doing the heavy lifting? And if the skill-like reading is correct, is the real disagreement actually about how a judgment can be both subjective and correct?
