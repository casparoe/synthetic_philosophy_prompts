I am taking a philosophy course on gratitude, beneficence, and our obligations to the past, and I have been developing a position I would like pressure-tested before I build a term paper around it.

I call it the entrustment account of gratitude. Its core claim is that gratitude, properly distinguished from admiration and appreciation, is made appropriate not primarily by the size of a benefit or by the cost to the benefactor, but by entrustment: the benefactor deliberately makes some part of their future welfare depend on how the recipient subsequently conducts themselves. On this view, gratitude is the acknowledgment of that deliberately accepted asymmetry, and its proper magnitude tracks how much the benefactor knowingly placed in the recipient's hands, not how much actually changed hands.

Examples: co-signing a lease for me before I had any income is a large entrustment — if I become unresponsive, the benefactor is on the hook — so deep gratitude is due even though the realized benefit was modest. Returning a lost wallet involves almost no entrustment, so only mild gratitude is due even if the wallet contains what would have been a life-changing amount for me. An inheritance from someone who formed no intention regarding me involves no entrustment and therefore no gratitude, only appreciation or good fortune.

The account thus separates three things that common sense and much of what we have read seem to run together: the magnitude of the benefit, the cost to the benefactor, and entrustment. My claim is that entrustment does the real normative work.

Two predictions worry me. First, the view says a failed attempt can still ground gratitude: a benefactor who wires money to me that is never delivered through no fault of theirs has taken on a large entrustment, and gratitude becomes fitting even though no benefit arrives. Second, the anonymous benefactor who sacrifices a great deal but asks nothing becomes awkward: there is sacrifice and benefit but no entrustment, because the benefactor's future welfare is not keyed to my response. I am torn between treating this as a counterexample that narrows the account's scope and saying the fitting response here is admiration rather than gratitude.

Please give me an honest assessment. Reconstruct the account in its strongest form; say whether the central distinction is doing genuine work or mainly rephrasing a standard view according to which gratitude responds to benevolence; identify whether the core thought already exists under another name; and point to the exact place where the view fails or stops being distinctive. Finally, say how confident you are in the claims you make about both my proposal and the surrounding conceptual terrain, and single out the point you are least sure of.
