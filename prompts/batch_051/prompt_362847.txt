There's a debate in Buddhist ethics that's been running for decades now, and I can't shake the feeling that it's stuck for structural reasons rather than because the texts are ambiguous. The two sides, roughly: one camp says Buddhist ethics—especially Mahāyāna—is basically consequentialist, because it evaluates actions by their effects on suffering, and the texts keep defending apparently rule-breaking acts, like a bodhisattva killing someone to save many others, by appealing to the outcome. The other camp says no, it's really a form of virtue ethics, because the central concern is the gradual cultivation of character traits like compassion and wisdom over many lifetimes, and the precepts are more like training aids than binding constraints. Everyone cites the same passages and nobody moves.

My working hypothesis: the disagreement isnt really about the content of Buddhist ethics at all. It's about what a normative theory is supposed to do. The consequentialist side seems to treat a theory as something that evaluates acts (or maybe states of affairs) as right in virtue of their consequences, while the virtue side treats a theory as something that explains how agents become good and what good deliberation looks like from the inside. If that's right, the two camps are answering different questions under one heading, and the exact same Buddhist material can look consequentialist or virtue-ethical depending on which question you foreground.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey of the literature. Is my hypothesis right? Or is there a real substantive disagreement hiding underneath—say, about whether Buddhist sources actually do evaluate actions by their outcomes, or about how tight the link between the bodhisattva's rule-breaking and consequentialism really has to be? And what would count as progress given the diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves the debate, a better question to ask, a formal or interpretive test, or just an argument that the question is ill-posed and should be dropped?
