For the past two years I have supervised the nurse advice line at a community health center. The hardest calls are not emergencies but gray-zone complaints — months of fatigue, sadness that will not lift, weight that will not come off — and my nurses have to decide in roughly ten minutes, without a physician in the room, whether the caller has a medical problem or an ordinary life problem; a physician signs off on each appointment, but only after triage has already happened. Our decisions vary from nurse to nurse. I have been reading on my own about the naturalist and normativist views of disease: roughly, that a disease is a biological dysfunction measured against how the body normally works, versus that calling something a disease always carries a value judgment about what states are bad or undesirable. Please turn that distinction into a procedure my nurses can actually use. Lay out the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops by classifying the complaint as not a medical matter, and where it must hand the call to a clinician because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Then run the procedure on three calls: an adult requesting an evaluation for ADHD because inattention is damaging their work; a woman asking whether menopause is a disorder the center should be treating; and a parent seeking growth-hormone treatment for a short but otherwise healthy child. Make at least one outcome one you expect me not to like, and end by stating what the procedure cannot settle.
