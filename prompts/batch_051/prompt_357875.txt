I am a retired neurophysiologist who has recently come to philosophy, and I am trying to settle whether the following four convictions form a tenable package. I would rather not signal in advance which of them I would give up, so please treat them all as genuine commitments of mine and audit the set.

(1) Ryle was essentially right that Cartesian dualism rests on a category mistake: expressions such as believing, intending, deciding, and feeling are not reports of episodes in a private non-physical medium, nor names of hidden inner causes; they are dispositional terms that characterize what a person is able and liable to do.

(2) Accepting that diagnosis does not impugn ordinary psychological talk. That talk remains legitimate, useful, and largely true, because it is competence talk rather than proto-scientific mechanism talk; it was never in the business of describing internal machinery.

(3) Cognitive neuroscience has nonetheless discovered real internal mechanisms—working-memory systems, neural representations, predictive models—that are causally responsible for the patterns of behavior we call intelligent. These mechanisms are not merely re-descriptions of those behavioral patterns.

(4) But the explanatory gap is not a category mistake. There is something it is like to be in pain or to see red, and this phenomenal character is not captured by any dispositional or functional description, however detailed.

Please tell me which commitments pull against which, and how badly. I want the genuine tensions ranked from most to least severe, each with a note on what would have to give and what that revision would cost. Where a tension is merely apparent, say why it dissolves. Then recommend the single least costly revision that would make the package consistent. End with a machine-readable line of the form:
