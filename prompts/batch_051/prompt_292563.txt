Analyse the Nixon shock of 15 August 1971 — the suspension of the US dollar's convertibility into gold, which marked the decisive turn of the major currencies from the Bretton Woods system towards fiat money — through the lens of Searle's account of institutional facts. On that account, a dollar note is money not because of its physical composition but because a community collectively assigns it a status function of the counts-as kind: the paper counts as money in certain contexts, and that collectively recognised status carries deontic powers the paper itself does not possess. Set out, by the lights of this account, what Nixon's announcement accomplished as a declaration, what it did and did not change, and what collective conditions it had to presuppose or secure for dollars to go on functioning as money. Then say which features of the episode the account explains well, which it misdescribes or leaves untouched — consider legal tender laws, the state's acceptance of dollars in payment of taxes, habit and confidence, and the dollar's asymmetric position in the international monetary system — and whether the episode is best read as confirming the account, as an anomaly for it, or as falling outside its intended scope.
