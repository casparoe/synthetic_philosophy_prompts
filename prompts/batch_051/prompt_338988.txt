I have a rule I've been running for the last couple years, and I honestly don't know if it's epistemic discipline or laziness. I won't endorse a policy unless I can point to a conditional prediction market on the exact outcome the policy is supposed to move, and I require the market to be clearing above 70 cents before I treat it as probably better than the status quo. In my head this is futarchy-as-personal-practice: I'm trying to make my beliefs pay rent by making them bettable. Last month it meant I had to stay neutral on a local rent control measure, because there's no market on vacancy rates conditional on it passing, and I didn't trust the one on a national rent index to tell me much about my city. It's kept me from getting talked into things by confident people, but it's also left me effectively agnostic about zoning, criminal justice, trade, and most climate policy, since for those there either is no market or the only one that exists tracks some convenient metric—arrest rate, GDP, tons of CO2—rather than the thing I actually care about. Is this a defensible division of epistemic labor, or have I just built an elaborate machine for deferring to a mechanism that is thin, manipulable, and biased toward whatever is easy to measure? If some version of the rule is worth keeping, what features of a market would need to hold before it get's to override my own read of the evidence, and what's the right fallback when there's no market at all?
