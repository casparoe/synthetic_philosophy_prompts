I am an undergraduate editor for my department's student-run philosophy journal, and I have been asked to conduct the featured interview with a visiting speaker who defends one-boxing in Newcomb's problem on broadly evidential-decision-theoretic grounds. Our readership mostly knows the standard two-box setup, and the guest has given introductory presentations of her view many times, so the published interview should not spend time re-explaining it. Please draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, arranged so that the sequence moves from the familiar objections toward less familiar pressure points and so that a deflection that works early in the interview becomes harder to sustain later. The questions should cover at least: the dominance principle and the one-boxer's reason for thinking dominance cannot be applied when the act is evidentially tied to the state; the challenge that if one-boxing is rational, one-boxers ought to be able to enrich themselves through real-world predictors, together with the stock reply that predictors of the needed sort are rare; a transparent-box variant in which a reliable but imperfect predictor's prediction is revealed before the choice, so that the act no longer supplies new evidence; and the smoking-lesion case, in which a gene raises both the probability that a person smokes and the probability of lung cancer while smoking itself causes no cancer, so that evidential reasoning appears to condemn a harmless pleasure merely because it is bad news. For each of these, the follow-up should anticipate and press the reply she is most likely to give; for instance, if she answers the smoking-lesion case by distinguishing what an act indicates from what it causes, or by appealing to ratifiability, the follow-up should ask what principled constraint prevents that move from reaching back into the original Newcomb case.

Please end with one or two questions about Newcomb-like cases in which the predictor predicts, not the agent's one-shot act, but the agent's standing rule or decision procedure. For the final question, the follow-up should ask whether evidential decision theory is thereby being pushed into a theory of which dispositions to cultivate rather than which acts to perform, and whether it can accept that result without surrendering its claim to be a theory of rational action. The questions should require precise, substantive answers rather than yes-or-no answers, and should be written in the polished but conversational register of a student journal interview.
