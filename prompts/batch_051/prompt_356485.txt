I am a retired research chemist, and my habit of trusting formal identities has been unsettled since I started reading philosophy of probability and decision theory. Simpson's paradox is the thing doing the unsettling. I have been trying to formalize an argument that displays exactly where the paradox bites, but I cannot determine which numbered step is bad. I am not asking for references or a reading list, only for a precise diagnosis.

Let X be a binary treatment (T = experimental drug, C = standard care), Y a binary outcome (1 = recovery, 0 = failure), and Z a binary covariate (M or F) recorded for every patient. Write q_x(z) = P(Y=1 | X=x, Z=z) for the recovery rate in subgroup z within arm x, and Q_x = P(Y=1 | X=x) for the aggregate recovery rate in arm x.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) By the law of total probability, for each arm x:

Q_x = q_x(M) P(Z=M | X=x) + q_x(F) P(Z=F | X=x).

(2) Suppose the treatment has the higher recovery rate in each subgroup: q_T(M) > q_C(M) and q_T(F) > q_C(F).

(3) Under (2), each term on the right-hand side of the identity in (1) is larger for x=T than the corresponding term for x=C. Since a weighted sum of larger terms cannot be smaller than a weighted sum of smaller terms, it follows that Q_T > Q_C.

(4) The following well-computed table satisfies (2):

                Men               Women
T (drug)        91/100 = 91%      20/1,000 = 2%
C (standard)    180/200 = 90%     1/100 = 1%

Aggregating: Q_T = 111/1,100 ≈ 10.1%, and Q_C = 181/300 ≈ 60.3%.

(5) Since (1) is an arithmetic identity and (4) is a correct transcription of the table, (3) would force Q_T > Q_C. The table reports Q_T < Q_C. Therefore either probability theory is inconsistent, or the aggregate percentages have been computed on the wrong denominators.

Is this argument valid? If it is not, which step is the first to fail? State exactly what goes wrong at that step, explain how (2) and (4) can both be true of a single table, and give the condition on the two arms under which an inference of the form (3) would be sound.
