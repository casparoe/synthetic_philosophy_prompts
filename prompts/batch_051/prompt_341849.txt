I do backend work on model serving infra — mostly Kubernetes and CUDA debugging — and I keep getting dragged into an argument among coworkers about the open letter from a couple years back calling for a six-month pause on training anything bigger than GPT-4. none of us has any actual power over any of this, so it stays abstract. The thing that bugs me is that people treat "pause frontier AI" as one undifferentiated position, when the actual reasons people give are a bunch of different claims pointing in slightly different directions. I want to split them out and score them separately.

Here are the strands I can pick out, put in my own words (correct me if I'm garbling one):

(a) The control problem / alignment worry: if you train a system you can't evaluate or verify, you're essentially shipping something you can't test; we shouldn't do that until interpretability or evals are better.
(b) The misuse angle: a general-purpose model is a dual-use ingredient; the scary part isn't the model itself but that weights or API access lower the cost of nasty stuff (bio, cyber, persuasion at scale).
(c) The "social tech is lagging" angle: red-teaming, auditing, and the relevant laws got left behind by the last capability jump, and a pause is a moratorium so slower institutions can catch up.
(d) The coordination/race argument: almost everyone at the labs says in private they'd rather slow down, but nobody can unilaterally because the other guy won't; you need a kind of Schelling stop.
(e) The externalities one I half-remember from an AI governance blog: a big training run generates general capabilities that impose risk on people who never consented, like a factory dumping waste; a pause is basically demanding an environmental review before the next bigger run.
(f) The concentration-of-power thing: a handful of private companies is deciding the trajectory of a technology that touches everyone, which isn't a legitimate decision procedure, and a pause is the only lever left before the thing is too big to steer.

What I want is a rating, not another pro/con essay on the pause as a blob. Give each strand a weight in percent of the total public case for pausing (they should sum to 100), and tier them S/A/B/C by how much actual work each one does. Then for each, say whether it really supports a temporary pause, a permanent halt, or mostly supports some other policy (compute caps, lab liability, deployment licensing, evals, a treaty). Finally, tell me which of these collapses hardest if you poke at it and whether you think its got a motte-and-bailey thing going on, because I suspect one or two of them are doing more rhetorical work than argumentative work.
