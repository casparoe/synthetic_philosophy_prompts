Design a 75-minute classroom game for an upper-level philosophy seminar on markets and character, with roughly 18 students and no economics background assumed. The game should make participants feel the two sides of the standing dispute before either is named: the doux-commerce thesis, that market exchange disciplines self-interest into honesty, reliability, and courtesy, and the corruption thesis, that market framing turns moral scruples into tradable goods and crowds out norms that depend on doing something for its own sake. Structure it as two or three short exchange games with real but trivial stakes (sweets, points, or small privileges), identical except that one institutional feature is changed at a time: anonymous one-shot exchange, repeated exchange with reputations, and a market in something participants initially treated as norm-governed, such as a queue, a promise, punctuality, or a small act of care. Provide rules, materials, a payoff scheme that does not require real money, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps the observed behavior onto the rival positions only after play. Include a facilitator's note on what predictably goes wrong (collusion, demand effects, students play-acting a moral lesson rather than acting on the incentives), how to handle it, and which feature of real market morality the game cannot reproduce and the discussion must supply.
