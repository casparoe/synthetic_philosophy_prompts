For a seminar on distributive justice that I co-teach, the same argument about the original position recurs every year and never seems to move. The question is whether parties behind the veil of ignorance should choose principles by the maximin rule or by maximizing expected utility. The Rawlsian side says the veil leaves one with no defensible probabilities over social positions, so the only rational basis for choosing principles is to rank arrangements by the worst position they allow; choosing so as to make the least advantaged as well off as possible is not pessimism but a way of taking each person's distinct claim seriously. The Harsanyi side says that impartiality itself is properly modeled by giving each person's position equal probability and then maximizing expected welfare, which yields the highest average; refusing to assign probabilities, on their view, is not caution but a disguised prior that weights the worst-off above everyone else. Each side can accept the other's inference once the other's modeling assumptions are granted, which is part of why the debate stalls.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really a disagreement about decision theory at all. It is a disagreement about what the veil is meant to model: Rawls uses it to model fairness between persons whose claims must be respected even when those persons are not interchangeable, while Harsanyi uses it to model the choice of a single impartial observer trying to maximize overall good. If that is right, the maximin-versus-expected-utility argument is downstream of two different modeling decisions, and the appearance of a single question obscures that. But I might be wrong; perhaps there is a genuine, decidable question about whether complete ignorance forbids probability assignments, and both positions are really answers to that question.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is the dispute verbal, substantive, or are the two sides answering different questions under one heading? Where exactly does the reasoning come apart, and is there a fixable disagreement about rationality under uncertainty hiding inside it? And given the diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result about what the two models have in common, a variant of the original position designed to force convergence, or an argument that no progress should be expected. If minimax regret bears on the diagnosis, mention it; if it is a red herring here, say why.
