In the essay *Of Money* (1752), Hume defends the view that a country's absolute stock of money is, considered in itself, of no consequence for real trade: prices always proportion themselves to the plenty of money, so a greater or lesser supply of coin merely changes the numerical level of prices. Money, he says, is not one of the wheels of trade but only the lubricant that makes the wheels turn more smoothly. I am collecting objections to this long-run neutrality claim, as it appears in *Of Money* and *Of the Balance of Trade*. So far I have these four:

1. The transition-delay objection: Hume himself accepts that while prices are adjusting, an influx of money stimulates production; neutrality therefore holds only after a lag, not during the adjustment process.
2. The hoarding and reflux objection: money need not circulate and alter prices, since it may be hoarded or sent abroad, so the proportional price adjustment Hume assumes can fail to occur.
3. The credit objection: in an economy with bank-created money or paper credit, the link between the coin supply and the price level is loose, and an outflow of specie may be offset by domestic credit expansion rather than by falling prices.
4. The injection/distribution objection: new money enters the economy unevenly, changing relative prices and the distribution of wealth along its path, rather than raising all prices proportionally.

I want a fifth objection that is not on this list and is not a restatement, special case, or combination of any of these four. It should be aimed specifically at Hume's claim in *Of Money* that the quantity of money is indifferent to real commerce, and it should have a different structure from the objections above. Then tell me honestly whether the fifth objection is any good, and whether it damages Hume's long-run neutrality claim more than the first four do.
