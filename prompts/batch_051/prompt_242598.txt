In my medical ethics course we read the 1968 Harvard Medical School Ad Hoc Committee report, A Definition of Irreversible Coma, chaired by Henry Beecher. The report’s opening rationale says that the committee’s primary purpose is to define irreversible coma as a new criterion for death, and it gives two kinds of reason: that respirators and other supportive measures now keep bodies going after the brain has irreversibly ceased functioning, and that this creates burdens for families and hospitals while also delaying the use of organs for transplantation. The committee then specifies operational tests—unreceptivity and unresponsivity, no movements or spontaneous breathing, absent brainstem reflexes, and a flat EEG, repeated after 24 hours and after excluding hypothermia and drug intoxication. I want to know what this policy presupposes about what death itself is. Is it treating death as loss of personhood, loss of integrated organismic functioning, or something else? Does the same view underwrite declaring someone dead by circulatory criteria, or does the report quietly change the concept rather than merely add a new test? And if we rejected that presupposition, what would the practice of declaring death have to change? Please separate what is historically and clinically settled from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and clinicians, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
