A friend in my historiography seminar has been pushing an argument he calls the presentism-proves-too-much argument, and I can't find the exit. It runs:

(1) A historian can't step outside the concepts, questions, and values of her own time; there's no neutral perch outside all historical standpoints.
(2) So the standards she brings to the past — what counts as a good explanation, what counts as significant, what counts as a wrong — are always the standards of her own time.
(3) That means a historian today who calls Roman chattel slavery an injustice and a nineteenth-century historian who called ancient slavery a necessary stage in the progress of civilization are both just applying the standards of their own times to the past.
(4) If both judgments are just applications of time-bound standards, then there's no time-independent standpoint from which the later judgment is better or truer than the earlier one.
(5) And that would mean we can't ever claim that our understanding of the Holocaust, the slave trade, or the colonial famines is an improvement over the understanding of the people who justified them.
(6) So presentism in historiography collapses into a relativism that would make the whole practice of historical judgment arbitrary.

I won't accept (5), but each step looks airtight, and my friend says the only way to avoid it is to deny (1) or (2) and admit that historians sometimes escape the standards of their own time. Which step should I reject, why did that step look so safe, and what would rejecting it cost for how I write history? I'd most like to keep the idea that historical understanding can improve, and I'd give up presentism itself if I have to — but I want to know whether there's a less expensive exit. And if the argument is rigged somehow, tell me that straight instead of pretending I have to pick a premise.
