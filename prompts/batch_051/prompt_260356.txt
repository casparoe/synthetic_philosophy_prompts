I'm a software engineer, so I'll ask this as someone used to thinking about systems and expected failure modes rather than as a philosopher. Lately I've been reading about the insect-farming companies that raise black soldier fly larvae, mealworms, and crickets by the billion, mostly for animal feed and increasingly for human food. What I can't get past is the mismatch with animal-welfare law. In the jurisdictions I know about, the legal line for welfare protection sits at vertebrates, with only occasional and recent extensions to cephalopods or decapod crustaceans; insects are simply outside the frame. The result is an industry whose entire stock is insects, killing at a scale where a single facility may process hundreds of millions of animals a week, with essentially no welfare constraints.

I take it this arrangement presupposes some view of what insects are, mentally: roughly, that an insect is so unlikely to be capable of felt pain that farming and killing them by the billion needs no welfare ledger. But I've encountered an argument that undermines that inference. Call it a scale argument. If you assign even a low probability — one percent, a tenth of a percent — to the hypothesis that an individual insect can suffer, then at the throughput of these facilities the expected number of suffering individuals is still enormous. In my own work I do exactly this kind of aggregation when I estimate the real-world impact of a rare defect across millions of users, so the form of reasoning doesn't strike me as exotic.

What I'd like to understand is what theory of moral status the existing practice — the vertebrate cutoff and an insect-farming sector with no welfare standards — actually presupposes. Is it committed to the claim that insects are not conscious at all, or only to the weaker claim that even if they might be conscious, their suffering counts for very little or is outweighed by human benefits? Or is the real commitment a bet that the probability of insect sentience is effectively zero, because any nonzero probability at this scale would be impossible to farm around? I'd also like to know where the practice is underdetermined: which rival views about moral status would license the same policies even though they disagree with one another. Are the practice's commitments mutually consistent, and consistent with how the same legal systems treat other low-probability, high-magnitude risks? And what would have to change, in law or in farming practice, if the implicit theory were replaced by one that took the scale argument seriously?

Please also say what would most change your mind: which argument or empirical finding would push you to a different verdict, and where in the reasoning above you think a mistake is most likely.
