Two moral philosophers have just concluded a year-long adversarial collaboration and now need to produce the joint statement they can both sign. One is a Scanlonian contractualist: they hold that an act is wrong if it could not be justified to each affected person on grounds none of them could reasonably reject, and they accept the individualist restriction, meaning that justifiability is owed to persons taken one by one rather than to some pooled entity, so harms cannot be simply summed across persons. The other is a defender of aggregation within constraints: they accept that there are non-negotiable constraints against causing serious harm as a means, but they hold that within those constraints the numbers themselves can count, so that several equally weighty individual claims may jointly outweigh one. Neither side is to be written as a strawman.

They already agree on the verdicts in two pressure cases. In the rescue case, one can save either one stranger or five strangers from equal peril, and both hold that the five should be saved, though each explains this differently. In the transplant case, a surgeon could kill one healthy person and distribute that person's organs to five patients who would otherwise die, and both hold that the act is wrong. Their dispute is therefore not about the verdicts but about the grounds of the contrast between the two cases, and about whether a principle that passes the reasonable-rejection test can deliver that contrast without surreptitiously aggregating.

Draft the document this collaboration should have produced. It should not be a report on their debate or a verdict on who is right; it should be the constructive text both could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact.

Organise the response under the following headings, in this order, at roughly the lengths indicated:

1. Preamble (about 100 words). State the shared datum — the pair of verdicts and the fact that they are not in dispute — and record what each side now recognises it must explain to the other.

2. Agreed claims (about 400 words). Numbered, substantive, non-vacuous claims about the reasonable-rejection test, the individualist restriction, the two cases, and the burden each side accepts. They should include what both regard as the strongest reason for holding the transplant to be wrong, and why both regard the rescue case as the place where the individualist restriction must prove itself.

3. Disagreements table (about 350 words). A table with three columns — Issue; Scanlonian formulation; Aggregationist formulation — with each formulation phrased so that its own side would accept it. Cover at least: whether the reason to save the five is aggregative or a tie-breaker that each of the five could separately accept; whether the individualist restriction can accommodate the rescue verdict; and whether the wrongness of the transplant is best explained by a means constraint, by the reasonable-rejection test, or by both.

4. Cruxes and predictions (about 350 words). State the sub-questions such that settling one of them would move at least one side, in wording both sides accept as bearing on the dispute. For each crux, give the case or argument both sides agree would test it, and record each side's prediction in advance.

5. Drafting note (about 200 words). State how confident you are that each camp would sign the wording as written, name the single point in the document you are least sure about, and note any place where the phrasing leans on a contested reading of Scanlonian contractualism.

Write the whole response in British English.
