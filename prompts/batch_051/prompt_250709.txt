I sit on the grants comittee of a mid-size environmental foundation, and we've got a $400,000 request in front of us to restore a drained salt marsh on the Gulf coast — breach the old dike, let the tides back in, turn a marginal cattle pasture back into wetland. The pitch is standard conservation: fish nursery, flood buffer, carbon. But one of our members, a wildlife biologist who's been reading the wild animal welfare stuff, has thrown a wrench into the vote. Her argument is that restoring the habitat will create an enormous number of new animal lives — mostly small invertebrates, fish, and small vertebrates — and almost all of them will die young from predation, starvation, or disease. She says that if you actually add it up, a functioning marsh may be a net-negative ecosystem in welfare terms, and the drained pasture might be better from the animals' point of view precisely because it supports fewer of them. Her bottom line is that we should deny the grant and, more broadly, stop treating habitat restoration as an automatic good. Two other members find this so perverse they want to wave it through without further discussion.

I need to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action: fund the full project, fund a smaller pilot, deny it, or fund something else (for example, a monitoring phase that actually tracks welfare-relevant outcomes before we spend the full amount). Whichever way you come down, give the strongest version of the losing side a real run — don't just call the biologist's view repugnant or the conservationists naive. And give the rest of us criteria we can apply the next time a restoration grant comes up, because I doubt this is the last one. Be honest about how little we actually know about the net welfare of wild ecosystems, rather than papering over it with optimism.
