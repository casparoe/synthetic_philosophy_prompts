Me and a friend just had a long argument about Hofstadter’s old Scientific American essay “Dilemmas for Superrational Thinkers, Leading Up to a Luring Lottery” (reprinted in Metamagical Themas), and I keep coming back to two passages that seem to pull in opposite directions.

Early on, he writes: “Such individuals—individuals who will cooperate with one another despite all temptations toward crude egoism—are more than just rational; they are superrational, or for short, sane.” That sounds like superrationality is precisely a kind of refusal to give in to the selfish temptation.

But then, when he sets up the Platonia dilemma—twenty people can each send a telegram, and if exactly one sends, that person gets a billion dollars; if two or more send, nobody gets anything; if nobody sends, nobody gets anything—he writes: “Clearly, everyone will want that billion. Clearly, everyone will realize that if their name is not submitted, they have no chance at all.” That sounds like it’s steering each person toward sending, which sure doesn’t look like cooperating.

So do these two passages actually conflict? If not, what’s the honest reconciliation? Is the superrational move in Platonia to send with probability one in twenty, and if so, how does that fit with the first quote’s talk of cooperating despite temptations? Or is there something about the Platonia set-up—no identifiable partner, no shared payoff, no one to cooperate with—that makes the first quote not the right lens? I’d like a verdict on which reconciliation is faithful to the text and which is just special pleading, because my friend kept insisting the only consistent superrational move is to not send at all, and I dont see how that can be right if each person knows that not sending gurantees zero.
