I have been arguing with friends on both sides of the death penalty, and I keep catching myself converting their objections into decision rules, a habit I have never quite shaken. Take the old maxim (what gets called Blackstone's ratio) that it is a far worse thing to convict one innocent person than to let ten guilty people go free. For several of my interlocutors this is a moral absolute. But to me it looks, structurally, exactly like a binary decision rule with asymmetric losses. There are two possible errors: convicting the innocent, and acquitting the guilty. The maxim assigns the first error roughly ten times the weight of the second, and in a decision-theoretic framework a ratio like that does not impose an absolute ban; it fixes an evidence threshold, perhaps together with the base rate of true guilt among defendants. The threshold should shift when the stakes change. The death penalty is the obvious stress test: executing the innocent is much worse than imprisoning the innocent, which should push the threshold very high, while executing the actually guilty is meant to deter future murders more than incarceration does, which pushes it back down. On this way of looking at it, the whole fight becomes a dispute about utilities, base rates, and the irreversibility of the punishment, not about an absolute.

What I cannot decide is whether this is a deep identity of structure or a seductive false analogy, because retribution does not sit easily inside the framework. If the justification for executing a murderer is that he deserves death, then the act is not simply a lever for reducing future killings; its value may not be commensurable at all with the cost of executing the innocent. And the legal standard of proof might not be trying to maximize expected utility at all—it might be a deontological constraint against treating innocents as means to general deterrence, which would explain why the maxim is framed as a principle of rights rather than a tunable parameter. I would like a precise diagnosis. Please spell out the exact mapping between legal error-avoidance and loss-function thresholds; state what would have to be true of both domains for the decision-theoretic results to transfer cleanly; and then give me at least one concrete prediction this analogy makes that turns out false—the cleanest way I know to separate a real isomorphism from an attractive family resemblance.
