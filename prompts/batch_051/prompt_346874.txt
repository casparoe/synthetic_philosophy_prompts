I got dragooned onto our neighborhood council's ethics committee after the previous members all quit over a fight about whats even acceptable as a gift, and I've been using my friend Dana as a kind of oracle because she spent years auditing local-government contracts and has a very settled gut about this stuff. The problem is I can't tell what rule is actually generating her gut. Here are the verdicts I've collected from her over the years:

- A mayor gave a paid staff job to the son of his campaign manager without advertising the post; the son had no relevant experience. Dana: corrupt, and she was genuinely angry.
- A resident gave her city councillor a fifty-dollar gift card after he helped get a broken sidewalk fixed on her block. The gift arrived after everything was settled, and the councillor hadn't expedited anything. Dana: not corruption.
- A procurement officer awarded a software contract to his wife's firm without any public bid; the firm was competent and its informal quote was actually the lowest. Dana: corrupt, no hesitation.
- A clinic director in a country where officials routinely expect small informal payments gave the local records officer about a day's wage to issue a death certificate for a patient. The certificate was legally owed and uncontested, and the officer would have simply sat on it for months otherwise. Dana: not corruption, she described it as paying a toll on a road you already had the right to use.
- A school principal accepted a bushel of mangoes from a parent right after the parent's daughter was admitted, but the principal was on leave during admissions, had no role in the decision, and the daughter's scores were safely above the cutoff. Dana: fine.

Now there's the case I actually need her verdict on. My uncle runs a small clinic, and a building inspector has been sitting on his occupancy permit for weeks. The building itself is fine — the inspector admitted as much — but he keeps pointing to two trivial infractions that inspectors normally waive, and a local business association told my uncle the only way to get the permit is facilitation money, about fifteen dollars. Even the local chamber's ethics officer just shrugged and said that's how business is done there. It's not quite the death-certificate situation, because there is some discretion in whether the infractions get waived, but it's also not like my uncle is trying to get an unsafe building through.

If I bring this to Dana, what would she probably say, and more importantly what rule from her five earlier verdicts generates that answer? She could be running something like corruption iff a benefit is tied to an official act, corruption iff someone unfairly loses out, or corruption iff it violates the norms of the place itself. I'd like to know which of those rules her pattern actually supports, which of the earlier cases does the most work in telling them apart, and which detail of my uncle's case she'd treat as load-bearing.
