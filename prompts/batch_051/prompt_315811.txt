I keep seeing people argue about whether hope is an epistemic vice or a moral virtue, and each side just reaches for its one favorite case: the terminal patient hoping against the odds, the climate "hopium" critique, Stockdale's point that the POWs who made it home weren't the optimists, the therapist who says recovery requires hope. Before I read any more of it, I want the actual spec. Write the desiderata a theory of hope has to meet if it's going to settle whether hope — as distinct from optimism and from wishful thinking — is an epistemic vice, a moral virtue, both, or neither: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it can't collapse (hoping-that-p versus being hopeful as a whole; what I may hope for myself versus what I may encourage in a patient or a public), and the empirical regularities about unrealistic optimism, placebo responding, or depressed realism it has to respect, with the reason behind each requirement. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just camp preferences, flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied — the tension between responsiveness to evidence and persisting when the evidence is bad is the one I most want probed — and give me a one-line scorecard of the usual contenders.
