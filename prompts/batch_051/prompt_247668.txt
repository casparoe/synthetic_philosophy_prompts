I am a retired physicist — most of my career was in condensed-matter physics, with a late detour through quantum field theory — and since retiring I have been reading the philosophy of physics more systematically than I ever managed during my working life. I have settled into a cluster of convictions about whether physics could have a final theory and whether we could know we had found one, and I would like a consistency audit of the whole set from someone who can see the joints between these positions better than I can.

The commitments are these:

1. There is a mind-independent physical world with a determinate fundamental structure, and the defining aim of fundamental physics is to describe that structure.

2. The pessimistic meta-induction: the record of successive theoretical replacements in the history of physics gives us good reason to expect that the posits of our current fundamental theories will in turn be replaced, so we should be cautious about reading ontology off any present theory.

3. Structural realism: the right lesson to draw from (2) is that what survives theory change is mathematical structure rather than the entities or intrinsic natures the theories postulate. We should be realists about the structure and agnostic about the relata.

4. A theory would deserve to be called final only if it did more than unify the known forces and fit the data. It should also make intelligible why the laws, symmetries, and constants have the specific character they do, rather than merely recording them as brute.

5. In principle, any finite body of observational evidence underdetermines theory choice: empirically equivalent rivals can be constructed for any candidate theory. It follows that no amount of successful testing could ever certify a theory as final.

6. Despite (5), I treat convergence on a final theory as a legitimate regulative ideal. The successive unifications in the history of physics — terrestrial and celestial mechanics, electricity and magnetism, the electroweak theory, and so on — are a rational guide, and the search for a single fundamental theory should remain the organizing goal of the discipline.

Please go through these and tell me: which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be; for each genuine tension, how severe it is and what would have to give; and finally, which single revision would do the most to make the set hang together, and what that revision would cost. Rank the tensions rather than treating them as equally serious, and do not blunt the harder points on my account.

One practical request: please write the answer in British English (spelling, punctuation, and usage), even though I have written the question in American English.
