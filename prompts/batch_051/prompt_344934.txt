I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about whether punishment requires basic desert. He defends a Dennett-style compatibilism: if someone is sane, informed, and responsive to reasons, the fact that her actions were caused is irrelevant, and punishing her can be justified as a fair kind of social regulation. I have been gravitating toward the Caruso-style view that basic desert is an illusion and that we should gradually replace punitive institutions with something like a public health or quarantine model. After several hours my friend produced the following chain of reasoning, and although I remain convinced something is wrong with it, I cannot locate the mistake without either conceding too much or smuggling desert back in under another name.

(1) If no one ever deserves punishment, then retributive considerations cannot justify punishment.
(2) If retributive considerations cannot justify punishment, then punishment can be justified only by forward-looking considerations such as deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation, and public safety.
(3) If punishment can be justified only by forward-looking considerations, then whether we may inflict hard treatment on a person depends only on the expected consequences of doing so.
(4) But if that is right, then we would be permitted, and perhaps required, to punish an innocent person whenever the expected consequences of doing so are at least as good as those of every alternative.
(5) No acceptable view may permit the deliberate punishment of the innocent.
(6) Therefore either there is some basic desert after all, or the combination of claims I accept commits me to permitting the deliberate punishment of the innocent.

He then said I can keep my rejection of basic desert only by accepting (4), or I can avoid (4) only by admitting that some non-consequentialist constraint, such as treating persons as ends in themselves, already does the needed work—but that, he insists, is just desert under another name. I told him this was a false choice, but after the argument I could not articulate the third option. Which step, if any, goes wrong? If the argument is valid, which premise should I give up, and what would accepting its denial require me to accept about punishment, moral responsibility, or the constraints on how the state may treat persons?
