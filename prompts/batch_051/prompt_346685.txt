I teach a philosophy of law seminar and I run a recurring exercise where students sit as a law-reform commission that's been building a doctrine of legal personhood from its own decisions. Their precedents so far: (1) a chimpanzee was denied personhood for a habeas-style transfer to a sanctuary — the commission said the chimp clearly has a point of veiw and interests, but the proposed remedy would require treating it as a duty-bearer it couldn't understand, and animal-welfare law was the better tool; (2) a river was granted personhood to bring a pollution suit, with a human guardian appointed, on the grounds that 'personhood' here is just a device for getting a claim before a court; (3) a corporation was granted personhood to sue a former director for self-dealing, because it acts through agents, has a distinct interest in its assets and contracts, and can be sued back; (4) a charitable trust was granted personhood to hold property in its own name.

Now a new application comes in: a commercial AI system that autonomously manages an investment fund, executes trades, and signs contracts (with a human operator able to override it) wants to be recognized as a legal person for the limited purpose of entering contracts in its own name, so its operators' liability is limited to the fund. The system has no awareness or preferences and holds no assets apart from the fund. If the commission wants to cohere with its own precendents, what should it decide? Which precedent is the closest analog, and if the precedents underdetermine the answer, what principle is the commission adopting with each option? Then name one further case that would most sharply tell those two principles apart.
