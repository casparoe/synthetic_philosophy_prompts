After retiring from a career in field ecology, and after several years reading philosophy of risk, I now chair the safety review committee of a regional outdoor association. Each week we approve or reject proposals—hikes, paddles, youth cycling outings, ski tours—and the recurring question is whether the added fatality risk of a proposed activity is acceptable. We all know what a micromort is, but we have never had a disciplined way to use it: our deliberations drift among gut feeling, anecdotes, and whatever figure a trip leader happens to bring. I want you to turn the micromort idea into a reusable written decision procedure that the committee can run in under an hour, without a statistician, and often without any credible estimate for the specific activity at hand. The procedure should fix, rather than offer, the decision rules: whether to compare micromorts per trip, per hour, or per mile and why; how to treat the participant's baseline risk; how to weigh risk to participants against risk to bystanders and rescuers; what to do when the relevant number is missing or borrowed from another country or decade; and how, if at all, serious non-fatal injury should enter a framework built around death. It should also mark the exact point where calculation ends and the committee is making a value judgment rather than a technical one.

Then run the procedure on three proposals and give a definite verdict for each. (1) A routine hike for which ten participants would each drive roughly forty miles each way to the trailhead: one leader asks whether that driving is simply background risk the club may ignore, or whether we must require a hired minibus that would nearly quadruple the trip fee. (2) A youth programme proposes a thirty-mile bicycle outing on a rural road with no shoulder; the alternative is cancelling the programme's fitness component. (3) An experienced leader requests approval for an advanced ski tour in a range for which we have no recent fatality data; the only figures available come from a neighbouring country with different terrain.

I want a committed response: set the thresholds and rules, run the cases, and defend the verdicts—including at least one you predict the committee will resist. Where the procedure cannot decide, say what specific missing fact would settle it, and say whether that fact itself hides a value choice.
