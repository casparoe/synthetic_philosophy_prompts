Most anti-discrimination regimes operate through a closed list of protected characteristics: race, sex, religion, national origin, age, and disability are the standard core, with sexual orientation, gender identity, and a few others added in some jurisdictions. Legally, what matters is whether the ground is on the list. An employer generally needs no justification for selecting on height, facial attractiveness, weight, or educational pedigree, because those are not protected; by contrast, a grooming rule or dress code can be attacked as sex or religious discrimination in disguise, and a "culture fit" screen as a proxy for race. Unenumerated grounds raise no legal question unless they can be mapped onto a listed one.

This produces a puzzle about the practice's own commitments. The two rationales most often invoked for making a ground suspect sit very awkwardly with the actual list. If the implicit criterion were immutability — people should not be disadvantaged for traits they did not choose and cannot change — religion would be hard to justify as a protected ground, since it is often treated as chosen, while height and facial appearance, which are largely unchosen and frequently irrelevant to job performance, would have to be protected. If the criterion were merit-irrelevance — a ground is suspect when it is a poor proxy for what the decision is supposed to track — then educational credentials and attractiveness would become suspect in many hiring contexts, and age-based cutoffs might more often be defensible than the law allows. Yet the law protects religion and age, leaves height and appearance unprotected, and rarely treats educational selectivity as discrimination at all.

What theory of what makes a ground of discrimination suspect does this practice actually presuppose? I want a reconstruction of the implicit theory rather than a survey of positions. Does the practice strictly presuppose a single criterion — such as a history of group subordination, a view about which traits are central to identity or to a life one should not have to renounce, or a focus on status hierarchies maintained through group-based exclusions — or is the list a patchwork of different rationales that never add up to one principle? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely permits or happens to fit. Then say what would have to change if one candidate principle were imposed across the board, for example the principle that any trait which ought to be irrelevant to a decision counts as a suspect ground of selection. Which currently protected grounds would become insecure, which currently lawful selection criteria would become actionable, and how would the treatment of proxy discrimination have to change if a single principle replaced the closed-list approach?
