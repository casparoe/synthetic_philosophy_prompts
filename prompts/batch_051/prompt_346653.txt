I have been unable to settle a disagreement with a colleague who works in conservation, and I would appreciate a careful analysis of the argument at issue rather than a policy recommendation. The familiar argument for protecting wild habitats runs in broadly welfarist terms: to fell a forest or drain a wetland is said to harm the animals that live there, by destroying their homes, dispersing or killing them, and depriving them of the conditions of a decent life. That way of putting things quietly assumes a baseline — that, but for human interference, the animals in such a place are on the whole leading lives worth living, so that the destruction makes them worse off than they would otherwise have been.

A newer strand of thinking about wild animal welfare unsettles that baseline. If most wild animals die young, and death typically comes through predation, starvation, parasitism, disease or exposure, and if the pleasures available to such animals are in general outweighed by their pains, then an ordinary wild life may be a net negative. On that assumption, destroying a habitat would not on balance harm the animals it removes; it would largely prevent the birth or continuation of lives dominated by suffering, in something like the way one might think it no cruelty to refrain from breeding animals destined only for misery.

My question is what becomes of the original conservationist argument under this assumption. Does it survive, because its real basis was never the welfare of individual animals and must therefore fall back on species, ecosystem or future-human considerations? Does it need repair, perhaps by adding a precautionary premise about how little we can measure wild welfare before acting on its presumed sign? Or is it dead as a welfarist argument, leaving only non-welfarist reasons for preservation? I would ask you to identify exactly which premise of the familiar argument the net-welfare challenge bears on, and to say whether the argument can be restored in another form, or should be given up. Please write your answer in British English.
