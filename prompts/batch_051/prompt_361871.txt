I am a PhD student in moral and political philosophy, and I find myself caught between a theory I admire and an intuition I cannot shake.

The theory is broadly Jaspersian. Guilt in the strict sense—moral and criminal guilt—attaches only to individual persons for what they themselves have done; collective guilt is a category mistake. What members of a nation can share is at most political co-responsibility for what their state has done in their name, together with something like an existential liability incurred by standing by while wrongs are committed. Extended to intergenerational cases, the view says that later generations can inherit repair obligations, historical responsibility, and even shame, but never guilt. This account appeals to me because it respects the separateness of persons, gives a principled answer to who may be blamed or punished, and refuses to make ancestry or national membership a ground of guilt.

The intuition is this: when I think of the descendants of slaveholders, or of a nation decades after a genocide, it seems to me that saying they inherit responsibility but never guilt misses something important. I do not merely think such people bear costs or owe repair; I find it apt to say that the nation, or the family line, can be guilty in a way that outruns individual wrongdoing—and that denying this lets a community disown what was done in its name and by its ancestors. I do not want to give this up merely because a tidy theory says otherwise.

Please work through this collision with me. What would it take for the intuition to be debunked or dropped? What would it take for the Jaspersian picture to be amended or abandoned? Or is there a reading of guilt—or a defensible replacement for it, such as inherited responsibility or moral taint—on which the theory's insights and the intuition can both survive? I am less interested in being told which side wins than in understanding what would justify each move.
