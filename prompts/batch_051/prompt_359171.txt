I have taught a seminar on comparative ethics for several years, and the unit I find hardest to teach well is the debate over whether early Buddhist ethics is best classified as consequentialist. The affirmative reading has become increasingly common: skillful (kusala) action is justified by appeal to the suffering it prevents or alleviates, karmic consequences are understood as a natural law connecting action to wellbeing, and in some Mahāyāna literature the bodhisattva is permitted, even required, to breach an ordinary precept when doing so prevents far greater harm. I am putting together a handout for the seminar that catalogues the standard objections to this consequentialist reading, and I want the handout to be genuinely comprehensive at the level of structural types rather than a list of restated formulations.

So far my list contains five distinct objections. First, the agent-relativity objection: karmic results ripen primarily within the agent's own continuum, and the path is presented first and foremost as the purification of one's own mind, so the theory lacks the impartial, agent-neutral structure of consequentialism. Second, the intention objection: the morally decisive feature of an action is the intention (cetanā) behind it rather than the balance of actual outcomes it produces, which makes the criterion of rightness a property of the will rather than of the world. Third, the precepts-as-constraints objection: at least in the standard Theravāda presentation, the training rules, above all the prohibition on intentionally killing, are not treated as guidelines to be discarded whenever aggregate welfare would be improved. Fourth, the virtue-ethics objection: the brahmavihāras — loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity — together with the perfections such as generosity and patience, are best read as excellences constitutive of a good life, not as instruments for producing welfare. Fifth, the renunciant-ideal objection: the highest ideal in the Theravāda is the arahant who withdraws from the round of rebirth through self-cultivation rather than through a program of maximizing the welfare of all sentient beings, which sits poorly with any view that makes the production of consequences the ground of right action.

I am nearly certain that at least one further objection exists whose structure is not reducible to any of these five — not a rewording, not a special case, and not a combination of two or more of them. Give me that missing objection and explain precisely why it earns a separate entry rather than being folded into one of the five. If, in your judgment, one of my five entries is itself misclassified and should be folded into another, I would also like to hear that, since I intend to circulate the handout.
