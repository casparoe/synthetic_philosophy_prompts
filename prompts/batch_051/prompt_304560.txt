In F. H. Bradley’s *Appearance and Reality*, Chapter III, “Relation and Quality,” Bradley writes: “You can never, we may argue, find qualities without relations. Whenever you take them so, they are made so, and continue so, by an operation which itself implies relation. Their plurality gets for us all its meaning through relations; and to suppose it otherwise in reality is wholly indefensible.”

I take Bradley to be making a point about propositions. When I judge that, for example, an apple is red, I distinguish the quality *redness* from the subject *apple* and then relate them by a copula or predicative relation. There would have to be a second relation to tie that relation to the subject, a third to tie the second to the first, and so on without end. Doesn’t this show that the unity of a proposition is never actually achieved, so that ordinary declarative sentences do not express determinate thoughts but are only pragmatic ways of speaking?
