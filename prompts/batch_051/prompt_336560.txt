I've been reading Peirce for a story on how scientific communities actually change their minds, and I can't tell weather the cold fusion episode vindicates him or embarrasses him. Here's the episode as I remember it: in 1989 Pons and Fleischmann hold a press conference at the University of Utah and announce nuclear fusion in a tabletop electrochemical cell; the claim is enormous, labs everywhere try to replicate, most find neither the expected neutrons nor the excess heat, a smaller number report anomalous heat effects, and within a couple of years the mainstream has mostly closed ranks while a marginal community keeps the work alive under the label low-energy nuclear reactions. Peirce's theory of inquiry says belief gets fixed by four methods: tenacity, authority, the a priori method, and the method of science, where you hand the question to something outside your own head that doesn't go away when you stop believing. I'd like you to run the episode through that apparatus. Which actors were using which method at which points? What would a Peircean have told Pons and Fleischmann to do once the first big replication attempts came up empty, and what would they have told the mainstream to do about the handful of positive results? What would the pragmatic maxim have said the dispute was really about—what observable consequences the claim commits someone to, and whether those consequences showed up? What did the failed replications, the calorimetry fights, and the missing fusion products settle, and what did they leave open? Then run it in the opposite direction: does the episode confirm Peirce's fallibilist story about inquiry, or does it show that the method of science is hard to distinguish in practice from authority plus group tenacity? And tell me where the framework goes silent—for example, how a fallibilist community should budget its attention on a low-probability but potentially enormous claim, and how it should treat the lab that keeps insisting the anomalies are real after everyone else has gone home. I'm not after citations or a bibliography; I want the analysis itself.
