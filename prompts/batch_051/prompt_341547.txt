I'm an econ PhD student (mostly market microstructure) but lately I've been down an Austrian rabbithole, and there's a counterfactual I can't get out of my head. The Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem, which came along in the early eighties, several years after Mises died, says more or less the following: if traders are rational Bayesians and start from common priors, they won't trade with each other purely on the strength of differing private information, because the very fact that someone is willing to take the other side of a trade already reveals their signal — and once that leakage gets priced in, there's no informational gain left to capture. That seems to cut against the whole Austrian picture of markets as the institutions that mobilize dispersed knowledge through speculative trade. Mises never got to answer it. But he did draw a line in Human Action between class probability, which covers repeatable, insurable events where frequencies are meaningful, and case probability, which covers unique, non-repeatable situations where the actor just has to exercise judgment; speculation, as he saw it, lives in the second category.

So here's what I'd like from you: a carefull reconstruction of how Mises would probably have responded to the no-trade theorem if he'd lived to read it. Would he say the common-prior assumption simply builds in a kind of agreement about the structure of the world that his own theory denies, so the theorem only applies to class-probability situations and doesn't touch actual speculation? Or would he take it as a genuine challenge to the idea that speculative profit is what pulls dispersed information into prices? Could he have bit the bullet and said that real-world trade mostly runs on divergent priors — people genuinely disagreeing about how to interpret the same facts — rather than on information asymmetry in the narrow sense? I want you to work through what his position would have been, including the spots where it would come under the most strain. Please give the answer in American English.
