I'm writing up my views on the paradox of voting for a seminar on rational choice and democracy, and I want a consistency check before I circulate the document. The position I've arrived at is the following:

(1) An action is rational only if it maximizes expected utility; expected-utility reasoning is the right framework for individual choice.

(2) In any large election, the probability that my own vote is decisive—that my ballot makes or breaks a tie—is minuscule, far below the ordinary risks I act on in daily life.

(3) Taken together, (1) and (2) imply that my vote, considered purely as a causal lever on the electoral outcome, is not worth the time and inconvenience of casting it. Instrumentally, voting is irrational.

(4) Yet voting is rational, because it has expressive value: the act affirms my values, my political identity, and my standing as a member of the community, quite apart from what it causes.

(5) I also hold that citizens have a moral duty to vote, grounded in fair play. I benefit from democratic institutions whether or not I participate, and fairness requires that I bear a share of the cost of maintaining them.

(6) The outcomes of elections matter enormously. The difference between the better and the worse candidate can affect millions of lives, so the total social value at stake is vast.

(7) In an election with a clearly better candidate, one should vote for that candidate. Abstaining, or voting in a way that makes the better candidate's victory less likely, is a civic failure.

(8) I do not think appeals to what would happen if everyone did the same carry much rational weight by themselves. My vote does not cause other people to vote or abstain, so the fact that universal abstention would be disastrous does not, on its own, give me an outcome-based reason to vote.

(9) Finally, I am skeptical of treating my vote primarily as an exercise in self-expression. If a ballot makes no difference to the outcome, then voting in order to express oneself looks self-indulgent—a way of purchasing a good feeling rather than doing something with genuine civic value.

I believe these commitments hang together, but when I try to write them up as one connected view I lose track of where the joints are. Could you audit the set and tell me which commitments are in real tension with which, which pairs only appear to be in tension, and how the genuine tensions should be ranked by severity? For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be; for each merely apparent tension, say why the conflict dissolves. Finally, tell me which single revision would do the most to restore coherence across the whole set.
