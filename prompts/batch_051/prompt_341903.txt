I need a worked example for something I'm writing on commitment tactics, and I want it built to a spec rather than the usual chicken-game illustration.

Construct a thought experiment in which two parties get into a commitment race that ends with both worse off than the deal they both would have accepted at the start, but where every single commitment along the way is individually rational — in the specific sense that at each decision node, backward induction (subgame-perfect equilibrium) recommends making the commitment, given the other side's best response. Constraints: keep it in an ordinary setting, like a small-business partnership dispute or a condo board standoff — no geopolitics, no nuclear brinkmanship, no one throwing the steering wheel out the window. The parties are fully informed about each other's payoffs, neither is bluffing, and neither is angry, biased, or locked in by face-saving; the bad outcome has to come out of the structure, not the psychology. Also, make each commitment costly and only partially irreversible — not the classic irreversible gesture — and have the costs escalate from small to large across the rounds.

After the scenario, do the actual tracing: lay out the sequence of moves, and at each round show why making the commitment is the unique best response and why the other side's ratcheting response was predictable rather than a surprise. Then answer three things. First, which structural feature is really doing the work in making it a race rather than a one-off bargaining failure — frame it in terms of whether the commitments are strategic complements or substitutes. Second, what happens if you remove that feature (or flip it): does the case collapse back into an ordinary bargaining problem? Third, how would someone defend the idea that this still isnt a case of rational escalation — what would they have to say about the costs being known in advance, and about whether the parties could simply have agreed ex ante not to open the race? Be honest about where that objection bites.

The case is for a short piece arguing that "they escalated, so they must have been irrational" doesnt always go through, so if the scenario just stipulates the bad outcome rather than deriving it from the incentives, that counts as cheating.
