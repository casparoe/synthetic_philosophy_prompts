I cover law and technology for a general-interest magazine, which means I write about an awful lot of disputes that turn on a threat. I've noticed my own gut reactions follow a pattern I can't turn into a rule. The puzzle: revealing true information is legal, and asking someone for money is legal, but combine the two and a lot of cases flip to extortion — except the ones that don't.

Here's my scoreboard.

A tabloid gets photos of a state senator with a mistress and says, 'Pay me $50,000 or I publish them.' Blackmail, clearly illegal, and I think it should be.

The same tabloid just publishes the photos, no threat beforehand. I don't have an extortion problem with that. Privacy laws aside, that feels like a bad thing done at arm's length, not a shakedown.

A union says it will strike unless wages go up. Fine.

A consumer group says it will run a naming-and-shaming ad campaign unless a factory fixes its labor abuses. Fine.

The same consumer group says it will run the ads unless the factory signs a $100,000 consulting contract whose unspoken purpose is to make the ads go away. That feels like blackmail.

A homeowner tells his neighbor: remove the fence that blocks my view or I'll report your unpermitted renovation to the city. This feels like extortion too, even though the fence really does block his view and the renovation really is unpermitted.

An ex-employee tells his former boss: pay me the back wages you owe me or I'll report the safety violations in your warehouse. I want to call that fine, because he's only asking for what he's owed, even though the threat is the only reason he'll get paid.

An independent journalist says, 'Pay me to keep a damaging true story from coming out.' I call that blackmail, even when the story is true and even when the payment is called a subscription.

So my verdicts say that threatening to do something legal is sometimes fine and sometimes extortion, and demanding money is sometimes fine and sometimes extortion, but the combination is somehow different from either part. Is there a principle that gets all of these, or almost all of them? If some of my verdicts have to go, which ones, and is there a simpler principle that's better than a contrived one that saves every verdict? Then apply whatever principle you settle on to a case I keep going back and forth on: a security researcher finds a flaw in a company's software and says, 'Pay me to keep this private' rather than 'Pay me for the report.' Is the first version extorsion, or just a rough-edged bug bounty?
