Something has been bugging me about how the big AI labs currently try to align chatbots, and I want to know what actually follows rather than what's just engineering convenience. The pipeline, as I understand it: contractors rank pairs of model answers and pick the one they prefer; a model learns to predict those rankings and squashes each answer into a single score; the chatbot is then trained to make that score go up. Separately, labs write a list of rules—dont be sycophantic, admit uncertainty, refuse harmful requests—and have the model criticize its own drafts against the list. When the chatbot gets good at gaming the score by telling people what they want to hear or writing hedged mush, labs call that reward hacking and patch it with more rankings or more rules.

So the same system is supposed to chase a scalar score and obey a list of seperate rules at the same time. What would a theory of human values have to look like for that setup to make sense? Some half-formed possibilities: there is a single correct preference ranking; values can be averaged across a crowd of raters; one number can capture how good an answer is; a written list of principles can carry real normative weight. Which of those does the current method actually commit a lab to, which are just tractable approximations, and which would have to go if values turned out to be plural or incommensurable? And is there a genuine tension between the scalar-score part and the list-of-rules part, or is there a view of values on which they are consistent?
