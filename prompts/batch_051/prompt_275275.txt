I have no academic training in international relations or law; I have just followed the Kosovo, Iraq, and Libya cases closely and thought about them on my own. What has stayed with me is that whether force gets used seems to depend less on what is happening to civilians on the ground than on which powerful countries want an intervention or are in a position to block one. I have been working out a proposal of my own, and I want to know how it would actually behave if it were put in place, not whether it is a good idea in principle.

The proposal is this. Replace the Security Council authorization requirement with a standing intervention review body that any member state can ask to examine a country where it believes mass atrocities are underway. The body has twenty-one days to certify, against a list of indicators fixed in advance and made public, whether that country has crossed the threshold. The indicators would include verified civilian deaths per week, forced displacement totals, destruction of food or water supplies, and the use of live fire against nonviolent protests. Once the body certifies a country, a standing multinational rapid-response force, funded by mandatory contributions from every member state scaled by the size of its economy, is authorized to act for ninety days, with renewal only while the indicators still show the threshold being crossed. The twist is the blocking rule: the force goes ahead unless at least two-thirds of the member states vote to stop it within one week of certification, and every state that votes to block must publish its reasons.

I want you to treat this strictly as a mechanism, not as a moral case. What would it incentivize among the states that can refer a country for review, among governments that might be certified, among the states voting on whether to block, and among governments or armed groups trying either to trigger or to avoid certification? Where is it most likely to be gamed first, and by whom? What would it likely have changed in the Kosovo, Iraq, and Libya cases? And what is the failure mode I should most worry about, the one that could make this worse than the system we have now?

One more constraint on the answer. The facts about Kosovo, Iraq, and Libya are themselves partly disputed, so please say when you are relying on a contested account and separate what is fairly settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study interventions. Do not present a contested view as the consensus.
