I've been turning over a little argument and I want to know whether it goes through, and if not, which step exactly is the one that gives out. It goes like this:

(1) A choice between two options can be rational only if the options can be ranked: one is better than the other, one is worse, or they're exactly equal.
(2) Incommensurable values are values that can't be measured on a single common scale.
(3) If two options can't be measured on a single common scale, they can't be ranked as better, worse, or equal.
(4) So a choice between options that instantiate incommensurable values is a choice between options that can't be ranked.
(5) Genuinely hard choices—staying in the city for the job versus moving home to care for an aging parent, that kind of thing—just are choices between incommensurable values.
(6) Therefore, in genuinely hard choices, no rational choice is possible.

I keep having the feeling that (3) is doing something slippy, like "incommensurable" is doing one job in (2) and another job in (3), but I can't quite pin it down. Is the argument valid? If it isn't, which step fails, and what would a corrected version of that step have to say?
