Suppose a city-state decides to organize its entire public historical culture around a single principle: only monumental history may be practiced, taught, or publicly displayed. By monumental history I mean the selective narration of past figures and events as simplified models of greatness, intended to inspire present action. Antiquarian history, understood as preserving and revering the inherited past for its own sake down to minor customs and details, and critical history, understood as subjecting the inherited past to judgment and breaking its authority so that a people can free itself for new life, are both prohibited; private practitioners are denied public funding, and commemoration outside the approved monumental canon is taxed. Treat this as an institution rather than a moral position, and analyze how it would actually behave. What kind of civic memory and public temperament would it produce over a generation or two? What incentives does it create for historians, teachers, politicians, and ordinary citizens, and how would each group game or quietly circumvent the restriction? Where is the system most likely to fail—in the choice of exemplars, in the simplification their stories require, in public trust, in the accumulating past that can no longer be shaped into usable models, or elsewhere—and are those failures repairable from inside the system, or do they eventually force the reintroduction of the prohibited modes?
