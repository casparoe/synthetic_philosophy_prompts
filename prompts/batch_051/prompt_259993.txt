I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about why "lost civilisation" and "ancient aliens" documentaries keep coming back. Last week I recorded a conversation with two people I respect a lot: a historian who works on fringe archaeology, and a sociologist of science who studies how disciplines police their boundaries. They got into a proper argument, and I've transcribed the relevant bit below. I genuinely don't know whose side I'm on, so please don't guess which one I'd like to win — I want you to referee it on the merits.

You're a professional philosopher of science who works on demarcation problems (how to separate science and legitimate historical revision from pseudoscience and pseudohistory). Think carefully, and before you give your verdict, brainstorm the main candidate criteria for drawing that line and note which ones are doing real work in this exchange. Then go through the argument move by move.

Here's the exchange, with moves numbered as I noted them:

(1) Historian: I'd start with falsifiability. A claim about the past is pseudohistory when no concievable discovery could count against it. Real historical work takes risks with evidence; pseudohistory arranges itself so that every single find confirms it.

(2) Sociologist: That's much too clean. Any half-competent author can make a claim falsifiable on paper by adding clauses — "Atlantis will be found, or it was destroyed without a trace, or it's under the ice" — and mainstream history is full of untestable details anyway. Nobody can falsify what a Bronze Age merchant had for lunch. So your test, applied seriously, either throws out enormous amounts of ordinary history or fails to catch what you say you want to catch.

(3) Historian: The lunch example is a red herring. Untestable micro-details aren't the issue; the issue is how a theory responds when evidence does show up. Pseudohistory absorbs contrary evidence by adding auxiliary hypotheses — epicycles. Real history, when a new dig contradicts it, revises the claim or drops it. That's the real asymmetry.

(4) Sociologist: But that asymmetry doesn't separate fringe from mainstream; it separates a theory under pressure from a theory that's doing fine. Every serious research programme adds auxiliary hypotheses when anomalies appear. Continental drift did that for decades before plate tectonics reorganised the whole thing. If adding epicycles is the mark of a crank, half the history of science was cranky.

(5) Historian: Then I want to look at what the epicycles cost. Wegener's additions eventually connected to independent lines of evidence and made the theory simpler and more predictive. Fringe theories move the opposite way: each new auxiliary hypothesis makes the story more complicated, more isolated from everything else, and less able to say anything specific in advance. Pseudohistory is all postdiction and no prediction.

(6) Sociologist: But "cost" and "simpler" are aesthetic judgements, and "independent lines of evidence" just means "the lines of evidence the current disciplines already respect." Continental drift was treated as isolated nonsense for a long time precisely because it didn't connect to the physics of how continents could move. By your criteria, a good theory can look pseudohistorical right up until it wins. And the winners get to write the story of why they were right all along.

(7) Historian: I'll grant you the point about winners. But if you give up on epistemic criteria entirely, you seem stuck saying there's no difference in kind between a careful reconstruction of Bronze Age trade routes and a twelve-part series about Atlantis building the pyramids. That's a high price to pay. Ordinary readers will hear it as "both sides are equally legitimate."

(8) Sociologist: I'm not saying they're equally legitimate. I'm saying the difference isn't a litmus test you can read off the hypothesis. It's procedural: source criticism, peer review, replicability of the argument, and a general conservatism about overturning consensus. Legitimate revisionists work within those institutions and earn trust over time. Cranks don't. The difference lives in the practice, not in a rule about falsifiability.

Please go through each numbered move seperately and say who is right about what, step by step. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and who is more right overall — or whether the argument as they've had it is simply unresolved. Please answer in British English (e.g., 'analyse', 'colour', 'programme'), and don't include a bibliography or reading list — I want the reasoning, not sources.
