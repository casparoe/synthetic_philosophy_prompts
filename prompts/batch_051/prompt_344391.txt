I am a retired evolutionary biologist, and in the years since I left the laboratory I have drifted into philosophy and joined a reading group that has been working through metaethics. I have been asked to write down my present commitments so the group can examine them, and I have discovered that I am not sure where the strain in the set is real and where it is merely apparent. Here is what I believe.

I accept the is–ought gap. I take it to be a point about deductive inference: from premises that are entirely descriptive, no conclusion about what one ought to do follows unless at least one normative premise is already in play. I regard this as a logical point rather than a claim about subject matter.

I am also persuaded by the open question argument, and I take it to establish that good is not identical with any natural property. However one proposes to define good in natural terms — pleasure, desire satisfaction, whatever empirical science could describe — the question whether that natural thing is good still seems to me genuinely open, in a way that should not be possible if the definition were correct.

At the same time, I am a naturalist. I believe the natural world is all there is and that whatever exists is in principle accessible to scientific inquiry. I also believe there are objective moral truths, and since I cannot see room for anything outside the natural world, I conclude that these truths must themselves be natural facts.

As a biologist, I also believe that human moral judgment is a product of natural selection, and that a great deal of the content of our moral sentiments — cooperation, loyalty, indignation at free riding, revulsion at certain harms — can be explained by our evolutionary history. I do not think this debunks those judgments. I think the evolutionary origins of our moral sense are compatible with its reliability.

I hold an etiological view of biological function: the function of a trait is the effect for which it was selected. And I am inclined to say that such proper functions can carry normative weight — that the proper function of a heart, for instance, is relevant to judgments about health and malfunction, and that the naturalistic fallacy need not block a move from a claim about proper function to a claim about how an organism or organ ought to operate. I realize this is a step beyond plain biology, but I find it difficult to surrender.

I am also suspicious of the idea that being natural is, by itself, a moral recommendation. I do not think that what is natural is thereby good, or that what is unnatural is thereby bad. I treat the appeal to nature in ethics as a mistake.

Finally, I believe that well-being is what ultimately matters morally, and that well-being is a complex natural property of organisms — something like flourishing in the circumstances of a life — which could in principle be studied empirically.

I would be grateful for an audit of this set. Where is the genuine tension, and where is the apparent tension that dissolves on inspection? For the genuine tensions, order them by severity and say in each case what would have to give and what the cheapest fix would be. Then give an overall judgment: which single revision of the set would remove the most strain, and what would it cost me?

Please write your answer in continuous prose only — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — the way one would write an email or a letter.
