I am a novelist, and I am writing a science fiction novel whose central speculative premise is an answer to the Fermi paradox: the tension between the apparent likelihood that technological civilizations would arise many times over in our galaxy and the fact that we detect no sign of them. I have assembled what I take to be the standard families of answer, and I am refusing to use any of them, because my readers would recognize them at once. This is my list:

1. The rare-life answer: the chain from prebiotic chemistry to a technological civilization contains at least one step so improbable that we should expect to be alone, or nearly alone, in the observable universe.
2. The great-filter answer: some step in that chain is not so much improbable as nearly always fatal or permanently blocked; if the dangerous step lies behind us, we are rare, and if it lies ahead of us, we are probably doomed.
3. The self-destruction answer: technological civilizations reliably annihilate themselves, or are annihilated by their own technology, soon after acquiring the capacity to signal or to travel.
4. The infeasibility answer: interstellar travel or detectable interstellar signaling is in practice impossible—energetically, biologically, or economically—so nobody spreads out and nobody chatters.
5. The silence-by-choice answer: advanced civilizations converge on quiet, whether out of prudence about predators, because they upload or turn inward, or because they simply lose interest in cosmic visibility.
6. The timing answer: civilizations are common across the life of the galaxy but seldom overlap in time, and their detectable phases are brief; we may simply be early, late, or unlucky in the arrangement of epochs.
7. The search-space answer: civilizations are signaling now, but in modalities or at powers we cannot yet detect, and we have sampled only a negligible corner of the possible search space.
8. The zoo-or-quarantine answer: we are deliberately left unaware by observers who treat Earth as a preserve, an experiment, a kindergarten, or a quarantine.
9. The simulation answer: the silence is an artifact of our being inside a simulation that contains no other civilizations.

I believe this list already contains everything standard; the novel fails at the premise level if a reader can file the answer under one of these headings.

What I need is one further candidate resolution to the Fermi paradox that is not on this list and is not a rewording, special case, or combination of items already on it. It should be different in structure from all of them, coherent enough to carry a novel's premise, and it should actually dissolve the tension rather than merely denying one of the two horns. After you propose it, tell me honestly whether it is any good: does it explain the silence in a way that survives obvious objections, and does it rest on assumptions more contrived than the ones on my list? Then close by telling me how confident you are in your overall appraisal, and identify the single point you are least sure of—whether a factual premise, an inference, or a judgment of novelty.
