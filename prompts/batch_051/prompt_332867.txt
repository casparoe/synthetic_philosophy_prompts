I have been teaching Christopher Browning's study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 and the ordinary-men thesis he draws from it, and I have become unsure what remains of the argument now that its social-psychological underpinning looks less secure than it did when the book was written. As I understand it, Browning's argument runs roughly as follows. The men who carried out the killings were not, for the most part, preexisting fanatics or sadists; they were unremarkable middle-aged men placed in a specific situation—orders from legitimate authority, unit solidarity, peer pressure, the escalating character of their tasks, and the difficulty of asking to be excused—and it was the situation that turned them into perpetrators. This is meant to undercut the rival thesis that an entrenched eliminationist culture and character drove ordinary Germans to kill. The argument leans on the experimental literature for a crucial step: without the Milgram obedience studies and the Stanford prison experiment, it is hard to move from the particular behavior of these men to the general claim that ordinary people, under pressures of that kind, will do much the same.

That experimental picture has since come under sustained criticism. The original protocols and the inferences drawn from them have been reanalyzed, and the broader replication crisis has made claims about the overwhelming power of situations considerably less secure than they once looked. Let us grant, for the sake of argument, that the experimental support for the situationist premise is now much weaker than Browning assumed.

What should happen to the ordinary-men argument? I am not asking for a survey of the historical literature; I want an analysis of the argument's structure. Which premise does the erosion of situationist social psychology actually attack, and which steps of Browning's case does it leave standing? Can the conclusion be rebuilt from the battalion's own record—for instance, the fact that the men were offered the chance to step out and most did not at first, or the absence of evidence that these were already hardened killers—or does the argument become an empirical claim that has lost its main support? Does Goldhagen's culturalist explanation become comparatively stronger once the situationist alternative loses experimental authority, or does it inherit equal or worse problems under the same scrutiny? In short: does Browning's argument survive the replication crisis, and in what form—intact, in need of repair, or abandoned?
