Think of Dennett's digger wasp: it carries prey to its burrow, leaves it at the entrance, goes inside to check, comes back out, and then drags it in. If you move the prey an inch while it's inside, it doesn't notice the check is already done — the moved prey is a fresh cue, so it does the whole inside-check again, and you can loop it forever by moving the prey each time. Call an agent "sphexish" to the extent its behavior runs on such cue-triggered routines rather than on a representation of what the routine is for. Dennett's point is that sphexishness isn't the same as observable rigidity: it's about whether the agent tracks the reason behind its current step. I want a pair of cases that force that apart. Case A: a person who improvises a lot, varies their moves, and looks flexible, but is sphexish on Dennett's criterion. Case B: a person who repeats the exact same routine every day, punch-clock rigid, but is not sphexish. Constraints: both work in the same ordinary institution (hospital, warehouse, whatever), no sci-fi, no impossible knowledge, and don't just stipulate hidden inner states; the difference has to show up in what each would do in nearby counterfactual cases. Keep each case to about a paragraph. Then run the check for each: state the trigger, what the agent represents (or fails to represent), and why the cue-versus-reason criterion gives the verdict it does. Say which feature of the cases is doing the asymmetry and what happens if you remove it. Finish with how a critic who equates sphexishness with behavioral rigidity would attack your verdicts, and how you'd answer. Start your reply with Case A, no preamble.
