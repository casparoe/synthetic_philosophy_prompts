I teach a mixed upper-level seminar on reasoning under uncertainty, with about twenty-two students drawn from philosophy, economics, and statistics; we meet once for seventy-five minutes. The students can already update on evidence with Bayes' rule in toy problems, but they regard persistent, honest disagreement between reasonable people as the normal, fully rational state of affairs, and they have never seen a formal constraint on how much two rational agents may disagree.

Design a single-session classroom activity — a game with rules and a payoff scheme that uses points or tokens rather than real money — that makes them feel the force of the common prior assumption: the principle that two ideally rational agents with the same background information must begin with the same prior probabilities. The activity should be built so that the associated formal result emerges from what happens rather than from any preliminary reading or lecture, namely that Bayesians who share a prior and whose individual posterior probabilities are common knowledge cannot agree to disagree, so persistent public disagreement between them must be explainable by something other than their private evidence alone.

I want the session to unfold in three phases, with the terms common prior and Aumann's agreement theorem first named only in the debrief. Phase one should give different students different private signals or roles, so that they form genuinely divergent probability judgments and find their own judgment reasonable. Phase two should introduce a mechanism — a market, an auction, a posted-odds bookmaker, or a forced public justification — that either drives the divergences into convergence or exposes the remaining disagreement to a risk-free arbitrage, so that unresolved disagreement becomes visibly costly rather than merely permitted. Phase three should rebuild the theory from what the students experienced: prior, private information, and posterior; what changed when the disagreements became public; the role of common priors; and the exact point at which the assumption did the work. By the end of the session the students should see that rejecting common priors is not a modest adjustment but a substantive commitment that licenses indefinitely persistent rational disagreement, and they should be able to say what such a world would look like inside the game they just played.

Please provide: complete rules and materials; the exact payoff structure; how to divide the room, pair the students, and track beliefs across phases; a seventy-five-minute timeline marked to each phase and transition; what the facilitator says and does not say at each point, and where the technical vocabulary is first introduced; a debrief outline that maps specific moments in the game onto specific parts of the theorem; the three most likely ways the activity goes wrong on a first run and how to recover from each; and an honest note on what a game cannot reproduce — in particular, I doubt a game can make students actually occupy two different priors rather than watch two stipulated priors doing work, so say where the facilitator must supply that by hand and what the activity should not be expected to settle.
