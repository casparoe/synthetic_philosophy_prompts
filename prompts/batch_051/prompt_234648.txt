i want to actually audit the analogy instead of just gesturing at it. treat the circle as a picture that's doing real argumentative work: give me the correspondence table — what in morality plays the circle, the center, the radius, the boundary line, the act of redrawing it, the area inside — and then the places where the mapping breaks. i can allready spot a few: a circle has a fixed center, it expands uniformly in all directions, the boundary stays sharp the whole time, and redrawing a bigger circle leaves no trace of who was outside before. are those just harmless features of the image, or do they smuggle in substantive claims about how moral inclusion actually works?

then the actual verdict: once the disanalogies are on the table, does her conclusion survive — that inclusion in the moral circle is a one-way ratchet and the direction of progress is basically fixed, even if the speed isn't — or was the circle doing all the lifting? and if the circle is a bad map, what's a better one that keeps the idea of expanding who counts without the parts that broke?
