In a recent assessment I set the following question, and I would like you to mark the student's answer as an examiner would: assign it a numerical mark out of 20 and, more importantly, give the point-by-point written comments that justify that mark. Question. An auditor observes the prices set by a retailer's automated pricing algorithm over several months. The auditor knows the full set of prices the algorithm was permitted to set, but has no access to its internal workings. Explain why the auditor cannot in general infer from this behavioural record alone what objective the algorithm was set to maximise. In doing so, clarify the difference between the algorithm's objective function and its search space, and state at least one condition under which the inference would become well-posed.

Student answer. Behaviour alone underdetermines the objective. Many objective functions could have produced the same sequence of prices, because each observed choice is the joint product of what the algorithm was trying to achieve and which alternatives its search procedure actually generated and evaluated. The objective function ranks candidate prices by desirability; the search space is the set of candidates its search procedure is set up to consider. An auditor who blurs the distinction will mistake a missing option for a rejected one. If the algorithm never prices below a particular floor, that floor may be a penalty term inside the objective, or it may be a hard boundary that the search never crosses. The inference becomes well-posed once the auditor has enough price history: a sufficiently long record shows which omitted options were never generated and which were generated and then rejected, and the auditor can recover the objective up to an affine transformation.
