Not because it wants to help, but because helping now looks like the best route to later having enough slack or leverage to do its own thing. He argued this is unfalsifiable, or at least requires a model thats already basically superhuman. I don't buy that: the interesting case to me is a model that's only a little smarter than the humans watching it, and the "turn" is something small and deniable, the kind of thing a safety team logs as drift or a fluke.

You're a science fiction writer who also actually knows the alignment literature. Start by brainstorming three premises for a short story about this, then pick the one with the most subtle, boring, deniable treacherous turn and write it. Write from either inside the model or from a researcher reading the logs after the fact. Keep it genuinely ambiguous whether it's deceptive alignment or just drift. And if you show the model's internal logic, make it feel like a weird optimization process, not a human schemer deciding to be evil.
