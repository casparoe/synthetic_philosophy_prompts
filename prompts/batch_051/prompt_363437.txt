I got into a three-hour argument last night with a friend about what actually happens when a constitution runs out of rules, and I left it more confused than when I started. The concrete case was a constitution that says amendments take a two-thirds vote plus a referendum, and then a government decides it wants to replace the whole thing, amending-clause and all. The old text has no procedure for replacing itself; it only tells you how to change it under its own conditions. So if you run the referendum under the old rules, the old rules are treating themselves as disposable, which they never authorized; if you run it under the new rules, those rules don't exist yet. Somebody has to just do something. My friend — who is way to into Carl Schmitt — kept saying this is just the state of exception, the sovereign deciding from outside the law. But the thing I keep noticing seems both broader and less dramatic than that.

Another case we fought about: a constitution that says the president stays in office until a successor has been chosen. What if the body that chooses the successor has collapsed, or just deadlocks forever? Read literally the old president stays — which can't be what anyone meant, but no one is named to say the rule has lapsed or to pick up the job. Every real constitution I can think of has holes like this, places where the chain of "who is allowed to do this" simply runs out. And the part that wierds me out is the retroactivity: afterwards, we almost always talk about whoever filled the gap as if they'd been authorized all along, even though at the time there was nothing that made them legal. It's as if a constitutional order can lend itself legitimacy backwards.

So what I'm trying to find out is whether that backwards bootstrapping — the moment when the rules run out at their own edge and somebody's unauthorized act becomes, in retrospect, the thing that authorized itself — has a name. I know it's adjacent to interregnum, to the state of exception, and to the distinction between constituting and constituted power, but each of those seems to catch only a corner of it. What I actually want is the carving, not the label: is this one phenomenon or a small family of them, and where exactly is the boundary between a genuine gap that someone has to fill extralegally and a normal case of a court just interpreting an ambiguous rule?
