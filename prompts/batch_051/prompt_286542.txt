I teach epistemology and am currently advising a strong PhD student who wants to spend the next three years on the value problem, the puzzle that runs through the discussion in Plato's Meno and remains open in contemporary epistemology. The puzzle can be put as follows. Suppose knowledge is analyzed as true belief plus some further condition, such as justification, reliability, safety, or aptness. It is then unclear why the resulting composite should be more valuable than true belief alone, or more valuable than true belief combined with that further condition taken separately. Socrates proposes in the Meno that true beliefs are worth little until they are bound down by reasoning about the explanation, but it is hard to say why the binding, or the bound state, should have a value that mere true opinion lacks.

I am not asking for a survey of the literature or a list of authors. I am asking for a defended research agenda. Treat this as the assignment. Rank the main open questions in this area for a philosopher with roughly three years, training in conceptual analysis, and no special background in formal methods or experimental work. The list below is a starting point; you may revise, merge, or replace its items as you see fit.

1. Is the value problem one problem or several? At least three questions are often run together: why knowledge is better than true belief; why knowledge is better than any of its proper parts; and why knowledge, unlike mere true belief, is what a person may properly assert or rely on in practical reasoning. If these questions come apart, which is prior, and which are downstream of it?

2. Does the problem depend on a questionable assumption about how value composes? The standard puzzle assumes that the value of a complex state should be visible in the value of its parts, so that adding a worthless part cannot produce a more valuable whole. But if value can be holistic or emergent here, the problem needs to be posed differently. How could one show that without making the move ad hoc?

3. Should the datum itself be denied? Is knowledge, on reflection, more valuable than true belief, or is the intuition an artifact of the particular contrast cases used to elicit it? If the intuition is stable only in some practical or social contexts, which ones are they, and how much of the contemporary debate would need to be redrawn?

4. Is the norm-of-assertion approach a solution or a postponement? If knowledge is the norm of assertion and practical reasoning, then knowledge has a practical value that mere true belief lacks. Does that explain the epistemic value of knowledge, or does it merely explain why we care whether someone knows?

5. Do credit and virtue-theoretic accounts solve the problem or overgeneralize? Such accounts hold that a knowing success is valuable because it is creditable to the knower's exercise of ability. Does this yield the right kind of value, and can it handle testimonial knowledge without reducing it to a mere special case of true belief?

6. Is the unit of analysis wrong? Perhaps the debate should not be about the value of an individual's mental state at all, but about the value of knowledge ascriptions in a community of inquirers, where they license trust and divide epistemic labor. If that is right, is the resulting account still a solution to the original problem or a replacement for it?

7. What, if anything, should formal epistemology contribute? Are there results in epistemic logic or decision theory that would sharpen one of the questions above, or expose it as ill-posed, that a conceptually trained student could realistically acquire?

For each question in your ordering, say which criteria you are applying and how you weigh them: importance, tractability, neglectedness, and the degree to which other questions depend on it. In particular, I want a clear answer to these four things:

(i) Which question is most upstream, in the sense that real progress on it would reframe, dissolve, or accelerate several of the others?

(ii) Which question is most tractable with conceptual tools alone over three years, and why?

(iii) Which question would you deprioritize even though it is a genuine question, because the student could not realistically settle it or because settling it would not change much elsewhere?

(iv) What evidence or result over the next two years would most lead you to promote or demote an item in your ranking?

Please write your answer in American English.
