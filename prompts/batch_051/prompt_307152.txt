A close friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about whether the small commitments of everyday life — keeping appointments, answering when one says one will, returning borrowed things on time — are genuine moral matters or merely matters of etiquette and convenience. He defended what he called the coordination view. On that view, a small promise has whatever moral force it has only through the practical reliance the other person places on it: if I say I will meet you at seven and you organize your evening around that, then leaving you waiting is wrong because it wastes your time and treats your planning as disposable. But when no one actually relies on the promise, or when people have already adjusted their expectations to your unreliability, he thinks there is nothing left to be wronged about; in his phrase, reproaching such failures is "scrupulosity dressed up as ethics."

I resisted this, but the argument exposed that my own verdicts were less coherent than I would like. I want to know whether they can be brought under a single principle. Here they are:

1. I agreed to meet a close friend for dinner at seven. Nothing urgent delayed me; I simply left home too late, and she waited outside the restaurant for twenty minutes. I take this to be clearly wrong.

2. A friend asked me to email her a recipe by Sunday evening because she planned to cook it on Monday. I said I would, forgot, and sent it on Tuesday with an apology. I take this to be wrong, and I do not think forgetting fully excuses it, since I could easily have set a reminder.

3. I promised to bring napkins to a picnic and then forgot. Another guest bought napkins on the way, so the picnic was unaffected and no one was inconvenienced. I still think I did something wrong, though only mildly.

4. A new acquaintance invited me to a party. I said I would try to make it while already expecting that I probably would not go. He said there was no pressure. I did not go. I do not think I did anything wrong.

5. I have a friend, Sam, whom I have kept waiting many times. He now expects me to run ten or fifteen minutes late, gives me artificially early times, and waits in his car rather than outside. When I am late now, I no longer feel I am wronging Sam in the way I once did; and yet I also feel that my unreliability itself is a defect, not merely an inconvenience to him.

6. I borrowed a neighbor’s toolkit and promised to return it the next weekend. I kept it for six weeks without mentioning it. He never asked for it and seemed not to need it; when I finally returned it, he waved it off. I think this was wrong, and more than mildly.

My friend’s principle accounts for 1, 2, and 5 quite well, and it handles 4 by saying no promise was really made. But it seems forced to conclude that 3 and 6 were not wrong at all, since no one relied on me. I am not prepared to give up 3 and 6 just to preserve the principle. Are my verdicts inconsistent? Is there a principle, or a small family of candidate principles, that captures most of them and explains where my friend and I actually disagree? And what would the best reconstruction of my view say about a person who habitually makes small promises without intending to keep them, but who never inconveniences anyone because, by now, no one relies on him at all?
