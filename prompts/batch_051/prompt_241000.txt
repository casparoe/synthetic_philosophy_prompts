I’m putting together a short unit on consent and community, and I want a case built to test a classic liberal principle rather than the usual stock examples. John Locke, in the Preface to the Second Treatise of Government, writes: “to make good his title, in the consent of the people, which being the only one of all lawful governments.” That sentence makes consent the sole basis of lawful government. The communitarian objection I want to stress-test against it is that most people never perform anything like a consenting act; they are born into political memberships they never chose, and the liberal story depends on a right of exit that is often legally real but socially costly.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. The case must be realistic, set in a small, cohesive immigrant religious community inside a liberal democracy, and no longer than one paragraph. In the case, a communitarian critic must end up appealing to an individual’s actual decision to stay or leave more heavily than a liberal consent theorist does, and the liberal must end up relying on inherited membership as doing more normative work than consent. The communitarian’s position must not rest on any romantic value of belonging, tradition, or cultural preservation; it should arise from observable social pressures. The liberal’s exit option must be legally genuine and widely known, not a sham. After the scenario, walk through what each side would say and which verdict each theory reaches. Then identify the feature of the case doing the work, say what happens if it is removed, and explain how a defender of the side that comes out worse would attack the case. Please answer in American English.
