I am working on a paper about whether the standard descriptivist case against the very idea of bad grammar still works now that large language models are the main technology through which people's writing gets corrected. The classic case runs roughly as follows: every natural language variety is rule-governed and capable of expressing what its speakers need, so no variety is linguistically defective; prescriptive rules track social prestige rather than linguistic fact; therefore calling a construction bad grammar is at best a disguised social judgment. Now suppose the dominant channel of grammatical correction is an LLM trained overwhelmingly on standard written English, which quietly rewrites nonstandard forms into a prestige standard at a scale and consistency that no earlier prescriptivism could achieve. Does the descriptivist argument survive this development, does it need repair, or does it simply stop applying? I am interested in the fate of the argument itself, not in the policy question of whether such correction is desirable.

Write the response as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. The five items should jointly identify which premise is under pressure, state whether the argument survives and if so in what repaired form, and illustrate the point with at least one concrete stigmatized construction such as double negation or habitual be.
