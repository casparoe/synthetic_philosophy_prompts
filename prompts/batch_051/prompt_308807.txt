I've been trying to decide whether an analogy I keep returning to is deep or merely a surface resemblance. In economics, trade between two people with different preferences can make both better off without either changing their preferences: each gives up something they value less for something they value more. It seems to me that moral compromise ought to work the same way. Suppose I believe, on moral grounds, that a town should fund a clinic and I care only mildly about whether it also builds a park, while you have the opposite weighting, also on moral grounds. Each of us should be able to concede on our low-stakes issue in exchange for the other's concession on the high-stakes one, leaving us both better off in our own moral terms than if we had each fought for everything. That would be a kind of gains from trade in the space of values.

Yet real moral compromise rarely feels like this. It often feels like losing, or like being morally compromised, even when the deal gives both sides more of the outcomes they claim to care about most.

I would like a committed verdict rather than a list of considerations. Is the gains-from-trade picture the right way to understand moral compromise between rival value systems, or is the analogy superficial? State the mapping precisely, say where it holds and where it breaks, and point to something the economics side would predict — if the parallel were a genuine identity of structure — that turns out false when applied to actual moral compromise.
