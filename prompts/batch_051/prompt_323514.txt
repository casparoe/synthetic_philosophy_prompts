In a debate about whether a hereditary head of state can be reconciled with democracy, my interlocutor kept answering every objection with the same line: the monarch reigns but does not rule. He was using the slogan to do two distinct jobs at once — first, to establish that the monarch has no real political power, and second, to establish that whatever ceremonial role remains is democratically harmless. I suspect the sentence is bearing more weight than it can. I would like you to disassemble it. What are the different precise claims that "the monarch reigns but does not rule" could be used to assert? Please lay them out as a menu, covering at least: a purely ceremonial reading (the office involves only ritual and pageantry); a formal-powers reading (the monarch retains legal prerogatives — appointing a prime minister, assenting to legislation, dissolving a parliament — that are in practice never exercised independently); an influence reading (the crown has regular access to government business and an advisory voice whose effect comes through deference rather than decree); and a symbolic reading (the monarchy's real work is to personify and stabilize the state, which is itself a form of political significance). For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but contestable constitutional claim, or something false or misleading, and say which reading people repeating the slogan are most likely relying on. Then tell me which of these readings, if any, actually deliver the two conclusions he was using it to draw. Where it helps, you may distinguish how the slogan fares in different constitutional monarchies — the United Kingdom, the Scandinavian kingdoms, Japan — but keep the focus on what the sentence itself could mean.
