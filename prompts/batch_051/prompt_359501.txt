In my moral philosophy seminar we've been reading about deontological side-constraints, the idea that certain acts are forbidden for you even if doing them would prevent more people from doing that very act. A friend in the class offered an argument that he thinks shows the view collapses under its own weight, and I've been turning it over since. Here's the argument as he gave it:

(1) If murder is wrong, then the existence of murders is a bad thing: other things equal, a world with fewer murders is better in at least one respect. (2) If the existence of murders is a bad thing, then any agent has reason to act so as to bring about fewer murders. (3) Sometimes the only way for an agent to bring about fewer murders is to commit one murder herself, e.g., killing the person who has hired a group of hitmen when there is no other way to stop the killings. (4) So in such a case the very wrongness of murder gives her a reason to commit murder. (5) But the side-constraint says murder is forbidden for her, even when committing it would stop more murders by other people. (6) A moral view that grounds a prohibition in a certain property (the wrongness of murder), and then uses that same property to recommend violating the prohibition, is incoherent. (7) So deontology should give up side-constraints.

I want to resist the conclusion, but I can't tell which premise is doing the damage or what it would cost to reject it. If the argument is valid, then I'd like to know which premise is the least expensive to abandon, because I'd rather not end up saying that nothing is bad about murders happening as long as I'm not the one doing them.
