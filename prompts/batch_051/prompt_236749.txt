I have been trying to make sense of a feature of criminal law that legal theorists sometimes call the paradox of blackmail, and it strikes me as a question about how wrongs can be built out of rights rather than a mere technical quirk. Consider two things that are, taken separately, lawful. First, a person may in many cases lawfully disclose true, lawfully acquired information about someone else, even where the disclosure would humiliate the subject or damage the subject's reputation; in the core cases I have in mind, the disclosure would not be defamation, an invasion of privacy, or a breach of confidence—it would be the kind of true story a newspaper could publish with impunity. Second, a person may lawfully ask someone else for money: as a gift, as a loan, as an investment, or as the price of future cooperation. Yet when these two lawful acts are fused into a single conditional proposal—"pay me and I will keep quiet; refuse and I will disclose what I know"—the law no longer treats the result as the sum of its parts. It treats it as blackmail: a serious criminal offense, often classed with extortion, carrying heavy penalties.

This asymmetry ought to be embarrassing. We would not accept an explanation of a chemical reaction according to which two harmless compounds turn into a poison only when mixed, without being told what the poison is and why the mixing produces it; yet the law seems comfortable treating two independently lawful acts as criminal once they are combined, without any obvious account of what the additional wrong consists in. I want to know whether any principle can justify this practice. Please state the strongest candidate principle that permits each component act taken alone while forbidding their combination, apply it to nearby cases—such as selling a true story to a newspaper for publication, accepting payment to suppress a story, settling a threatened defamation suit, threatening to expose a crime, and threatening to disclose true information whose disclosure would itself be unlawful as a breach of confidence—and then give an honest verdict on whether the law's treatment of blackmail should be kept as it is, narrowed, extended, or abandoned as inconsistent.
