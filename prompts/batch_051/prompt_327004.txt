I'm a novelist, and for a book I'm writing I've been reading the whole-brain emulation stuff, and there's a clash between the old thought experiments and the way the engineers actually propose to do the thing. I want you to tell me whether the old argument survives the change, or whether it needs to be retired or rebuilt.

Here's the old argument, as it usually goes, for thinking an emulation would be the same mind. Replace your neurons one at a time with artificial units that do exactly the same signal-processing job. You stay awake and talking the whole while; nothing feels different; by the end your skull is full of replacement parts but the same mind has continued step by step. From this people conclude that what matters for the mind is the functional organization, not the biological stuff: build something with the same functional organization and you've preserved the mind.

The trouble is, the actual path to whole-brain emulation doesn't look like that at all. The proposed pipelines preserve a brain, slice it destructively into thin sections to map the connections, and only later boot up a simulation from that static structural snapshot. The original brain is gone before the emulation first runs. There's no stage where a living brain is gradually handed off to the hardware; there's a gap, and the emulation is a reconstruction of a fixed structure, not a replacement of an ongiong process.

So my question: does the gradual-replacement argument survive this development? If you think yes, I want the exact step that lets an argument about continuous in-place replacement license a destructive scan-and-reboot. If you think no, name the premise the new procedure undercuts, and say whether a weaker claim can be salvaged from the wreckage — for instance, that the upload has the same mental contents but not the same mind, or that it counts as a successor rather than a continuation. Don't bother with citations or a reading list; I care about the reasoning.

Please end with exactly one machine-readable line: \answer{survives}, \answer{needs-repair}, or \answer{dead}.
