hey, i've been chewing on an essay about the line between a moral preference and a bias, and how the line does a lot less work than people pretend. The draft's below. I'd love comments before I revise — I'm less interested in copy-editing and more in whether the argument survives a few obvious pushes. And please write the comments as plain continuous prose, like an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, none of that.

Here's the draft.

Start with a standoff. One person thinks it's fine to hire a less-qualified relative over a better-qualified stranger. The other says that's just nepotism, a bias. The first person fires back that the second has a bias toward strangers and abstract fairness, against the claims of family. Each regards their own view as a value and the other's as a bias. The question is whether there is any neutral way to decide which one is right about that, or whether 'bias' is just the name we give to a moral view we've already decided to reject.

I think there is no neutral way, and the essay argues that by going through the main candidate tests for what makes something a bias rather than a preference.

The first candidate is an origin test. A bias, on this story, is an attitude whose shape was produced by a process that doesn't track moral reasons: conformity, self-interest, in-group loyalty, or some heuristic that misfired. A genuine value, in contrast, tracks the reasons. The problem is that almost all our values, including the ones we're most confident in, have the same kind of origins. People absorb egalitarianism from parents and peers exactly as they absorb nepotism. And the origin is anyway the wrong thing to lean on: a view is not discredited just because of how someone arrived at it, any more than it's credited by a good origin. So the origin test overreaches; it would demote nearly everything, and it answers the wrong question.

The second candidate is a content test. Biases respond to morally irrelevant properties, such as skin color or kinship; preferences respond to relevant ones. But this just moves the fight. Saying kinship is irrelevant to hiring is the very claim the nepotist rejects, so the charge of bias presupposes the substantive view it's meant to establish. Nor do the examples help: some canonical biases are biases of degree rather than kind, like giving too much weight to a candidate's accent rather than any weight at all. And some of the partialities people most want to protect are partialities toward properties that look irrelevant by any impersonal test. I care far more about my own child's welfare than a stranger's, and we mostly don't call that a bias — or if we do, we don't condemn it. So the content test can't draw the line without first settling which properties matter, which is the original disagreement.

The third candidate is a reflection test. A bias is something that wouldn't survive reflection, or that conflicts with the person's own considered values; a preference is what remains after reflection and can be endorsed. The trouble is that reflection is easy to game. People are very good at producing principled-sounding defenses of attitudes they hold for shabbier reasons, and someone intelligent can reflectively endorse almost any view, including principled nepotism or racial partiality. Meanwhile plenty of values people hold with zero reflection are values we wouldn't dream of calling biases. Endorsement is cheap, and its absence doesnt make a bias.

The fourth candidate is an interest test. A bias serves the interests of the person who holds it; a value doesn't, or at least can be justified to others. But biases are not reliably self-serving. A parent who rigs a job for their child may damage their own reputation; in-group bias can be costly to the individual. And plenty of legitimate preferences are self-serving in exactly this sense — whom to marry, where to live, which cause to donate to. Self-service doesn't line up with bias.

If all four tests fail, I think the honest conclusion is that 'bias' is not a category that sorts attitudes independently of our prior moral commitments. It's a status we assign to an attitude we've already found wanting, with a story attached about how it got that way. That doesn't make the charge empty. It can flag distrust in someone's judgment, and it can invite people to revisit how they formed a view. But it can't do the foundational work people sometimes want from it: we decide what counts as a bias by first deciding what's wrong, not the other way around.

Specific things I'd like comments on. First, is the second test doing too much work — does it just collapse into the claim that people disagree about which properties are relevant, which is old news and not a point specifically about bias? Second, is my use of the child example fair? Someone could say partiality to one's own children is a bias, just a permitted one, and if that's right it weakens the point that we don't call it a bias. Third, the fourth test feels like the weakest section to me; should I cut it or fold it into the first? Fourth, does the conclusion overclaim? Should I end with the weaker line, that the preference/bias distinction is local and comes after first-order moral views are in place, rather than the stronger line that it's rhetorical? And overall: what's the strongest objection to the central argument and where should I preempt it? Also, would a room of grad students find this obvious, or is there something genuinely surprising here?
