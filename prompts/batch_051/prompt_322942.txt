Most people would condemn someone who tells a suicidal acquaintance, in explicit detail, that suicide is rationally justified and describes a reliable method that the acquaintance then uses; they would treat the speaker as morally, perhaps legally, responsible for the death. Yet we rarely treat an author as similarly responsible when they publish an essay or memoir describing the same method in the same detail, several strangers later die by it, and the publisher had been warned in advance that this was likely. In both cases, death follows from words whose likely effects were foreseen, and silence was available at little comparable cost. The apparent difference is that the first addressed a particular person while the second addressed a public. Is that a difference Mill's harm principle can justify, or does a consistent application of the principle force us to treat the two speakers alike—and if so, in which direction? If you defend the asymmetry, say how your justification handles a case in which the author knows that one particular reader is suicidal and has already said he intends to follow the description.
