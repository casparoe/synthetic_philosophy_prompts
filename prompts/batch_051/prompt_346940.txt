I've been teaching decision theory for years, and here's the thing that's started to trip me up. The standard argument for the independence axiom in expected utility theory is built on a picture of the chooser: a single agent with a stable utility function over outcomes, making a one-shot decision for its own life. The argument says, roughly, that your preference between two lotteries should be settled by the parts where they differ; gluing the same third lottery onto both of them as a common branch shouldn't reverse the preference, so a rational agent's preferences must satisfy independence. The Allais paradox is usually wheeled in as the exhibt that human preferences don't actually obey that, at least not in the classic common-consequence version.

Now the development. In plenty of real settings today the 'chooser' is not a human facing gambles about its own life but a large language model producing decisions on someone else's behalf — credit approvals, triage, insurance-plan recommendations. We can elicit its preferences by running it through thousands of Allais-style pairs, and suppose we find the textbook violation: it prefers the sure million in the first pair and the riskier option in the second, mirrors the common-consequence pattern, flips under framing. But there's no inner life there, no special pull of certainty, no single life over which outcomes get integrated — just a system whose choice patterns are learned from human text and human choice data.

So my question is about the argument, not about alignment or policy. Does the standard independence-axiom argument survive this shift, and does an Allais-style violation still have the same force when the agent is like that? Which premise in the argument is actually carrying the weight, is it under more or less pressure then in the human case, and is there a repaired version that still says something weaker but worth saying — about the system, about the data it learned from, or about when we're entitled to use its outputs as 'preferences' at all?
