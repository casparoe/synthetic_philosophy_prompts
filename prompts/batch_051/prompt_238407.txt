Hey—I'm a PhD student in philosophy of law, mostly working on philosophy of language and vagueness, and I keep circling an example I can't settle. My mom's subdivision has this restrictive covenant from 1987 in the deed, copied just as it appears:

"No building, structure, or improvement of a permanent character shall be erected or placed on any lot without the prior written approval of the Architectural Review Committee. For purposes of this Covenant, 'structure' includes any framework or construction fixed to the ground or having a fixed location, but excludes garden plantings, seasonal decorations, and moveable play equipment."

A neighbor has put up a geodesic dome greenhouse: steel struts bolted together with polycarbonate panels, resting on a gravel bed, no foundation, fourteen feet across. He says it's moveable because four people can lift it, and he disassembles it each June and stores it till September. The Committee says its a structure and he needs approval; he says its seasonal garden equipment, a big cold frame. The 1987 drafters were plainly thinking of carports, addition, and tool sheds, not this.

What I can't get clear on: what do epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness each commit you to about the legal question of whether this dome needs approval? The epistemicist line (Williamson) is that 'structure' has a sharp extension and the boundary is just unknowable; the supervaluationist says the case is true on some admissible sharpenings and false on others, hence neither true nor false. But a court can't hand down a neither-true-nor-false ruling: it has to either order the dome removed or not. Does that fact—the law needing a decision—favor epistemicism? Or does it show the two theories aren't competing accounts of what judges should do, only of what the semantics says before anyone sharpens it? And can a supervaluationist say "the judge should sharpen the term" without quietly becoming an epistemicist, and if so, what's left of the claim that borderline cases lack determinate truth values in the first place?
