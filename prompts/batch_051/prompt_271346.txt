I am writing a novel, and I need a speech that one of the central characters delivers at a town council hearing. The novel is set in a small coastal town that is deciding whether to demolish an old, low stone breakwater along the harbour. The harbour mouth was moved decades ago, the fishing fleet has gone, and the breakwater now stands in the way of a proposed waterfront development. An engineering report, written by the character's son, recommends demolition: the breakwater, he argues, has no current function and would cost more to repair than to remove.

The speaker is the son's mother, a woman in her late sixties who taught local history for thirty years and who holds a particular, slightly idiosyncratic view of old structures. She accepts a version of Chesterton's fence—the principle that old structures should not be removed until the reasons for their existence are understood—but she adds a further twist that does the real argumentative work. On her view, the reasons for an old structure are rarely written down and cannot be recovered by any single observer. What can be known is that the breakwater is a record: every repair, every heightening after a storm, every patched breach is a decision made by someone who knew the harbour under conditions that no longer exist. Its value is therefore not that it still performs some forgotten job. It is that the wall is the only surviving archive of those vanished conditions, and demolishing it would be an irreversible experiment on the town's memory, run without controls.

The speech has to accomplish several things at once. It must give at least some council members serious reason to hesitate, despite the fact that they are mostly worried about budgets and liability. It must answer the son's report honestly and without humiliating him, because his professional credibility matters to him and because the mother wants to remain his ally after the meeting. It must not slump into nostalgia, and it must not lecture the room about political philosophy; the idea has to operate inside the argument rather than being announced as an idea. The audience is mixed: a few residents and one council member will recognize the Burkean shape of what she is saying, but most will simply hear a woman trying to save an old wall.

Write the speech she actually delivers. Aim for four to five minutes when spoken aloud.
