Two members of my department's reading group argued for most of an evening last month, and when they asked me to settle it I found I couldn't. The argument was about whether a person can have a reason to do something that has no connection to anything he already cares about — in the jargon, about internal and external reasons — but it stayed anchored to a single case.

The case: a man in his late fifties is diagnosed with a condition that will kill him within a year. An effective treatment exists; it has tolerable side effects and would cost him nothing. He understands the medical facts and is competent, but he says he simply does not want the treatment. He would rather take a long-planned trip and let the illness run its course. His doctor suspects a mild depression, though it is not impairing his grasp of the situation.

Elena opened. He has no reason to accept the treatment, she said: nothing in what he currently cares about would, on reflection, lead him to want it, and a reason he has must be capable of moving him as he actually is. Saying "you have a reason to accept treatment" is either false or a disguised way of pressuring him. Marcus answered that this has the matter backwards. The fact that the treatment would save his life and preserve his capacity to pursue whatever he might later come to value counts in favor of accepting it whether or not he is now moved by it. His indifference may explain why he does not respond to the reason, but it does not cancel it. If Elena were right, he said, we could not call the man's refusal a rational failing; we could only call it unfortunate. But a person can be wrong not to care about his own survival.

Elena replied that we can call his state bad, and we can say that if he cared about living, he would have decisive reason to accept the treatment; those are third-person evaluations. But "he has a reason now" is a claim about what can actually engage his practical thought. If nothing can engage it, the reason-attribution floats free of him. The real question is whether practical criticism requires that kind of engagement, and she thought it did.

Marcus then tried a reductio. On her view, he said, a person whose only stable aim was to make others suffer would have a reason to make them suffer, because that aim can move him. That should make her uneasy, since it shows her test has drifted from what "reason" means: a consideration that counts in favor, not merely whatever happens to move an agent. Elena said she was not uneasy, because she denied the conclusion: such a person has no reason to make others suffer. He has a cruel aim, and the rest of us have strong reasons to restrain him. What she denied, she said, was that every reason that exists from our practical standpoint must also be a reason that is his.

Marcus turned her denial back on her. By her own test, hurting others serves the cruel aim and so can move him; so on her own terms she should say he does have an internal reason to hurt them. Instead she ruled out the cruel aim on the ground that it is wicked, and that is precisely the kind of external normative judgment she had said we cannot make. Either he has a reason to hurt people, or her criterion was not doing the work she claimed. Elena answered that she would accept the first option: in the minimal, explanatory sense, hurting others is something he has reason to do, given his aims. That is exactly why we need the further judgment that his aim is wicked — but that further judgment, she insisted, is our evaluation of his ends, not the attribution to him of a reason to refrain. Marcus then said the disagreement had finally surfaced: she was committed to saying that "he has a reason to hurt them" and "hurting them is wicked" can both be true. The latter, he said, implies a reason for him not to hurt them, one he fails to acknowledge. If she denies that, moral criticism becomes spectator commentary with no practical claim on the agent; but moral criticism just is a practical claim on the agent.

They went in circles after that. Please adjudicate the disagreement. Go through the exchange move by move and say, for each move, whether the speaker is right, partly right, or mistaken about that specific claim, and why. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing or conflating, and give an overall verdict about which of them, if either, is more right overall.
