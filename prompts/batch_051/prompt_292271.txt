I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, although I run a small evening ethics seminar for engineers at my company. This term we have been working through the question of whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible. We have covered the standard machinery: ought-implies-can, the contrast between pro tanto and all-things-considered oughts, the agglomeration argument (if one ought to do A and ought to do B, then one ought to do A-and-B, which together with ought-implies-can appears to rule out dilemmas), and the argument from moral remainders — roughly, that an agent can appropriately feel regret or guilt even after doing what she ought to do, which some take to show that the 'overridden' obligation was still binding. The group can recite these positions, but I keep noticing one conflation, and I want the final exercise to expose it. They do not distinguish a decision's being hard — there is a determinate right act, but the agent cannot reliably arrive at it — from its being real — there is no determinate right act even once all the reasons are in. In engineering terms, this is the gap between a function that is merely expensive to evaluate and a function that has no defined output for a given input. My suspicion is that the remainder argument looks stronger than it is because people slide from the right act's leaving a genuine loss to there having been no uniquely right act.

Please draft a final take-home essay question for this seminar. The students are sharp but non-specialist, so the question should be demanding without presupposing technical training; it should be answerable in about 90 minutes and 800–900 words. The question should force them to use the hard-versus-real distinction — whether you state it in the question or leave them to draw it out — to assess whether moral remainders establish genuine dilemmas or only show that right choices can be costly. Then provide a model answer and a marking scheme. The marking scheme should identify four moves a strong answer must make, distribute 20 marks across them, and for each move give a one-sentence note on the characteristic error made by a student who has only memorized the seminar's standard positions. No citations or reading lists, please; the exercise should be self-contained.
