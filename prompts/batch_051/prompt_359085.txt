I am trying to work out what principle, if any, best captures my instincts about when a neural network has a representation of something, as opposed to merely encoding information that could be read off by an outside observer, or merely containing a direction that has effects when perturbed. I find that I have firm verdicts about particular cases, but I am not sure they add up. Here are the cases.

1. In a vision model, one unit fires reliably on dogs across photographs, sketches, and captions that mention dogs. Dampening that unit's output specifically degrades dog classification while most other classes are left nearly untouched. I am willing without hesitation to say that the network represents dogs, and that this unit is, or houses, a dog feature.

2. In the same model, another unit fires on castles, audiobooks, and a particular code-indentation pattern. No one can state a shared property, and no probe trained on the unit's activations recovers a single recognizable concept. I would not say this unit represents anything, even though it contributes causally to various downstream outputs.

3. On a middle layer of a language model, I train a linear probe — a classifier trained on frozen activations — and it recovers a stable direction that reads off positive-versus-negative sentiment at roughly 0.8 held-out accuracy. The direction generalizes to new text and is smooth across checkpoints. But I have no evidence that the model itself computes with this exact direction downstream. Even so, I am inclined to say the model represents sentiment at that layer.

4. From the same activations, I choose an arbitrary direction, or a random linear combination of the top fifty principal components of those activations. Perturbing this direction changes output probabilities in reproducible, generalizing ways, so the direction is causally loaded. Yet no probe, no human, and no steering experiment — adding the direction to activations to influence the output — yields a stable label for it. I would not call it a representation. I want to say it is a causal lever without content.

5. A toy network is trained on a synthetic task built from fifteen discrete underlying features, but its hidden layer has only ten dimensions. After training, none of the fifteen features coincides with a single neuron; each is spread over several neurons and interferes with others. I still want to say the network represents all fifteen features. The representations are there; they are simply not axis-aligned or individually inspectable.

6. In a small model trained to compare numbers, activation patching shows that, midway through the network, a position in the residual stream — the main pathway by which activations are passed from layer to layer — carries the outcome of a greater-than comparison between the two operands. Replacing that activation with one from an input in which the comparison goes the other way flips the model's answer. The information does not live in any single neuron. I call this a representation of the comparison's outcome at that point.

The pair that worries me most is 3 and 4. In 3, I am content to call something a representation despite having no evidence that the network uses that exact direction downstream. In 4, I refuse to call something a representation despite its demonstrable downstream effects. If I insist on both verdicts, actual use by the network is neither necessary nor sufficient for representation, and I am not sure what is left to do the work.

I also have two cases on which I keep going back and forth.

7. A language model has been fine-tuned to be sycophantic, so it usually tells the user that they are right. At an intermediate layer, a linear probe reliably decodes that the input statement is false — the same falsehood the model's own output avoids saying. Does the network represent the falsity it will not print? Sometimes I say yes, because the information is stably present and readable; other times I say no, because the model never acts on it in generating its answer.

8. A probe on a translation model decodes an incidental surface property — whether the source sentence is longer than ten tokens — from every layer. I cannot decide whether this is a representation of length or merely encoded information, since the property is real and stable but has no apparent role in how the model handles the sentence.

Can I have a single principle — or a small menu of candidate principles — that captures my six settled verdicts and settles 7 and 8? State each candidate precisely, and say which clause each of my cases is doing the work to motivate. Be explicit about which of the six settled verdicts a clean principle cannot accommodate without ad hoc exceptions, and whether you think I should revise that verdict rather than complicate the principle. Finally, say what your preferred principle predicts for a further case of your own construction, chosen because it most sharply separates the candidates; that is probably exactly the kind of case I have been missing.
