Over the years I have kept a running list of my friend's judgments about which machines genuinely believe things and which ones we describe that way only as shorthand. He is an industrial repairman with no academic training, but he has thought about this more carefully than anyone I know, and his rulings are precise. I would like to know what he should say about a new case, because his past rulings seem to me to point in two directions at once.

Below are four of the rulings I recorded.

First, a mechanical thermostat built around a bimetallic strip. He says it does not believe the house is cold when it turns on the furnace. The strip bends with the temperature, and that is the entire story; saying "it thinks the house is cold" is only a figure of speech.

Second, a small line-following toy that steers by two light sensors, turning left when the left sensor leaves the line and right when the right one leaves it. He says it does not believe the line has moved to one side. There is nothing inside the toy that stands for the line or its position; it is just a pair of reflexes wired together.

Third, a robot vacuum that builds up a map of the apartment as it cleans and keeps correcting that map when it finds that a chair has been moved or a door has been shut. He says this machine does believe things about the apartment — that the dock is down the hall, that the kitchen has already been cleaned. The map inside stands for the apartment, and the vacuum steers itself by the map rather than by whatever its sensors are brushing against at the moment.

Fourth, a small autonomous delivery robot that keeps a running model of the cars and pedestrians around it and updates that model when the world departs from what the model anticipated — for example, when a car it expected to turn continues straight. He says the delivery robot believes the car is about to turn at that moment, because it holds a prediction about the scene and treats that prediction as open to correction by further evidence.

The new case is the automated forecasting system used by a national weather service. At the start of each run it absorbs a large batch of current observations into a fine-grained three-dimensional model of the atmosphere, and the day's predictions are calculated from that model; the model is plainly a stand-in for the atmosphere, and the predictions come out of it. But the model's structure is fixed. When the forecast turns out wrong, no trace of the error changes the model used for the next run; the next run is rebuilt from the next batch of observations by the same machinery. The system never learns from its own mistakes.

Should my friend, if he wants to be consistent with his four recorded rulings, say that this system believes something like "there is a low-pressure system off the coast" during a run? Which of the four earlier cases is the closest analog, and what principle would he be committing himself to with each verdict if the record genuinely leaves the matter open? Finally, describe one further case whose verdict would settle which principle he actually holds.
