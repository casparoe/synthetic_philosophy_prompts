I'm putting together a handout of objections to the doomsday argument for a reading group, and so far my list has five entries. (a) The reference-class problem: the result is exquisitely sensitive to whether you rank yourself among all humans, all sapient beings, all observers who will ever live, etc., and there's no principled way to pick a class; the conclusion flips depending on how you draw the boundary. (b) The prior problem: the argument only gives you a likelihood, not a posterior, so unless your prior over near-term survival is basically diffuse, your birth rank doesn't force much of an update. (c) The ancients problem: someone in, say, ancient Rome could have run the same calculation with their own birth rank and concluded doom was near, and they'd have been wrong by an absurd margin. (d) The self-indication response: the fact that you exist at all is itself evidence for a larger total population, and on the usual assumptions it cancels the doomsday shift. (e) The no-sampling-mechanism objection: there is no process that actually drew your birth rank uniformly from {1,...,N}, so the urn analogy is doing work the world never does.

I want one more objection that is genuinely different from these five—not a rewording, not a special case of the reference-class point or the prior point, not just a combination of them. It can be something you've read or something you construct yourself, but it should have a distinct structure from everything on the list. Then say how it relates to the others and, honestly, whether it's any good. If you think the way I've carved up the list is confused, or that there's no sixth objection because they all collapse into a couple of moves, tell me that straight instead of generating something that just sounds philosophical.
