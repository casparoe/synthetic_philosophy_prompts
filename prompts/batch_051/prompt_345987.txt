I'm a machine learning engineer, and I spend most of my time on credit assignment: determining which features or parameters deserve credit for a model's output, using tools like Shapley values, integrated gradients, counterfactual explanations, and training-distribution analysis. I know these tools well, but I have no formal philosophy background. Lately I keep getting pulled into arguments about whether people deserve what markets give them. On one side, roughly, people who work harder or contribute more are entitled to more. On the other: talent, inheritance, and the circumstances one starts from are all morally arbitrary, so "desert" is the wrong category. I keep having the intuition that the second side is doing bad credit assignment, failing to isolate the effort or contribution that should be rewarded. But I also suspect my own vocabulary quietly assumes answers to the very questions philosophers are arguing about.

I want you to restate the main positions in the meritocracy-and-desert debate in the vocabulary I actually use: features, labels, models, baselines, training distributions, Shapley values, gradient attribution, counterfactuals, and credit assignment. Take at least these positions: the naive meritocratic view, the Rawlsian point that natural talents and starting circumstances are morally arbitrary, the luck-egalitarian view that only inequalities traceable to choice are justified, and the entitlement view that what matters is whether goods arose from just acquisitions and transfers rather than whether anyone deserved them. For each, tell me what translates cleanly, what had to be bent to fit, and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary. I am not asking for a loose analogy; I want a systematic translation, including the places where translating forces you to distort the ideas.

Finally, give me a residue report. Of the things that fail to translate, how much is a limitation of machine-learning notions of attribution, and how much is evidence that the philosophical concept of desert is doing work a credit-assignment framework cannot even state, let alone answer? In particular, I want to know whether my "it's just bad credit assignment" instinct is right, or whether it mistakes a normative question for a computational one.
