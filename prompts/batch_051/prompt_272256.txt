Below is the set of commitments on well-being and novelty that I'm planning to defend in a workshop paper. I want it stress-tested before I present, since I can no longer see where the load-bearing cracks are.

1. Hedonism: what ultimately makes a life go better or worse for the person living it is solely the felt character of their experience — the balance of pleasant and unpleasant states, broadly understood.
2. I count novelty, variety, and adventure among the most important prudential goods. I also accept the hedonic treadmill: people adapt to stable circumstances, good and bad. I have long taken the second claim to explain the first — variety matters because it keeps felt experience from flattening out — and I would resist any view on which variety has value beyond its effects on felt experience.
3. Case verdict: consider two eighty-year lives with equal total felt pleasantness and equal total felt unpleasantness. The first is a single very pleasant day repeated across the whole life. The second is varied and adventurous: unfamiliar projects, real risks, occasional hardship, and no long stretches of sameness. I judge the second better for the person living it, not merely more interesting from the outside.
4. I reject objective-list and narrative-shape views of well-being: achievement, relationships, autonomy, and the overall storyline of a life matter only insofar as they alter felt experience, and there is no independent good of having done something or of a life's having a good narrative arc.
5. I think the fear of a boring life is rational and tracks a real prudential defect. But I also think that fear can be fully accounted for by anticipated felt boredom, so it gives me no reason to move beyond hedonism.

Go through these and separate the surface conflicts that dissolve under a closer reading from the genuine fault lines. For each genuine fault line, tell me how deep it runs, what would have to give, and what the least expensive repair is. Then name the single revision that would do the most to make the set cohere, and say plainly which commitment you think I should abandon.
