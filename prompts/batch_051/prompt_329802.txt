Last month, during a design review, two engineers on my team got into an argument about whether the system we are building is genuinely creative, and the argument has continued in Slack and over lunch ever since. They have now asked me to referee, but I have no formal philosophy background, so I want a careful adjudication of the exchange as it actually happened.

The context is this. We are building a feature that generates album-cover art from a user's text prompt. The model is a diffusion model fine-tuned on a corpus of mid-century abstract expressionist paintings. The dispute is not about output quality, copyright, or deployment risk. It is about whether the output is creative in any meaningful sense.

Priya, who built the sampling pipeline, argued that the outputs are creative in a straightforward way. The model does not copy any individual training image, she said. It learns a probability distribution over a very high-dimensional space of visual features and then draws new samples from that distribution during inference. The outputs can be genuinely surprising, including to the people who built the system. If creativity means, at minimum, producing artifacts that are novel relative to the training set and that can hold a viewer's aesthetic attention, she said, then the model passes that threshold often enough for the word to apply.

Marcus, who works on evaluation and reads philosophy in his spare time, countered that novelty from a sampling process is too cheap. A pseudorandom number generator produces numbers no one has seen before, but nobody calls it creative. What matters, he argued, is not just novelty but the source of the novelty. In a human painter, the new work is downstream of intentional choices, a felt sense of tradition, the search for a visual problem, and the capacity to judge one attempt as better than another. The model has no visual problem it is trying to solve; it is maximizing a learned objective. The creativity, if any, belongs to the artists who made the training corpus, or to the engineers who designed the architecture and the loss function, not to the system at inference time.

Priya replied that Marcus's account of human creativity is idealized. Most working artists, she said, do not have introspective access to why a promising line emerged. Much of the process is iterative variation and selection against preferences that are themselves difficult to articulate. If we applied Marcus's standard strictly, she said, many human artists would fail it too. She also pointed out that the model can produce visual relationships that were not explicitly represented in any single training image, and users often say they see stylistic connections they had not previously noticed. That is at least a functional analogue of finding a visual problem.

Marcus answered by granting that human creativity is messy, but he said there is still a difference in counterfactual dependence. A human artist who had never seen a particular painting could, in principle, arrive at a similar formal solution through independent experimentation, because humans live in the world and face constraints beyond their aesthetic inputs. The model cannot do that. Its outputs are always posterior to the training distribution. It can interpolate within that distribution, and perhaps extrapolate slightly across it, but it cannot recognize that distribution as one style among others or decide to refuse it. He said Priya was confusing a creative product with a creative producer.

At that point Marcus read out a passage he had been reading from Kant's Critique of the Power of Judgment, where Kant distinguishes fine art as the art of genius from merely mechanical art. His summary of the passage was that the work of genius does not arise by following an already known rule; it is itself the source of a new rule for others to follow. Marcus said a generative model is close to the limiting case of mechanical production, because it does not give a new rule; it instantiates the rules latent in its training data. Priya responded that the fine-tuned model is not merely applying an explicit rule someone wrote down in the codebase. The rule, if there is one, was not known until the training process extracted it, and no human reviewer could state it completely. She also asked whether the human-machine system, including the person prompting and curating the outputs, might be the relevant creative agent even if the model alone is not.

I can see the force of both positions, and each time one of them lands a point the other seems to land a point on a different level. Please adjudicate the disagreement point by point. Where is each of them right, where is each of them wrong, and who is more right overall about whether the output of this image-generation system can properly be called creative?
