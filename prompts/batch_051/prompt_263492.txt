This is a final-exam essay question for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in intergenerational justice. Students have 90 minutes and should write roughly 600 to 800 words. The question is worth 100 marks. A model answer and a marking scheme follow.

Question. A policy analyst proposes that future wellbeing should be discounted at the market interest rate. Assess this proposal. Your answer must do all of the following:

(i) define pure time preference precisely, and distinguish it from at least three other factors that can make future benefits count for less in present calculations: uncertainty about whether the future will arrive, the expectation that future generations will be richer, and the opportunity cost of capital;

(ii) explain why an argument for discounting consumption is not thereby an argument for discounting future wellbeing, and reconstruct the objections to pure time preference offered by Ramsey and by Parfit;

(iii) present the strongest attempt you know to defend a positive pure rate of time preference, evaluate it, and reach a definite verdict on whether a positive pure time preference rate should be applied to wellbeing in intergenerational policy evaluation.

Model answer. Pure time preference is the assignment of less weight to a unit of wellbeing solely because it occurs later in time, while holding fixed its size, its probability, and the identity of the person who experiences it. It is easily confused with three distinct factors. First, future benefits are less certain: people may die, institutions may fail, or the species may not survive. A benefit that is merely probable is worth less in expectation, but that is discounting for risk, not for temporal distance. Second, future generations may be richer. If people in the future consume more than we do, an additional unit of consumption gives them less marginal utility, so a unit of consumption arriving late is worth less in wellbeing terms. Third, capital is productive: a unit of consumption invested now yields more consumption later, so present consumption has an opportunity cost. The second and third factors justify a positive consumption discount rate; neither justifies discounting wellbeing itself. The distinction between the two rates is standard: the consumption discount rate decomposes into the pure time preference rate, plus the product of the elasticity of marginal utility of consumption and the growth rate of consumption. Only the first component expresses an ethical judgment that later utility matters less simply because it is later. The second component reflects expectations about future affluence. An argument from future wealth, or from the productivity of capital, therefore does nothing to establish pure time preference.

Ramsey and Parfit attack the first component directly. Ramsey holds that equal quantities of wellbeing command equal ethical attention regardless of when they occur; to care less about a future pleasure merely because it is temporally remote betrays a failure of imagination rather than a requirement of rationality. Parfit argues that no point in time is objectively privileged: the later time is merely later, in much the way that a distant place is merely distant, so discounting by time is as arbitrary as discounting by spatial location. Both conclude that pure time preference is indefensible.

The most serious attempt to defend a positive rate is the Koopmans-style existence argument. In an infinite-horizon maximizing framework with a zero pure time preference rate, the sum of utilities may fail to be finite, or the maximization problem may have no optimal path, so a small positive rate is introduced in order to make the model well-defined. But this is a repair to the model, not a moral reason. It shows that a particular formalism needs adjustment; it does not show that late wellbeing is less important than early wellbeing. Infinite horizons can be handled with finite truncations, explicit uncertainty about the future, or path-comparison criteria that avoid summing all utility, and these devices do not smuggle in an ethical preference for the present. Other purported defenses generally collapse into the factors already distinguished: appeals to extinction risk are appeals to probability, and appeals to future affluence are appeals to diminishing marginal utility, not to pure time preference.

The verdict, then, is that a positive pure time preference rate should not be applied to wellbeing in intergenerational evaluation. Future monetary benefits may legitimately enter the calculation at a lower value than present ones, but only because of risk and expected growth, never because a unit of wellbeing matters less when it arrives later.

Marking scheme (100 marks).

An A-grade answer contains all of the following moves:

1. Definition (10 marks). Defines pure time preference as discounting for temporal location alone, and explicitly states what is held fixed: the size of the benefit, its probability, and its recipient.

2. Three confounds (30 marks; 10 each). Correctly characterizes (a) uncertainty about whether the future, or the benefit's delivery, will occur; (b) higher expected future consumption and hence lower marginal utility; and (c) the opportunity cost of capital. Each is treated as an alternative to pure time preference, not as a version of it.

3. Consumption versus wellbeing discounting (25 marks). States the decomposition of the consumption discount rate into a pure time preference term and a growth-times-elasticity-of-marginal-utility term, and identifies which term carries the ethical judgment. Shows that growth-based and capital-productivity arguments establish, at most, a positive consumption discount rate.

4. Ramsey and Parfit (15 marks). Reconstructs their actual arguments rather than merely reporting that they opposed discounting: the failure-of-imagination objection to privileging the present, and the no-privileged-time argument from temporal neutrality, including the spatial-distance analogy.

5. Strongest defense and evaluation (10 marks). Presents a substantive candidate defense, such as the Koopmans-style point that zero utility discounting can leave an infinite-horizon optimum undefined, and shows that this is a technical constraint on the formalism rather than a moral justification, while identifying alternative ways of handling infinite horizons.

6. Verdict (10 marks). Reaches a definite, justified conclusion, and closes by noting that rejecting pure time preference does not require a zero consumption discount rate, because positive consumption discounting can still arise from risk and growth.

A B-grade answer typically: defines pure time preference roughly but at some point identifies it with the market interest rate; lists future uncertainty or future affluence as reasons for pure time preference rather than as alternatives to it; reports that Ramsey and Parfit objected without reconstructing their reasoning; writes down the consumption-utility decomposition without saying which term is pure time preference; and ends indecisively, or with a compromise that does not separate consumption discounting from wellbeing discounting.

Specific mistakes that should cost marks: (a) treating observed present-bias in saving behaviour as a moral justification for pure time preference; (b) inferring pure time preference from the mere fact that markets discount; and (c) arguing that future people do not yet exist or cannot vote, without addressing the demand for temporal impartiality.
