For a research workshop on chance and modality, I have been asked to write out my philosophical position and then have it audited by the group before the session. Here is roughly where I have landed, and I am no longer sure the pieces fit together.

(1) Objective chances are real: the chance of a fair coin's landing heads is a fact about the toss setup, not just a summary of anyone's ignorance. (2) Chance and possibility are linked in one direction only: if an outcome has a positive chance at a time, then it is genuinely possible at that time; the converse fails, since an exact but possible outcome — a pointer's landing at 0.5000... — can have chance zero. (3) A chance distribution is a measure over a space of possible outcomes; probabilities attach to sets of possible outcomes, and a probability of zero does not by itself mark a set as empty or its members as impossible. (4) Nonetheless, possibility itself is not graded: I treat 'more possible than' as a category mistake, and I want talk of degrees of possibility paraphrased into probability talk or dropped entirely. (5) Chances are not primitive propensities laid over the course of events: what the chances are at a time is fixed by the best systematisation of the whole mosaic of local particular facts, past and future. (6) Rational credence should defer to chance, roughly in the Principal Principle sense: on learning that the objective chance of an event is x, and barring inadmissible information, my credence in that event should be x. I have not settled how wide the inadmissible-information clause may be, but I do not want it widened so far that the deference principle becomes trivial.

I cannot see where this package strains, but a position this tidy is usually hiding something. Please audit it for me. Write your answer in British English, with the following sections in this order: 'Commitments in brief' (about 150 words; restate (1)–(6) as a coherent package, as I currently understand it); 'Genuine tensions, ranked by severity' (about 500 words; for each, name the commitments involved, explain why they pull against one another, and say what the least costly revision would be and what it costs); 'Apparent tensions that dissolve' (about 250 words; which pairs look inconsistent but are not, and why); and 'Overall verdict' (about 200 words; whether the package can be made jointly tenable, and which single revision buys the most consistency).
