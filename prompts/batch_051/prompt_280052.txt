The idea is that people in the epistemology-of-markets world talk a lot about prices aggregating dispersed information, but they mostly ignore the no-trade theorem side of things: under common priors and common knowledge of rationality, rational people arguably shouldn't trade on pure information differences at all. That makes a market's silence weird. Practitioners read low volume as smart money sitting on its hands, or everyone waiting for a signal, but there's no settled story about whether an outside observer is entitled to update on the fact that nobody traded, and the theorem seems to say silence is consistent with too many different underlying states to be informative. I want the proposal to ask something like: under what conditions, if any, can the absence of trade count as evidence about what market participants know or believe, and is there a coherent epistemology of market silence that isn't just reading tea leaves?

Turn that into a one-page proposal: the question stated so it has a determinate answer, why existing positions leave it open, the approach (and why conceptual work or modeling rather than, say, a lab experiment), what a successful deliverable would look like, what would count as failure, and a realistic scope for a year. Don't add citations or a bibliography; I'll fill those in myself. Write it continuously—no headings, bullets, bold, or tables—like a letter to a funder. Act as a philosopher of economics, think carefully, and in the opening few sentences lay out two or three candidate sharpenings of the question and say which one the proposal commits to.
