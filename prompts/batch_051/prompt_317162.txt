Retired spectroscopist here, recently got into philosophy. I've been reading Sellars and I've pretty much come around to his view that the given is a myth. The way I'd put it: you can't have a layer of raw, unconceptualized sensory experience that also serves as the ultimate court of appeal for what your justified in believing. Either it's raw and nonconceptual, in which case it can't stand in logical relations to a belief, or it's already conceptual, in which case it's the sort of thing that can be wrong and can't do foundational work. So I've given up the picture I implicitly had back in the lab, where observation was the neutral bedrock and theory was the risky superstructure.

What I want to know is what would actually count against Sellars on this. Not just that people find it counterintuitive. Are there live empirical findings or psychological cases (infant cognition, animal perception, blindsight, trained perceptual expertise) that should make a Sellarsian worried? Which of those would a Sellarsian concede as counting against the view, and which would they dismiss as just re-describing the given in new jargon? And is the position in danger of being insulated? If every purported case of nonconceptual but justificatory experience gets recast as conceptual after the fact, is that a problem for the view, or is it just what a high-level philosophical thesis looks like? I'd personally be most moved by a case where a creature or system is demonstrably responsive to a distinction it cannot in any way categorize, and that responsiveness seems to improve its epistemic standing. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.
