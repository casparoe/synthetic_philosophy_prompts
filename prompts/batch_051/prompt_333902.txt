I need a second look at an argument a friend of mine makes about the dollar auction, because I can't find the hole but the conclusion feels too clean. Setup: a $1 bill sells to the highest bidder, the highest bidder pays their bid and gets the dollar, the second-highest bidder pays their bid and gets nothing, bids go in 5-cent increments, and for the sake of argument assume no one has a binding budget cap. The argument:

(1) Once two people are bidding against each other, whoever is in second place at any moment faces a choice: stop and lose your current bid for sure, or raise by a nickel.
(2) As long as raising keeps the next bid below $1, raising is strictly better than stopping, because stopping turns your current bid into a guaranteed loss while raising still lets you break even or come out ahead.
(3) Since this is true at every stage below $1, two rational bidders can never stop once they've started below $1.
(4) Above $1 the same logic is even stronger: stopping now locks in a certain loss, while raising gives the other person the chance to quit and leave you losing less.
(5) So once started, two rational bidders will keep raising with no stopping point.
(6) Since they can foresee this before entering, a rational person never makes even the opening bid.
(7) So a dollar auction among rational players gets no bids, and the auctioneer keeps the dollar.

I half-suspect the problem is in step (2) or in the jump from (2)-(4) to (5), but I can't say which. Walk through the steps and tell me which fail, whether the argument is valid, and what the repaired principle would say.
