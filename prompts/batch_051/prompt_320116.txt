I am a novelist. Six years ago I began a novel, and it has since been rejected by nearly forty agents and editors; every reader I trust has told me its central conceit does not work. I nevertheless continue to believe, not merely to hope, that the book is good, and this belief is what brings me back to my desk each morning. A friend who teaches philosophy tells me this is blameworthy: I am holding a conviction on evidence I admit is weak, and I have arranged my life around it. I grant that the evidence points the other way. But I suspect, without being able to prove it, that finishing the book requires believing in it fully rather than proceeding on mere hope, and that giving up the belief just means giving up the book. Is my friend right that I ought to abandon the belief?

Please structure your answer in three titled sections, in this order: (1) The case for giving up the belief, about 150 words; (2) The case for retaining the belief, about 150 words; (3) Recommendation, about 100 words. In the first two sections, present the strongest version of each position rather than a caricature. In the final section, state explicitly what epistemic attitude I should take toward the manuscript and what I should do next.
