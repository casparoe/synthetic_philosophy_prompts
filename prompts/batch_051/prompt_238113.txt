I'm writing a paper on musical expressiveness and keep going back and forth about a new case, so I'd like you to reason through it with me. One influential argument about how music can be sad runs roughly as follows: the sadness of a passage is not simply a feeling the music happens to cause in listeners, and it is not merely an association or a projection. Rather, the music is heard as the expression of a feeling someone—whether the actual composer, the performer, or an imagined persona that we hear in the contour, harmony, and tempo. On this view, calling a passage sad is closer to hearing sadness in a voice or a posture than to inferring it from an effect: we hear an agent behind the gesture, and that is what the sadness consists in.

Now suppose a generative model trained only to predict the next event in musical audio—with no score, no lyrics, and no human shaping the output past training—produces a new piece that listeners in blind tests reliably describe as sad, and that moves them about as much as a human-composed adagio. The production history contains no moment at which any person felt the sadness listeners attribute to the piece. As a philosopher of music, I'd like you to think carefully about what this does to the expression-based argument. Does the argument survive intact because it was always about the heard persona rather than the actual producer? Does it need repair, for instance by relocating the sadness in the listener's response after all? Or is the generated piece a genuine counterexample that kills the argument? Start by laying out the argument's structure, identify exactly which premise the new development bears on, and then say whether that premise can be restored in a modified form rather than simply abandoned. I'm less interested in the technology than in what this case forces us to say about where musical sadness is located.
