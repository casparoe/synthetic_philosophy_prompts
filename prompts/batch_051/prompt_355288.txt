I'm a journalist who covers philosophy and cognitive science, and Grice has been quietly driving me crazy. You know the setup: sentence meaning versus speaker meaning, the cooperative principle, the maxims of quantity, quality, relation, and manner, and the way a speaker can flout a maxim while still being cooperative, so the listener works out an implicature from the very fact that the maxim was so blatantly ignored. The features that always trip me up are that conversational implicatures are supposed to be calculable (recoverable from the maxims) and cancelable (you can always add but I didnt mean that without contradicting yourself). I keep thinking these properties are basically a poetic device waiting to happen, not just a topic for some explainer.

Could you write a sonnet where the octave is literally about something flat and true, like giving directions to a train station or explaining how to make coffee, but with the speaker flouting the maxim of quantity by piling on far too much detail about left turns and platform numbers, so a careful listener could calculate an implicature the speaker never says out loud: they don't want the other person to leave. The volta should be the moment the reader reconstructs that implicature and feels sentence meaning and speaker meaning split apart. Then, in the closing couplet, have the speaker try to cancel the implicature outright, something like not that I meant anything by it, while making it obvious that the cancellation itself flouts a maxim and starts the whole implicature up again. It should be precise enough that someone who teaches pragmatics would nod at the cancelability and calculability on display, but someone who has never heard of Grice should still read it as a genuinely good poem, not a textbook example wearing a rhyme scheme.
