For the past several years I have been following the published decisions of a small regulatory tribunal that hears appeals against penalties imposed for breaches of rules at the boundary between national and local law. The tribunal is not bound by the courts, but it treats its own previous rulings as decisions it should try to remain consistent with, and its record seems to me to be working out an unstated view of when people may fairly be held to a rule they did not know. In one of its first rulings it fined an importer who had never registered under a newly created chemicals-safety scheme, even though the scheme had been announced only in the official gazette and in trade circulars: a commercial operator in a hazardous sector, the tribunal said, is expected to keep itself informed of regulatory change. In a second ruling it set aside a penalty against a foreign lorry driver who had parked in a controlled zone marked only by a sign in the local language and no pictogram, on the ground that a rule enforced against strangers must be made legible to them before it binds them. In a third, it set aside a penalty for a woman who had missed a new digital-only filing deadline while caring for her dying husband without any broadband connection, emphasising that an officer of the relevant agency had told her on the telephone that the old paper route remained open. In a fourth, it upheld a penalty against a landlord who had broken a fire-safety requirement buried in an unindexed technical schedule, but reduced the penalty sharply, calling the rule discoverable by a diligent professional yet published in a manner that fell short of what fairness requires. In a fifth, it upheld a penalty against a restaurateur who had relied on her solicitor's mistaken assurance that a new licensing provision did not apply to her, on the ground that the provision itself was public and unambiguous and that reliance on a private adviser cannot have the same force as reliance on a public official.

The case now before the tribunal is this. A retired farmer keeps a handful of hives and sells the honey at a weekly market. For decades his small-scale sales fell under a general exemption for farm-gate produce, and three years ago the local authority wrote to him confirming that his stall was covered by that exemption. Four months ago a new food-hygiene regulation came into force, superseding that exemption and requiring everyone who sells animal products to register with the authority; the only publicity has been a notice in the official gazette and a page on the authority's website. The farmer has no internet access, has received no letter or visit, and, since the exemption had stood unchanged for as long as anyone could remember, had no particular reason to suspect it had been altered. He continued selling as before, did not register, and now appeals against the penalty imposed on him.

I am interested less in what the tribunal's members might privately feel than in what consistency with their own record would require. Which of its earlier decisions is the closest analogue, what competing principles could each explain the whole run of decisions so far, and what would each of those principles dictate in the farmer's case? If the record genuinely underdetermines the result, say what principle the tribunal would be adopting, for good or ill, whichever way it decides, and which additional fact or further case would best separate the principles. Please give your answer in British English.
