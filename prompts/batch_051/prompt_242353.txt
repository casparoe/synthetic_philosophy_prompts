A friend and I were arguing about whether the idea of historical progress is salvageable, and she dismissed it as a local European story dressed up as universal. That seemed plausible enough as a criticism of that particular story. But then she added that every universal story is really a local story dressed up as universal, that all such stories are instruments of power, and that they are destined to be replaced by smaller local narratives. Since then I've noticed the same move in a lot of writing: someone sets out to reject a grand narrative — history as the unfolding of reason, class struggle, national destiny — and then offers an equally grand account of why grand narratives arise, what they do, and what happens to them. The critique of grand narratives becomes a grand narrative about grand narratives.

I know this is somewhere near Lyotard's idea that postmodernity involves a suspicion of metanarratives, and near the familiar charge that such critiques are performatively contradictory. But I'm after the specific configuration in which the content of the critique is itself a claim about the universal fate or function of all totalizing stories — not merely a case where the critic uses general concepts while denying that general concepts can be justified. Is there an established name for that configuration, the critique that totalizes about totalization? If not, what would you call it, and how would you distinguish it from a plain performative contradiction, from a self-referential paradox, and from the weaker claim that any critique must rely on some general vocabulary? I'd also like to know whether the pattern is best treated as a fatal objection to the critique, a tolerable irony, or something that can be avoided by more careful phrasing. A bare name won't settle it; I want to know what exactly the move consists in and where its edges are.
