ok so i have a distinction i keep using and i want it checked before i build anything else on it. i call it legacy guilt vs standing guilt. legacy guilt is what people usually have in mind when they talk about inheriting guilt: a taint that passes down a bloodline, so a child born decades later is guilty just because of who their ancestors were. i think that's incoherant, or at least morally poisonous. standing guilt is something else. it doesn't attach to persons at all. it attaches to a continuing body — a state, a corporation, a family firm, a university — and it gets carried forward not by blood but by the fact that the body persists: same name, same assets, same debts, same legal personality, same claim to be the same entity across time. on this view there were no guilty German babies in 1950; there was a guilty German state, and later citizens are implicated because they inherit the state (its benefits, its institutions, its treasury), not because they inherit guilt. the guilt is a standing liability of the institution, like a debt attached to a house that every subsequent owner has to deal with even if they didn't sign the mortgage.

i think this mostly dissolves the question "can a nation be guilty?": a nation isn't guilty the way a person is, but it can be guilty in this standing sense, which i want to say is a real kind of guilt and not just a metaphor. i'm also aware this might just be me redefining "guilt" until it stops meaning anything, or quietly collapsing into something like Jaspers' political guilt (which i only half-remember). so: steelman it first — tell me what it does actually capture — then give me the three strongest objections, and say whether any of them is fatal rather than just the kind of thing i'd want to patch. also be honest about whether the legacy guilt side is a strawman; maybe almost nobody believes the bloodline version, in which case my distinction is solving a fake problem and i'd want to know that too.
