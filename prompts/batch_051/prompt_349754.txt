I'm a retired civil servant in England, reading around church-and-state stuff for fun, and I keep ticking over a passage in Locke's A Letter Concerning Toleration that I don't have a name for. Near the end he refuses to extend toleration to athiests, saying—roughly—that an atheist can't be bound by promise or covenant, because such bonds, which Locke calls the bonds of human society, have no grip on them. I know this gets discussed as an awkward exception to his whole toleration argument, but I'm less interested in whether Locke was right than in the underlying move. It seems to go: a group can be denied toleration, or included only on special terms, not because of anything they have actually done, but because they are claimed to lack whatever internal mechanism would bind them to social compacts. So their mere presence would dissolve the tie that holds the whole thing together. That seems to have a long afterlife in justifications for established churches and church taxes—not always about atheists, sometimes about dissenters or people who don't share the national church. But I'm not sure if that argumentative move has an established name. It isn't quite civil religion or public reason, and it isn't plain intolerance, because it's a structural claim about what makes society possible, not an empirical prediction about conduct. Does this have a name of its own? And if it doesn't, what would you call it—and where exactly does it end? I'm asking because the distinction between you've done something harmful and you can't be part of the compact because it wouldn't bind you keeps coming up in older English and German debates about church taxes, and I want to know if someone has already carved it cleanly.
