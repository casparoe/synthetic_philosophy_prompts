Im a geologist, not a philosopher, so forgive the framing. For most of the last decade and a half the Anthropocene Working Group has tried to make the Anthropocene a formal geological epoch, with the lower boundary set around 1950, marked by the plutonium spike from nuclear weapons tests and pinned to a sediment core from Crawford Lake. In 2024 the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy voted the proposal down, and the International Commission on Stratigraphy and the IUGS let the rejection stand. Since then the debate among the geologists I know has completely stalled. The pro side says the physical signal is real, globally distributed, and exactly the kind of marker we use for other boundaries, and that the no side is just defending an outdated idea of what geology is. The no side says an epoch has to be legible in the rock record millions of years from now, that a few decades of human noise doesnt qualify, and that the term was never really a stratigraphic concept anyway, it was popularized by an atmospheric chemist and its now doing political work under a geological label. My working theory is that this isnt really one disagreement: its a disagreement about what a geological time unit is for, with one side treating it as a useful name for an ongoing planetary change and the other treating it as a technical classification that has to be conservatively defined to stay meaningful. Is that diagnosis right? Or is there a purely stratigraphic question here that could settle it if we got the evidence right? And either way, what would actually count as progress — a different rank for the Anthropocene, a different boundary, or just accepting that the decision belongs to institutions and not to rocks?
