The unilateralist's curse, in its usual form, runs like this. A group of agents each possesses private, imperfect evidence about whether some risky and effectively irreversible action—publishing a leaked document, releasing an engineered organism, broadcasting a message into space—would be good or bad. Each agent may take the action alone. Because it takes only one acting agent to make the action happen, and because in a large group the most optimistic agent is very likely someone who has misread weak or neutral evidence as strong, the action tends to be taken even when most properly informed agents would oppose it. The standard remedies all amount to replacing unilateral discretion with collective judgment: appointing a decider, requiring public deliberation, or raising the threshold to something like a supermajority.

Now suppose a new social-technological arrangement becomes available. Groups can, at negligible cost, hold an anonymous pre-action referendum before anyone acts. A would-be actor announces an intention; any member who privately judges the action bad can cast an encrypted no-signal; the actor sees only the tally, never the identities; and the operative norm is to proceed only if the no-signals fall below some small threshold. The group might also adopt an affirmative quorum, so that the actor proceeds only if at least k other members signal approval.

Does the unilateralist's curse survive this development? One could argue that it does not: the curse arises because a single actor can override the caution of everyone else, and an anonymous veto restores the power of the cautious. But one could also argue that the curse is only half of a deeper problem about aggregating private judgments, and that the veto merely installs the mirror-image failure—a single unduly pessimistic member now blocks an action that nearly everyone would rightly favor. Settle this. State precisely what the original argument must assume about the agents' evidence, the independence of their judgments, their action thresholds, and the irreversibility of the act. Identify which of those assumptions the anonymous-referendum/quorum arrangement changes, and say what a repaired argument would then predict about large groups using such arrangements—including any parameter settings under which they do better than unilateral discretion, and any under which they do worse.
