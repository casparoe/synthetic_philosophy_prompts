I was recently appointed to the orthography commission of a minority language (I'll call it Sayu here), and I need to circulate a written view before the commission votes next month. Sayu marks stress with an acute accent, and only stressed vowels keep their full quality; every unstressed vowel reduces to a central [ə]. The root /katal/ 'roof' therefore appears as <kátal>, pronounced [ká.təl], and, with shifted stress, as <katál>, pronounced [kə.tál]. The current orthography writes underlying vowels rather than surface ones, so the root <k-a-t-a-l> remains visible in every inflected form. The proposal before us would introduce a letter for [ə] and write what is heard, giving <kátəl> and <kətál>; pronunciation would be transparent, but the stable root spelling would disappear. One faction says an alphabet that does not record the sounds is failing at the one thing writing is for; the other says the present system succeeds precisely because it records the morphological skeleton that pronunciation obscures, which is what fluent readers actually track. Draft the decision memo I will circulate: recommend whether to adopt the reform, reject it, or adopt it with named changes; lead with a recommendation concrete enough to be put to a vote; state the principle about what a script should encode that your recommendation commits us to; and give the strongest consideration on the losing side a serious answer rather than a caricature. Keep the memo to no more than 650 words. Act as an expert on writing systems and on what alphabetic scripts represent; before drafting, brainstorm at least two defensible principles about what an alphabet should encode and test the proposal against each, but present only your final recommendation and reasoning.
