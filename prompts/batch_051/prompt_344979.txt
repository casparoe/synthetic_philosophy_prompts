I'm auditing a philosophy of technology seminar and also doing UI work on the side, and the two just collided. My designer friend has a line she reaches for constantly: "you only notice a tool when it breaks." She says it like its self-evident, and she uses it to justify a design rule — that when a feature fails, the interface should deliberately foreground the failure rather than quietly rerouting around it, because breakdown is the one moment the user actually sees the tool. I pushed back with two cases: a pen that fit my hand so well I kept noticing it while I wrote, and a door hinge in my apartment that has been broken-creaking for months and that I've apparently stopped noticing entirely. She says those don't count because "notice" means something specific. Fine — but then I want to know what exactly she is claiming.

Can you disambiguate "you only notice a tool when it breaks" for me? By that I mean: lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be used to express (about focal attention, explicit awareness, beliefs about the tool, the tool showing up as an object with properties, the tool's role in the larger activity becoming visible, appreciating the tool, and so on), and for each say whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or just false. I'd also like to know which readings, if any, actually support her design conclusion that breakdowns should be made salient in interfaces, and where my two counterexamples land — which readings do they genuinely kill, and which do they leave untouched? If there's a version a Heideggerian about readiness-to-hand would defend, tell me what that version has to commit to about what "notice" amounts to, since that seems to be where all the action is.
