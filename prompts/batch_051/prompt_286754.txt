Write a dialogue between two equally sophisticated characters about the paradox of hedonism. One of them, call her Mia, takes the Sidgwick line from Methods of Ethics: pleasure, if deliberately pursued as a goal, tends to slip away, and it is best obtained as a byproduct of engaging with things you value or find absorbing. She should be defending a version of this claim, not just asserting it. The other, call him Jon, thinks the so-called paradox is overblown: it is really just an empirical fact about attention and self-consciousness, and someone who knows the relevant psychology can go after pleasure directly in a smarter way than the folk wisdom suggests. Neither should be a strawman.

Have them argue about a single worked example: a person who has to choose between going to a party because she think it will be fun, or staying home to read a novel she finds genuinely absorbing even though she predicts less overall pleasure from the reading. Push each character to say something specific about what evidence about this person's experience would count against their own view. Also, at some point one of them should bring up the deeper question whether the paradox, if it is real, is just a contingent feature of how human attention and mood work, or whether there is something about pleasure — something in the very structure of being pleased — that makes it unsuited to be a direct target. The other should push back rather than concede.

Write the dialogue so that by the end neither side has clearly lost, but the precise location of their disagreement has become visible.

Then add a short note from you, the author: which character you think is closer to correct, why, and what argument or consideration would make you switch or substantially revise your view — or, if you think the disagreement is ill-posed, say what reframing would help.
