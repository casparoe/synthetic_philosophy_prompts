As a novelist researching the rise of the Nazi regime, I keep meeting a particular argument about historical explanation inside my own craft. A fellow novelist maintains that history is a story, and a story needs a protagonist: a well-made novel makes one or two characters the load-bearing causes of the plot, and a well-made history does the same. On his account, the novel form is therefore not a distortion we bring to the period but a dependable guide to its causation — the Third Reich had something like an authorial decision at its center, and without Hitler the plot would not have happened, or not in any form worth telling.

I find this attractive and suspect at once, since both of us earn our living fabricating exactly this effect: we construct artificial histories in which chosen individuals carry every event and call it craft when the seams vanish. The analogy is doing real argumentative work in my circles, not merely describing the novelist's task; it is being used to license the conclusion that Hitler's agency was the load-bearing element in a very large structural event, and that is precisely what I am trying to decide for my book.

Please audit the analogy rather than give me a flat verdict. Lay out the correspondence table between a well-made novel and a well-made historical explanation — protagonist and individual actor, plot and causal sequence, theme and structural force, narrator and historian, and any further correspondences worth setting out. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the great-man question from the ones that are incidental. In particular, what does the idea that history is a story quietly import from the craft of fiction — legible motives, an economical cast of world-moving persons, dramatic unity, closure — that a real causal explanation need not have? How much of the conclusion that, without Hitler, there would have been no Nazi Germany worth naming can the analogy actually support once those disanalogies are on the table? And if it cannot carry that conclusion, does the analogy remain serviceable for some narrower purpose in fiction or in a first pass at history, and what better analogy would you substitute?
