For the past few years I have been using a rule to decide which monetary and macroeconomic arguments I take seriously. I refuse to accept any claim of the form that printing money caused inflation, or that expanding the money supply created wealth, unless the person making it can specify at least a rough sequence of balance-sheet changes: whose assets and liabilities move, who ends up holding the new claims, and who becomes able, or is forced, to spend differently as a result. I do not demand exact accounting identities, but I do demand that the causal story run through actual transactions rather than through an aggregate acting directly on the price level. The rule has practical consequences. During the arguments about the 2020 inflation, it led me to dismiss explanations that stopped at the observation that central banks had created a large amount of reserves and therefore prices had to rise; and it led me to be skeptical of claims that a government could make its citizens wealthier in any serious sense simply by crediting their accounts, since unless the credits expanded what was actually produced, the question remained whose command over real resources had fallen. A friend says I have turned a useful diagnostic into a prejudice. He points out that many real macroeconomic mechanisms operate through expectations, asset prices, and the revaluation of existing wealth, which cannot always be displayed as a tidy sequence of ledger entries, and that central bank announcements move markets before any balance sheet has changed. He also argues that my filter is less demanding than I pretend, because in practice I reject arguments whose accounting I cannot reconstruct rather than arguments whose accounting is false. I suspect he is partly right, but I do not want to give up a rule that has protected me from loose talk.

I would like help deciding whether the rule is defensible as a standing policy for a non-economist. Under what conditions does a balance-sheet requirement separate reliable causal claims from noise, and under what conditions does it screen out true claims that are only visible at the aggregate level? Is there a form of the sentence that printing money creates something that can be made precise and defensible? In particular, before such a sentence is well posed, what exactly should the created thing be taken to be: bank reserves, broad money, net financial assets, real wealth, claims on future output, or something else, and what differences follow? How should I revise the rule so that it keeps the protection against hand-waving without becoming fake rigor?
