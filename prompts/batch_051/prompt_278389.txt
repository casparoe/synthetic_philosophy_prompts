I was reading about interactive proof systems the other day — the setup where a polynomial-time verifier can interrogate an all-powerful prover, and it turns out (IP = PSPACE) that interaction plus private randomness lets the weak machine check things enormously beyond what it could compute on its own. And I had a probably-too-cute thought: isn't that just the formal skeleton of how I trust experts? I'm the bounded verifier, the expert is the prover, I can't derive what they tell me from first principles, yet asking questions back and forth can leave me justified in believing something I couldn't establish alone. So my question is whether this is a real structural analogy or just the word "convince" doing all the work.

If it's real, do any results actually transfer? In the complexity setting, interaction is provably stronger than just receiving a static proof in some models, and zero-knowledge proofs let someone convince you a claim is true without handing over the reasons. That sounds like it would predict that live back-and-forth with an expert can justify beliefs that no written document could, and that testimony can transmit knowledge without transmitting understanding or the grounds. Are those predictions true, and if not, does the analogy just fail? I can also see places it might break: the prover in these proofs is unbounded and actively adversarial, whereas real experts share our basic cognitive architecture and (usually) aren't trying to trick us; and the verifier's hidden randomness, which is load-bearing in the proof, doesn't have any obvious epistemic analogue. Is the boundedness doing all the work, or is the interaction doing some too? Be as precise as you can about which parts of the mapping are carrying the analogy and which parts are decoration.
