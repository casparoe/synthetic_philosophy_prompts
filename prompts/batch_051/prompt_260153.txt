I'm giving a presentation on the dogmatism paradox in my epistemology class in a couple weeks, and I've realized I keep going in circles on my own starting assumptions. A friend asked me not long ago whether I would seriously weigh a new study that contradicted something I claim to know, and I found myself wanting to say both yes and no at the same time. So I wrote down everything I find myself wanting to assert, and now they look like they might not all fit together. The claims are these. (1) Knowledge is factive: if I know that p, then p. (2) Knowledge is closed under known entailment: if I know that p and I know that p entails q, then I can come to know q just by competently deducing it from what I know. (3) So if I know p, and someone brings me evidence E that points toward not-p, I can reason that p entails that E is misleading, since not-p is false; by closure I'm then in a position to know that E is misleading before I even look at it. (4) But I also think knowledge is defeasible: a normally reliable source can present what looks like strong evidence against p, and if I can't explan away the conflict, I should stop knowing p, even if p was true all along and the evidence was in fact misleading. (5) And I want to say that flatly refusing to consider E because I already know p, so E must be misleading, is a paradigm of dogmatism rather than rationality. (6) Separately, I'd call myself a fallibilist: for any particular belief I hold, I should admit there is some chance it's false, even when I'd call the belief knowledge.

I'm not asking for the standard solution from the literature so much as an audit of my own set of commitments: which of these are genuinely in tension, which pairs only look incompatible, and what is the least costly revision that makes the rest hang together? It would also help if you ranked the tensions by severity instead of treating them all as equally bad.
