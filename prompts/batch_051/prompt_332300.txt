I'm a retired physical chemist, roughly a year into reading philosophy, and I've fallen hard for Peirce. After thirty years of having to operationalize a variable before I believed a measurement, the pragmatic maxim felt like home. I've now turned it into a standing rule for myself: I'm not allowed to assert, or even file as a settled belief, any philosophical claim until I can say what I would expect to observe or do differently if it were true. If I can't produce that after an honest try, I mark the claim 'unfixed' and leave it alone. The rule has done genuine work. When someone in my reading group said consciousness is an illusion, I asked what observation would be different, what decision would change; he ended up restating it as a claim about predictive models of behavior, and we could actually make progress on that. But the same rule leaves me flat-footed in conversations my friends think matter. I can't say what would be different for me if numbers exist in a Platonic realm rather than just being a useful instrument, so I have no view on mathematical realism. Same with the question whether there are three truth-values or four. A friend with a philosophy PhD says Ive reinvented the verification principle, and that Peirce meant the maxim as a way of clarifying ideas, not as a gate for deciding which questions are worth having beliefs about. He says some distinctions only show their cash value after years inside a theory, not after an afternoon of effort. So: is my rule defensible as an epistemic policy for a retired scientist reading alone, or is it a bias in a pragmatist costume? Is there a version that keeps the benefits — operationalizing claims, refusing to hold beliefs I can't tie to anything — without collapsing into the old verificationism? And how would I tell from the inside the difference between a distinction with no practical bearing and one whose practical bearing I simply haven't found yet?
