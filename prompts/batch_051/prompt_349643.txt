I've become convinced that acting under moral uncertainty just is expected value reasoning over moral theories: for each action, for each theory you give positive credence to, take how much choiceworthiness that theory gives the action, multiply it by your credence, sum over theories, and pick the action with the highest total. Call it MEC. I know the usual pressure points — intertheoretic comparability, risk attitudes about moral disagreement, theories that only give ordinal rankings, tiny credences in fanatical theories — so don't just list those. What I want is a map of the view's actual exposure. What would have to be true for MEC to be wrong? In particular: what does MEC predict that its rivals (do what your most probable theory says, avoid the worst-case moral downside) don't, such that those predictions could themselves look wrong; which cases, arguments, or results would be decisive against it rather than merely awkward; and which of these would a defender of MEC concede as counting against it, versus which would they dismiss as problems for everyone? Has the view been patched and qualified to the point where nothing is allowed to count against it anymore, and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind? The thing that most shakes my confidence is a single theory inside my own credence distribution that condemns the MEC procedure itself — say, a virtue-theoretic view on which treating right action as the output of a weighted sum is itself a vice. Is that just one more term in the sum, or is it the kind of objection MEC can't swallow?
