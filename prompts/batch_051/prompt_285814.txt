I keep running into this argument in discussions of the counterfeit-people worry (the Dennett-flavored fear that AI lets anyone flood the public square with fake humans who advocate whatever their paid to, so you never know whether you're arguing with a person or a product). It seems to prove too much, but I can't find the bad step. (1) What makes counterfeit people wrong is that they are personae with no real, accountable human behind them: no reputation to lose, nobody to shame or sue, and trivially cheap to multiply. (2) Ghostwriters, PR flacks, corporate spokespeople, and pseudonymous forum accounts are also manufactured personae sitting between a claim and whichever human is ultimately responsible; they are often hard to hold accountable, and they can be produced in bulk. (3) If feature F is what makes counterfeit people wrong, and F is present in those older practices, then those older practices are wrong for the same reason. (4) But most of us treat ghostwriting and pseudonymity as legitimate, or at worst mildly sleazy; we don't treat them as attacks on the public sphere itself. So either the anti-counterfeit-person argument proves too much, or we should be much more worried about ordinary PR and anonymity than anyone actually is. Which premise do I reject first, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it force me to say about ghostwriting and pseudonymity? End with \answer{...} containing a short label or premise number for the step you reject first.
