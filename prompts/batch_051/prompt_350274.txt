I am a student in a course on game theory and the philosophy of rationality, and the unit on the centipede game has left me puzzled about why the surrounding debate feels so frozen. Backward induction appears to settle the game: at the final node a player would take the larger payoff, so the player at the previous node should take, and so on back to the very first move, where the first player should end the game immediately. In the laboratory, however, subjects routinely pass well beyond the first move, and many theorists are reluctant to call them irrational for it. The standard reply is that experimental subjects lack common knowledge of rationality, or that their choices are shaped by fairness, trust, or other concerns not represented in the listed payoffs. Critics answer that this concedes the crucial point: add any uncertainty about the other player's reasoning, any other-regarding preference, or any relationship between the players, and one is no longer analyzing the game backward induction was supposed to solve. They also press a sharper objection—that once a player deviates from the path backward induction prescribes, the very observation of that deviation is inconsistent with common knowledge of rationality, so the chain of reasoning can no longer tell the remaining player what to do after a deviation. This exchange has been going around for decades without, as far as I can tell, anyone moving.

What I would like is a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a verdict on who is right. Is it a substantive dispute about what rationality requires, a verbal dispute about the word 'rational,' a dispute about which features belong in the model of the game, or a case of two different projects—one formal and normative, one descriptive and behavioral—being conducted under the single heading of the solution to the centipede game? My own hunch is that much of the apparent conflict comes from an ambiguity about what the game itself is supposed to be: a fully specified mathematical object in which the players' rationality and information are stipulated, or a real social situation in which payoffs, common knowledge, and motives are all to some degree open to interpretation. But I am not confident that hunch survives the details. Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this is, grounded in what each side actually says, and say what would count as progress given that diagnosis—a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question—or why we should expect the impasse to persist.
