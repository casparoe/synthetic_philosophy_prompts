okay so i've been reading berkeley on money — the querist, the one that's just a list of questions? — and i want to know whether my slightly fancier version holds up. i've been calling it 'ticket monism,' or sometimes 'queue-jump theory.' money is not a thing, and not exactly a representation of value. a note in my pocket is a standing promise that other people, right now, will let me take my place ahead of them for goods and labour. the 'value' is not in the note, it's in the shared willingness. the mood is the economy. so here are the commitments:

- hoarding cash is cancelling a claim on the world and mistaking the cancellation for the thing claimed. a pile of notes is a stack of unused theatre tickets: less like wealth, more like a heavily documented refusal to go to the show.
- the actual wealth of a nation is in its inhabitants' industry and mutual trust; gold and reserves are the scoreboard not the game. i'm fairly sure this is just berkeley — money as a counter, credit and work as the real power — and it fits his whole immaterialism, because if a coin is just a stable idea then its buying power was never anywhere but in minds.
- inflation is the public quietly renegotiating how far ahead each old ticket lets you jump. it devalues everyone's place in the queue at once, which is why it feels like theft and egalitarianism at the same time.

i assume this already exists under other names — chartalism, credit theory of money, maybe MMT — but i can't tell where mine stops and those begin, or whether mine has anything of its own. tell me.

and here's where i can feel it breaking but can't finish the objections myself:

- bank runs: people stop accepting the tickets, so the mood becomes the reality, so the money really does stop working. is that a successful prediction of the theory or just a fatal circularity? how would i tell the difference?
- taxes: if a note is a ticket, something has to have started the queue. i suspect the state is the only thing that can get the first tickets accepted by demanding them back. is that an objection to me or just the missing first chapter?
- the gold bug: a coin stamped with gold is still just a ticket, so why do people behave as if the metal is doing the work? and is that collective belief itself something my theory has to count as real wealth, which would make the theory kind of circular?

give me the strongest steelman you can, tell me who i've accidentally reinvented, then rank the three strongest objections and say whether any of them is fatal rather than just uncomfortable. and be honest about whether 'mood' is doing too much work.
