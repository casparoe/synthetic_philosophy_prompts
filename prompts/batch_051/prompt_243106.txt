A friend has recently become convinced that having children is always morally wrong. When I pressed him for a justification, he kept returning to the phrase "Better Never to Have Been". I have since seen the same phrase used in discussions of whether the human species should die out, and it seems to be made to do different work each time: sometimes it sounds like a judgement about how good a particular life is, sometimes like a judgement about the value of a world containing no people at all, and sometimes like a claim about what prospective parents owe to possible future children. I suspect the phrase is carrying more weight in my friend's argument than it can bear, but I cannot yet say exactly what it means or which of its meanings would actually support his conclusion.

Please lay out, as a menu of distinct readings, the precise theses that "Better Never to Have Been" could be used to express. For each reading, state it in a form precise enough to be assessed, say whether it is trivial, plausible but contentious, or probably false, and explain what else would have to be true for that reading to support the conclusion that procreation is always wrong. In particular, please separate (a) claims about the well-being of a person who exists; (b) claims about the impersonal value of a possible world in which no one exists; and (c) claims about the rightness or wrongness of the act of bringing a person into existence. Also distinguish readings that depend on an asymmetry between absent pleasures and absent pains from readings that do not.

Please write the answer in British English, and end with a final line that is a one-sentence summary of which reading is doing the real work in the anti-natalist argument.
