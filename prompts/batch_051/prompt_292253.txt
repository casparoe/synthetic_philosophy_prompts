I hold the following view, and I want you to stress-test it rather than defend it.

The view: a rational agent who is in the middle of deliberating about what to do cannot, at that same time, coherently assign determinate probabilities to her own possible future choices, except where the choice is degenerate, already effectively made, or pre-committed. Prediction and deliberation are different stances toward the same future act. From the predictive stance the act is an event to be estimated; from the deliberative stance it is a live option to be settled by reasons. Assigning a probability to one of my own options either collapses into an intention (when the assignment feeds back into the choice) or pretends that something I am about to settle is already a chancy fact (when it does not feed back).

What would count against this view? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, drawn from anywhere they can be found: decision-theoretic cases (Newcomb-style choices, randomized acts, mixed strategies in games where assigning probabilities to one's own play seems mandatory), introspection and self-knowledge (predicting lapses, akrasia, habit), the theory of probability itself (whether refusing to assign probabilities to one's own acts violates the norm of having a credence over every proposition one can formulate, or violates coherence or exchangeability), and cases where deliberation has ended but the act is not yet performed. For each defeater, say which specific claim in the view it attacks, whether it shows the view false as stated or only in need of qualification, whether the escape route is live or merely logically possible, and what a committed defender should concede.

Then give an honest assessment of whether the view, once qualified against all of this, has become unfalsifiable — a claim about stances rather than a risky claim about what rational agents can and cannot do — and whether that would be a problem for a view of this kind.

Format: first write the assessment as continuous prose, then append a table with these columns:
- Defeater: a short name for the case, argument, or result.
- Target: which part of the view it attacks (the coherence claim, the feedback claim, or the scope restriction to deliberation).
- Decisiveness: fatal, serious, awkward, or merely apparent, with a one-line justification.
- What a defender concedes: the minimal concession a committed defender must make.
- Surviving position: the qualified version of the view that remains if the defeater is absorbed.

Order the rows from most to least decisive.
