I recently had a long argument with a friend about negative utilitarianism, specifically about the lexical-threshold version according to which pains above some fixed intensity can never be outweighed by pleasures, while pains below it trade against pleasures in the ordinary way. My friend defended the threshold; I kept objecting that it avoids repugnant trade-offs only by making the location of the threshold arbitrary and the boundary between the two classes mysterious. The argument pushed me into a position I cannot find stated anywhere, and I want to know whether it is defensible or just a confused restatement of the same idea.

I have been calling the position "split-ledger hedonics." The proposal is that there are two ledgers, not one. In the first ledger are ordinary, below-threshold states. There, pleasure and pain may be exchanged, but only at rates sensitive to how close a pain is to the threshold: the nearer a pain is to the threshold, the more pleasure a given unit of it demands, with the rate rising without bound as the threshold is approached. In the second ledger are above-threshold states. Here no pleasure may be entered at all; the only admissible entries are above-threshold pains on the other side. My central claim is that an above-threshold pain is offset only by preventing or removing an above-threshold pain of at least equal intensity. I call this the symmetry requirement.

An example may help. A mild headache might be outweighed by a sufficient quantity of mild diversion, and a severe but still sub-threshold burn might be outweighed by very many ordinary pleasures. But no number of ordinary pleasures can enter the second ledger to offset even one moment of agony. At the same time, the symmetry requirement would allow the infliction of one agony to be offset by preventing another, equal or worse agony.

My question is whether this is coherent. Does the first ledger's rising exchange rate actually avoid the arbitrariness of a sharp threshold, or have I merely moved the sharp edge one step away while keeping all the same problems? Is the second ledger genuinely independent, or is it the lexical threshold restated as a metaphor about bookkeeping? Has anyone already defended something like this under a less clumsy name? And the place where I suspect the construction breaks is the symmetry requirement: it seems to permit balancing one person's agony against another's, provided only that the second is equal or worse, and I am not sure the position was entitled to that. Is that a repairable detail or a sign that the whole account fails?
