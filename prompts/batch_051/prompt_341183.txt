I teach a seminar on authority and interpretation, and there's a debate that keeps recurring in it, year after year, that never moves. The students who defend sola scriptura and the students who think you need some kind of magisterium (or at least a creedal community with the authority to settle disputes) talk past each other for three months and end up exactly where they started. The first group says the text is its own interpreter: the essentials are plain enough, and for the harder passages the text plus the Spirit's private witness is sufficient, so no merely human institution gets the last word. The second group says that's the whole problem — every reading is an interpretation, and once two sincere readers disagree about what is 'plain' or about what the Spirit is telling them, you need a judge. But then the judge is itself a text, or a body who's authority has to be read in turn, and the same regress starts one level up. Both sides, it seems to me, are stuck on the same staircase and only disagree about which step is the floor.

Here's my working theory, and I'm honestly not sure it's right: maybe the dispute was never really about exegesis, or even about which reading is correct. Maybe it's a disagreement about what interpretation itself has to terminate in — a public, appealable judgment that can in principle compel assent, versus a private, self-authenticating conviction that can't be appealed and doesn't need to be. If that's the diagnosis, then better hermeneutics can't settle it, because the two sides are operating with different pictures of where justification has to stop. Is that right? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that would count as genuine progress — some distinction, a reframing of the question, an ecclesiological move, a formal result about interpretive regresses — or should we expect the impasse to be permanent? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a historical survey of who said what in the Reformation.
