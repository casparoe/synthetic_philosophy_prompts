I'm in a reading group on Bayesian epistemology and we've got stuck on something. The informal slogan you hear everywhere is that if you give rational people enough shared evidence, their priors "wash out" and they converge, so disagreements that are purely down to a prior are supposed to be temporary. But when I look at the actual theorems, there seem to be at least two quite different things going on. There's the Doob-style consistency story, where a single agent's posterior concentrates on the true hypothesis (with prior probability one, under the right conditions), and there's the Blackwell-Dubins merging story, where two agents' predictive distributions merge over time even if they never fully agree about the hypothesis. These feel related but they dont seem to say the same thing.

Could you compare those two as attempts to make the slogan precise: Doob-style convergence to truth versus Blackwell-Dubins merging of opinions. In particular, which of the two does a better job of formalising the "priors wash out" claim, and does either of them actually license the practical thought that people with very different priors will end up agreeing once they've seen enough of the same evidence? I'd rather you not just list the theorems; I want a view on how they hang together and where the slogan overreaches.
