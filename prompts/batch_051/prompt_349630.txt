I am an undergraduate taking a course on law and the moral relevance of time, and a puzzle from the course has been bothering me. I would like you to help me think it through.

Consider two people, A and B, who commit frauds that are identical in every relevant respect: same method, same amount, same kind of victim, same degree of planning and culpability. A's victim discovers the fraud within a few months, and A is prosecuted and punished. B's victim discovers the fraud only after the limitations period has expired, when a box of financial records happens to be found. In B's case the documentary evidence is, if anything, stronger than in A's; the offender is alive and no less blameworthy; the victim still wants prosecution; and the usual rationales for statutes of limitations—fading memories, dead witnesses, degraded evidence, the state's interest in prompt investigation, the defendant's interest in eventual repose—do not seem to apply. Yet in many jurisdictions the law would punish A and bar prosecution of B.

I am struck by an asymmetry here. Ordinary moral judgment does not seem to treat the wrongness of an act as decaying with the years: if someone did wrong and was never held to account, we do not ordinarily think the wrong has become less wrong merely because time has passed. But the law, through statutes of limitations, treats the passage of time as extinguishing liability even where time has not damaged the evidence or changed the parties. Is that asymmetry justified, or is it merely an inconsistency between our moral judgments and our legal practice that we tolerate for administrative convenience?

More sharply: is there a defensible principle that makes the passage of time itself morally relevant to whether a wrong may still be answered for, rather than treating time as a rough proxy for evidentiary and practical difficulties? I would like you to test the strongest candidate justifications against nearby cases: what if the offender had deliberately concealed the evidence until the limitations period had passed; what if the victim had been incapacitated or deceived; what if the wrong were a killing rather than a fraud; what if the offender had since become a markedly different person? Please say whether the asymmetry between A and B should be kept, narrowed, or given up, and answer in American English.
