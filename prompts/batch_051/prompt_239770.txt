Consider a rating system in which hospitals are scored on numerical indicators such as the percentage of heart-failure patients readmitted within thirty days, and hospitals with worse scores face financial penalties. In response, some hospitals hire nurses to call patients after discharge and manage their medications; others change how patients are classified in records so that the hospital's case mix appears sicker, or hold patients in observation units rather than admitting them because observation stays are excluded from the readmission measure. The rating agency knows this second kind of response happens and periodically revises the measure; the hospitals' own executives call that response gaming and the first response genuine improvement. What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose about measurement, value, and human behavior? Reconstruct the implicit view of how an indicator relates to the underlying quality it is meant to stand in for, of what distinguishes gaming from real improvement, and of how people respond to being measured. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify any internal inconsistency. Finally, explain what would have to change in the practice if one instead held that any indicator used as a target gradually stops tracking what it was introduced to track, or that the underlying quality is not a stable feature of hospital performance prior to its being measured. Write your answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a letter or a long email, and use American English.
