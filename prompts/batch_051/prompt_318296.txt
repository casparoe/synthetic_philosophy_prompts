A friend walked me through this and I can't find the exit. (1) If I may do one permissible thing and I may do another, then—absent some further wrong-making feature—I may do them together; I may open my umbrella, and I may walk through the park, so I may walk through the park with it open. (2) Suppose A knows a true and damaging fact about B, owes B no confidentiality, and would not be acting maliciously in revealing it: then A may reveal it. (3) A may also ask B for money; a request for payment is not itself coercion. (4) When A says to B 'pay me, or I will reveal it,' A has combined a permissible disclosure made conditional with a permissible request, and nothing further has been added. (5) Therefore blackmail should be permissible. But that strikes almost everyone as false: blackmail seems clearly wrong, and most legal systems treat it as a crime. The natural exits also seem blocked: if I say the proposal is coercive, I still need to say why a neighbor who says 'pay me, or I'll build the shed I'm legally entitled to build' isn't likewise blackmail; and if I say the whole is not just the sum of its parts, I need to say what extra thing has been added and why it matters. Which step should be rejected, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep (1), because giving up on combining ordinary permissions seems too high a price.
