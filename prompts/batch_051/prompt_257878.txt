I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine. Every few weeks I need to decide whether a heated-looking dispute is a real disagreement about the world or a fight about words. After interviewing both sides, they often seem to accept the same facts and differ mainly over a classification — whether something is alive, a disease, a species, a cause, intelligent, conscious. I know there is philosophical work on verbal disputes, some of it under the heading of metaontology, including the idea that some disagreements are merely verbal while others, though verbal, still matter because the chosen term has practical stakes. I do not need a reading list; I need a reusable editorial procedure. It should get me from a proposed story to a justified framing decision in a few hours, on deadline, with an editor who wants a crisp conflict. Specifically, it should determine (a) whether the dispute is substantive — the parties are committed to different facts, predictions, or norms — or merely verbal; (b) if verbal, whether it is merely a labeling difference, to be reported as such, or whether the choice of term has practical consequences that make the dispute worth reporting as a fight; and (c) what to do when a party will not offer a definition or accept a paraphrase, and which steps remain irreducibly a matter of editorial judgment rather than a rule. Then run it on two hypothetical pieces: one in which neuroscientists agree on every measured fact but disagree about whether a reactivated pattern of brain activity is the same memory, and one in which clinicians and advocates agree on a symptom profile but disagree about whether that profile should be called a disease. Include one case where the procedure tells me to drop or recast the language my lede was built on, and say what I should write instead.
