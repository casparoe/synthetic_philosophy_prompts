At a church committee meeting about whether to introduce a new style of music into Sunday worship, someone invoked the regulative principle — roughly, the view that acceptable public worship is limited to what God has prescribed — and closed the discussion with the line that, in worship, whatever is not commanded is forbidden. I know the slogan has a long history in Reformed and Puritan debates about worship, but it seems to compress several distinct principles, and I suspect the conclusion about the music depends on which of them is doing the work. Disambiguate the slogan into the distinct precise claims it could be making. In particular, distinguish readings that differ along at least these axes: its scope (public worship only, all corporate church life, or the whole of the Christian life); what counts as a command (an explicit scriptural precept, an approved biblical example, or a conclusion properly drawn from Scripture); what is being forbidden (the practice itself, the practice as an element of worship, or the imposition of the practice on a congregation); and whether the principle governs the elements of worship, its circumstances, or both. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or an overreading that the regulative principle does not support, and identify which reading would actually support the conclusion that the new style of music must be excluded.
