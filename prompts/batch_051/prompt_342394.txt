Our philosophy of probability group has been stuck on the reference class problem for about a month, and I'm starting to suspect we're not actually arguing about any one thing. The recurring example is "what's the probability I'll live to eighty?" You can class me with men, American men, men with my health markers, men in my zip code, men with my exact genome, and the number jumps around every time. None of us thinks the class is just read off the world anymore, but the frequentists want to say the solution is something like the narrowest class with stable-enough statistics (and maybe some condition about random place selection), the Bayesians say it's a modeling decision that gets absorbed by priors and doesn't need a unique answer, and the propensity people keep insisting there's a single chance of my reaching eighty that the classes are noisily tracking. We agree on all the textbook moves — Venn, Reichenbach, Hajek's point that the problem isn't just a frequentist embarrassment — and still nobody's budged.

So what kind of disagreement is this at bottom? Is it a verbal fight about the word "probability," a hidden normative dispute about how much simplicity, sample size, or projectibility a reference class should have, or a factual disagreement about whether there are such things as propensities at all? My hunch is that the propensities question is doing most of the work underground, but I'm not sure. And if you had to say one thing that could actually move the argument — a theorem, a distinction, a different question — what would it be, or should we just accept that the sides are solving different problems?
