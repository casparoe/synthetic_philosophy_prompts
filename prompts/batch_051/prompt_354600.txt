I'm drafting a novel set in a small town in upstate New York that's governed by an assembly, and every contested issue gets settled by a recorded vote on a bundle of yes/no propositions — not on the final policy itself, but on the reasons behind it, with the final policy supposed to follow mechanically from whichever reasons carry a majority. (There's a whole backstory about a bad land deal that made them distrust any decision made without on-the-record reasons; doesn't matter.) My protagonist eventually notices that the town can vote yes on a majority of the premises and still end up committed to a conclusion almost nobody actually voted for, or that the same set of individual votes can be recombined to give opposite outcomes depending on what gets tallied first. This is obviously the discursive dilemma / doctrinal paradox, but I don't want the novel to just name-drop it; I want the institution's failure to be argued all the way down.

So here's the actual ask: write me the desiderata that a theory of group judgment — of what it takes for a collective body to genuinely believe or decide something — has to meet. What must an adequate account get right? I'm not looking for a survey of positions; I want the requirements themselves, with the case behind each. Specifically cover at least: when we're entitled to say the assembly as a body decided or believed something (as opposed to thirty-one people happening to agree), what role logical consistency has to play, whether majority rule on each proposition and joint consistency can both be respected at once, the difference between a group that's wrong and a group that's indecisive or incoherent, and whether group belief has to be closed under implication the way an individual's ideally is. Then flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of particular camps, and tell me which subsets, if any, can't be jointly satisfied — that's the part I'll actually use, since my protagonist has to explain the flaw to people who have lived under the system their whole lives. I ocasionally see people treat the group's attitude as just an average of individual attitudes, and I'd like to know why that is or isn't enough. You can invent the close-call cases rather than borrowing real ones; that's easier for me to adapt.
