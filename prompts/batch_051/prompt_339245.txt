I'm currently in a high school philosophy of religion elective, and we have begun reading about the medieval Islamic dispute between the Mu'tazila and the Ash'ari over divine command. As I understand it, the Mu'tazila held that at least some moral distinctions hold independently of God's will, so that God's justice is a real constraint on what He can command and reason can recognize wrongness on its own. The Ash'ari, by contrast, held that good and evil are whatever God commands and prohibits, so there is no independent standard of justice outside God's will against which His acts could be measured.

Two Qur'anic passages stood out to me as pulling in opposite directions. One English translation renders 21:23 as: "He cannot be questioned about what He does, but they will ˹all˺ be questioned." That seems to fit the Ash'ari insistence that God's acts are not answerable to any standard we bring to them. Another passage, 4:40, is rendered: "Indeed, Allah never wrongs ˹anyone˺—even by an atom’s weight." That seems to presuppose that there is a fact of the matter about what would count as His wronging someone, independent of His will, which sounds more Mu'tazili.

Are these verses actually in tension with each other? If so, can they be reconciled, and what readings of the key terms would each reconciliation require? Which reconciliations are genuinely available to the texts, and which are special pleading that quietly reduces one verse to the other? If they cannot be fully reconciled without cost, which verse do you think bears more of the cost, and what does that imply about the broader debate?
