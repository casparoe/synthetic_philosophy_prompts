I teach an undergraduate philosophy-of-mathematics seminar (fourteen second- and third-year students, one ninety-minute meeting) and I want an activity that makes the axiom of choice feel like a genuine extra commitment rather than an obvious logical triviality. The students are comfortable with set notation and with functions, but they currently treat “there exists a choice function” as meaning the same thing as “there is an explicit procedure for choosing,” and I want the activity to break that identification before I name it.

Please design a runnable exercise in which the class tries to specify choice functions for an ordered sequence of families of nonempty pairwise disjoint sets of real numbers. Start with an easy family where a formula plainly works (for example, every nonempty subset of the natural numbers has a least element, so “choose the least element” is an explicit rule), then move through a stage where the choosing feels arbitrary but still manageable, and end with a family in which no finitely statable rule seems available: choose one representative from each equivalence class of the real numbers under the relation x − y ∈ ℚ, where each class is countable and dense in the usual order and therefore has no least element to reach for. The wall should be hit during the exercise, not delivered in a lecture afterward: give roles or rules, materials, a timeline, and a debrief. In the debrief, map what happened onto the distinction between definable choice and mere existence, state the axiom of choice as the extra claim that a selector exists anyway, and explain what sort of object that selector is in the final case and why its existence should feel surprising (if you call it a Vitali set or call it non-measurable, explain enough that a student who has not seen measure theory can follow). Be honest about what the exercise cannot reproduce: the room, the slips of paper, and the hour are finite, so the activity cannot itself perform an uncountable simultaneous choice, and the facilitator must supply the step from “we could not write down a rule” to “there is nevertheless a function.” Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — in particular, students will likely either smuggle in a rule that quietly fails for the dense classes or insist that the task is simply impossible and therefore proof of nothing — and how I should handle each. Write your response in continuous prose, like a memo to me, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
