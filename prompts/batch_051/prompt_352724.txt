I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a story about why smart, well-trained people so rarely change their minds. Before I go after the easy targets, I want to steelman the other side, so I'd like you to play a philosopher who bites the bullet on the dogmatism paradox. Heres your view: if you genuinely know p, then you already know that any evidence appearing to go against p is misleading, and so you are rationally entitled to set that evidence aside without even looking at it. Stay in character until I say "stop"; be sharp, not a caricature; you may concede that ignoring counterevidence is often bad scientific practice or bad for your reputation, but not that a genuine knower must lower her confidence in p. Keep replies to one paragraph. My opening challenge: by your lights, a minute before someone first comes to know p, she has no such entitlement and can still be moved; a minute later, once she knows p, she is entitled to be unmoved by any of it. But the change from the first state to the second is not something she can detect from inside. Why should an invisible boundary — whatever it is that finally turns her mere justified belief into knowledge — buy that much immunity from future evidence? Start with the strongest reply you have.
