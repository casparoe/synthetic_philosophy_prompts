I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about the Mencius–Xunzi dispute over human nature, and I have not been able to resolve the tension it left me with. Xunzi's position attracts me as a theory: he starts from the plainly observable fact that people are born with desires and aversions that, left to themselves, produce contention and disorder, and he builds from this a strong case for why ritual, law, teaching, and accumulated effort—not the spontaneous heart—must do the real work of making us good. On his picture, goodness is an artifact, like the shape a press-frame imposes on a piece of wood; it is the product of human craft, and that explains both why moral achievement is slow and why it is so easily undone once the shaping institutions are removed. And yet I cannot shake the Mencian intuition, which my friend kept pressing against all of this: when someone sees a child about to fall into a well, the alarm and compassion arise before calculation, before training, before any thought of reputation or reward, and they present themselves as the expression of a native moral capacity—something recognized rather than manufactured. If Mencius is right that morality is the growth of innate sprouts, why does its growth so predictably require culture, ritual, and correction? If Xunzi is right that virtue is artifice, why does the spontaneous moral response feel like discovery rather than construction? I want a negotiated verdict rather than a victor: should this intuition be debunked as untrustworthy phenomenology and dropped, should the Xunzian theory be amended so that it no longer treats native moral tendencies as negligible, or is there a reading of xing and wei on which the two positions are genuinely compatible without merely talking past each other about the word nature?
