I have been covering the dispute among cognitive scientists and AI researchers about whether large language models literally have beliefs, goals, or understanding, and I keep running into an argument that seems to cut across the empirical results. Daniel Dennett's notion of the intentional stance was introduced as a pragmatic strategy: treat a system as a rational agent whose behavior is caused by beliefs and desires, but adopt that stance only when the intentional vocabulary earns its keep in predictive power. On this view, we do not first establish that a system has an inner mental life; we ask whether describing it as wanting, expecting, or deciding compresses and anticipates its behavior better than physical or design-level descriptions would. Dennett's criterion was deliberately not a claim about hidden mental substance. It was about whether belief-desire explanation is the best available predictor for the system at hand.

The development that seems to strain this argument is how contemporary language models are actually built. Their dominant training objective is next-token prediction over enormous quantities of human text, not action selection in an environment where the system pursues outcomes, updates on the consequences of its choices, or bears the costs of acting on a false belief. The result is a system that emits fluent intentional language because such language pervades the training distribution, while the system's optimization target is statistical fidelity to human text rather than practical success. Does Dennett's argument survive contact with a system whose task is to predict language rather than to act in the world? Which premise is under pressure: the link between rational action and belief-desire explanation, the assumption that the stance is vindicated by predicting observed behavior rather than by producing plausible text, or something else entirely? If a repaired version says we may attribute beliefs to such systems only in a scoped, as-if, storytelling register that does no real predictive work beyond the language itself, is that still Dennett's intentional stance, or has the argument quietly turned into a different one?
