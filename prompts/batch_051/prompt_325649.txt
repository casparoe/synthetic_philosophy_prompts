I spent most of my career as a working biologist and picked up the habit of thinking group selection was just a fallacy you outgrew. Lately I've been reading the multilevel-selection people and I realize I can't state crisply what seperates the claims anymore. I'd like you to write me a decision tree, something I could use to sort arguments rather than people, that takes someone's explanation of why an evolved trait persists and routes it to one of these leaves: gene-level/replicator, organism-level, group/multilevel selection, species- or clade-level, or no separate level, just different bookkeeping of the same facts. Build it from diagnostic questions a person could answer without knowing the labels. The questions should get at things like: does the trait lower the bearer's own relative fitness within its local group; is there between-group variance in fitness that can actually be selected, and is it strong enough relative to within-group variance; is the group a real vehicle with its own birth and extinction, or just a context that changes what's good for an individual. Then test each leaf against concrete cases, at least: a meiotic-drive allele that cheats in gamete production, alarm-calling of the risky warn-the-group sort, the stalk-vs-spore split in a social amoeba, and one artificial-selection setup of the select-among-groups sort. If two positions land at different leaves but make the same predictions for a case, the tree should say so rather than force them apart; same if kin-selection and group-selection accounts of a case are just two ways of partitioning the same numbers. And flag any branch of the possible answer-combinations that no sane position occupies, with a one-line reason: incoherent, unworkable, or just never explored. Aim for something a bright undergrad could follow in about seven or eight questions.
