I am a student in a seminar on distributive justice, and we have been working through the question whether the number of people affected should count in itself in rescue decisions. Our instructor has given verdicts on several cases, all with unequal stakes. A scarce treatment could save one stranger from death or prevent five strangers from each losing a foot below the ankle; she said the single stranger should be saved, and she added that the five on the other side did not by themselves overturn the stronger claim. In another case, the choice was between saving one stranger from permanent total blindness and preventing eight strangers from losing a finger; she again saved the single person. In a third, she said she would save one stranger from death rather than prevent ten strangers from suffering severe but fully temporary burns. Next week we take up the first equal-stakes case: one stranger needs the entire dose of a drug to survive, while five strangers would each need one fifth of the same dose to survive, with no other differences among the six of them. I want to anticipate her verdict. Please lay out the leading rules that fit her record so far, at least a strict no-aggregation view, a tiebreaker view on which numbers matter only among equally strong individual claims, and a weighted-sum view. Say what each predicts for the one-versus-five case, then give your best prediction and the reason for preferring it, name a further case that would separate whatever candidate rules remain, and tell me how confident you are in the prediction and which part of it you are least sure about.
