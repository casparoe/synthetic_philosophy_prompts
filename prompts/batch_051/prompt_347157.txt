I am a statistician by training, comfortable with likelihood ratios, posterior odds, multiple comparisons, and selective inference, but I have never read the philosophical literature on the epistemology of anecdote. I keep hearing otherwise careful people say that some striking coincidence in their lives is evidence that "something is going on": a dream that came true the next day, a phone call arriving at the very moment they were thinking of the caller, a stranger on a train who turns out to have grown up in the same small town. The standard skeptical position I want to understand better is the debunking argument that such anecdotes are weak evidence for non-chance explanations because the denominator of opportunities is enormous, because people record the hits and forget the misses, and because the description of the "remarkable" event is usually fitted to the event after it has happened. I am not sure I could state that argument rigorously myself.

Restate this skeptical position in the vocabulary of a working statistician — likelihood ratios, reference classes and ill-defined denominators, multiple testing and selective inference, prior odds, and the distinction between a pre-registered prediction and a pattern noticed only post hoc — and do it separately for three kinds of case: (i) the birthday-paradox case, where some coincidence of a broad type is nearly guaranteed; (ii) the dream-that-comes-true case, where a specific content matches a later event; and (iii) the simultaneous-call case, where the mutuality is itself discovered only after the fact. Then give me a residue report: which parts of the skeptical argument survive the translation unchanged, which are distorted by being forced into statistical terms, and which have no expression in the statistical vocabulary at all. My suspicion is that the genuinely hard conceptual issue is what gets to count as "the event" whose probability is being assessed — the reference class problem — and that there is a first-person/third-person asymmetry in how an anecdote presents itself as evidence that statistics may be unable to capture. Tell me whether the residue shows that the skeptical position has been leaning on an under-specified notion of "an event of the same type," or that the statistical framework is missing distinctions it needs. Keep the translation honest: do not build the conclusion that the skeptic wins into the choice of statistical machinery.
