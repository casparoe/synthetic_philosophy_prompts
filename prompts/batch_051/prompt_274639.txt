I am a researcher for a state historical commission that arbitrates conflicts between a community's collective memory and the documentary record when a historical marker, a school curriculum, or the state's official account is contested. Next month the commission hears a case, and I need a memorandum predicting how it will rule if it tries to stay consistent with its own precedents.

The commission's prior decisions are these.

1. Renford Street fire (1931). The city's African American community has long maintained that the streetcar company deliberately set fire to a block of tenement housing to clear the land for a rail yard. The official fire marshal's report concluded the fire was accidental. The commission amended the official account to state that the fire was most likely set to clear the land. It relied on the fact that the original inquiry had interviewed only company and city officials and excluded neighborhood testimony, and on independent evidence: a fire-insurance map showing the company had purchased the affected lots two months before the fire, and a retired firefighter's private letter describing orders to protect the rail yard while the homes burned.

2. Cold Creek removal (1863). An indigenous nation's oral histories recount that a territorial militia forced their ancestors on a winter march along Cold Creek and that several died at a camp the official state history does not mention. The commission amended the official history to include the march, the route, and the deaths. It cited both the systematic absence of indigenous testimony from the archival record and a contemporaneous trader's ledger recording the supply of the militia during a winter removal along the same route.

3. West Fork dam failure (1947). A mostly white farming town has long taught that the power company opened the dam gates without warning, causing the flood that destroyed the lower town. The state engineering record, stream-gauge readings, and rainfall data attribute the flood to extreme rainfall, with the gates opened only after the reservoir had already overtopped. The commission declined to revise the official account. It found the local account vivid and sincere, but there was no independent support for it and no sign that the engineering record had been compromised.

4. Harrow Green skirmish (1778). A local heritage society claims a Revolutionary War skirmish was fought on the town green. Muster rolls, contemporary correspondence, and a 1901 battlefield survey place the skirmish two miles outside town. The commission declined to amend the location and approved only a small interpretive panel noting the local tradition; it found no independent support for the town-green tradition and no reason to distrust the archival record.

5. Driscoll parish register (1888). A single family's tradition says an ancestor was married in 1888 in a particular chapel; the parish register records a different year and parish. The commission upheld the register, declined to amend the genealogical record, and filed the family's version in a separate oral-history collection, finding no independent support for the family tradition and no reason to doubt the register.

The commission has never written down a general principle; its practice is recoverable only from these decisions.

The case on next month's docket is Tanners' Quarter (1952). The city's old African American working-class neighborhood was demolished under an ordinance the city described as slum clearance and redevelopment. The official record presents the project as a public-health and redevelopment measure. The neighborhood's residents and their descendants have maintained, with near-total uniformity across many families for seventy years, that the clearance was deliberately designed to disperse the community and break up its ward organization, and that a named city commissioner admitted as much at a public meeting. No transcript or contemporaneous newspaper account of that meeting survives, and there is no independent document stating the purpose of the clearance.

Given those precedents, what should the commission decide in Tanners' Quarter if it wants to be consistent with itself? In the memorandum, please do the following: identify two or three principles that fit all five prior decisions; state what each principle would require in the new case; identify which prior decision is the closest analog; offer a best guess about the commission's ruling, with reasons for preferring one principle over the others; and, if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the new case, say what principle the commission would be adopting with each available decision and specify a further piece of evidence or a further case that would cleanly separate the remaining principles.
