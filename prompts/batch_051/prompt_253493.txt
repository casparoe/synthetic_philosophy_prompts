I'm a third-year political theory PhD student and the grad student rep on my university's disciplinary review panel. We're hearing a case from last month where about thirty graduate students and two junior faculty occupied the lobby of the main administration building for roughly a day and a half, protesting the university's continued research contracts with a defense firm. They were public about it, nonviolent, and told security from the start that they'd accept whatever sanctions came; the problem is that for a few hours they blocked the main doors, so staff had to be walked around to a side entrance. That detail is basically what's splitting us.

One camp says this is textbook civil disobedience — conscientious, public, nonviolent, willing to take the penalty — and the panel should largely defer to that, maybe a formal warning at most. The other camp says the blockade tips it from persuasion into coercion: they weren't just appealing to the community's sense of justice, they were making staff and students run their day on the occupiers' schedule, so the usual civil-disobedience defense doesn't cover what actually happened. The undergrad rep keeps arguing, separately, that the university has no standing to punish people for protesting its complicity in arms production at all. There's also a quieter worry from one faculty member that declining to sanction isn't neutral either — it's the panel taking a position on the protest by other means, so "staying out of the politics" isn't really an option.

The chair has asked me to draft a short decision memo with a concrete recommendation — no sanction, a formal warning, disciplinary probation, something like that — plus reasons the rest of the panel could actually act on, and to make sure the strongest argument on the losing side gets a fair run rather than a strawman. Please actually commit: pick a course of action and defend it, don't lay out three readings and leave the vote where it was. I should add I'm not asking you to settle whether the defense contracts are wrong; take the students' sincerity as given and think about what a panel like ours is doing when it punishes or declines to punish them. And fair warning, I've been in this meeting too long — its honestly started to feel like we're rehearsing the same two speeches, so a draft that cuts through would genuinely help.
