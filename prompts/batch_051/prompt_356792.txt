I am a student in an upper-level bioethics course, and we have been working through Persson and Savulescu's thesis that human moral psychology has not kept pace with the destructive powers modern science and technology have placed in our hands, and that moral bioenhancement—biomedical means of improving our moral dispositions, rather than education or institutional reform alone—may eventually be needed to close that gap. I want to set aside the abstract question of whether moral bioenhancement is desirable and instead think through how a concrete attempt to implement it would behave.

Here is the proposal. A national regulator creates a licensing scheme for catastrophic-risk roles, defined to include officials who can authorize major pandemic measures, operators of early-warning and escalation systems, and safety officers with real decision authority at high-containment biolabs, advanced AI laboratories, and civilian nuclear facilities. As a condition of license renewal, anyone in one of these roles must complete an approved moral-enhancement protocol: a supervised course of an empathy- and impulse-control-enhancing drug, followed by a structured decision-training program and a battery of tests meant to measure relevant dispositions—for instance, resistance to sunk-cost reasoning, resistance to authority and group pressure, and the way one trades off risks to one's own institution against risks to outsiders or future generations. A person can be denied renewal solely on the strength of these test results, even with an unblemished professional record. To prevent coaching, the board does not disclose which items a candidate failed or how items are weighted, and it gives only a summary verdict plus a right to retake the battery after a waiting period.

Please analyze this as a mechanism. What would it incentivize among the people subject to it, among the institutions that employ them, and among companies developing and selling moral-enhancement interventions? How would each of these parties game it, and what workarounds become especially tempting? What second-order effects should we expect on how these roles select for and define good judgment, on where responsibility falls when a catastrophic decision goes wrong, and on public trust in the regulator and the regulated institutions? Finally, which failure modes could be fixed by redesigning the tests, the disclosure rules, or the appeals process, and which are likely baked into any scheme that tries to license ethical dispositions rather than conduct?

In your answer, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the literature on moral enhancement—for example, whether any existing biomedical intervention reliably improves moral dispositions, or whether Persson and Savulescu's diagnosis of the mismatch between human moral psychology and technological power should be accepted—and please do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus. Please write in American English.
