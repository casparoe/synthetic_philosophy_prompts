I have been struck by an asymmetry in ordinary moral thinking about population size, and I want to know whether it can be justified or whether it is simply an inconsistency we live with.

On the one hand, ordinary judgment treats mere addition as morally neutral. A couple is not missing something, and is not acting badly, if it stops at two children rather than three. A city of 950,000 equally happy residents is not plainly a better city than one of 940,000. A species with a million flourishing members is not plainly a more valuable species than one with a hundred thousand. Holding average quality of life fixed, a larger population is not treated as a better outcome.

On the other hand, ordinary judgment treats subtraction by deaths as a serious moral loss. A couple that loses one of its two children has suffered a tragedy, not merely moved to the one-child state it would have been content with. A plague that kills ten thousand residents is condemned as a disaster even if the surviving city has the same average well-being as before. The death of the last hundred cranes is treated as an irreplaceable catastrophe, not as a slightly smaller population of equally well-off cranes.

So the same person seems to hold that bigger by addition is morally neutral while smaller by deaths is morally worse. Some candidate explanations: that we value average well-being rather than total; that only harms to existing individuals count, so deaths matter but possible people cannot be benefited; that what matters is not the number of members but the continued existence and sufficiency of the unit, so additions above a threshold count for little while losses below it count for much.

I want the strongest principle you can find. Test each candidate against the family, the city, and the species, and also against a further case: conservationists who spend heavily to keep a crane population from falling from 200 to 190 may treat spending to raise it from 200 to 210 as optional. Say whether the asymmetry should be kept as justified, revised, or given up, and what your preferred principle implies about the original pair of judgments.
