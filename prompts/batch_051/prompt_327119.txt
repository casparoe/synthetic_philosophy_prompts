Ok so I keep running into these three fairness criteria for a recidivism risk score, plus a theorem that says you cant have all three at once. The three criteria:

- Predictive value parity (sometimes called calibration for a binary decision): among the people the tool flags as high risk, the fraction who actually reoffend should be the same across groups.
- False positive rate parity: among the people who would not have reoffended, the fraction the tool flags as high risk should be the same across groups.
- False negative rate parity: among the people who do reoffend, the fraction the tool misses should be the same across groups.

The impossibility result, as I understand it, says that if two groups reoffend at different base rates and the predictor is imperfect, you can satisfy at most two of the three. Something has to give.

Now the borderline case. Group A has 1,000 people and a 52% base rate, so 520 reoffend. Group B has 1,000 people and a 48% base rate, so 480 reoffend. The tool flags 180 people in group A as high risk, of whom 108 reoffend and 72 dont. It flags 195 in group B, of whom 117 reoffend and 78 dont.

So for group A the PPV is 108/180 = 60% and the FPR is 72/480 = 15%. For group B the PPV is 117/195 = 60% and the FPR is 78/520 = 15%. Two of the three match exactly. But the false negative rate is 412/520 = 79.2% for group A and 363/480 = 75.6% for group B, so the third is off by 3.6 points. The vendor says that gap is structural, not a bug: closing it would break one of the other two, because of the base-rate difference.

Does this algorithm count as fair under the three criteria? And does the case show that one of the three criteria is the one we actually shouldnt be using, or that fairness should be assessed some other way?
