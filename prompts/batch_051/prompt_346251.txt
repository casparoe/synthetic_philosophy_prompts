I am a retired evolutionary biologist, and only in the last few years have I begun to read the philosophy of science with any seriousness. A friend in my reading group recently pressed on me Karl Popper’s 1977 Darwin Lecture, *Natural Selection and the Emergence of Mind*, and I am having difficulty with a passage near the start of its programmatic section. Popper writes:

> Natural selection is, obviously, Darwin's most central theme. But I shall not confine myself to this theme alone. I shall also follow Darwin in his approach to the problem of body and mind, both the mind of man and the animal mind.
> I shall try to show that the theory of natural selection supports a doctrine which I also support. I mean the unfashionable doctrine of mutual interaction between mind and brain.

My difficulty is this. I know that elsewhere in the same lecture Popper denies that Darwinism is a testable scientific theory, describing it instead as a metaphysical research programme, a framework within which testable scientific theories can be proposed. Yet here he says the theory of natural selection supports the doctrine of mind–brain interaction. I do not see how these two claims sit together: if natural selection is not itself testable, in what sense can it support anything? Is he using support in a different way from the empirical sense a working biologist would expect? And does his argument for mutual interaction between mind and brain depend on treating natural selection as an explanatory theory, or merely as a useful heuristic for generating testable hypotheses? Some members of my reading group have concluded that Popper simply changed his mind, or that he was never fully serious about the metaphysical-research-programme label. I suspect the passage is doing something more careful, but I cannot articulate what. Could you help me unpack the argumentative role of natural selection in this passage, and say whether the reading group’s conclusion is fair?
