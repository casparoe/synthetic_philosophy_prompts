I'm teaching the philosophy of language section of an undergraduate course, and I want to use George Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four as a serious test case rather than as the usual one-minute illustration of censorship. The section sets out a contrast between conceptual analysis and conceptual engineering. On the analysis side, the job is to describe the concept we actually have: what conditions something must meet to fall under it, what inferences it commits us to, and what it rules out. On the engineering side, sometimes called ameliorative analysis, the job is different: ask what the concept is doing for us, whether we would be better off revising or replacing it, and then propose a better one. A standard reply to the worry that this amounts to abandoning the original question is that abandoning the original question is not always a defect, since the original question may be confused, harmful, or unearned.

Newspeak is fiction's most thorough program of deliberate concept revision: the Party does not merely punish disfavored speech but rebuilds the vocabulary so that forbidden thoughts eventually cannot be thought at all, and the novel's closing appendix on Newspeak lays the project out as a technical procedure. I want a reading that treats the novel as making a philosophical argument about conceptual engineering and that is faithful in both directions: it does not flatten the novel into a slogan, and it does not pretend the philosophical position says only what the novel needs it to say. In particular: does the book show that conceptual engineering is inherently coercive or authoritarian, or only that it is a powerful instrument that authoritarian regimes can capture? Which features of the Party's project genuinely resemble a defensible ameliorative proposal, and which are different in kind rather than degree? Where does the novel itself resist the idea that concepts can be deliberately fixed, as opposed to being slowly warped by power and habit? I will use your response to structure the class discussion, so please write in American English and avoid outside citations or reading lists; argue from the novel and from the philosophical position itself.
