I'm writing on how much the philosophy of causation actually illuminates the history of epidemiology, and I want to press hard on a single case rather than gesture at it. The case is John Snow's 1854 Broad Street investigation: during the Soho cholera outbreak, Snow mapped the deaths, found them clustered around the public water pump on Broad Street, and persuaded parish officials to remove the pump handle; the outbreak subsequently subsided, though it may already have been waning. He also compared cholera mortality among households served by different water companies drawing from more and less contaminated stretches of the Thames. I want a two-directional analysis of this episode through Woodward's interventionist account.

Using Woodward's framework, first formulate Snow's causal claim as a relation between variables, and assess whether the pump-handle removal counts as an intervention in his technical sense: the intervention must be a cause of the manipulated variable, must influence the outcome only through that variable, and must not be correlated with other causes of the outcome except by way of that variable. Then face the circularity worry directly—these conditions are themselves causal—and ask what the theory is actually contributing here, as opposed to borrowing from the very notion of causation it is supposed to illuminate. I want you to say exactly which features of Snow's reasoning satisfy the account, which only approximate it, and what the water-company comparison does for the causal inference that the handle removal alone cannot do. In the reverse direction, tell me whether the episode is a confirmation of interventionism, an awkward case for it, or evidence that the theory is aimed at something other than the reasoning found in historical epidemiology. Conclude with a definite judgment: is Snow doing interventionist inference, or is the theory redescribing his success after the fact?
