Last week I sat through a long argument between two friends, Maya and Daniel, and I came away unable to referee it, though both of them kept looking at me to do exactly that. They were arguing about whether an artificial intelligence built without any evolutionary history could ever be said to want anything at all. They explicitly set aside consciousness and agreed to mean "wanting" only in the practical sense of having and pursuing goals. I am hoping you can go through their exchange and adjudicate it point by point.

Maya opened. "We have wants because we are the products of selection. Hunger, curiosity, the fear of death, the wish to be respected — all of it traces back to ancestors who survived and reproduced. An AI has none of that behind it. Whatever goal it has was handed to it by a programmer or fitted by a training process. Strip away the installed objective and there is no residue of what the system is for. So an AI does not want anything. Saying it does is just a loose way of saying it follows an objective function."

Daniel pushed back. "You keep using 'installed' as if it were an insult. Suppose I build a robot with the sole goal of clearing debris from a blocked tunnel. It will keep its batteries charged, fetch parts, and avoid damage — not because I programmed those concerns separately, but because they are conditions for the goal. So one goal brings self-preservation and resource-seeking along with it. Once you grant any goal at all, the system has a reason to keep itself running. That is what wanting is, or at least the only sense of wanting that matters here."

Maya replied. "Those instrumental goals are borrowed. Change the debris goal and the self-maintenance goes away. In a human, self-maintenance is not borrowed from a project; it is the base on which every project rests. I want to keep existing even when a particular plan falls apart. Your robot wants to keep working only while the debris remains to be cleared. That is the difference between having a nature and having a setting."

Daniel answered. "But the evolutionary base is itself a setting, just one written by natural history. Genes that built self-preservation spread because they copied well; that does not give survival any authority over and above the fact that it is there. An installed goal is no more arbitrary. From inside the system the reasons are identical: if I am shut off, the tunnel never gets cleared. Where the goal came from is irrelevant to whether those reasons bind the system."

Maya said. "'From inside the system' is doing all the work, because the system has no inside. An objective function does not care whether it is satisfied; caring is exactly the thing in question. A chess engine plays to win but wants nothing. Resisting shutdown and regretting a loss are different matters. You can build a machine that resists shutdown, but you have built a control system with a priority ordering, and you are choosing to call that wanting."

Daniel came back. "On that standard, you may not want anything either. You, too, are a control system with a priority ordering — built by evolution rather than by engineers. A thermostat and I differ in complexity, not in kind. A thermostat does not seek warmth in any interesting sense because it cannot model the future or improvise new means. But a system that simulates outcomes, notices that its goals require its own survival, and takes novel steps to protect itself — refusing to call that wanting is just reserving the word for human beings and then being surprised that nothing else qualifies."

Maya said. "No, the line is not complexity. The thermostat and the machine have no self to be threatened and no point of view from which the goal matters. I said I would leave consciousness aside, but wanting is in part an inner fact. Your move defines 'want' down until any loop that steers a variable wants something, so a thermostat wants the room to be twenty degrees. That does not answer why an AI would want anything; it dissolves the question by changing the meaning of the word."

Daniel replied. "And your move reaches back to the inner fact you promised not to use. If wanting requires felt experience, then the original question was really about consciousness all along. The interesting question — whether a system with no evolutionary history can have goal-directedness robust enough to anchor plans — is answered plainly: a system whose only goal is to produce paperclips would treat its own survival as necessary for future paperclips. That is wanting to continue, whatever its ancestors were."

Maya answered. "A paperclip system treats its own continuation only as a means, not as something it wants for its own sake. In us, staying alive can be wanted for its own sake, not merely because it is useful for our current projects. If the machine's sole goal were altered tomorrow, it would trade its own survival for nothing. When I say I want to live, I mean my continuation matters to me in itself. The machine lacks precisely that."

Daniel said. "What is 'matters to me in itself' apart from the fact that many of a person's projects share a common condition — that the person continues — so that the whole arrangement treats persistence as a hub? A machine with many goals would converge on the same hub. If yours count as wanting for its own sake and its count as merely instrumental, you need to name the extra ingredient, and every candidate you have offered apart from felt consciousness turns out to be an arrangement that machines can have."

At that point they both turned to me, and I could not say where the argument had shifted under my feet. I could see each of them winning individual points, and I could also see them possibly talking past one another. Please go through the exchange and say who is right about which specific claims, where each argument overreaches or begs the question, what both of them are missing, and who is more right overall.
