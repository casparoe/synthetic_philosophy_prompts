I spent way too long last night arguing with a freind about whether "antimeme" names a real category or is just a cute word for stuff nobody remembers because its boring. I mean it in the plain sense: an idea that is bad at being remembered or passed along. I'm trying to turn my side of the argument into a short essay, and I'd like comments before I show it to anyone. Below is the essay in note form. Can you tell me (a) where it's most likely to be wrong, (b) whether the passive/active distinction is doing real work or just relabeling things, and (c) how to make the last point land without sounding like a fortune cookie? For each comment, say how confident you are in it, and single out the point you're least sure about overall. If the whole thing seems like word games, you can say that too.

Title idea: "Some ideas throw themselves away"

- The standard picture: ideas spread or don't spread because of how catchy, useful, simple, or emotionally grabbing they are. On that picture, an antimeme is just the low-fitness tail of the distribution: a random digit string, a boring fact, a fiddly distinction.

- I think this conflates two ways of being bad at spreading. A passive antimeme fails to spread because it has nothing going for it. An active antimeme fails to spread because part of its content directs the person who has it not to store it, not to trust it, or not to repeat it. The failed transmission isn't a side effect; it's a predictable effect of what the idea says.

- Candidate examples:
  1. "This does not affect you." Legal boilerplate and terms-of-service clauses are full of statements announcing their own irrelevance, and people skip them because of what they say, not just because they're long. The idea tells you not to retain it.
  2. "Your memory of this argument is already being rebuilt, not replayed." Believing that lowers how confidently you repeat the argument you think you remember, including that very point. The belief turns down its own host's volume.
  3. "You had to be there." Some experiences are antimemetic because their main content is that words will flatten them, so the act of sharing announces its own failure in advance.

- If this is right, an active antimeme needn't be trivial or false. It can be a deflating, self-applying truth that makes the person who holds it less inclined to insist on it in public. So a culture could lose some true ideas not because they were beaten by better ideas, but because they made their hosts quieter.

- Tentative last point: maybe the ideas that dominate a conversation aren't selected for truth; they're selected for making their hosts loud. Truths that make their hosts quiet get filtered out, and the information environment gets a little worse for reasons that aren't anyone's malice.
