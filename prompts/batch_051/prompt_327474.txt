I've been chewing on this since a thing at work. I was on a commitee that gave a unanimous thumbs-up to a rollout I was pretty sure would screw over a bunch of our long-term customers. The decision was already made before we met — my vote didn't change anything — but I didn't recuse or object either, and the "unanimous approval" got used in the announcement. I can't tell whether I'm on the hook or just a bystander with a lanyard.

So I'd like a thought experiment built to order. Use two views of complicity and define them up front. (1) The counterfactual view: you're only complicit if your contribution made the wrong more likely, more damaging, or harder to stop. (2) The ratification view: you're complicit if you attach your agency to the wrong — you endorse it, sign it, help make it look normal — even when your contribution doesn't change what happens.

Construct a case where the ratification view clearly counts the person as complicit and the counterfactual view clearly says no. Constraints: ordinary workplace or neighborhood setting (no mafia, no war, no trolley), the person isn't coerced and could refuse at moderate cost, and the contribution can't be a purely hollow gesture — the signature (or whatever it is) has to do real work in how the wrong is read by others, just not in whether or how badly it happens or whether anyone actually stops it. Keep the scenario to one paragraph. Then walk me through the reasoning each view gives, name the single feature of the case that's doing the work, and say what the case collapses into if you remove it. End with the strongest thing a defender of the counterfactual view could say to explain away the intuition that the person is complicit, and whether you'd buy it.
