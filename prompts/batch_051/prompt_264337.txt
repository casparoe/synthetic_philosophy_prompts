I'm a software engineer on a team building a support-ticket auto-tagging system — incoming tickets get classified so they route to the right queue. Every few weeks someone proposes a new category ("subscription confusion", "billing dispute", "feature request", that kind of thing), and I'm the one who has to turn it into something the system can actually use: a data model, a rule set, training examples, similarity features. The categories people talk about are fuzzy but the implementation has to be exact, and I keep re-inventing a different approach each time. The stakes aren't life-or-death but they aren't nothing: a badly modeled category means misrouted tickets, rework, and our PM getting angry emails. My PM also has to sign off on the approach, so I need to be able to explain why I chose it in two sentences. I've usually got maybe half a day per category before I have to commit, and I have no psychology or philosophy background, so I need something plain.

Someone pointed me to the cognitive-science debate about how people actually represent concepts: prototype theory (concepts as a central tendency, a typical member, with weighted features), exemplar theory (you store a bunch of specific examples and judge new things by similarity to those), and the "theory theory" (concepts as miniature theories with parts, relations, causes, functions). This maps suspiciously well onto implementation choices: a prototype is basically a centroid or weighted feature checklist; an exemplar approach is basically k-nearest-neighbors or keeping canonical stored cases; a theory-theory approach is basically an ontology with relations and causal rules, which is expensive and I'd rather avoid.

What I want is a reusable decision procedure for choosing among those three ways of representing a category, not a one-off answer for today's category. I want a checklist or a flowchart in words that I can run in that half a day before I write any code, that tells me which questions to ask in what order and when the answer is "this is just a judgment call, stop pretending it's a calculation." It should be honest about the hard cases — categories with vague boundaries, borderline members, contested definitions, categories that will probably shift in production. Then work through two or three examples, including at least one where the procedure pushes me toward the expensive theory-theory/ontology model when I dont want it, or away from the cheap prototype model when I would have defaulted to it. And tell me where the procedure itself is likely to steer me wrong, in plain engineering terms.
