I teach practical rationality, and I've started to suspect my beloved baseball team is a better test case than anything in the textbook. For two seasons now they've kept starting an outfielder hitting around .180, because he's on a seven-year deal worth more than $150 million and cutting him or benching him won't get a cent of that back. Every time a younger player comes up from AAA, hits .300 for a week, then gets benched again, my students would call it a textbook sunk cost fallacy: the salary is paid no matter what, so the only rational question is who helps the team win going forward. But the pattern is so stable across teams and general managers that it's hard to believe they're all just making an elementary mistake. Maybe they're not honoring sunk costs at all — maybe they're avoiding a clubhouse revolt, or signaling to future free agents that the organization doesn't abandon it's commitments, or using the size of the contract as a (bad) proxy for the player's underlying talent.

So here's what I want to understand: what theory of practical rationality does this pattern presuppose, if it presupposes one at all? Does the practice commit a front office to thinking that past costs can be reasons in themselves, or can a strictly forward-looking theory explain every part of it — the roster decisions, the contract structure, the public statements? Where does the practice strictly presuppose a controversial theory rather than merely fitting well with one, and if we replaced whatever theory is doing the work with a clean let-bygones-be-bygones rule, what would actually have to change in how the organization behaves?
