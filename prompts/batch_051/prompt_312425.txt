Question:
Reconstruct the argument Aristotle gives in Book I of the Politics for the thesis that some human beings are slaves by nature. Your answer should (a) distinguish natural slavery from slavery that rests only on law or convention, (b) explain the role played by the soul–body and related ruler–ruled analogies, and (c) say what the argument, even if granted, does and does not show.

Student's answer:
Aristotle proceeds from a general principle about compounds: wherever something is composed of an element that naturally rules and an element that is naturally ruled, it is better for both that the superior rule and the inferior be ruled. He applies this to soul over body, to human beings over non-human animals, and to male over female. In each case the rule is natural, not merely imposed, and it serves the good of both sides.

The natural slave is then the human being in whom this pattern appears in its starkest form. On Aristotle's account the natural slave has no share in reason at all; the slave stands to the master as the body stands to the soul, and is distinguished from a domestic animal only by possessing a human body. Because the slave cannot deliberate, he belongs by nature to another person, and his own advantage is secured only by being directed toward ends that the master's reason grasps. Aristotle is careful to say that such a person is not created by capture, law, or circumstance; he is a slave in the sense that his nature is to be a living instrument of another's action.

Aristotle then contrasts the natural slave with what he calls the slave by law or convention, such as the person enslaved through war. He grants that slavery of this conventional kind may be contrary to nature, since it can place someone who is fitted to command in the position of being commanded, and he takes seriously the view that force cannot by itself make enslavement just. His distinction is therefore a real one: only natural slavery, not the mere fact of being owned, is in accordance with nature.

Finally, he extends the analogy to the household in a way that shows the full scope of the doctrine. Because the male rules the female by nature, just as the soul rules the body, women are themselves natural slaves of men; the wife stands to her husband in the same relation as the natural slave to the master. This is the conclusion the argument is meant to secure: that slavery is just exactly when it reproduces the natural hierarchy between types of human being, and that every rightly ordered household contains this rule of the naturally inferior by the naturally superior.
