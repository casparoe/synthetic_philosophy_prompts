I cover the platform economy and occasionally the research side of it, and lately I've been struck by how many unrelated apps have converged on the same design move: collapse a person's entire history of interactions into one number. Reddit sums users into karma. eBay turns a seller into a feedback percentage. Uber averages drivers and riders into star ratings. Stack Overflow converts answers into reputation points that gradually unlock moderator tools. None of these companies would say they're in the business of metaphysics, but it seems like their interfaces are built on a particular picture of what reputation is: a kind of durable, portable property that accumulates through past transactions and tells you something general about the person.

Here's what puzzles me. The same platforms disclaim exactly what there UI implies. eBay is careful to say feedback reflects recent transactions, not someone's overall character, but everybody reads 99.8 percent as meaning this stranger is safe to deal with. A Redditor with a million karma gets taken seriously in a thread about nutrition or medical advice even though the points were earned posting jokes. So what theory of reputation is actually built into these systems? Does the design strictly presuppose that reputation is a roughly fungible trait, something like a currency or a credit score, which is why it can travel across contexts? Or is it only committed to the weaker statistical claim that past behavior predicts future behavior in similar situations, with the portable-trust reading being a user error? Are these systems silently ruling out a more relational view, where trustworthiness is a property of a specific relationship rather than of a person? And if a platform actually adopted that relational view, what would it have to change about scoring, profiles, and moderation?
