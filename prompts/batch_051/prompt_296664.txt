I'm the chief of staff for a state senator. My boss is a devout Catholic and he keeps getting into fights about when he can bring his faith into public political argument. Last session he argued against a bill to legalize medical aid in dying by saying, on the floor, that life is a gift from God and that only God gets to choose when it ends. Half the caucus loved it, the other half wanted him to shut up, and the editorial board accused us of religious lawmaking. He's asked me to write the office policy for when it's legitimate, as a matter of political justification in a plural democracy, to use a faith-based argument in floor speeches, committee testimony, and press statements — and when it isn't. We know he has a First Amendment right to say it; that's not the question. The question is whether he should.

The policy has to be something a 22-year-old legislative aide with no philosophy background can run in about 20 minutes before a hearing, and the senator will actually sign off on it and make the staff follow it, so it can't be advisory mush. I know some of the public reason literature has a proviso-type idea: you can bring religious reasons into the room as long as you eventually give reasons that citizens who don't share the faith could in principle accept as reasons. I dont know how to turn that into an actual step-by-step. The procedure needs to seperately decide three things: whether an argument may be offered as the weight-bearing justification, whether it may be mentioned but not carry the load, and whether it should stay out of the senator's public statements altogether. It should also say whether the rule gets stricter for bills about constitutional essentials or matters of basic justice than for ordinary policy, since that distinction keeps coming up in the literature and I don't know what to do with it. And it has to handle the cases that keep blowing up in our office: claims phrased in neutral language that everyone knows are doing theological work (like human dignity or sanctity of life talk in end-of-life debates), appeals to the state's Judeo-Christian heritage, and a member who offers a secular rationale for a position while her actual objection is religious. For each step, mark whether it's mechanical or a judgment call, and give me a sentence or two on which bit of public reason theory justifies it so I can defend the checklist when he pushes back.

Work it through on three cases: the aid-in-dying speech I mentioned; an argument for expanding prison education where he wants to say that every prisoner is made in God's image and can be redeemed; and a zoning variance for a mosque where the stated objection is traffic and parking but the member's real objection is that it's a mosque. Make one of the worked examples a case where the procedure tells my boss he cannot do something he really wants to do — otherwise I won't trust that it's a real procedure.

End by saying what the procedure can't settle, where it will misfire, and where I've built in a contestable view that a reasonable public reason theorist would push back on.

Format: start with the procedure itself — the checklist, with the one-line rule at the very top — no preamble, no restating my question, no throat clearing. Then the step-by-step, the notes on the hard cases, the worked examples, and the limitations.
