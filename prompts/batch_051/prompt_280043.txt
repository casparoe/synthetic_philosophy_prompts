For the past two years I have helped run a small organization that advocates for medicine pricing reform, and over that period I have watched several funders shift their support toward interventions whose outcomes can be counted more cleanly—deworming campaigns, bed-net distribution, cash transfers. The feedback is always the same: our work is harder to model, so it cannot be shown to be cost-effective. I am not hostile to effective altruism; in many ways I find the demand for rigor attractive, and I am even weighing whether to earn to give instead of staying in policy work. But I have come to take seriously an institutional critique of effective altruism: that its methods systematically select for measurable, tractable, short-feedback-loop interventions and undervalue work that operates through changing institutions, norms, or political incentives, even when the latter is where the largest long-run gains may be. Call this the measurability-bias critique. The claim is not that EA is merely imperfect; it is that the bias is structural—it follows from the demand to base funding on tractable cost-effectiveness estimates—and that it shapes not only what gets funded but which problems come to look real, and which people enter or leave the movement.

I want to know what would count against this critique. If I were trying to persuade myself that it is false, or only trivially true, which evidence and arguments should move me? Specifically: what would the funding landscape, the internal trajectory of the movement, and the actual record of institutional versus direct interventions have to look like for the critique to be decisively undermined? Which predictions does the critique make that a rival view—say, that EA-style measurement is self-correcting rather than systematically biased—would not make? Please sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, including developments that have arguably already occurred, and be honest about whether the critique has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that would be a problem for a view of this kind. I would personally be most persuaded by considerations about where the movement is heading, not only by isolated success stories.

At the very end, after your analysis, give me a final one-sentence line beginning "Verdict:" that states how exposed you judge the measurability-bias critique to be.
