Merleau-Ponty argued that skillful bodily action cannot be understood as a mind representing a body and then orchestrating it from outside. On his account, the lived body is not an object for consciousness but the very subject of perception and movement; it has its own pre-reflective grip on the world, a motor intentionality that knows how to reach for the cup or settle into a familiar room before any explicit thought intervenes. His usual examples pointed the same way: the blind man's stick through which he senses the street, the typist whose fingers find the keys, the phantom limb that still answers to a world of habitual actions. All were meant to show that bodily competence is not a matter of internal representations.

Since then, motor neuroscience has largely converged on a very different-sounding picture. Skilled action is standardly explained by internal models: the central nervous system is thought to maintain forward and inverse models of the body and its environment, continually predicting the sensory consequences of movement and correcting those predictions through prediction error. On this picture, exactly the abilities Merleau-Ponty took as proof of a non-representational bodily intelligence are underwritten by rich, subpersonal representations of the body's state and dynamics. The development is not merely that prostheses or brain-computer interfaces can be made to feel like parts of the body; it is that science now explains such incorporation by saying the body and its tools are internally modeled.

Does Merleau-Ponty's argument collapse if motor intentionality turns out to be carried by internal models? Which precise premise does the new picture put under pressure: the claim that the lived body is the subject of action, the claim that motor intentionality is non-representational, or something else? And is there a repaired version of his position that concedes internal models while still saying something importantly different from the intellectualist view he was attacking?
