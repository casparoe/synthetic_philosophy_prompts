I teach a unit on Turing's imitation game and Ryle-style behaviorism, and my students assume the two are basically the same thing with different accents. Build me a case that splits them. I want a thought experiment where a Turing-sufficiency theorist (passing the test is sufficient for thought) says a system is thinking, while a Rylean dispositionalist denies it is a thinker. The verdicts have to fall out of the theories, not be stipulated. Constraints: the system genuinely passes an open-ended, fairly demanding version of the test—long conversations, follow-ups, irony, philosophical puzzles—so no lookup tables, no hidden humans, no prewritten replies, and no tech much beyond current language models. But the Rylean denial has to be principled too: it should come from the absence of certain broad behavioral dispositions, the practical capacities intelligence normally shows up in, not from grumbling that the thing is made of silicon. Keep the scenario to a paragraph. Then do the check: give each view's verdict and the reasoning behind it, name the one structural feature of the case that's doing the real work, say what happens to the disagreement if the system gets a robot body that learns to navigate an unfamiliar room, and finish with how the Turing-sufficiency defender should push back on the Rylean without watering the test down.
