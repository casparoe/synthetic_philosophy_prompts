I work as a software engineer at a company with a heavily distributed engineering organisation: part of the team sits in the country where the firm is headquartered, but a large portion lives and works remotely from other countries, with no visa, no residence status, and no physical presence in the headquarters country. That arrangement has made me curious about a line of argument I have seen in debates over immigration: the view that a state has a right to exclude prospective immigrants because a political community is entitled to control its own membership in something like the way a club or association decides whom to admit, and because the community's shared institutions, norms, and solidarities depend on regulating who joins it. On the standard version of this argument, joining the community is assumed to mean entering its territory. The border is where the question of membership gets settled, and permanent residence on the soil is the main thing the would-be member is seeking.

Cross-border remote work seems to me to decouple part of what the argument cares about from territorial entry. A person can contribute daily to a country's economy, participate in its firms, practices, and workplace culture, build durable relationships with its citizens, and in a real sense become part of its labour market, without ever crossing the border or acquiring any immigration status. The receiving state has little direct power to prevent this at the point of entry, because there is no point of entry; it could try to regulate its own employers or payment systems, but it cannot simply turn the person away. My question is whether the right-to-exclude argument survives this change. Does it still justify the conclusion that states may exclude foreigners from their territory and their labour markets, or does it need to be repaired into a narrower claim — and if so, what claim? Or does the argument turn out not to depend on territorial entry after all, so that remote work changes the illustrations but not the structure of the debate? I am most interested in which specific premise is under pressure and whether a weaker but still important version can be defended.
