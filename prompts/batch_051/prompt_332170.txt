I recently joined a reading group on the foundations of mathematics, and one dispute has now consumed the group for several sessions without either side moving. It concerns a small-sounding question: is the number 2 a member of the number 4? Everyone agrees on the formal facts. On the von Neumann construction of the natural numbers, 0 is ∅, 1 is {∅}, 2 is {∅, {∅}}, 3 is {∅, {∅}, {∅, {∅}}}, and 4 is {∅, {∅}, {∅, {∅}}, {∅, {∅}, {∅, {∅}}}}, so 2 is an element of 4. On the Zermelo construction, each number is the singleton of its predecessor, so 2 is {{∅}} and 4 is {{{{∅}}}}, and 2 is not an element of 4 — only 3 is. Both constructions satisfy the second-order Peano axioms.

The two reductionists in the group have settled into a standoff. One holds that the von Neumann construction gives the real natural numbers, and that the membership facts of that construction are simply part of what those numbers are. The other says exactly the same about the Zermelo construction. The structuralist members say the question is ill-posed: numbers are places in the natural-number structure, and a bare string like "2 ∈ 4" has no determinate truth-value until a coding is stipulated; fix the von Neumann coding and it is true, fix the Zermelo coding and it is false, and neither verdict tells you anything about the number 2 itself. The reductionists reply that this dissolves rather than answers the question, and that it leaves out exactly what they are trying to settle — what the numbers really are.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse keeps regenerating because the two reductionists each have a true-but-relativized answer, while the structuralists are offering a dissolution rather than a rival answer, which the reductionists experience as a refusal to take the question seriously. That makes me think the disagreement is not mathematical at all, but concerns what it takes for something to be the number 2 — identity conditions, whether sameness of structure is enough, and whether it even makes sense to ask for "the real" membership facts across codings. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a hidden formal or conceptual question we should be arguing about instead? Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, and say honestly what could move it — a formal result, a distinction, a rephrasing of the question — or whether this is a dispute to be dissolved rather than won.
