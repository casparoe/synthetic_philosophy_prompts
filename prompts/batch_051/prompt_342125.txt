Last week in my grad decision theory seminar two of the sharper students got into it over the counterfactual mugging, and they've both emailed me seperately since, wanting a verdict. I couldn't referee it on the spot in a way that felt fair to both of them, so here's the exchange as best I remember it; I'd appreciate a point-by-point adjudication.

First the case, as I put it on the board. Omega—honest, and a perfect predictor of what you'll do—flips a fair coin. If it comes up tails, Omega asks you for $100, and paying gets you nothing. If it comes up heads, Omega gives you $10,000 iff its model predicts that you would have paid in the tails case. Omega now tells you the coin came up tails, and asks for the hundred.

One naming thing I stopped them on early, just to keep the debate honest: the pro-pay side shouldn't be called evidential decision theory. Plain EDT updates on the fact that the coin is tails, so it refuses too. The pro-pay view is the updateless one. I only mention it so nobody spends the whole hour on wrong labels.

Marcus opens. "Don't pay. The coin is tails, Omega said so, and my paying now can't causally influence whether the heads payout happened, because that was fixed by Omega's prediction before I ever saw the question. CDT evaluates acts by their causal consequences, and the causal consequences of paying here are just minus a hundred bucks."

Priya says that's the mistake of evaluating the act instead of the policy. Omega's prediction in the heads cases is a prediction of exactly this choice—the one you're making right now. If your policy is pay-when-asked, Omega's model sees that and you get $10,000 on heads. If your policy is refuse-once-you-know-it's-tails, the model sees that too and you get nothing. The payer policy nets $4,950 over the two branches; the refuser policy nets nothing. So pay.

Marcus answers that this is an argument for precommitting before the coin is flipped, and he agrees with it completely. If he could have bound himself ex ante to pay in both branches, he would have. But ex ante isn't now. The coin is tails, the precommitment has done whatever it was going to do, and paying after the fact is just taking a loss you no longer have to take.

Priya says a precommitment you drop the moment it costs you something isn't a commitment, it's just a description of what you were going to do anyway. If Omega's model is genuinely predicting you, then "I'd have precommitted, but I won't pay now" is a contradiction. The model sees that actual tails-you defects, so heads-you gets nothing. You can't purchase the heads payout with a plan you were always going to abandon.

Marcus says that's only right if the precommitment wasn't binding. If he'd actually bound himself—escrow, a side contract, whatever—then the model would see that and the heads payout would have been secured. But a binding device is a different causal setup from a free choice after Omega has announced tails. His real worry is that Priya's rule makes him payable on demand to anyone with a coin and a smooth story about the branch that didn't occur.

Priya says he's changed the case. In the stipulated case Omega is honest and infallible, so the evidence that the heads payout really was on offer is as strong as evidence gets. Updateless agents aren't required to pay random story-tellers; they update on evidence about who's asking. But given the stipulation, refusing is choosing the policy that leaves the $10,000 sitting on the table every time heads came up.

Marcus comes back with what he calls the real sticking point. The ten grand is never on his table. On heads it goes to the heads-guy, in a branch that did not occur. On tails, the branch he's actually in, there's no payout in either direction. So the expected value Priya is pointing at counts a benefit to some other possible self as if it were his. He says CDT isn't irrational for declining to pay for another branch's windfall.

Priya's last move: every decision rule already does that across time. You precommit on Tuesday for the benefit of a Wednesday self who is, in the relevant sense, another guy, and we call that prudence. Updateless reasoning extends the same move across branches. If Marcus refuses to care about the heads-guy, fine—but then his earlier admission that he'd gladly precommit stops making sense, because the only reason to precommit is to benefit that same heads-guy.

That was roughly where class ended. Could you adjudicate the whole thing move by move? Take the main moves one at a time—Marcus's CDT point, Priya's policy point, Marcus's precommitment point, Priya's "that's not a commitment" point, Marcus's exploitability point, Priya's "you changed the case" point, Marcus's other-possible-self point, and Priya's cross-time reply—and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a sentence or two of reasoning. Then say who is more right overall about what to do in the stipulated case (not about real-life muggers), and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
