Heres one I keep going back and forth on. Suppose you show a group of people a painting, or play them a song, and have them rate how good it is. Then half of them learn something true but awful about the person who made it. Ratings drop; that part isnt very interesting. The claim I actually care about is stronger: that the work itself starts to look or sound worse to them. Not that theyre knocking points off on purpose because they think they should, or because they dont want to be seen praising a monster, but that the moral information changes the perception itself, kind of like how a food you used to love starts to taste different after you learn what it really is.

Design the best empirical test you can of that stronger claim. I had one half-baked idea: give people speeded judgments about formal properties—balance, rhythm, tonal warmth, whatever—right after priming them with moral information about the artist, and see whether their perceptual baselines shift the way they would after adapting to genuinely bad art. But I dont trust that design, so do better if you can.

What pattern of results would push you toward saying the perception itself genuinely changed, and what pattern would make you say its mostly self-presentation, social desirability, or experimenter demand? Be specific about the measures and the comparison conditions. And then tell me what the test would still leave open even if it came out looking like a real perceptual shift—for instance, whether the work actually is worse, or whether we ought to value it differently, or whether the perceiver is being rational. This is basically the empirical shadow of the whole "separate the art from the artist" fight, so try to say exactly which part of that fight your test can and cannot reach.
