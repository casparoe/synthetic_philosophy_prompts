I keep going back and forth on whether Borges's 'Funes the Memorious' actually works as an illustration of the Hofstadter and Sander thesis in Surfaces and Essences that all cognition is analogy-making, and I'd love a patient reading that doesnt just mash them together. Their line, roughly, is that applying a concept is always an analogical stretch from old cases to a new one: calling this thing a dog is an analogy between this thing and the earlier dogs you have met, and without that stretch our categories would never get off the ground. Funes, after the riding accident, remembers every perception in excrutiating particularity and refuses to call two different things by the same name, so he looks like the cleanest fictional test case: a person with perception and memory but no usable concepts. But I'm not sure Borges is really showing analogy-less thought. First, is Funes missing an analogical faculty, or is he just refusing to use it, and does that distinction matter for what the story can teach? Second, and this is what I most want pushed on: maybe Funes isn't analogy-poor but analogy-rich in a pathological way. Every instant he generates analogies so situation-specific that no two moments ever get treated as the same, so his problem is that his analogies never generalize. If that's right, the story might not be a counterexample to Hofstadter and Sander at all; it might be a magnification of what they are describing. I'd like a reading that goes both ways: where Borges dramatizes their view better than their own examples do, and where the story resists or quietly mocks the idea that analogy is sufficient for thought. End by saying what the story adds to the philosophical debate that the book's prose can't do.
