For a seminar on AI strategy, I reconstructed what I take to be a Fearon-style rationalist argument, and its conclusion now strikes me as absurd, so I want to know where it breaks.

(1) If several rational parties all prefer outcome A to outcome B, and A is jointly attainable, then the parties will end up in A rather than B.

(2) In a race to develop transformative AI, all leading parties prefer the coordinated outcome to an all-out race: the race duplicates effort, forces safety shortcuts, and may leave everyone worse off, whereas coordination would share the progress and the benefits.

(3) The coordinated outcome is jointly attainable, since nothing in the technology stops rival labs or states from pooling research and dividing the returns.

(4) So, by (1), leading parties will end up in the coordinated outcome rather than the race.

(5) But they do not: leading labs and states demonstrably build rival programs rather than one joint effort.

(6) Therefore at least one party to the race is irrational.

I accept (5), and I would most like to hold on to (2). But (6) seems to prove too much. Where does the argument go wrong? Which step should I reject or refine, why did that step look innocent, and what would a corrected version have to add or give up?
