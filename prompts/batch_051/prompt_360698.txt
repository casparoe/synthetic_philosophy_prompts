I work as a software engineer, mostly on backend logging and access control, so I spend alot of time thinking about who can see what and what breaks when they can't. Recently I was reading Jeremy Bentham's Political Tactics, and near his discussion of publicity he says something like this: secrecy is the natural home of sinister interests, and only to the extent that proceedings are public can ordinary people function as a real check on official power; without publicity there's no accountability, no matter what formal rights exist. I'm heavily paraphrasing, but that's the gist.

Now I've been comparing that to the way governments actually use nondisclosure agreements. On the one hand we have open-meeting laws, freedom of information requests, published audit logs, that sort of thing. On the other hand, the same agencies routinely sign settlement agreements with NDAs attached, put broad secrecy clauses in employee or contractor agreements, and redact internal decision-making documents under deliberative-process rules. It seems like the first set of practices presupposes that the public is a legitimate check on officials and that information is necessary for that check. But the NDA practice seems to presuppose something like: public knowledge is sometimes inherently damaging to good governance, or that officials should be able to decide unilaterally what the public can be trusted with.

So I'm trying to figure out what the NDA practice is actually committed to, philosophically. Is there a coherent view of democracy or accountability that supports both open-meeting laws and routine government NDAs? Or do these practices just rest on inconsistent ideas about the public? And if you took Bentham's publicity argument seriously, what would have to change in how governments design NDAs, redaction rules, and settlement terms?
