After four decades in the physical sciences, I retired and began reading philosophy of science in earnest, and the experience has made me suspicious of one of my own long-standing habits. The habit is this: I assign essentially zero evidential weight to internal theoretical virtues—mathematical elegance, uniqueness, explanatory unification, coherence with neighboring theories—unless the theory in question also makes novel predictions that are testable at least in principle and that would distinguish it from live rivals. I treat testability even in principle as a gate, and I treat arguments of the form, this framework is the most mathematically natural or constrained way to unify what we know, so it is probably true, as rhetorical rather than evidential. The case that has forced the habit into the open is the current discussion of string theory and the multiverse: when I read sophisticated defenses of what philosophers now call non-empirical theory confirmation, I find myself dismissing them almost out of hand, and I recently told a neighbor that even if string theory were the only known mathematically consistent route to unify gravity with quantum mechanics, that fact should not by itself move my confidence in its truth at all.

A philosopher friend says my policy is a crude verificationism, that some non-empirical criteria are unavoidable in any real theory choice, and that I am pretending to a cleanliness that no practicing scientist ever actually had. I take the point, but I am not sure the opposite policy is any better, especially since I no longer have the technical background to check the claims about uniqueness and mathematical consistency and must therefore fall back on something. So I would like to know whether my epistemic habit is defensible. When, if ever, should non-empirical virtues move a rational agent's credence, and at what rate? Does my situation—retired, no longer able to follow the technical literature closely—make the policy more or less defensible than it would be for an active researcher? And can the habit be restated so as to keep its benefits, chiefly protecting me against untestable speculation and motivated reasoning, without collapsing into a dogmatic dismissal of a form of evidence that may be legitimate?
