Here’s an asymmetry I keep coming back to. If you proposed picking MPs by lot — say, 650 people drawn at random from the electoral roll and sent to Westminster for one parliament, no election, no manifesto, no chance of being voted out — almost everyone would call it a gimmick or a nighmare: rule by people with no expertise, no accountability, and no mandate, who never even asked for the job. But the same people are perfectly comfortable with a jury of twelve randomly selected citizens deciding whether a defendant goes to prison for fifteen years. Both cases put ordinary, unelected, untrained citizens in a position to exercise serious public power. So why is one obviously legitimate and the other obviously absurd?

I can think of some candidate distinctions. Juries decide particular cases rather than general rules; they’re hemmed in by judges, evidence law, and instructions; a defendant has a right to be judged by their peers, whereas nobody has a right to be legislated at by their peers. But I’m not sure any of them fully does the work. Civil juries set damages, and in some places juries have effectively made policy through nullification or by acquitting in assisted-dying cases. And the expertise point cuts both ways: we happily let randomly selected people weigh forensic evidence and cross-examination, things that baffle actual experts, so lack of expertise can’t be the whole story.

So here’s the question. Is there a principle that generates both judgments — sortition for juries is fine or even required, sortition for legislatures is not — or is the asymmetry mostly just path-dependence and familiarity? If you defend it, defend it against the hardest case I can think of: a carefully run citizens’ assembly of randomly selected people, with expert witnesses and trained facilitators, which drafts a law that is then put to a referendum. If that’s legitimate, why is drawing legislators by lot illegitimate, other than by degree? And if you’d instead bite the bullet and go full lottocracy, what do you do about the fact that a randomly selected legislator answers to no electorate and can’t be thrown out? I want the strongest principle you can find, and an honest verdict on whether it actually survives.
