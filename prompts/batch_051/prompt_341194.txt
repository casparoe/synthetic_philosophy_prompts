Okay, so I've been in a social choice theory rabbit hole lately and I've managed to talk myself into a position that feels clean and maybe a little too clean, and I want someone to attack it properly rather than hand-wave at it. The position is that Harsanyi's aggregation theorem doesn't just make a technical point — it basically forces utilitarianism, or at least weighted-sum welfare maximizing, on anyone who wants social evaluation to be rational in the expected-utility sense. As I understand the theorem, roughly: if each person's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and the social evaluator's preferences over lotteries also satisfy those same axioms, and social preference satisfies a mild Pareto condition (everyone indifferent means society indifferent; if someone strictly prefers one option and no one strictly prefers the reverse, society goes along), then the social utility function has to be an affine combination — effectively a weighted sum — of the individual vNM utility functions. And in the impartial-observer version, where the evaluator assigns equal probability to being each person behind a veil of ignorance, the weights come out equal and you get classical utilitarianism. So the ethical content seems to fall out of two rationality axioms and a bit of impartiality, which is what's been keeping me up at night.

I'm less interested in a restatement of the theorem than in a genuine map of where this position is exposed. What would have to be true for it to be wrong, or for the theorem to be true but ethically inert? In particular: how much work is the move of making the social evaluator face risk and assign probabilities to being different people actually doing — is an ethical premise being smuggled in as a technical axiom, and what happens if an evaluator refuses to form those probabilities? What if social preferences are allowed to be incomplete or intransitive or otherwise non-vNM; which departures are defensible rather than just logically possible? Does the theorem quietly assume interpersonal comparability in a way a skeptic shouldn't grant, and does dropping or changing comparability kill the result or just change it? And which concrete counterexamples — fairness-style cases, Sen-style objections, whatever — should actually trouble me, reconstructed in your own words rather than cited. Then rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether I've stated the view vaguely enough that its insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether that's a problem with the argument or just with my phrasing.
