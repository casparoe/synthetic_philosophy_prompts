Software engineer here with a counterfactual I'd like you to think through. Suppose the only thing different about the world from the word2vec moment in 2013 onward is this: every embedding model researchers train ends up with a final vector space that is maximally uncooperative. The models themselves work fine — they predict contexts and help downstream tasks just as well as in our world — but the geometry of the final embedding space carries no clean semantic structure. Cosine similarity doesn't track word similarity, vector arithmetic like king − man + woman lands nowhere near queen (and nothing else sensible either), there are no linear directions you can label as concepts, and debiasing by subtracting a gender direction does nothing useful. The semantic competence is locked inside the network that computes the vectors, not in the geometry of the final space.

Give me your best reasoned prediction of how the last decade of NLP and the whole idea that meaning is locations in a high-dimensional space would have played out differently. I want the causal chain, not a story. Which research programmes and applications depended on that linear geometry? Analogy arithmetic, vector-space debiasing, probing for interpretable directions, maybe similarity search. Which would have been basically unaffected? Transfer learning, contextual models, maybe just using embeddings as an internal bottleneck. What would people have had to invent instead — nonlinear probes, different similarity measures, or would they have stopped treating embeddings as meanings at all? How much of the philosophical claim that a word's meaning is it's position in a vector space was doing real work, and how much was just riding on a convenient linear structure?

Also tell me which parts of your prediction you're confident in, which are guesses, and what would have to be true about how humans represent meaning for the whole prediction to be wrong. Please write the answer in British English. Hard limit: 500 words.
