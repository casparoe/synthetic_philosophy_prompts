I am a retired natural scientist — I spent my career in geology — and I have only recently begun reading political philosophy in a serious way. I find that my training keeps steering me toward concrete comparisons, and one comparison has been troubling me. I hope you can help me think it through systematically.

Consider three established practices for addressing the underrepresentation of groups.

The first is American university admissions. For roughly half a century, admissions officers were permitted to weigh an applicant's race as one "plus" factor among many in a holistic, individualised review, while it remained unconstitutional to set aside a fixed number of places for any racial group. In 2023 the Supreme Court ended the use of the "plus" factor as well, while indicating that an applicant's own discussion of how race shaped them may still be considered as evidence of individual qualities, and that universities may still pursue diversity through race-neutral means of selection. What strikes me is the consistency at the centre: the fixed numerical quota, the one device that would guarantee a specified racial composition, was the one device forbidden throughout.

The second is India's reservation system. Under India's constitutional framework, a fixed percentage of places in public higher-education institutions and in government employment is reserved for members of the Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and Other Backward Classes — altogether on the order of half of all such places. Here the quota is not only lawful; it is the constitutionally mandated remedy. The instrument American courts treated as the paradigmatic constitutional wrong is, in India, the centrepiece of the constitutional response to caste inequality.

The third is Norway's quotas for corporate boards. Norwegian law requires public limited companies to ensure that each sex holds at least 40 percent of board seats, enforced by escalating sanctions up to dissolution. Again a fixed share is the chosen instrument, this time for sex rather than caste or race, and this time applied to private corporate governance rather than education or public employment.

The three systems thus disagree not merely about policy but, it seems to me, about what kind of wrong underrepresentation is, and what kind of good proportional presence achieves. A quota seems to assume that the group's share itself matters: that a numerical shortfall is itself the injury and its correction itself the remedy. A holistic "plus" seems to assume that the injury lies in how individuals are treated, so the remedy must remain individual in form, and a group-level guarantee would be a fresh wrong. The post-2023 American position seems to assume something further: that race may count only when it passes through an applicant's own account of their life, not as a demographic classification.

I would like you to reconstruct, for each practice, the theory of equality or fairness that it presupposes, and then to answer four questions.

First, which kind of wrong does each presuppose: a wrong of distribution, in which a group holds too few places and the remedy is to reallocate places; a wrong of standing or stigma, in which persons are denied equal status; a wrong in the selection procedure itself, in which the criteria are distorted; or an instrumental claim that mixed groups make better decisions and produce more legitimate institutions? Or some combination of these, and if so, which carries the weight?

Second, are the three practices jointly coherent? Could a single principled theory warrant India's and Norway's quotas while forbidding quotas in the United States? Or is the divergence best explained by different historical wrongs, different empirical beliefs, different views of whether the group categories themselves are morally odious, or different confidence in the relevant institutions — and in what sense is that an explanation rather than a restatement of the disagreement?

Third, I would like you to separate what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Must a quota presuppose that group membership gives rise to a non-instrumental claim to a certain share, or is a quota equally consistent with a purely instrumental, diversity-based rationale that happens to be administered numerically? Does the American ban on quotas strictly presuppose a view of equality as standing, or is it compatible with a distributive view that is held back only by an empirical prediction about how quotas would be administered?

Fourth, what would have to change — in doctrine, in institutional design, in the categories themselves — if one theory were swapped for another? Imagine an American university that administered an Indian-style numerical reservation for Black and Hispanic applicants while defending it purely on Norwegian-style grounds that diversity benefits all students. Which parts of that arrangement would be philosophically underdetermined, and which parts would commit the institution to a stronger egalitarian claim than its official rationale can carry?
