I am a retired field ecologist who spent most of my career working on nutrient dynamics and disturbance regimes in forests and rivers, and I have recently started reading environmental philosophy seriously. My working vocabulary is ecosystem ecology and evolutionary biology: stocks and flows, limiting factors, ecosystem functions and services, resilience, alternative stable states, functional traits, adaptation and exaptation, life-history trade-offs, and the distinction between proximate mechanism and ultimate function. I would like you to translate a debate from environmental ethics into that vocabulary and then tell me what the translation loses.

The debate concerns how to characterize the value of nature and wilderness. As I understand it, there are three main positions. Instrumental value treats nature as valuable as a means to something else, typically human welfare: water purification, carbon sequestration, pollination, recreation, genetic resources. Intrinsic value treats at least some parts of nature as valuable in themselves, independently of whether anyone happens to value them or benefit from them. Relational value treats value as constituted by relationships—a person's attachment to a place, a community's identity in a landscape, or interdependencies among living things—and its defenders claim this is not reducible to either of the other two.

Restate each position, one at a time, in the vocabulary of ecosystem ecology and evolutionary biology. For each, say which of its characteristic claims carry over cleanly, which carry over only with distortion, and which have no expression in that vocabulary at all. Where a translation distorts, identify precisely which term in the scientific vocabulary quietly changes or discards part of the original claim. Then give me a residue report: of what failed to survive translation, tell me whether the failure reveals something my scientific vocabulary is structurally unable to say, or something the philosophical position was simply assuming. I do not want the general conclusion that science and ethics are different enterprises; I want the exact points of friction.

To sharpen the exercise, pay particular attention to wilderness. I suspect it will fare worst, since so much of my vocabulary is organized around function, process, and management, whereas wilderness is often characterized by the absence of deliberate human purpose. Tell me whether that suspicion is right, and whether the residue around wilderness indicts my scientific vocabulary or the way the wilderness concept is being used. Assume I need no explanation of the ecological and evolutionary terms, and do not give me a survey of authors or references; work the ideas themselves.
