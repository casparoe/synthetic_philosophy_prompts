I'm on the bylaws commitee of a large union local, about 40,000 members, and we're rewriting how leadership gets chosen. Right now every officer is elected for three-year terms with no limits, and the reform caucus thinks that's how we got a layer of career politicians inside the union: good at winning elections, bad at staying in touch with the shop floor. Some of us want to introduce sortition for a few positions—random, stratified selection from the membership, assembly-style—plus term limits on the elected ones. The proposal goes to the full membership for a ratification vote this spring, and whatever passes is locked in for five years, so we have to get it right. I want a decision procedure we can actually run in committee, office by office, to decide which positions should stay elected, which should be filled by lot, and where term limits by themselves do the job. Ground it in the actual arguments for and against sortition and rotation in office: descriptive representation, accountability to the membership, the competence problem, independence from factions and staff, corruption, amateur versus experienced judgment, and the fact that a random draw doesn't give anyone a mandate. Write it so a shop steward with no theory background can follow it, and be honest about the places where the procedure is really just the committee making a values call.

Then give me three sections:
1. "The procedure" (about 450 words): the ordered questions we should ask about each office, what counts as a good enough answer to each, and where each answer pushes us—elected, lot, or term limits.
2. "Worked applications" (about 350 words): run the procedure on three positions—chief negotiator/president, trustee on the audit committee, and convention delegate—and make sure at least one comes out the way the pro-sortition people won't like.
3. "What the procedure can't settle" (about 150 words): the residual judgment calls and the main risks of relying on this thing anyway.
