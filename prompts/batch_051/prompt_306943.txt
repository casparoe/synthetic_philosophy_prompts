Okay so I work for a state fish and wildlife agency, in the unit that decides which populations go on the state endangered list, and one of our recurring headaches is that the law protects "species" but nature keeps refusing to divide itself up that way. I just got back from a long argument with a friend who's a botanist, and she spent an hour gleefully telling me species aren't real, that the biological species concept breaks on asexual dandelions and ring species and those orchid complexes that hybridize all over the place, and that any line we draw is a judgment call dressed up as science. Honestly she's right, but I still have to produce defensible decisions, and my field staff mostly are not taxonomists.

So here's what I need: a reusable procedure my biologists can run when deciding wether to treat two populations as one species or two for listing purposes. They'll have field notes, photos, maybe museum records, and usually not much genetic data because we can't afford it. The stakes are high — a listing triggers land-use restrictions and gets challenged in court — and we do a couple dozen of these a year, under deadline, and a review board has to sign off on every call. Give me the decision procedure, grounded in whatever the philosophy of biology actually knows about species, with a checklist or a sequence of tests to run in order, and told me where judgment can't be eliminated. Include two or three worked examples, at least one where the procedure gives an answer I won't like, and say plainly what the procedure can't settle.
