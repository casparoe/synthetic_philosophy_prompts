Build me a thought experiment where two ordinary people acausally trade with each other. By "acausal trade" I mean: each takes an action that costs them something and benefits the other, and each does it because their own choice is evidence about what the other person will do — not because their choice can cause the other's, and not because anyone will punish them for refusing. The two people cannot communicate, the choice is one-shot, and no third party enforces the exchange; if one refuses, they still get whatever the other gave. I want it set up so a causal decision theorist defects, while a timeless/updateless-decision-theory-ish agent (the sort that picks the output of the whole correlated deliberation, not just its local act) cooperates.

Constraints: no perfect predictors, no Newcomb demons, no simulation-hypothesis stuff, and no exotic physics. Whatever correlation exists between the two choices has to come from plain psychological or structural similarity — both running the same kind of deliberation, both knowing their situations are symetrical, that kind of thing. Stakes should be normal-person stakes: money, time, effort, not the fate of the galaxy. And the traders should be non-altruists: their reason for paying the cost should be self-interested, given their theory of decision.

Make it concrete: name the two people, say exactly what each chooses, what the payoffs are, and what each believes about the other. Don't just stipulate that A is 80% sure B will cooperate; earn that belief from the setup. After the scenario, do the walkthrough: state each person's payoffs, why a causal decision theorist defects, and then exactly what doxastic fact lets the acausal trader cooperate anyway — what makes choice A evidence about choice B when there's no causal arrow between them. Tell me how confident you are about which step is doing the real work. Then delete that one feature and say what the case collapses into. Finish with how a defender of causal decision theory would attack it: do they deny the evidential link, call it altruism or a reputation effect in disguise, or something else? Commit to one scenario and one diagnosis; don't give me a menu. If a detail is a guess, flag it.

The point of the case is to isolate what does the work in acausal cooperation once causal channels, enforcement, and altruism are gone — so don't smuggle any of those back in.
