Give me a working definition of exploitation that correctly classifies the following six cases. I mean the sense of the word at work when people call sweatshop employment exploitative while conceding that the worker is better off taking the job than refusing it — exploitation within a transaction that benefits both parties relative to their fallback options. Assume throughout that the weaker party consents without being deceived and understands the terms, so whatever makes a transaction suspect is not ordinary coercion or fraud. Your definition should not make being made worse off a necessary condition for exploitation, and it should say what feature of a transaction does the moral work rather than simply restating that the terms are unfair.

Case One. A woman in a poor country takes a job in a garment factory exporting to wealthy countries. The wage covers her food and rent and little else, and she dislikes the conditions. Her alternative is occasional casual labour that pays less overall, and she is glad to have the factory job. The owner could double her wage and still make a comfortable profit.

Case Two. Two neighbours, neither of whom is under financial pressure, negotiate over a second-hand bicycle and agree on a price that both are happy with. Each gains from the exchange, and neither has any relevant vulnerability.

Case Three. A storm cuts off the public water supply for a week. A shop owner raises the price of bottled water to five times its usual level, though his own costs have not changed. A family buys enough water at the higher price to get through the week, and would have paid more rather than go without.

Case Four. In a jurisdiction where organ sales are lawful, a man with heavy debts sells a kidney to a wealthy patient for a sum that clears those debts. He is fully informed, passes the medical tests, and regards the sale as his best option by a wide margin. The recipient gains years of life.

Case Five. A lender gives a loan at an annual equivalent rate of around 400 per cent to a borrower with no access to cheaper credit, to pay for a car repair she needs in order to keep her job. She repays in full. The lender could lend at half that rate and still earn a return, but sets the rate at the highest level the borrower can be brought to accept.

Case Six. A graduate takes a three-month unpaid internship at a prestigious firm. She has savings and could refuse, but believes the line on her CV will be worth the foregone pay. The firm gives her routine work that it would otherwise pay a junior employee to do, and it could easily afford to pay her.

I am confident that Case One and Case Three are exploitation and that Case Two is not. I keep changing my mind about Case Four, Case Five, and Case Six, so I especially want to see how your definition sorts those three. Propose the definition, run it through all six cases, and then tell me which of your verdicts is least secure and what further fact about that case would lead you to change it.
