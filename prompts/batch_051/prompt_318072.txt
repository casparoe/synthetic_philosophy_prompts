Here is a passage from Peter Suber's "Nomic: A Game of Self-Amendment," Appendix 3 of The Paradox of Self-Amendment:

If law-making is a game, then it is a game in which changing the rules is a move. Law-making is more than changing the rules of law-making, of course, and more than a game. But a real game may model the self-amending character of the legal system and leave the rest out.

A course on legal self-reference included the following exam question and a student's answer.

Question: Suber claims that a real game can model "the self-amending character of the legal system and leave the rest out." In light of the two-tier structure of mutable and "immutable" rules in Nomic, explain (a) what feature of a legal system the mutable/immutable distinction is supposed to model, and (b) how the two-tier structure is supposed to regulate self-amendment. Then critically assess (c) whether Nomic actually avoids the paradox of self-amendment or merely relocates it.

Student's answer: Suber's point is that legal systems contain rules about how rules are made, and those rules can sometimes be changed by the very procedures they govern. Nomic models this by making every rule subject to amendment through the game's own procedures — even the rules that specify how amendments work. The mutable/immutable distinction is meant to model the difference between statutory and constitutional rules: immutable rules are harder to amend and take priority over mutable rules in cases of conflict, just as constitutional rules are harder to amend than statutes and prevail over them. Since the immutable rules cannot be amended directly, the paradox of self-amendment cannot arise: the game's foundational rules are fixed for the duration of play, so there is no possibility of a rule that alters its own validity conditions. The only real limitation is that Nomic's "immutable" rules are not really immutable, because they can be transmuted into mutable rules and then amended; but this just shows that the game is a model, not an actual constitution. Overall, the two-tier structure does avoid the paradox because the rules that would have to be self-referential are shielded from their own effects.
