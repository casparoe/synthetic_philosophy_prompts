A friend of mine has spent years working through a single question: when, if ever, is it legitimate to assign a probability to one's own choice? She has reached settled verdicts about a series of cases, and I want to know what she would say about a new one. Her six verdicts are these.

1. Two flights home. Choosing tonight between two equally good flights, she says there is no probability to assign. You are deciding, not predicting, and someone who says she is 70% likely to pick the red-eye has confused herself with a spectator of a decision she is actually making.

2. Next year's job. Asked whether she will accept a particular job next year, she says she can assign a probability, roughly 0.6, because her next-year self is, for these purposes, a stranger whom she knows from the outside.

3. Newcomb's problem. In the one-box/two-box puzzle, where a reliable predictor has already predicted what she will do, she says she must not assign a probability to her own act and then update on it. That treats her act as a symptom of the predictor rather than as something she does.

4. Flossing at midnight. She has resolved to floss every night. Standing in the bathroom at 11 p.m., she says she cannot responsibly assign a probability to whether she will floss, because any number she names would function either as a self-fulfilling prophecy or as an excuse.

5. The delegated coin. She has handed a difficult binary choice to a fair coin toss. Before the toss she assigns probability one-half to each outcome, precisely because the choice is no longer hers to make.

6. The idle hand. In an experiment she is instructed not to deliberate but to let her hand press whichever of two buttons it will. A brain-reading classifier announces an 0.8 probability that her hand will press the left button, and she endorses that number as her own, treating the impending press as a bodily event rather than as a decision.

Now the new case. She is about to decide, on the spot, whether to donate a significant sum to an urgent and highly effective public-health charity. The canvasser has told her that only 12 percent of people who score exactly as she does on a personality inventory actually donate when asked in person, and she knows the study is reliable. Should she, by her own lights, adopt 0.12 as her probability that she will donate?

Please identify the rule or rules that best rationalize her six verdicts; name at least one other rule that fits those verdicts equally well; state what each candidate rule predicts about the donation case; give your best guess about her verdict, with the reason you prefer one rule over the others; and describe the single further case that would most decisively distinguish the competing rules.
