In my reading group on the philosophy of death we keep discovering that the ancient Epicurean argument is where every discussion comes to rest, and after months of this I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself rather than another defense of either position. Both sides in our group grant the same core facts: death annihilates the subject, so there is no posthumous self left to undergo the loss; the absence of the dead person can plainly be a harm to the survivors; and the eternity before someone was born is not a state anyone regards with regret or dread. Yet the Epicureans infer that death is nothing to the person who dies, while the deprivation theorists infer that death can be bad for the person who dies precisely because it removes goods she would otherwise have had.

The passage that keeps getting quoted is not the familiar Epicurean slogan about death being nothing to us, but Lucretius's mirror argument from Book III of On the Nature of Things, in William Ellery Leonard's translation:

Look back:
Nothing to us was all fore-passed eld
Of time the eternal, ere we had a birth.
And Nature holds this like a mirror up
Of time-to-be when we are dead and gone.
And what is there so horrible appears?
Now what is there so sad about it all?
Is't not serener far than any sleep?

My working hypothesis is that the entire impasse turns on whether one accepts the symmetry Lucretius is insisting on here. The deprivation theorists treat the future goods death takes away as genuinely someone's—they would have been hers—while the past goods before birth were never hers to lose, so the mirror is misleading. The Epicureans just find that asymmetry question-begging: if nonexistence itself is nothing either way, the difference between would have had and could never have had is doing work neither side ever justifies. So I suspect we are not disagreeing about facts, nor even about values, but about a bedrock intuition about time and harm that neither side can argue for.

Is my diagnosis right? Or is there a distinct conceptual question underneath—about whether a harm must be experienced, or whether bad for someone requires a subject at a time, or about what harm means—that could be separated out and perhaps settled? I want a structural diagnosis of this impasse, grounded in what the two sides actually say, and an honest account of what, if anything, would count as progress given that diagnosis, or why none should be expected.
