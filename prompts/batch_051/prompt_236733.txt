I have been arguing with a friend about a problem from a course on practical rationality, and I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis. The old image is a donkey standing exactly midway between two identical piles of hay, with nothing to decide between them. I tried to turn that image into a formal argument against a certain conception of reason-based choice, but the argument now seems to prove far too much, and I cannot tell which move to blame.

Here is the argument as I presented it. (1) An act is rationally permissible only if the agent has a reason that favours that act over each of the alternatives open to her. (2) Consider a case of perfect symmetry: a shopper needs soup, can pay, and faces exactly two identical tins, one on the left and one on the right of an otherwise bare shelf. They do not differ in price, contents, quality, or ease of reach, their only difference is spatial position, and she has no standing policy, preference, or further end that would make that positional difference matter. Her alternatives are taking the left tin, taking the right tin, and leaving without soup. (3) She has a reason that favours taking some soup over leaving without it, but she has no reason that favours taking the left tin over taking the right tin, and none that favours the right over the left. (4) By (1), taking the left tin is impermissible: a reason does favour it over leaving, but no reason favours it over the right tin; the same, symmetrically, goes for taking the right tin. (5) By (1), leaving without soup is also impermissible, because her reason of wanting soup favours taking a tin over leaving, and no reason favours leaving over taking one. (6) The conception in (1) therefore renders every available act impermissible in an utterly ordinary shopping decision. (7) That cannot be right: taking either tin is, if not required, plainly permitted, and choosing arbitrarily between them seems like no failure of rationality at all. So at least one premise or step must be rejected.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Please name the step or premise you would reject, explain why it looked so plausible, and say what giving it up costs — which part of the picture of rational choice would have to be revised. I would most like to keep the claim that perfect symmetry supplies no reason for one option over the other, and the claim that choosing arbitrarily in such a case is fully rational, but I cannot see how to hold on to both while keeping the rest of the argument intact. Please answer in British English.
