I just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether the shift from foraging to farming was, on balance, a mistake. Her position was that it was a bad trade: humanity replaced a diversified and flexible subsistence portfolio with a concentrated, fragile bet on a few annual grains, and in doing so traded away free time, health, and a rough egalitarianism while buying the conditions for durable hierarchy. I work as a risk analyst, which means I think natively in terms of diversification, concentration risk, tail events, liquidity, optionality, path dependence, and discount rates, not in the categories historians and philosophers use when they talk about welfare across a change in way of life.

Restate the debate between the progress narrative and the bad-trade reading of the agricultural revolution in that portfolio-and-risk vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Treat the adoption of farming as a portfolio decision, and pause to say where the framing breaks. Then give me a residue report: which claims translate cleanly (for example, crop failure as concentration risk, sedentism as surrendering the option to move, granaries as reserves), which translate only under distortion, and which have no expression in the vocabulary at all (for example, calling the transition a mistake when no single agent decided it, or the claim that what welfare itself consisted in changed in the transition). I want to know whether the residue exposes a real weakness in my friend's bad-trade framing or only the limits of my professional vocabulary.
