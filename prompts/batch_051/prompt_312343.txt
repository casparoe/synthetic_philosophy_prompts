I'm grading a philosophy of perception exam and want a second opinion. The student's answer is typed out exactely as they wrote it. Here's the question the class got:

'Set out the argument from illusion, and explain how a disjunctivist responds to it. Then say, for each of the following cases, whether the disjunctivist takes it to be the same fundamental kind of mental state as the others or a different one: (i) veridically seeing a straight stick out of water, (ii) seeing a straight stick half-submerged in water and having it look bent, (iii) hallucinating a stick. Justify the classification.'

And here's one student's answer:

'The argument from illusion starts from the fact that a straight stick in water looks bent. Since the stick itself is straight, the bent appearance can't be a direct awareness of the stick; so the subject must be aware of a bent sense-datum. But this illusory experience can be subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical perception of a genuinely bent stick. If two experiences are indistinguishable, they must be the same fundamental kind of experience. So in veridical perception too we are only ever aware of sense-data, and we never directly perceive physical objects. The disjunctivist rejects this common-kind assumption. On their view, a veridical perception is one kind of state, and an illusory experience is a different kind of state, so the fact that they can be indistinguishable doesn't force them to have the same nature. So my classification is: (i) veridical perception and (ii) illusion are fundamentally different kinds, and (iii) hallucination is a third kind, since there is no external object at all. The tricky part is that all three can be subjectively indistinguishable from one another, which disjunctivism has to explain. It also seems to imply that in the good case the subject could not be in error about what they see.'
