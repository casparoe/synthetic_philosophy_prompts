I have no legal training, but I have been reading on my own and trying to understand a line of argument in Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., The Common Law, in the lecture called The Criminal Law. My understanding of the passage is this. Holmes seems to say that the criminal law did not always make liability turn on personal fault. In early law, what mattered was that a forbidden harm had been caused, and punishment followed the act rather than the inner state of the actor. He suggests that even when the law later began to use words like intent or malice, it often meant only that the harmful consequences were sufficiently foreseeable under an objective standard, not that the particular person was morally culpable. He seems to think there is no conceptual necessity that punishment require mens rea; that requirement is a historical and policy-created feature of the system.

I have been thinking about that claim, and I would like to test it with a counterfactual.

Suppose that in 2025 one American state adopts a constitutional amendment requiring the legislature and courts to eliminate all mental-state elements from the definitions of ordinary crimes. Purpose, knowledge, recklessness, and negligence would no longer be elements of murder, assault, theft, fraud, or drug offenses. For a conviction, the state would need to prove only that the defendant's conduct caused the prohibited harm, under the ordinary rules of causation. Sentencing would be set by a mechanical schedule tied to resulting harm, not to culpability. A person who carefully but mistakenly mislabels a medication would face the same homicide penalty as a person who deliberately poisons a patient, assuming both patients die. Reasonable mistake of fact would no longer be a defense. Duress and insanity would survive only as narrow mercy-based exceptions, not as negations of the offense.

Predict, as carefully as you can, what would happen to that state's criminal justice system and to the behavior of its residents over the following two decades. I want the reasoning to be visible, not just a conclusion. For each link in your prediction, say whether you regard it as robust, plausible, or a guess, and say what facts would have to be different for that link to fail.

In particular, I would like you to address these mechanisms: whether juries would refuse to convict in the most sympathetic strict-liability cases, whether prosecutors would quietly exercise charging discretion to avoid the harshest results, whether the amendment would drive people out of riskier productive activities such as medicine, pharmacy, and manufacturing, whether lawmakers would start defining prohibited harms more narrowly, and whether the public would come to see the criminal law as more or less legitimate. Finally, I would like to know whether this thought experiment tends to confirm, refute, or complicate the Holmes argument I summarized. Would the system be possible, but reveal that fault is what makes punishment tolerable? Or would it show that Holmes was right that fault is not necessary and may largely be a later addition?
