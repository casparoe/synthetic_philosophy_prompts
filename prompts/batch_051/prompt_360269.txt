John Stuart Mill died in 1873, before the two arguments that now anchor much of the academic abortion debate had been formulated. Judith Jarvis Thomson's violinist argument holds that abortion can be permissible even if the fetus is a person with a right to life, because a right to life is not a right to use another person's body: someone kidnapped and connected to a famous violinist who needs their kidneys for nine months would be entitled to disconnect. The future-like-ours argument associated with Don Marquis holds that killing is seriously wrong chiefly because it deprives the victim of the valuable future it would otherwise have had, and since a fetus has a future like ours, abortion is seriously wrong from early in pregnancy without any premise about personhood. Mill would approach this material with the harm principle, a utilitarian account of well-being and of women's subordination, and a deep suspicion of moral claims that rest on appeals to nature or intuition.

Predict where Mill would have landed had he encountered both arguments, giving special weight to the points where his commitments would push him to resist both camps or reframe the dispute rather than simply choose a side.
