I've been trying to write something about the Late Bronze Age and I realized I don't actually know what I mean by "collapse" beyond a vague feeling that a bunch of things fell apart at once. Give me a working definition of societal collapse, run it through the cases below, tell me where it gets them wrong or refuses to answer, and then either fix the definition or defend the weird verdicts.

The cases, roughly in order of how confident I am:

1. The eastern Mediterranean around 1177 BCE. The Hittite state disappears, the Mycenaean palaces are burned, Ugarit is destroyed and never reoccupied, and long-distance trade in things like tin and copper just stops. This one is definitely a collapse; if your definition doesnt count it, I don't want it.

2. The Classic Maya lowlands in the ninth century CE. Major cities empty out, carved monuments with royal dates stop being produced, populations drop hard. I'd call this a collapse even though Maya people obviously persisted and smaller communities kept going.

3. Detroit. It lost more than half its population and went through municipal bankruptcy, but I would never say American society collapsed, or even that Detroit's society collapsed. I just cant say exactly why, if collapse just means population loss plus institutions failing.

4. The western Roman Empire in the fifth century. Army, taxation, and central authority in the west are mostly gone, but a lot of daily life continues, cities still function in places, and the transformation-not-fall people have a point. Borderline, and honestly this is the case that got me confused in the first place.

5. Rapa Nui / Easter Island. The old story is they cut down their trees, had a resource crash, and the moai-building society imploded before Europeans ever arrived. The revisionist story says the population was fairly stable until contact, and the real damage came later from disease, slave raids, and sheep. I don't want a definition that quietly assumes either archaeology; I want one that tells me what would count as collapse once the facts are settled, whoever the facts favor.

6. The Soviet Union in 1991. A superpower dissolves itself, borders mostly stay, the bureaucrats go to work on Monday. Politically it sure looks like a collapse, but there were no hordes at the gates and no demographic catastrophe. Is political collapse alone enough?

7. The Norse settlements in Greenland. Farms and churches are abandoned in the fifteenth century; nobody conquers them, there's no obvious cataclysm, it looks like people just leave, or dwindle gradually. Is abandonment a kind of collapse or not?

8. The Aztec empire in 1521. Tenochtitlan is destroyed and the state is replaced by New Spain. I'd call that conquest, not collapse, but I couldn't tell you what's doing the work in that distinction.

So the definition has to separate collapse from mere decline, from conquest, and from ordinary abandonment or transformation. If you think one of those distinctions can't be made cleanly, say that and deal with the consequences. I especially want to know which of my "definitely" cases your definition ends up spitting back at me as the wrong answer.
