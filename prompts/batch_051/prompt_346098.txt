I'm in a reading group on free will, and one argument keeps ending at a slogan that nobody makes precise. One member argues that quantum mechanics rescues free will: because fundamental physics is indeterministic, the past plus the laws of nature do not fix a unique future, so when a person deliberates, there is real openness in their choice; it could, here and now, have gone otherwise. A second member always replies, "chance is not choice," and treats the discussion as settled. His point seems to be that the kind of openness quantum mechanics supplies is the wrong kind: if an action comes out differently only because some particle happened to decay one way rather than another, then the outcome is random rather than free, and randomness is not a form of agency. That sounds like it has real force, but I suspect the line compresses several different claims of very different strengths, and I want to separate them before our next meeting.

What are the distinct precise claims that "chance is not choice" could be making in this debate? Please lay them out as a menu, from the barely controversial (a random event is not, by itself, an act of choosing) through the substantive (adding an indeterministic element to a decision process does not by itself give the agent authorship, control, or responsiveness to reasons over the outcome) to the very strong (any indeterminism in the causal ancestry of an action turns the action into luck and destroys moral responsibility). For each reading, say what it must mean by "chance" and by "choice" to come out true — for instance, whether "chance" means uncaused, or not uniquely determined by prior causes, or outside the agent's control, and whether "choice" requires being settled by the agent's reasons, being produced by the agent itself as an uncaused cause, or merely not being coerced — and say whether the reading is plausible or only works against a strawman. Then tell me which of these readings would actually undermine the first member's quantum-indeterminacy argument, which would leave it standing, and what further premise the second member would need to add before the slogan could really settle the dispute.
