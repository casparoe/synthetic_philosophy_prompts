I've got a habit when I referee papers and book manuscripts, and I want to know whether it's a defensible policy or a bias with a tidy rationalization. I go through the manuscript claim by claim. For each substantive claim I ask whether I'd bet on its truth; if yes, I give it a check. By the end I might have forty checked claims. Asked about any one of them on its own, I'll say I believe it. But asked whether the manuscript is free of error, I'll say almost certainly not — with forty substantive claims, there's a real chance at least one is wrong, though I can't tell you which one. So I end up believing claim one, believing claim two, and so on, while also believing that at least one of them is false. I treat that as sound aggregation: screen each claim hard, then discount the whole. A colleague says this is just the preface paradox and that I'm being incoherent: if I believe each claim I should believe the conjunction, and if I expect a falsehood somewhere I should withhold from at least one line. But here's what's bugging me. In a lottery, I'm completely comfortable saying of each ticket that it loses while also believing one of them wins, and my colleague finds that unproblematic. So is my refereeing habit defensible in the same way the lottery beliefs are, or is there a real difference between the two cases that should make me give the policy up in the manuscript case? And if the policy is fine sometimes and not other times, what's the actual rule that seperates the good cases from the ones where I'm just refusing to let my beliefs face each other? Please give me that rule, not a survey of the two paradoxes, and answer in American English.
