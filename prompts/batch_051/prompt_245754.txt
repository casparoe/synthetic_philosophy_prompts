I recently had a long argument with a friend about whether what behavioral economists call hyperbolic discounting is just the old problem of akrasia wearing a new vocabulary. My friend held that the person who abandons a resolution at the last minute is simply discounting the future too steeply, and that this is all weakness of will has ever amounted to. I was not so sure, because Aristotle appears to be describing a defect in how an agent relates to his own judgment, not merely a defect in how much he values delayed outcomes. In the Nicomachean Ethics, Book VII, Chapter 3, he writes: "The profligate yields to his appetites from choice, considering it right always to pursue the pleasure that offers, whereas the man of defective self-restraint does not think so, but pursues it all the same." Please explain the conceptual issue this passage raises. Is Aristotle's distinction between the profligate and the unrestrained the same distinction that a hyperbolic-discounting framework draws between time-consistent impatience and time-inconsistent preference reversal? Or are akrasia and hyperbolic discounting two different explanatory projects that happen to converge on the same behavior? I want the conceptual architecture of how the two ideas do and do not map onto each other, not a survey of the literature.
