I have been running a philosophy reading group on the problem of other minds, and we ended up constructing a two-person version of Mary's room that none of us could settle; I would be grateful for a careful reconstruction of it. In the familiar thought experiment, Mary knows every physical fact about human colour vision but has never herself seen colour; when she first sees something red, the knowledge argument claims she learns a new fact, while the deflationary replies claim she acquires only an ability, a phenomenal concept, or a mode of acquaintance, rather than a new fact.

Now consider the following variant. Mary and Anne are twins raised in separate but identical monochrome rooms; each knows every physical fact about human colour vision, each knows that the other has had exactly the same history and is in the same epistemic position, both are ideal reasoners, and neither has ever seen colour. Anne is released, sees an ordinary red tomato in natural light, and is then returned to her room. Mary, who has still never seen colour, may converse with Anne through a hatch for as long as she likes. Anne is wholly sincere and maximally articulate, but no coloured objects, pictures, or direct neural stimulation may pass through the hatch, so Mary cannot simply be shown a sample.

The question is what, if anything, Anne's testimony adds to Mary's store of knowledge. Does Mary come to know a new fact — for instance, a fact about what red is like, or about what Anne is now experiencing — so that the knowledge argument carries over to this two-person case? Or does the variant expose the supposed gain as essentially first-personal and non-transmissible, thereby strengthening one of the deflationary replies? Please work through what each of the main positions would predict here — the knowledge argument, the ability hypothesis, and the phenomenal-concept or acquaintance replies — state which position you think the variant most favours, and explain why. Reason from the structure of the thought experiment rather than from any secondary source. End your reply with a single self-contained line of the form \answer{knowledge argument survives}, \answer{deflationary replies survive}, or \answer{variation is neutral}.
