I am a PhD student in metaethics, and I have been trying to determine exactly how much damage the Frege–Geach embedding problem does to sophisticated expressivism. I would like you to write a short dialogue between two equally able interlocutors. The first is a committed expressivist: roughly, someone who holds that the meaning of a moral sentence is explained by the non-cognitive attitude it conventionally expresses, and who thinks the Frege–Geach problem is serious but solvable within an expressivist framework. The second is a truth-conditional cognitivist: someone who holds that moral sentences have ordinary propositional contents, and who thinks the embedding problem exposes a structural defect in expressivism rather than a technical gap.

The dialogue should engage the real problem, not a caricature of it. It should work through unasserted contexts — the antecedents of conditionals, negation, disjunction, and attitude ascriptions such as "Fiona doubts that breaking a promise is wrong" — and it should give particular attention to the validity of inferences such as: from "If breaking a promise is wrong, then getting someone else to break a promise is wrong" together with "Breaking a promise is wrong" to "Getting someone else to break a promise is wrong." The critic should press the expressivist on why the attitude expressed by the consequent survives embedding intact, on mixed moral-and-empirical premises, and on the suspicion that the expressivist's explanatory resources secretly reintroduce propositions. The expressivist should offer the strongest available replies — for example, higher-order attitudes toward the attitudes expressed by component sentences, a compositional account of what gets expressed in embedded contexts, an inferentialist or commitment-theoretic account of validity, and a quasi-realist story about how moral language earns truth-talk and logical discipline without thereby acquiring descriptive content — and should not simply fall back on paraphrase or change the subject.

Both characters should be rendered with equal competence and should respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talking past one another. Each should make at least one genuine concession or refinement in the course of the exchange. By the end, the dialogue should locate with precision where the disagreement bottoms out — for instance, whether validity is fundamentally a matter of truth-preservation or of incoherence among attitudes, and whether the expressivist can state that very issue without already helping herself to the propositional contents she purports to reject.
