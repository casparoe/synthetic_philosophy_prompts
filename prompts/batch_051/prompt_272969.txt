I'm a philosophy of biology PhD student, and I keep getting dragged into the what-is-an-organism fight through whichever single case the speaker happens to like. One week it's the leafcutter ant colony, which is obviously a superorganism; the next it's a lichen, which is obviously two species cooperating; the next it's a Portuguese man o' war, where even 'one body' turns out to be doing a lot of work. Before I read yet another theory, I want the specification: what does an adequate theory of biological individuality actually have to get right, and which of the usual demands are real constraints versus one camp's prejudice?

Give me the desiderata list. For each requirement: the case or consideration that forces it, and a verdict on whether it's non-negotiable or a preference. The list has to face at least these cases, and I want to know what a theory is committed to saying about each:

- a honeybee or leafcutter ant colony: division of labour, most members sterile, reproduction only at colony level.
- a Portuguese man o' war: one embryo, but the body is a set of specialised zooids, each zooid basically a modified polyp embedded in a larger whole.
- a lichen: a fungus and an alga or cyanobacterium that look and function like one thing, can disperse as a unit via soredia, but carry two genomes and can in principle go their seperate ways.
- an aphid and its Buchnera endosymbionts: bacteria living inside specialised host cells, passed mother-to-offspring, essential for the aphid's nutrition, but with their own genomes and no shared life cycle.
- a coral and its zooxanthellae: algae acquired from the environment each generation; the coral bleaches and sickens without them, but the two lineages remain distinct.
- a quaking aspen clone: one genotype, many trunks, connected roots, no single birth event.
- a Dictyostelium slime mould: free-living amoebae that aggregate under stress into a slug and then a fruiting body, where some cells form a stalk and die, so the whole reproduces but is assembled from previously independent cells.
- a salamander whose egg capsules are colonised by photosynthetic algae, so the embryo develops alongside algae it didn't inherit; this keeps coming up in our reading group and I want to know what a good theory says about it.

I also want the distinctions a theory can't just collapse without argument: evolutionary individuality versus physiological or functional individuality; being an individual versus being a part; having a boundary versus being a closed system; and being a reproductive unit versus being a unit of selection. For each distinction, say whether respecting it is compulsory or just tidy, and which case gets misdescribed if you collapse it.

Then the part I most want: tell me which requirements can't be jointly satisfied. I've got a suspicion that 'a single reproductive bottleneck or shared fate' and 'take holobionts seriously' can't both be non-negotiable, but I want it made precise. If some subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable, say so and show which case forces the choice. And tell me which single requirement does the most work, i.e. knocks out the most popular candidates — unit-of-selection views, physiological-integration views, immunological views, life-cycle/bottleneck views, whatever else you take to be in the running.

If you add a scorecard of those contenders against the list, keep it to a few lines each; the criteria are the deliverable, not the review. Hard cap: 900 words. A table for the requirements is fine.
