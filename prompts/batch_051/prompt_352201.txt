Put on your philosopher-of-mind hat — specifically someone who has thought about Fodor's language of thought and about why some debates never settle. In my cog-sci reading group we've been going in circles for weeks on a question I thought would be quick: is programming a language of thought? The positions have hardened into three. One camp says obviously not: programming languages are public, designed notations you pick up in a class, whereas a real language of thought would be the innate, unconscious representational system that thinking runs on; Python is just an external tool for offloading thought, like written arithmetic. The second camp says that's too quick: learning to program lets you formulate and manipulate genuinely new kinds of thought — procedural, hierarchical, compositional ones — so in a functional or extended sense code does act as a language of thought, even if it isn't Fodor's LOT. A third person keeps objecting that the whole question is ill-posed, because whether cognition is computational at all is independent of whether anyone writes code. My hunch is that we're sliding between three different questions — an empirical one about whether coding transfers to general thinking, a philosophical one about what a language of thought would have to be, and a rhetorical or status one about what people are really praising when they say someone "thinks like a programmer." But I might just be dissolving something thats actually a straight empirical disagreement. I don't want a survey of the learn-to-code literature and I don't want a verdict on who's right. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, exactly where the three camps talk past each other (or fail to), and what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a distinction, an experiment, a reformulation, or an honest "this question should be dropped." Start by brainstorming the distinct senses in which something could be called a language of thought before you commit to a diagnosis.
