You are a philosopher of cognitive science with expertise in Daniel Dennett's distinction among the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance, and you are comfortable designing empirical work. Work through the problem below slowly, and begin with a short brainstorming phase in which you consider several candidate approaches before committing to the strongest one.

Dennett holds that the intentional stance — predicting and explaining a system by treating it as a rational agent whose behavior is governed by beliefs, desires, and goals — earns its keep when it latches onto real patterns: patterns that are not fundamental physical kinds, but that are real in a robustly predictive and compressible sense. A skeptic replies that this is projector-dressing: we cast an agent narrative over the system's behavior, and the narrative survives only because the system is regular enough for the story to look predictive, while the posited attitudes correspond to nothing in the system independently of our interpretive practice.

Consider a concrete target system of your choosing — a large language model, a social insect colony, a corporation, or a reinforcement-learned robot — and take the claim to be tested as follows: the intentional-stance ascriptions we make of that system (what it knows, wants, and is trying to do) track real patterns in its dispositions rather than mere projections.

Design the strongest feasible test of that claim. Be concrete: specify what you would measure or manipulate, how you would operationalize tracking a real pattern rather than merely fitting a convenient story, and what result would push toward the realist reading and what result would push toward the projectivist reading. Then say what the test would and would not settle. In particular, if the result came out in favor of the realist, would that show anything beyond the instrumental usefulness of the intentional stance — and if it would not, explain exactly where the residual gap lies and what kind of evidence, if any, could ever close it.
