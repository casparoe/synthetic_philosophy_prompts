Me and my brother got into an argument the other night about whether you can blame someone for what they did if the whole universe is deterministic, and he hit me with this thought experiment. A neuroscientist secretly puts a chip in some guy's brain (I think the guy might be named Jones, from some famous paper my brother half remembers). The chip is programmed so that if the guy is about to decide to vote for candidate A, the chip will kick in and force him to decide to vote for candidate B instead. But the guy decides to vote for B all on his own, so the chip never actually does anything. My brother's point was that the guy couldn't have done otherwise — the chip would have stopped him — but we'd still hold him responsible for voting for B, so responsibility doesn't actually require being able to do otherwise. He said this is supposed to refute something called the principle of alternative possibilities.

Here's the part I've been turning over in my head. The whole thing only works because the chip is 100 percent reliable; that's what guarantees the guy couldn't have done otherwise. So what happens if the chip is only, say, 90 percent reliable? If the guy had started to decide for candidate A, there'd be a 10 percent chance the chip would glitch and he'd actually go through with it. Now it's not so clean that he couldn't have done otherwise — but a 10 percent chance doesn't feel like the kind of could that matters for blame, or maybe it does and I'm wrong. I want someone to walk through how the case changes as the chip's reliability drops from 100 percent to 99, to 90, to 50, to completely broken. At what point does the original arguement stop working? And does it make a difference if the guy knows about the chip and knows the failure rate, versus him having no idea? Like, if he knows there's a 10 percent chance the chip won't stop him, does that give him the relevant kind of alternative possibility, or is it still too flukey to count? Work through all the moving parts and tell me where you land.
