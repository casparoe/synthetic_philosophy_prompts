Analyze the nineteenth-century arithmetization of analysis—Dedekind’s construction of the real numbers as cuts in the rationals, together with Cantor’s treatment of them as equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences—as a case study in the distinction between discovering the continuum and defining a substitute for it. On one reading, the constructions reveal a pre-existing completion latent in the rationals; on another, they merely define a formal object that behaves like the continuum while changing the subject. What exactly does each construction presuppose that is not itself arithmetic, and does that presupposition smuggle the geometric continuum back in? Then say what the episode implies for the broader claim that the real numbers are found rather than invented, and where the arithmetization leaves the intuitive continuum unexplained.
