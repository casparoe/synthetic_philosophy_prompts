I have been trying to turn the problem of logical omniscience into something empirically testable. Standard possible-worlds semantics makes knowledge closed under entailment: if an agent knows P, and P entails Q, then Q holds in every world left open by the agent's knowledge, so the agent counts as knowing Q. Real agents plainly fail to draw out many consequences of what they know, and the usual reply is that these failures are a performance limitation — the knowledge is present implicitly, and what is missing is only retrieval or computation. The slide from 'the formalism requires it' to 'the agent really knows it' has always struck me as suspicious, but I would rather settle it experimentally than relitigate the semantics.

Please design the best experiment you can to distinguish, in a human subject, between two readings: (a) the subject genuinely does not know that Q follows from P until the derivation or prompt supplies it; and (b) the subject knew Q implicitly all along and simply could not display it under the test conditions. It seems best to teach subjects a small artificial system of rules, or a made-up algebra, so that the premises and the target consequences are novel and controlled; say what should be measured, perhaps by varying the depth or type of inference required. Tell me which pattern of results would favour each reading, and then be precise about what a positive result for implicit knowledge would still not settle. In particular, I want to know whether 'knew it implicitly all along' makes any predictions that a view which ascribes only a disposition to derive would not also make, or whether the claim is unfalsifiable because any failure to produce Q can always be redescribed as a performance deficit. Is there a result that would force the defender of closure to concede that the knowledge was not there, even in principle?
