I’m a retired natural scientist (biology) who has recently gotten into philosophy, and I’ve been asked to give a short opening talk at a public library event on political echo chambers. Most of the audience thinks the other side isn’t just wrong but argues in bad faith. I want to make them squirm a little by starting from this sentence from John Stuart Mill’s On Liberty, Chapter II:

“Yet it is as evident in itself, as any amount of argument can make it, that ages are no more infallible than individuals; every age having held many opinions which subsequent ages have deemed not only false but absurd; and it is as certain that many opinions, now general, will be rejected by future ages, as it is that many, once general, are rejected by the present.”

Could you write me that talk? Five or six minutes, not a lecture. I don’t want it to turn into a cliché about “both sides have a point.” The hard part is that I also think some people genuinely do argue in bad faith, and I don’t want the talk to pretend otherwise. Is there a way to hold onto Mill’s fallibility point while still saying that bad faith exists and matters? It needs to connect with people who haven’t read Mill.
