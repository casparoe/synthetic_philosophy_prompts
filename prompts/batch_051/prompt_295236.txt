A legal system will sometimes try, convict, and punish a person decades after the offense. The seventy-five-year-old before the court may share little with the twenty-year-old who acted: much of the body has been replaced through ordinary biological turnover, memories of the act may be fragmentary or absent, and the commitments and dispositions that once made the act intelligible may have vanished. The court nonetheless assumes it is punishing the very same entity that committed the crime, and would treat as incoherent any suggestion that it is imposing punishment on someone else. At the same time, surrounding legal practice is full of time-indexing: it asks what the defendant's state of mind was at the moment of the act rather than what it is now; it asks whether a testator had capacity at the moment of signing rather than at the moment the will is contested; it treats causes of action as arising on particular dates and measures damages as they stood at a fixed point in time. In these moments the law sounds as if it is slicing persons into temporal parts.

What theory of persistence through time does this practice presuppose? Is the criminal law committed to endurantism, the view that a person is wholly present at each moment at which she exists, or to perdurantism, the view that a person is a temporally extended whole whose momentary slices are only parts of her, roughly as a hand is a spatial part of a body? Or is the practice genuinely underdetermined between the two?

Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics as carefully as you can, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and being explicit about where several rival theories could each license what the law does. Then say what would change, if anything, if a court explicitly adopted perdurantism rather than endurantism, or endurantism rather than perdurantism, in how guilt, punishment, and excuses are framed. In particular, is the law's routine time-indexing in matters like mental state and capacity a problem for the endurantist, and is the law's insistence that the same person is punished an obstacle for the perdurantist?
