In a jurisprudence seminar, a classmate dismissed concerns about desuetude — the phenomenon of statutes that remain on the books but are never enforced — by appealing to the maxim that we live under "a government of laws, not of men." His argument appeared to be that the rule of law is satisfied as long as the law is validly enacted and still on the books, whether or not officials ever apply it. That maxim can bear several different readings, and I doubt all of them support his conclusion. Lay out the distinct precise claims "a government of laws, not of men" could be expressing, from the relatively uncontroversial to the radical. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantively defensible, or false. Then explain which reading or readings would actually license his dismissal of desuetude, and whether any plausible reading cuts the other way — making systematic non-enforcement itself a failure of the rule of law.
