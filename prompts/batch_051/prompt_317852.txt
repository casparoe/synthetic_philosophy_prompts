I serve as the patient and family representative on my hospital's resource-allocation committee. I hold this seat because I spent years as a caregiver, not because I have any academic training, but the committee has forced me to think carefully about a question I keep circling back to: whether the number of people on each side of a decision like this is, by itself, a reason to choose one side rather than the other. We must set a standing policy for a recurring kind of shortage. A single stock of a scarce, life-saving medication can be used whole as one full dose, which would save one patient, or divided into five smaller doses, each of which would save one of five other patients. The drug remains equally effective in the smaller doses, and no partial dose is useful to the single patient. In the case now before us, all six patients are alike in urgency, prognosis, and chance of responding; none has a special claim, and the shortage will not resolve in time to save everyone.

Our debate has split roughly two ways. One physician argues that the rule should simply be: treat the larger group. Five deaths, she says, are worse than one, and any policy that did not save the five would be impossible to explain to families or to the public. Another member — who says she was once persuaded by an argument she read somewhere but cannot now reconstruct — answers that the number of people on a side is not by itself a reason that any particular patient should live or die. She thinks each patient has an equal claim to the drug, so the fair rule would give each side a genuine chance, perhaps by flipping a coin between the one patient and the group of five before deciding which individuals receive it. She also says that drawing a single name from all six would look neutral but would let the numbers decide anyway, since the group of five would win five times out of six.

I find myself pulled both ways. When I think of the five as five separate people, the coin-flip view seems to respect them as individuals; when I imagine standing before the families of the five, it seems to me that five funerals are somehow worse than one, and that a policy that knowingly allowed them must be mistaken.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete rule the committee can vote on — whether one of the two I have described, a modification of one, or something else — with the reasoning behind it, and it should take seriously the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than dismissing it. Keep it free of citations and of technical vocabulary; the other members are clinicians, administrators, and community representatives, and several of them are as untrained in philosophy as I am.
