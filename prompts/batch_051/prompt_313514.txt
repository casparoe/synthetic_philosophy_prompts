I recently watched a friend use a griefbot assembled from her late mother’s texts and voicemails, and it has forced me to settle on a view I mostly believe but can’t fully test. The position I’ve come to hold is this: when a person has, before dying, consented to the construction of such a system and to limits on its use, griefbots are morally permissible and typically beneficial, because they serve the living; the dead no longer have interests or welfare that can be set back. On this view, dignity, replacement, and the uncanniness of the simulation matter only insofar as they affect the mourning person.

That framing may be too comfortable, so I want to know what would genuinely count against it, not just the familiar worries that a bot is a flattened copy or that it might say something strange. Which observations, cases, or broader commitments would have to be true for the view to be false? For instance, would it be a serious defeater if even a consenting user’s memory of the deceased gradually gets edited by the bot’s averaged version of them? Could a sufficiently good simulation generate duties to the dead after all, or make it true that the dead have been conscripted into a relationship they never endorsed in that form? What would the ethics of memory, mourning, and representation have to show for my view to collapse? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to distinguish those a committed defender would have to concede count against the view from those they could dismiss without special pleading. Please begin with a one-sentence summary of your overall assessment and keep the response under 800 words.
