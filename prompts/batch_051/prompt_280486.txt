I am an undergraduate in a seminar on the philosophy of punishment. Each week the instructor gives us a short hypothetical case and announces, with only a one-line explanation, the sentence she would impose; we have been using these to reverse-engineer her underlying principle. She says, in substance, that each sentence is meant to match the gravity of the wrongdoing and the offender's situation, but that formula seems compatible with both retributivism and consequentialism. Next week she will give us the new case below, and I want to be able to predict her sentence and her reasoning.

Her recorded verdicts so far are:

1. The offender, a first-time offender with steady employment, stole $180 from an open cash register. No prior record, and the offender expressed remorse. Sentence: one year of probation and restitution, no custodial term.

2. The offender, a bookkeeper with no prior record, embezzled $410,000 from a nonprofit over four years by falsifying accounts. After discovery, the offender repaid $200,000 and expressed remorse. Sentence: three years in custody plus restitution.

3. The offender, a man with three prior theft convictions, snatched a handbag containing $70 from an elderly woman. No weapon was used and no injury resulted. Sentence: eighteen months in custody.

4. The offender, a first-time offender, threw one punch in a bar fight that broke the victim's jaw. The victim had insulted the offender, but the provocation was not legally adequate. Sentence: nine months in custody.

5. The offender, a store manager with no prior record and a gambling disorder, took $9,000 from the till to pay gambling debts. By sentencing, the offender had completed a treatment program. Sentence: six months in custody, suspended for two years, with restitution and continued aftercare as conditions.

The new case is this. A 58-year-old first-time offender embezzled $60,000 over three years from the small business where she kept the accounts, using falsified records in the same way as the offender in case 2. Part of the money went to private care for her disabled adult son, for whom she is the sole full-time caregiver; any custodial sentence of six months or more will place the son in emergency state care. She confessed when the discrepancy was noticed, has committed no further misconduct in the years since discovery, and is assessed as very unlikely to reoffend.

Given this record, I would like to know: which principle best explains all five of her verdicts, and is it retributive, consequentialist, or a particular hybrid? Which past case is the closest analog for the new one, and does it actually determine the outcome? If the record underdetermines her decision on the new case, I want the sentence each candidate principle would prescribe, your best guess about what she will say, and one further hypothetical case whose facts would cleanly separate the candidate principles. I am less interested in the conclusion alone than in the inference from the pattern.
