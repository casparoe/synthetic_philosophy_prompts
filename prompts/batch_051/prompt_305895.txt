I've come to think about morality in roughly Leibnizian terms, and I want to know what would count against the position before I lean on it further. The core I'm drawn to is this: moral goodness is not a separate faculty or a deliverance of unreasoned sentiment, but an extension of the same rationality that operates in prudence—concern for the well-being of all mind-like things, ordered by wisdom. Love is fundamentally disinterested pleasure in the perfection or happiness of another, and justice is charity regulated by wisdom: caring for the good of rational beings in proportion to their place in the best possible order. Moral truths are as objective as geometry, but they obligate through reasons rather than through bare will. This picture attracts me because it makes ethics continuous with rationality, avoids grounding morality in convention or arbitrary divine command, and promises to unify self-interest, benevolence, and justice under a single principle.

What would genuinely count against this view? I don't just want standard objections; I want a survey of the evidence, arguments, possible cases, and commitments that a defender of this position should treat as threatening, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Specifically: which Leibnizian commitments are doing the real work, and which are dispensable qualifications? Are the central notions—wisdom, proportion, disinterested love—sharp enough to rule anything out, or has the view been insulated by the very concepts that make it attractive? Where would a committed Leibnizian have to concede a cost rather than reinterpret the counterexample? And is the position's flexibility a feature of a rationalist moral theory, or a sign that it risks nothing?
