I've been reaading about desuetude — laws that are technically still in force but haven't been enforced in forever, like a town still having a no-cattle-on-Main-Street ordinance from 1890. The view I keep getting pulled toward is a strict rule-of-law one: if the legislature never repealed it, it's still law, and a cop or prosecutor who finally enforces it isn't doing anything weird; they're just applying what's actually on the books. Letting non-enforcement quietly kill laws seems way worse to me, because then officials get a backdoor repeal power by just ignoring laws they don't like, which is its own kind of arbitrariness.

But I also have a pretty firm intuition I can't shake: if a law hasn't been enforced in decades and everyone has reasonably arranged their lives as if it didn't exist, suddenly enforcing it feels unfair — more like rule by official whim than rule of law. Like, the no-cattle ordinance gets dusted off to hassle one guy, and it feels wrong even though the law is real.

I don't want a referee's pick. I want to know what each side would have to give up, at the strongest version of both. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes — what would have to be true about fair notice, reliance, or legal validity for me to say, ok, the old law was always there and enforcement is fine? What would it take for the theory to go, or to get amended to make room for desuetude — what kinds of cases or principles should push me there? And is there a negotiated reading of rule of law where both survive, like distinguishing a law's validity from whether a court should actually let it be enforced? I'm most moved by cases where the law is old but easy to find and the conduct is basically harmless.
