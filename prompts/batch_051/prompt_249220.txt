I work as a software engineer on an infrastructure team that builds and operates data pipelines, model evaluation, and monitoring. I have no formal philosophy background, but I've been told that a recurring problem on our team maps onto something in epistemology called the bootstrapping problem, and onto debates about easy knowledge: roughly, the worry that a process's outputs can't by themselves establish the reliability of that process without vicious circularity, versus the idea that some basic trust in a reliable process may be legitimate and that demanding independent checks all the way down is a regress we can't live with. We face this concretely several times a quarter, whenever someone proposes a validation, calibration, or self-monitoring check and we need to decide in design review whether it actually buys us independent evidence or just has the system confirm its own assumptions. A wrong call means either shipping a component whose evidence base was circular or adding months of redundant external instrumentation.

Act as a professional epistemologist who works on applied questions, think carefully, and first make explicit the key distinction or distinctions you take to be doing the work — for example between self-confirming circularity and benign basic trust — in terms a working software engineer can apply. Then produce a reusable decision procedure my team can run on any proposed check before we rely on it: a checklist or flowchart in words, with the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a verdict like "this is bootstrapping" or "this is independent enough for our purposes," and where it must stop and hand the decision to a human because the remaining issue is one of values or error costs rather than epistemology. We are a small team without training in philosophy; a staff engineer should be able to run the procedure in about thirty minutes, and the engineering manager signs off on the final verdict.

Then run the procedure on three cases we are actually arguing about: (1) validating a recalibrated temperature sensor by checking it against reference baths controlled by another unit from the same manufacturer running the same firmware; (2) using a model's calibration curve — computed from held-out labeled data, but at inference time applied to the model's own confidence scores — as evidence that those confidence scores are trustworthy in production; and (3) a watchdog process that judges system health from logs emitted by the same system it is guarding, where the watchdog runs on that same host. Say explicitly whether each is bootstrapping or independent enough, and mark any case where the procedure returns an answer my team will not like. Close by saying what the procedure cannot do and which judgments cannot be eliminated.
