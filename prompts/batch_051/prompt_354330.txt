Two researchers have spent a year running an adversarial collaboration on the doomsday argument. One side, call it the proponent camp, holds that the argument is basically sound: once you reason as if you are a random sample from the reference class of all humans who will ever live, finding yourself at your observed birth rank raises the probability that the total number of humans will be only modestly larger, rather than enormously larger, than that rank, and therefore that near-term extinction or permanent collapse is likelier than ordinary demographic and historical evidence would suggest. The other side, call it the skeptic camp, holds that the argument is a probabilistic artifact: the shift appears only because the reference class has been chosen arbitrarily, and it cancels once you properly condition on the fact that you exist at all.

Draft the joint statement the two camps should have produced. Include the following, in this order.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign. Include the bare likelihood arithmetic: under the self-sampling assumption (reasoning as if you were randomly selected from the reference class), the probability of being the n-th human, conditional on there being N total humans, is 1/N for all N ≥ n. Also include agreed claims about the difference between a likelihood and a posterior, about what the argument assumes regarding sampling, reference class, and priors, and about the fact that the disagreement is not over whether to update on evidence but over what the observation of one's own birth rank is evidence for.

2. Disagreements table. Give each remaining disagreement as a row in a table, with each side's formulation phrased so that a careful holder of that view would accept it as the definitive statement of their position. Cover at least these rows: the reference-class problem (why all humans, rather than all hominins, all conscious beings, all humans at your own stage of technological development, or you yourself across time); the self-indication assumption (the rule that your own existence is evidence favoring hypotheses with more observers like you) and whether it exactly cancels the doomsday likelihood shift; whether your birth rank is an indexical fact with evidential force or a fact that cannot discriminate among hypotheses that all require you to exist; and whether the resulting posterior is robust to reasonable variation in the prior.

3. Cruxes and tests. Identify the sub-questions on which the dispute actually turns. For each crux, specify a concrete toy model, thought experiment, or empirical fact that both camps agree bears on the crux; state each camp's prediction in advance; and explain what result would move at least one camp. Include at least two cruxes beyond the bare reference-class disagreement. One should compare an urn model in which a ball is drawn by an independent sampling mechanism with one in which the index is simply read off by an observer who exists under every hypothesis. Another should address whether the residual doomsday shift, even if smaller than the simplest version suggests, would be large enough to matter for practical long-term policy.

The document should be one both sides can actually put their names to: no wording that either camp would reject as a caricature of its view, and no dissolving the residual disagreement into vagueness.
