A linguist colleague of mine likes to say that every writing system is a theory of the language it writes. On this view an alphabet is not just a notation but a tacit claim that the speech stream decomposes into a small inventory of phoneme-sized units, since its letters mark roughly those units and ignore everything else; a syllabary would be the rival claim that syllables are the basic units, and a logography the rival claim that words or morphemes are. I want to know whether this slogan can be tested rather than merely enjoyed. Take English spelling as the case and ask the pointed question: does it encode phonemes, morphemes, or both at different levels, and how would one tell? I have in mind cases like sign and signal, where the written form of the stem is held constant even though the pronunciation changes. Design the strongest empirical test you can, specifying what data you would collect, what pattern would favor a phonemic analysis, what pattern would favor a morphological analysis, and how one separates the script's present structure from the fact that spellings freeze while pronunciations drift. Then be explicit about what a positive result would and would not establish. In particular, does finding largely regular grapheme-to-phoneme correspondences really show that the script itself posits phonemes, or only that the mapping can be described that way, with the talk of a script's 'theory' left as metaphor?
