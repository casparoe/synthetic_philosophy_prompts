A debate that's been going in circles for as long as I've been reading philosophy of science is the reductionism/emergence thing, and I don't want a verdict on the debate — I want a diagnosis of why it's stuck and what kind of disagreement it really is. One side says higher-level descriptions are just efficient shorthand: a cell is nothing but its molecules, temperature is nothing but mean kinetic energy, and all the causal work happens at the bottom. The other side says some higher-level structures are real in it's own right and can constrain or drive the parts: the shape of the whole protein changes what the amino acids do, the market's order book shapes individual trades, the population structure shapes which organisms live or die. What bugs me is that both sides lean on the same toy examples and draw opposite morals. The reductionist says the ideal gas shows temperature has been fully reduced to mechanics. The emergentist says the same example shows the higher level survived: temperature is a property of the ensemble, an average over molecules that only behaves as a clean macro-variable in the thermodynamic limit, so you've located rather than eliminated the higher level. And phase transitions make it worse, because everyone agrees real water boils, but the sharp discontinuity lives only in an infinite-system idealization. I can't tell if the impasse is verbal (different meanings of "real" or "nothing but"), about what should count as an explanation, about which cases are representative, or whether there's a genuinely open technical question underneath (whether finite systems have sharp-enough analogues, whether reduction will actually be completed in biology, etc.). My current hypothesis is that the fork is mostly about causation: one side means "whatever does the local pushing," the other means "whatever makes a stable difference to what happens," and each side thinks the other is changing the subject. Is that right? What would actual progress look like given the diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a new experiment, or an agreement that the original question was badly posed?

When you reply, lead with the bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
