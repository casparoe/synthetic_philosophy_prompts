A colleague in evolutionary theory and I have been going back and forth about whether Zahavi's handicap principle is quietly obsolote now that generative models can fake, for almost nothing, many of the signals that used to be expensive for humans to produce. The version of the principle I teach is roughly: a signal can only be trusted if it costs enough that a low-quality signaler couldn't afford it, and the peacock's tail is the standard example. But now a mediocre candidate can generate a plausible essay, a portfolio site, a tidy GitHub history, even an entire persona, in an afternoon. My colleague says the principle survives unchanged and simply predicts the next move: receivers stop trusting the cheapened signals and shift to signals that are still costly to fake, like live performance or work done in front of them. I'm not sure that saves the principle, though — it might just show the principle is empty, since any episode of cheating can be answered by picking a new signal and declaring that one the real handicap. So what I want is a verdict on whether the handicap principle survives the cheap-fake era. Say precisely which premise of the original argument generative AI puts pressure on. If it survives, tell me whether it survives in its original form or only in a repaired version, and what the repaired version says. If it doesn't survive, say what should replace it. And please separate the parts that are settled among people who actually study signaling from the parts that are genuinely contested — it's fine if the contest is where the interesting action is, just don't present a contested view as the consensus.
