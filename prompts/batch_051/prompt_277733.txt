I have been preparing a unit on supertasks for an undergraduate paradoxes course, and a colleague in the mathematics department left me with the following chain of reasoning. I find the conclusion unacceptable, but I cannot locate the mistake, and I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis.

Take the lamp example usually called Thomson's lamp. A lamp with a two-position switch is on at the start. Exactly half a minute later it is switched off; a quarter-minute after that, on; an eighth-minute after that, off; and so on, each interval being half the preceding one. Every switch specified by the rule lies before the end of the first minute, whose time is simply the limit of the switching times. The question is what state the lamp is in at that limit. The argument runs as follows.

(1) A lamp with a two-position switch is, at every instant at which it exists, either on or off.
(2) Suppose it is on at the one-minute mark. Since the switch is its only means of changing state, its being on then would have to be the result of some particular switching to "on." But every switching to "on" in the sequence is followed, before the minute ends, by a switching to "off." So no switching to "on" is the last switch, and none can leave the lamp on at the limit.
(3) By the same reasoning, the lamp cannot be off at the one-minute mark: every switching to "off" is followed, before the minute ends, by a switching to "on."
(4) So at the one-minute mark the lamp is neither on nor off, contradicting (1).
(5) Since (1) is built into the very idea of a two-position switch and the schedule of switches is perfectly explicit, the fault must lie in the assumption that the schedule could be carried out. The lamp supertask is therefore impossible; and, more generally, no sequence of acts whose times converge to a limit can have a determinate state at that limit.
(6) But that conclusion proves too much. Consider an ordinary marble rolling along a one-metre track and reaching the far end after exactly one second. Its journey contains the same convergent structure: it passes the halfway point at half a second, the three-quarter point at three-quarters of a second, and so on, with no last intermediate point before its arrival. If the lamp reasoning were sound, we could argue that the marble never reaches the end, because no particular intermediate position is the last one before arrival and therefore no stage delivers it to the far end. The marble plainly does arrive.

Where does the argument go wrong? I would like to know which step to reject, why that step initially looked compulsory, and what rejecting it costs. Does the cost fall on the principle that a two-position lamp must be either on or off, on the claim that the schedule is executable, or on something else? And can the lamp and the marble be separated in a principled way, or does any separation of the two cases require giving up something we would prefer to keep? If forced to say what I am most attached to, it is step (1); but I want to know what holding on to it really costs.
