i'm a staff attorney for a small city, and after a couple embarassing lawsuits the council asked me to build a screening checklist for proposed local laws. we get maybe a dozen ordinances and a couple emergency rules a year, usually pushed through in a hurry by the mayor's office, and i have under an hour per rule. i want the checklist to catch the ways a new law can fail basic rule-of-law norms before it passes—not whether it's good policy or constitutional, but stuff like secret rules, retroactive punishment, laws nobody could actually comply with, vague language, rules that change every few months, or a city that doesn't enforce the law as written. please give me a procedure my office can actually run, grounded in what philosophers of law have worked out about the rule of law, with the questions in order, what counts as a pass or fail on each, where it says 'fix this before it goes to council' and where it says 'this is a political call, not a rule-of-law problem.' then run it on three hypotheticals: (1) an ordinance creating a fine for 'any activity that tends to degrade the character of a residential neighborhood,' applied to conduct from before the law passed; (2) a food-truck rule that makes a license depend on complying with 'applicable departmental guidance' that has never been published and is only available by emailing the department; (3) a noise ordinance with a limit so low every bar in town would be in violation every weekend, while the police chief says they won't enforce unless someone specifically complains. be honest about which rule-of-law concerns this checklist is going to miss, and about where an attorney still has to make a judgment call rather than check a box.
