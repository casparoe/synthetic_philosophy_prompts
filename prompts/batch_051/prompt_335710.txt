I am taking an upper-level philosophy course on virtual worlds, and we have been working on the question whether harms that occur entirely inside a virtual environment are genuine harms. I am trying to bring that question to bear on a moderation policy from a social virtual-world platform, and I would like help with an interpretation problem.

The platform's user-safety policy includes this clause:

"You may not engage in conduct that harms another user. For the purposes of this section, 'harm' means bodily injury, damage to tangible real-world property, or a credible threat of either."

The clause was written when the platform was largely a text-based chat service, before it added the persistent user-built world in which the events below took place.

The case: E is a user who is largely housebound because of a disability and relies on the platform for most of her social contact. She has built a private virtual replica of her childhood home, which she has told her in-world friends is deeply meaningful to her. A group of other users discovered the replica, decided she was too attached to a place that is not real, and over several weeks built a mock cemetery on the adjacent virtual land. The headstones are inscribed with E's real legal name and with messages saying that she does not belong in the world and will never leave her house. The group ignored her requests to stop and invited other users to visit the cemetery. E's therapist has documented serious, worsening depression and anxiety caused by the campaign, but no one has threatened E with bodily injury or damaged her physical property.

The platform's moderators have declined to act, saying that under the clause as written this conduct is not "harm." I care less about the practical outcome than about what the clause actually reaches. Please work through what each of the following would say about whether this conduct is "harm" within the meaning of the clause: the plain words, the purpose of the policy, what the drafters would most plausibly have said if asked, and what a reasonable user would take the clause to mean. Then tell me which approach should win here and why, and whether the clause's narrow definition amounts to a philosophical position that virtual harms are not real harms, or merely to a failure of imagination on the drafters' part.

Keep your answer to no more than 850 words, and write in American English.
