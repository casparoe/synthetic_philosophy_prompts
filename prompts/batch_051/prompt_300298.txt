I've been mulling something over and I don't have the philosophy background to settle it, so I'd like a careful take. I'm a software engineer; I know a bit of formal logic and a decent amount about how large language models actually work, but I've never read much academic philosophy.

When I was younger I was really struck by Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach. The way I remember the core move is this: Gödel showed that in any formal system rich enough to talk about its own arithmetic, you can construct a sentence that says of itself that it is not provable inside that system, and if the system is consistent then that sentence is true but not provable by the system. Hofstadter then argues that the reason a human can see the sentence is true while the system cannot is evidence that a mind isn't just the running of one fixed formal system, and he ties that self-referential ability to what he calls strange loops and to self-awareness.

But that whole analogy was built at a time when the paradigm for intelligence was symbol manipulation in something like a formal system. The most striking minds we now have are large language models, which are huge neural networks trained to predict the next token. They clearly talk about themselves, reason about their own previous outputs, and can emit self-referential sentences, but they aren't doing much that looks like deduction in a formal system, and in practice nobody reads a transformer's weights as axioms plus inference rules. It feels like the analogy should be under pressure, but I can't put my finger on which bit actually breaks.

Does Hofstadter's Gödelian analogy survive this shift, is it dead, or does it just need repairing? I want to know which specific premise of the analogy the existence of large language models puts pressure on, whether the arguement still goes through in some form, and if a repaired version is possible what it would actually be claiming (probably something weaker). Please don't turn it into a reading list or a survey; I'm after the structure of the argument, not the history. Oh, and please answer in British English.
