I was listening to a podcast a while back about Christian ideas of hell, and one of the guests gave this tidy rundown of the whole debate that I jotted down from memory. He said there are basically three positions: the traditional eternal conscious torment view, annihilationism, and universalism. On the traditional view the damned suffer consciously forever; annihilationists hold that after the judgment the wicked are simply destroyed or cease to exist, so the punishment is permanent in it's consequences but not experienced forever; universalists hold that everyone is eventually reconciled to God. Then he claimed the traditional view is in deep trouble because it violates an obvious principle of proportionality: finite creatures committing sins during a finite lifespan simply can't deserve an infinite amount of punishment. That leaves, he said, only annihilationism and universalism as live options. And between those two he tipped clearly toward annihilationism, on the grounds that it's the only view that keeps justice intact—the punishment matches the crime because the person simply comes to an end—whereas universalism, as he put it, is mainly for people who find hell too upsetting to stomach, and it gets around the proportionality problem only by making the punishment short, or by turning it into therapy rather than real punishment.

I don't have any background in this, but a couple of steps in that summary felt too quick to me, especially the jump from "the traditional view seems harsh" to "therefore annihilationism is the only serious option." I'd like to know whether this is a fair summary of the live options. Could you go through it and flag what's accurate, what's a common simplification or mischaracterization, and which parts a defender of each of the three views would object to? In particular, does the proportionality objection really force the choice he says it does? And is his picture of what punishment amounts to on each view fair?
