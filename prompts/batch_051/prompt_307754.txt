I am a high school student in a small after-school philosophy discussion group. At our last meeting we watched a tragic film, and two things everyone seemed to accept afterwards were, first, that the final scenes had made us genuinely sorrowful, and, second, that we would all gladly watch the film again. I have been trying to work out whether those two admissions are really compatible, and my attempt produced the following argument:

(1) To enjoy a work is to take pleasure in the experience the work produces in one while one is engaged with it.
(2) A competent tragedy produces genuine sorrow or grief for its characters in the viewer.
(3) Genuine sorrow or grief is a painful state.
(4) One cannot take pleasure in an experience while feeling genuine pain from that same experience.
(5) Therefore, from (1) through (4), a viewer of a competent tragedy does not enjoy it.
(6) But viewers of competent tragedies do, in fact, enjoy them.
(7) So if (1), (3), and (4) stand, (2) must be false: the sadness tragedies produce in us is not genuine grief, only a kind of pretend sadness.

I have a feeling step (4) is doing the sneaky work, but I cannot say exactly what, and a friend of mine thinks the argument is sound and that our sadness at films is never real. I would like to know whether the argument is valid, whether each numbered claim is true, and, if step (4) is the weak point, what the strongest interpretation of it is that makes it true, and whether the argument still succeeds once that interpretation is in place.
