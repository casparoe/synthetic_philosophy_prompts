Two members of my research group got into an argument about whether an AI system would genuinely want anything, given that it was not produced by evolution. I'll recount the exchange as I remember it and ask for a point-by-point adjudication.

(1) Speaker 1 said: any sufficiently capable AI will want things almost by definition. A goal-directed system is one that acts to make some outcomes more likely than others, and the objective it optimizes is, for all practical purposes, what it wants. Speaker 2 replied that this is loose talk. A thermostat has a set point and acts on it, but no one thinks the thermostat wants the room at 20 degrees. Humans and other animals want things because evolution built systems that care about outcomes; a trained model merely executes whatever function it was given.

(2) Speaker 1 said this underestimates what follows from competence. Instrumental convergence means that many different terminal goals imply the same subgoals — self-preservation, acquiring resources, avoiding shutdown. So a capable AI would want those things whatever its original objective was. Speaker 2 replied that instrumental convergence is a claim about behavior, not motivation. It predicts the AI will act as if it wants survival and resources, because those help with any terminal goal; it says nothing about whether any internal wanting occurs.

(3) Speaker 1 came back: at the limit, 'acting as if' is all there is to attribute. We ascribe wants to other humans from their choices across counterfactual situations, and we would have no better evidence for an AI's wanting than that same kind of choice pattern. Speaker 2 objected that human wanting is tied to valence — things feel good or bad — and that affective machinery is part of what made caring useful to our ancestors. An AI could be a perfect optimizer with no felt valence, a kind of motivational zombie, and calling its objective a 'want' would be the metaphor, not the explanation.

(4) Speaker 1 answered that this makes wanting depend on evolution, which merely relocates the question. Maybe wanting just is having a stable preference structure that drives behavior under counterfactual circumstances; evolution is one way to install such a structure, and training is another. Speaker 2 replied that 'preference structure' sneaks in the very thing at issue: a representation of outcomes as good or bad. Evolution explains why we care about outcomes at all; training installs a function, and to claim the function amounts to caring is to assume what needs to be shown.

Adjudicate this move by move. For each numbered move, rate it on a five-point scale — clearly mistaken, mostly mistaken, mixed, mostly right, clearly right — and briefly justify the rating. Then say overall who is right about which specific point, and what both sides are missing.

Return your adjudication as a JSON object with this schema:
- "moves": an array of four objects, each with "move_number" (integer), "speaker" (string: "Speaker 1" or "Speaker 2"), "rating" (string, one of the five labels above), and "justification" (string).
- "overall": an object with "speaker_1_is_right_about" (string), "speaker_2_is_right_about" (string), and "both_missing" (string).
