I'm putting together a handout for my upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, and I want a "which response to the paradox of phenomenal judgment do you actually hold?" decision tree. The aim is to sort students by which of the three claims in the setup they're least willing to give up, not by which position they already identify with. The students will have read this setup, so the tree can take it for granted:

Here's the inconsistent-looking triad. (1) Phenomenal states exist — there's something it's like when I'm in pain, see red, and so on. (2) Phenomenal states are nonphysical extras over and above the physical story, and they never cause anything physical; the physical world is causally closed at the level of physical events. (3) When I judge "I'm in pain right now," that judgment is ordinarily caused in part by the pain itself, and it amounts to knowledge of my own experience. The paradox is that (2) plus (1) seems to make (3) impossible, but giving up (3) makes it hard to see why I should trust my introspective evidence for (1) and (2) in the first place.

Build the decision tree from the logical space, not from a list of names. Each branch node should be a question a student can answer about a concrete case or about which premise they'd revise, without knowing the labels. I'd like the tree to have something like seven or eight leaves, including at minimum: type-A physicalism, type-B physicalism, interactionist dualism, epiphenomenalism with a pre-established-harmony (or parallelism) response, epiphenomenalism that bites the bullet and gives up the claim that our phenomenal judgments are knowledge, and illusionism/eliminativism. Use the Mary-in-the-black-and-white-room case and the philosophical zombie case somewhere in the questions, but phrase them so a student can answer cold.

For each leaf, give the named position (or "unnamed" if it's a coherent spot nobody defends) and one sentence on its most awkward committment. Also flag any leaves the tree can't really separate — for instance, I suspect type-A vs type-B physicalism might come apart on a question about whether Mary learns a new fact, while both just deny premise (2), so say whether you can split them without warping the tree. And note any coherent combinations of answers that are unoccupied and why (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored).
