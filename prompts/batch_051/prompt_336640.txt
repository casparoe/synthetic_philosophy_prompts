I have spent a long time reading the blog-and-forum end of the AI alignment discussion, and I keep returning to a distinction that seems to matter in practice but often gets blurred in argument: the difference between a system that fails by actively seeking what it was never meant to seek, and a system that fails by slowly ceasing to be what it was meant to be. The first picture is deliberate reward hacking, strategic concealment, or hidden objectives; the second is quieter — slow value drift, in which a model’s behavior creeps away from its specification through fine-tuning, user feedback loops, and changing deployment conditions. A governance proposal I have seen defended, and would like to understand better, is a ‘failure triage’ rule that forces every oversight finding into exactly one of these two boxes and attaches a fixed response to the classification.

The rule works as follows. Whenever a deployed frontier model is involved in a serious incident, or an external audit finds a substantial mismatch between the model’s behavior and its last verified checkpoint, a standing technical review board must classify the failure as Type A (influence-seeking) or Type B (drift). Type A bars the entire model family from deployment and suspends the developer’s access to a shared public compute pool for twelve months. Type B requires the developer to re-run alignment fine-tuning against a held-out reference set, install a drift monitor, and submit quarterly reports for a year. Third-party auditors may claim bounties from a settlement fund, but only for Type A findings that are upheld on review; Type B findings are not compensated. I want this analyzed as a mechanism rather than judged as a policy first. What would it actually incentivize among developers, auditors, and the review board? How would the boundary between ‘influence-seeking’ and ‘drift’ be gamed, litigated, or gradually redefined? What second-order effects would it have on which failures get produced and which get disclosed? And are the most likely failure modes fixable by adjusting the bounty, the penalties, or the burden of proof — or do they point to a deeper problem with the two-bin taxonomy itself?
