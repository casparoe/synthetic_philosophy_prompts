I recently started representing some of my own uncertainty with imprecise probabilities—sets of priors rather than a single prior—because I had to make a medical decision whose evidence came with risk information I could not pin down to a single number. A colleague, on hearing about this, gave me the argument below. It seems to prove too much, since it concludes that no one should ever use imprecise probabilities at all, but I cannot find the faulty step.

(1) If a state of opinion is coherent, it satisfies a reflection constraint: before you learn the outcome of a fair coin you already know you will observe, your current opinion about any proposition should be your present expectation of the opinion you will hold after observing the coin.

(2) An imprecise state of opinion is represented by a convex set of probability functions, and updating consists in conditionalizing each member of the set on the new evidence.

(3) Consider a state of opinion about two binary variables, X and Y. X is known to be a fair coin, so P(X=Heads)=1/2. Let S be the set of every joint probability function with P(X=Heads)=1/2 and P(Y=1)=1/2, and no further constraints. S is convex, so it meets the usual coherence requirements for a credal set.

(4) In state S, your opinion about the proposition H, that Y=1, is sharp: every function in S assigns H probability exactly 1/2.

(5) But after you observe the coin, whatever the outcome, your opinion about H becomes maximally imprecise. For any q between 0 and 1, some member of S assigns P(Y=1|X=Heads)=q and P(Y=1|X=Tails)=1−q, and the same holds with Heads and Tails reversed. So, conditional on either outcome, the resulting probabilities for H range over the entire unit interval.

(6) Thus in S you begin with a fully determinate credence of 1/2 in H, and you know in advance that a gratuitous observation—the toss of a coin irrelevant to Y apart from this model—will replace that determinate credence with complete indeterminacy.

(7) Reflection forbids exactly this: a coherent agent cannot anticipate that her future self will hold a credal state that need not equal, in expectation, the determinate value she now holds.

(8) Therefore S is diachronically incoherent.

(9) But S is an ordinary, axiomatically admissible imprecise state; if imprecise probabilities are ever rationally permissible, S should be among the permissible states.

(10) So imprecise probabilities are not rationally permissible.

I would like to keep both my imprecise probabilities and some form of reflection, but I am prepared to revise one of them. Please say which step you would give up first, why that step looked right, and what your preferred alternative actually costs. I want your own committed diagnosis and the reasons for it, not a survey of possible positions.
