Okay here's the situation. Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about whether words have meanings, and it ended with both of us annoyed and neither of us changing position. He's a lexicographer, so his opening move was basicaly: of course they do, a dictionary records them, 'book' doesn't mean 'giraffe,' done. My line, which I've been pushing for months, is that the word itself is almost empty — all a word has is a fuzzy semantic range, and everything that gets said with it is assembled on the fly from context, common ground and inference, so the word 'book' contributes next to nothing to interpreting 'read the book,' 'book a table' and 'throw the book at him.' He says that's just polysemy plus context, not a threat to the idea that words have meanings. I say the thing he calls 'the meaning' underdetermines nearly every interpretation, so it isn't doing the work he thinks it's doing. Rounds and rounds of this.

I know this is also a long-running debate between the 'words have meanings' people and the 'meaning is something utterances get, not something words have' people, and what strikes me is that it never seems to move. Both sides use the same examples and nobody budges.

So what kind of disagreement is it, actually? Give me a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is it verbal — people using 'meaning' for at least three different things (standing meaning, occasion meaning, maybe a mental representation or a prototype or a typical use)? Is it a real disagreement about how much of communication is encoded in the lexicon versus left to inference? Or are the two camps answering different questions under one label — one about the public language, one about cognition or conversation? And is there a hidden empirical question in there, somthing like what a lexical entry actually has to specify for a hearer to get from word to interpretation?

My hunch, so you can react to it: one third of the fight is the word 'meaning' itself doing different work, another third is a boundary dispute over semantics versus pragmatics that dissolves once you draw the lines, but there's a remainder about whether a semantic range genuinely constrains interpretations or just retrospectively rationalises them, and that remainder feels real to me. Is that close?

Given whatever diagnosis you defend, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction that's actually load-bearing, a way of restating the question, a psycholinguistic experiment, a formal result — or whether the honest answer is that we should stop expecting this debate to end and instead change the question. Please answer in British English.
