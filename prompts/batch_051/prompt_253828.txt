I am taking an upper-division seminar on the problem of universals, and one argumentative move has come up so often that I have begun to suspect it does less work than its advocates think. The move is an appeal to the principle of parsimony — usually rendered as 'do not multiply entities beyond necessity' — treated as a decisive objection to realism about universals. For example, when a realist explains how two distinct particulars can be the very same way by positing a repeatable property that each instantiates, my instructor replies that this posits an unnecessary kind of entity, because the nominalist can account for their sameness using only the particulars themselves and whatever relations, sets, or primitive resemblances are needed. I would like to challenge this way of settling the debate, but I have realized that how I should challenge it depends on which precise claim the slogan is being used to express. Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct claims that 'do not multiply entities beyond necessity' might amount to in this context — from a weak methodological preference among otherwise equal theories to a substantive ban on repeatable entities — and for each reading say whether it is trivial, defensible, or question-begging when aimed specifically at universals, and whether it would actually license my instructor's move or merely restate the nominalist's conclusion in the language of a neutral principle?
