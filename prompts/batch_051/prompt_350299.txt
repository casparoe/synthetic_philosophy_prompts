In my graduate seminar on the classification of mass atrocities, we keep returning to a puzzle about what the legal category of genocide presupposes. The Genocide Convention defines the crime as a series of acts — killing members of a group, causing them serious bodily or mental harm, deliberately subjecting them to conditions of life calculated to destroy the group, and related measures — when these are committed with the intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group as a group. What interests me is the shape of that list. A campaign intended to destroy an ethnic or religious community as such falls squarely within the definition. A campaign of comparable scale, deliberateness, and cruelty aimed at destroying a political movement, a social class, or a linguistic community does not, and is typically handled, if at all, under some other heading such as crimes against humanity. The omission was not an afterthought; it reflected the interests and fears of the states that drafted the treaty, and the label now carries legal obligations and a moral stigma that neighboring labels do not.

I would like a rigorous account of what this framework presupposes, and whether the presupposition is defensible. On its face, the practice seems committed to the idea that certain kinds of group identity — national, ethnic, racial, religious — are objects whose destruction as a group is a distinctive wrong, whereas political or class identity is not protected in the same way. That would require a principled distinction among forms of group membership: perhaps that the protected identities are ascriptive and unchosen while political ones are voluntarily assumed, or that they are more constitutive of who their members are. But religious affiliation is often chosen or renounced, political identity can be as inherited and inescapable as ethnicity, and racial categories are themselves unstable artifacts. The intent requirement, moreover, seems to assume that a collectivity can be the target of destruction over and above its individual members, which is itself a substantive metaphysical and moral commitment. Reconstruct the implicit theory of group identity and moral wrongness on which the practice rests, distinguish what the legal definition strictly presupposes from what is merely a contingent political compromise, and say what would have to change — in the protected categories, in the intent requirement, or in how much moral weight the label is allowed to carry — if that theory were replaced by the strongest available alternative.
