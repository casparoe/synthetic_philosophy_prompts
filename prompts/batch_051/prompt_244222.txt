I teach an upper-level game theory course for philosophy and economics students—about twenty students, one seventy-five-minute session. Design an in-class game in which participants feel, before it is named, the force of mixed-strategy dominance and of the iterated elimination it licenses. Specifically, they should discover through play that a strategy can be strictly dominated by a randomized mixture of other strategies even when no single pure strategy dominates it, and that removing such strategies can cascade and leave a much smaller set of surviving choices. Ideally, the students should generate the payoff numbers themselves from an initial choice or vote, so the strategy that gets eliminated is one they were initially drawn to, and they should notice where they have arrived only in the debrief. Give me the rules, materials, a payoff scheme that needs no real money, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the formal distinction between pure-strategy and mixed-strategy dominance and onto why allowing mixed dominators lets elimination go further than pure-strategy comparisons alone. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of the idea the game cannot reproduce and so must be supplied in the debrief.
