I run a small forecasting shop, and one of my partners just read some old results about how rational Bayesians who start from the same priors can't rationally end up agreeing to disagree once they've exchanged their posteriors and each knows what the other has seen. She wants us to adopt the following internal rule, and I'd rather map out how it actually behaves before we vote on it than hear whether it's a good idea in the abstract.

Here's the mechanism. Every probability we publish gets worked by two analysts who write their estimates and a one-page rationale seperately, then send them to each other at the same moment. Both are required to start from the same firm base-rate file for that question category, so in theory any gap after a full exchange is either hidden information or a reasoning error. If the two numbers land within five points, we take the average and move on. If they're more than five apart, a mandatory reconciliation starts: each analyst sits at a logged terminal and reads the other's full rationale plus whatever private information the other collected, and formally they can't withhold anything, because the reader sees the list of what the other reviewed. Then each revises in private. The revised numbers go up on a shared firm-wide dashboard with a note saying whose reasoning has been read by whom, so after the first round it's common knowledge where both of them landed and what they've seen. If they still disagree by more than five points, the analyst with the wider self-reported confidence interval pays a fine into the team bonus pool, and the rounds repeat, up to five. After five rounds with a gap still bigger than five, we publish the midpoint and both analysts are barred from that question type for three months.

Walk me through what this does to how people form their initial numbers, what they put in their rationales, which questions they volunteer for or dodge, and what the dashboard looks like six months in. Where does it get gamed first, and what does that do to the quality of what we send clients? Does the rule actually surface the hidden information or does it train everyone into fake consensus? And is there a tweak that keeps the forcing function without the obvious workarounds?
