I'm on my department's curriculum committee and we're going in circles about the required intro logic course. Right now it spends about five weeks on the syllogistic unit — square of opposition, immediate inference, the moods and figures, Venn diagrams, the works — and then crams propositional and predicate logic into the back half. The modernizers want to drop syllogistic entirely: their line is that Frege showed the subject–predicate treatment is a kind of fragment that can't express relations or multiple generality, and that the time is better spent teaching students to handle ordinary sentences like "everyone respects someone" or "no one likes everyone who likes them," which the old machinery mangles. The traditionalists want to keep the full five weeks on the grounds that it's the only part of the course where students actually test real natural-language arguments, and the pre-law folks here specifically want their students to come out fluent in the old terminology. A third group wants a compromise: one week of syllogistic as a historical warm-up, no memorizing moods and figures, then straight into modern quantifier logic.

I need to circulate a short memo before Thursday's vote that picks one of those three options — kill it, keep five weeks, or compress to one — and gives reasons the rest of the committee can actually vote on. Don't just tell me "Frege fixed this, end of story." I want the strongest case for keeping at least some syllogistic to be taken seriously, for instance the claim that a five-week diet of Venn diagrams trains a kind of natural-language validity-checking that a rushed quantifier unit doesn't, or that dropping it leaves pre-law students unable to handle the classical forms that still show up in legal reasoning. But the memo does need a firm bottom line, and it should say what the one-week compromise would actually cost us if you recommend against it. Draft it.
