Our reading group has spent the past year on Machiavelli, and two camps have crystallized around a single divide. The first camp — call it the instrumental-strategic reading — understands virtù as a capacity for adaptive technique: a prince or a republic succeeds insofar as its leaders can read a shifting situation, change methods without being held back by standing scruples, and deploy force, fraud, caution, or audacity as the moment demands. On this reading, Machiavelli's advice about fighting like both fox and lion, and his portrait of fortune as something that yields to bold and well-timed action, tell us that virtù is not a moral property of persons but a technical property of strategies. The second camp — call it the civic-republican reading — understands virtù as an excellence primarily of orders and citizens: the self-discipline and public-spiritedness needed to found and preserve well-ordered free institutions, to keep private ambition from corrupting them, and to direct the ambitions of the powerful toward the common good. On this reading, the prince's virtuosity is a narrow special case of a broader political excellence, and fortune names the residual domain that no human excellence, individual or collective, can fully eliminate. The camps have repeatedly stalled on whether the prince's adaptive flexibility and the citizen's disciplined public spirit are two species of one genus, or merely two phenomena that happen to share a word.

We have decided to stop arguing past each other and to produce an adversarial-collaboration document that both camps can sign. Please draft that document. Begin with a section of numbered claims both camps accept — claims substantive enough to be worth signing, and not merely, for example, the observation that Machiavelli uses the word virtù in several senses. Then give a section stating the residual disagreements with precision: for each item, formulate the issue in neutral terms, then state the claim as each camp would accept it for its own side, and flag any disagreement that is at least partly verbal. Finally, give a section of cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. For each crux, identify a concrete piece of textual evidence or a concrete political example that both camps agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts that evidence, correctly read, will show. Where the two predictions do not genuinely diverge, say so and treat the crux as only apparent. In every section, avoid formulations that one side could dismiss as a caricature; the aim is a document each side would sign as an accurate statement of its own view and of where the real disagreement now lies.
