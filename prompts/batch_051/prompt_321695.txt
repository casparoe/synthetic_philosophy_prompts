I'm taking a metaphysics course this term and we're on the special composition question — the one about when a bunch of little things compose a bigger thing. My professor refuses to just state her position; she gives these one-line rulings on particular cases and makes us reverse engineer the rule. So far she's said: the molecules that make up a live cat, yes, they compose the cat; the cells of a living elm tree, yes; the parts of a single live bacterium, yes; grains of sand that happen to be touching on a beach, no; a heap of gears and springs sitting disconnected in a drawer, no; bricks stacked loosely on a pallet, no. She also keeps muttering something about how its not being stuck together that matters but doing something together, which makes me think she's after organization, but I'm not sure.

Next class she's going to rule on a working grandfather clock, all its gears meshed and actually keeping time. Predict her verdict on the clock — do the parts compose a clock or not? Also, which rule is she really running: something like van Inwagen's view that the only composites are living things (or things caught up in a single life), a functional-integration view where working machines are real composites, a fastening-only view, or something else? And what case should I ask about after the clock to settle whichever ambiguity is left?
