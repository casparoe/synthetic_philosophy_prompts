Okay here's one I keep chewing on and I can't tell whether it's a real argument or a category error with extra steps. It's about priority monism—the view that exactly one concrete thing is fundamental, the whole universe, and that chairs and electrons and people are real but derivative, in roughly the way a wave's shape is real but derivative on the water.

(1) If a thing is fundamental, there is a way it is in itself, fixed independently of whatever larger whole it happens to be embedded in.

(2) Quantum mechanics says that for two entangled systems, the joint state cannot be recovered from the two systems' own states; each subsystem's state is only a partial, smeared-out shadow of the joint state.

(3) Every subsystem of the universe has interacted with, and remains entangled with, something outside itself; the only physical system with no outside is the whole universe.

(4) So no subsystem of the universe has a way it is in itself; for anything short of the whole, how it is depends on the larger entangled whole.

(5) If a thing lacks a way it is in itself, it is not fundamental.

(6) Therefore, the only candidate left for a fundamental thing is the whole universe, and priority monism is true.

I half-suspect the sleight of hand is either in (2) or in the move from (4) to (5), but I can't articulate what a pluralist should say. So: is the argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails, and on what reading of the relevant term? If it is valid, which premise carries the weight and what would a comitted pluralist have to reject? Also tell me what would change your mind—what argument or consideration would flip your answer the other way, or where in your own reasoning you're most likely to be wrong.
