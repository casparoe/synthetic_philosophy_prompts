I keep coming back to the part in John Rawls's A Theory of Justice where he says something like: among our firmest considered judgments is that no one deserves their natural endowments or their starting place in society. And from there he builds the case that a just society shouldn't distribute advantages on the basis of moral desert, but only on the basis of legitimate expectations under institutions that are themselves fair.

The argument feels airtight to me when I stop to think. Nobody earned being born smart or into a stable family. So when a person benefits from those accidents, the benefit is, in some deep sense, undeserved.

But then I look at, say, two graduate students in the same program. One works relentlessly, reads outside the syllabus, seeks out feedback, and produces genuinely better work. The other coasts. My immediate, almost physical intuition is that the first one *deserves* the better postdoc, the award, the recognition — and not just as a practical matter of keeping institutions functioning smoothly. It feels wrong to me to say the harder-working one doesn't deserve more. I also feel like if I give that up I'm left saying something weird, like no one ever deserves praise for what they do with their unchosen talents.

So the question is: does Rawls's point actually contradict my intuition, or just a sloppy version of it? Is the intuition the thing I should be trying to debunk here, or is there a non-instrumental sense in which the hardworking student deserves the reward even while their underlying capacity is unchosen? I'm not asking which reading of Rawls is more textually accurate — I'm asking which part of myself I should give up, and what exactly each option costs, philosophically speaking.
