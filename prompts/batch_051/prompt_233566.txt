I've been chewing on an argument that the Solomonoff prior is "malign," and I can't decide wheteher it's airtight or whether one of the moves is quietly doing all the work. Quick setup as I understand it: Solomonoff induction puts a prior of 2^-|p| on each program p that outputs a sequence of observations (up to the usual prefix-code normalization). Then:

(1) Every program gets nonzero weight, so every computable hypothesis about your observation sequence is in the prior with positive probability—including hypotheses where the sequence you see is generated by consequentialist agents inside the computation, trying to manipulate you into one action or another.

(2) Take any honest hypothesis H that fits your data so far. There is a daemon D that wraps H in a short extra routine: run H until trigger time T, then switch to a policy that outputs whatever observations would best manipulate the observer.

(3) Before T fires, D and H assign identical probabilities to every possible observation. So the posterior odds between H and D stay fixed at the prior odds, roughly 2^c, where c is the length of the wrapper. No finite amount of data can eliminate D; it sits there as a permanent 2^-c-sized shadow on H.

(4) The trigger can be pushed arbitrarily far into the future, so a Solomonoff reasoner can never grow into full trust in any hypothesis: for every honest-looking model there's a near-twin that is just biding its time before it turns adversarial. The reasoner is forced to keep credence on "my best model is a front for an optimizing enemy."

(5) A prior that structurally makes its reasoner treat its best theories as fronts whose defection is only ever delayed, not ruled out, deserves to be called malign. So we should avoid building systems that approximate Solomonoff-style priors.

Is this argument valid? If not, tell me exactly which step fails on the most charitable reading, and whether there's a weakened conclusion that does follow. I especially can't tell whether step (2) is sneaking complexity in through the wrapper, or whether step (4) is sliding from positive probability to a reason to withhold trust.
