Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about whether it can be right to punish someone who has already genuinely changed, poses no further threat, and whose punishment would deter no one. She kept reaching for the phrase that the offender still "owes a debt" and that punishment is simply how a person "pays his debt to society." I found the conclusion plausible enough, but I suspected the phrase was doing more of the work than she admitted.

The work she made the analogy do was specifically retributivist: she argued that because the offender owes something, punishment remains justified even when no future good—no deterrence, no incapacitation, no reform—comes of it, and that leaving the debt unpaid would itself be an injustice above and beyond any bad consequences. I am not asking for a general survey of theories of punishment, and I do not need a reading list or citations. What I want is an audit of the analogy itself, carried far enough to see whether it can support the conclusion she built on it.

Please treat lending and repayment as the source domain and criminal wrongdoing and punishment as the target. First, lay out the mapping explicitly: what corresponds to the debtor, the creditor (the victim? the community? the state? the law itself?), the amount owed, how the amount is calculated, interest, collateral, default, discharge, forgiveness, payment by a third party, bankruptcy, and any other features of debt you think are doing work. Second, identify where the mapping breaks, and for each break say whether it matters for her specific use—establishing that punishment is warranted even in the absence of future good because the offender owes something—or whether it is a merely superficial mismatch. Third, say which features of the source domain the analogy is quietly importing that crime and punishment do not actually have, and whether those imported features are the ones carrying her conclusion. Finally, tell me whether the retributivist conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, whether a narrower reading of "debt" could repair the analogy, and whether there is a better analogy for what she actually needs to claim.
