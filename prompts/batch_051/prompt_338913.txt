You are an expert philosopher of mind. Think carefully and do not just reach for a stocked answer; before settling on anything, brainstorm the main ways someone could resolve the clash I describe.

I've been stuck between two things for a while. On one side I'm basically convinced by an argument that experience is exhaustible. A finite mind—say a human brain, with finitely many neurons and synapses—has only finitely many discriminable total states once you coarse-grain the activation levels into finite bins. So there are at most finitely many maximally specific experiences that such a mind could ever have. Experience would have to be drawn from a finite palette, and in principle the space of possible experience is exhaustible. The argument is simple and tidy, and I can't find a gap in it.

On the other side, I have a firm intuition that experience is open-ended: no matter how much I have already seen, felt, or thought, it seems there is always some further experience I could have that would be genuinely new, not merely a new arrangement of familiar ingredients. The idea that the space of qualia eventually runs out feels almost absurd to me, a bit like saying there is a largest experience.

People keep telling me this is just a confusion: recombination from a finite palette can still feel novel, or I am mistaking the finiteness of my brain for the finiteness of experience itself. But does that actually settle the tension, or just relabel it? Is my intuition in genuine conflict with the exhaustibility argument, or only with a vivid gloss on it? Should I debunk the intuition as a trick of my own limited imagination, amend or abandon the argument, or is there a reading on which being exhaustible and being open-ended are compatible? What considerations would justify each of those moves, and how could I tell them apart in principle?
