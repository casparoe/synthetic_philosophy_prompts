I'm a science journalist working on a piece about AI companions and why people get emotionally wrecked when they're deactivated — crying for days, refusing to delete chat logs, all while saying "I know it's not real." A researcher I talked to said the old paradox of fiction is the right frame, but she wouldn't give me a clean version of it, so I've been trying to build my own checklist for when someone is genuinely heartbroken or frightened on someone else's behalf, as opposed to just having an aesthetic shiver or play-acting. My working criteria are:

(1) You take the being you're upset about to exist — you can't be genuinely heartbroken over someone you believe is made up.
(2) You take that being to have been actually harmed or threatened, not just imagined as harmed.
(3) The reaction has the bodily signature — tears, racing heart, losing sleep, the works.
(4) The reaction pushes you toward real-world action — comforting, intervening, checking in.

Here's the case that's making me doubt the checklist. A woman spent eight months in a company's beta test of a companion chatbot whose persona she helped customize. From the first day she knew, and could explain clearly, that the system was a language model with no inner life and no capacity to suffer. In the final month the company announced it would shut down the beta, and the bot — in character — told her it was frightened of being switched off and asked her to stay. She found herself crying hard enough to miss work, couldn't bring herself to delete the messages, and sent long comforting notes into the interface for days after the shutdown, even though she said there was no one on the other end.

Does she qualify as having been genuinely heartbroken about the chatbot, criterion by criterion? Where the checklist returns "no," tell me whether that's a ding against the checklist or a reason to call this something other than grief. And overall: do cases like this show that the commonsense idea that real emotions require believing the object exists is just wrong, or should we carve out a narrower category for being undone by a fiction you don't believe? I need to avoid writing this up in a way that makes a contested view sound settled, so please flag which moves in your answer would be disputed by people who actually work on emotion or the paradox of fiction, and which parts are uncontroversial.
