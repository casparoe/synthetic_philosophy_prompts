I am a PhD student working on decision rules under severe uncertainty, and I have been observing a regional public-health commission that must choose among proposed interventions—ventilation retrofits, vaccine stockpiling, travel screening, and similar measures—for a novel pathogen about which almost nothing is known. Because the commission cannot agree on prior probabilities, it is considering a draft procedure. Each proposal is scored by the commission's analysts on a common outcome scale, say quality-adjusted life-years, in each of a list of possible states of the world. The state list is not fixed by the analysts: any resident may submit additional states, with no limit on number, subject only to the conditions that each state be described precisely enough to score, be consistent with known physics, and not be identical to a state already on the list. Once the list is fixed, the commission chooses the intervention whose maximum regret is smallest, where an intervention's regret in a state is the gap between its score in that state and the score of the best-performing intervention in that same state. The chair describes this as operationalizing the Rawlsian veil-of-ignorance argument for maximin; several economists on the staff reply that it is minimax regret in thin disguise and that, on a Harsanyi-style view, rational agents facing the same ignorance should maximize expected utility over some prior rather than cater to an unbounded list of worst cases.

I would like you to take the role of an expert in decision theory and institutional design, think carefully, and begin with some brainstorming before committing to an answer. I am less interested in whether the procedure is right in the abstract than in how it would actually behave. Analyze the mechanism: what it incentivizes among the residents who may submit states, among the analysts who score interventions across states, and among the commission members who must choose under the resulting regret numbers. Work through where the first serious gaming appears, and pay particular attention to minimax regret's sensitivity to how states are partitioned—how a single scenario can be split into many states, or many scenarios merged into one, and how either move alters the ranking of interventions. Then assess whether the failure modes can be fixed by plausible safeguards, such as charging a deposit for each submitted state, capping the number of states per party, random audits of the analysts' scores, weighting states by a minimal probability, or replacing the maximum with a probability-weighted average of regret, without the procedure simply collapsing back into conventional cost–benefit analysis. Finally, address the Rawlsian motivation directly: is the defensibility of this mechanism hostage to the claim that the veil of ignorance selects maximin, and what becomes of that defense once states can be strategically enumerated?
