I help run a weekly after-school club for sixteen secondary-school students, and I have a 75-minute slot coming up. For a while I've been bothered by a habit in my own thinking that I also see in them: when several people or reports all point the same way, I automatically treat each new report as a fresh, separate reason to believe, even though reports often copy one another and I only learn about the copying afterward. I'd like the session to make them feel the force of that default — why it's a sensible starting point and where it quietly goes wrong — through a game rather than a lecture.

My rough picture is a small-stakes investigation game. Teams play analysts who must say, after each new tip, how likely a claim is to be true (as a percentage, or by moving chips between two answers). In some rounds the tips genuinely come from unrelated sources; in other rounds several tips secretly trace back to a single origin, and the teams don't know which kind of round they're in until a reveal partway through. The scoring should reward honest uncertainty rather than bravado, but stay simple enough for teenagers with no statistics background. I'd also like at least one round where two tips share a hidden source but are worded very differently, so participants can't just smell copying from the phrasing. Please write the complete runnable design: the claim to investigate, the exact rounds and their source structure, rotating roles so one person doesn't dominate, the scoring mechanism with its payoffs, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the underlying idea, say what predictably goes wrong when the activity is run and how to handle it, and be honest about the part of the idea the game cannot by itself demonstrate — the thing I would still need to supply in a few sentences after the game ends, so the students come away seeing that treating separate-looking reports as independent is a policy we adopt, not a fact about how the reports were produced.
