Question: Some critics argue that image generators (for example, diffusion models) cannot be genuinely creative, because they only recombine and interpolate between images in their training data. Evaluate this criticism. In your answer, be sure to (a) explain Margaret Boden's distinction between psychological creativity (P-creativity: an idea or artifact that is new to the person or system that produces it) and historical creativity (H-creativity: new with respect to all of prior human history), (b) apply that distinction to a generative image model, and (c) take a position on whether "mere recombination" is enough to count as creative, using novelty, surprise, and value as your tests.

Student answer: Boden seperates creativity into two kinds. P-creativity occurs when someone makes something new to themselves, like a child inventing a word; H-creativity occurs when the result is new in all of human history, like relativity. Boden holds that only H-creativity is real creativity, while P-creativity is just a preliminary or trivial sort of novelty. Applied to a diffusion model, the system can be P-creative at best: an output may not have literally been in the training set, so it is new to the model in some minimal sense. But the model cannot be H-creative, because every output is just a weighted average of latent codes drawn from the training data. So the images it produces are straightforward combinations of existing artworks, and nothing genuinely new ever appears. The model also lacks conscious intention to create something surprising or valuable, so it fails the novelty-surprise-value test. Surprise and value are properties that only human audiences can assign, not the machine. Therefore the critics are basically right: recombination by itself is not creativity, and an image generator is best seen as a very sophisticated form of automated collage.
