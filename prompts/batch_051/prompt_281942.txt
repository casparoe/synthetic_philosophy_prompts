I was interviewing a philosopher of biology for a feature on whether natural selection is secretly a tautology, and he walked me through a version of the objection that I can't find the exit from. Quick setup if the terms are dusty: the old complaint is that if fitness just means actual reproductive success, then the fittest-survive slogan reduces to survivors-survive, and natural selection explains nothing. The standard reply, sometimes called the propensity interpretation of fitness, is that fitness is not actual output but a propensity — roughly, expected reproductive output given an organisms traits and environment, a built-in tendency that can fail to be realized in any particular case. That was supposed to dissolve the tautology. Fine. Then he gave me this.

(1) If natural selection is explanatory, fitness has to be definable independently of who actually survives and reproduces; otherwise the slogan is empty.

(2) The propensity reading only gets you that independence if a propensity is something more than a roundabout way of pointing at actual outcomes.

(3) A propensity can bottom out in one of two ways: it's a frequency-style summary over actual or hypothetical repeats, or it's an irreducible single-case chance that floats free of frequencies.

(4) On the frequency way, for the single actual run of history we get — or the single population in front of us — fitness either collapses back into actual outcomes, and the tautology returns, or it's defined only over hypothetical reruns nobody can observe.

(5) On the single-case way, if fitness is an irreducible chance, then no finite pattern of outcomes can count against a fitness ascription: the higher-fitness type can lose out ten generations straight and you just say the chance didn't materialize. So fitness claims stop being confirmable in practice.

(6) So the propensity reading either lets the tautology back in or makes fitness empirically empty. The standard fix doesn't solve the problem; it just moves it up a level.

What bothers me is that this argument seems to prove too much. The same two-horned dilemma appears to apply to every probabilistic disposition I cover in my day job — a biased die's tendency to land six, a drug's efficacy rate, nuclear decay propensities. Each would have to be either frequencies-in-disguise, which looks tautological, or an unfalsifiable primitive chance. But probabilistic explanations clearly do real work, and I'm not prepared to give them up. So one of these steps must buckle — but which one, and what exactly do I have to swallow to reject it? I most want to keep two things: that natural selection is a genuine empirical claim, and that propensities can do explanatory work. I don't care so much which premise goes, but I want to know what it costs.
