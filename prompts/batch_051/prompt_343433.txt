My friend and I went back and forth all evening about the Allais paradox and I came away with a variation I still couldnt settle on. She thinks expected utility is dead and that risk-weighted expected utility is the fix, because a risk function r(p) less than p lets you be extra worried about the 1% chance of nothing in the first pair while still taking the 10% shot at the big prize in the second. Her version is the one where you order outcomes worst to best and each step up in utility gets weighted by r(probability of doing at least that well); r(p)=p is plain expected utility, and r(p)=p^3 is the curve she likes. I'm not convinced its doing anything principled, so I tried varying one parameter to see when the fix actually kicks in.

Take the usual Allais numbers, in millions: A = $1M for sure. B = $5M with probability .10, $1M with probability .89, $0 with probability .01. C = $1M with probability .11, $0 with probability .89. D = $5M with probability .10, $0 with probability .90. Now replace the .89 common-branch probability with a parameter q: B becomes $5M with probability .10, $1M with probability q, and $0 with probability .90−q; C becomes $1M with probability 1−q and $0 with probability q. A and D stay fixed, and q=.89 gets you back the original.

Use linear utility in dollars (so $1M has utility 1 and $5M has utility 5) and r(p)=p^3. First, work out for which values of q this risk-weighted expected utility version produces the Allais preference pattern, A over B and D over C, with both preferences strict. For comparison, does ordinary expected utility produce that pattern for any q at all? Second, hold q fixed at .89 but allow the risk function to be r(p)=p^k for k≥1. For which k does the Allais pattern come out right, and what should we make of the two boundaries: the one where it stops favoring A over B, and the one where it stops favoring D over C?

My friend thinks the fact that the original q=.89 sits inside the working range shows risk-weighted expected utility is genuinely explaining the paradox. I think if the range is just a band of free parameters that nobody ever measures independently, it's curve-fitting with extra dials. Work through the calculations and then give your own verdict on what the ranges actually show.
