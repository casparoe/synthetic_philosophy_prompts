My father died earlier this year, and in the months since I have kept returning to a question I had never had reason to take seriously: what kind of thing is grief — a judgment about what has happened, a bodily disturbance, a mental feeling? A close friend, a doctoral student in clinical psychology, gave me a confident summary of the theory she thinks settles this. Her summary was this: "The James–Lange theory says an emotion just is the awareness of your body's automatic response to a situation. Grief isn't a mental state that causes tears; grief is the tears, the tight chest, and the other bodily disturbances themselves. If you could subtract all those bodily feelings from the experience, nothing emotional would remain, only a detached thought about the loss. That is also why you can change an emotion by changing the body first: deliberately slow your breathing, unclench your jaw, soften your face, and the corresponding feeling will follow." She then sent me the passage she said her summary was based on, from William James, "What is an Emotion?", Mind, vol. 9 (1884):

"I should say first of all that the only emotions I propose expressly to consider here are those that have a distinct bodily expression. That there are feelings of pleasure and displeasure, of interest and excitement, bound up with mental operations, but having no obvious bodily expression for their consequence, would, I suppose, be held true by most readers."

I do not know enough of the surrounding debate to tell where her gloss departs from the text. I am inclined to trust her, since she works with this material, but the passage seems to me to contain a qualification her summary left out, and I suspect she has run together a claim about what emotions are with a claim about how to manage them. Is her summary a fair reading of James, especially in light of the passage? Please separate what is accurate from what is a simplification or overstatement, say what a careful defender of James would object to in her version, and tell me whether her practical conclusion — that you can change an emotion by changing the body first — survives the corrections.
