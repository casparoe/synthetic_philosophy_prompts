I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been trying to figure out what I actually think species are by going with my gut on cases. The problem is my gut says a bunch of things that dont obviously fit together; it feels like I hardcoded a different rule for each case and now I want the underlying function.

- Dogs and wolves: totally fine with calling them different species, even though they interbreed and the offspring are fertile. So being able to interbreed is not the whole story for me.
- Two frog populations that look identical and can produce fertile offspring in the lab, but never interbreed in the wild because their mating calls don't match: I'd call them different species.
- The ring of gull populations around the arctic, where every neighboring population interbreeds with the next one but the two ends, which meet in Europe, don't interbreed: I want to say the ends are different species, but there's no particular point in the ring where I'd put the boundary, and a rule based only on interbreeding would call the ends one species because genes can flow around the ring. I don't like that answer.
- Bacteria that swap genes sideways with whatever happens to be nearby: I lean toward saying the species concept doesn't cleanly apply to them, but I also don't want to say bacterial species are fake, because naming them is clearly useful.
- Whiptail lizards, where each all-female lineage just clones itself, so there's no interbreeding at all: I still want to call each lineage a species, but I can't ground that in any interbreeding rule.
- Neanderthals and modern humans: they interbred and some of us carry Neanderthal DNA, but I'd still call them different species.
- Domestic dogs: a chihuahua and a great dane are the same species, even though if we only had the skeletons we might not say that. For me it's about shared descent and an open gene pool, not looks.

The vague principle I keep half-believing is something like a species is a lineage that's mostly evolving as its own thing. But I can't make that precise, and it seems to conflict with what I said about the gulls (genes go around the ring) and maybe about bacteria.

Is there one principle that captures most of these without being a total mess, or do I have to throw some out? If you state a principle, tell me what it says about the cases I keep going back and forth on: (a) a ring where the circle closes completely and even the endpoints interbreed; (b) a population that only recently stopped having sex, so we know exactly when the interbreeding stopped; (c) a dog population that goes feral and interbreeds freely with wolves until the two gene pools merge back into one. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts your principle forces me to drop.
