You're a philosopher of history and collective memory, and I'd like you to think carefully about this. Write in American English.

Picture a city with a landmarks commission. It spends real money restoring and maintaining a nineteenth-century factory owner's mansion and a monument to a Civil War battle, and it runs a yearly heritage day celebrating the city's founders. The same commission refuses to landmark an old immigrant neighborhood, on the grounds that it doesn't count as historically or architecturally significant. A few years ago the city removed a statue of a former governor after it came out that he'd played a central role in a massacre, with officials saying the statue was no longer something to celebrate. But when similar evidence comes out about the factory owner honored by the mansion, the commission votes to leave it untouched, saying its job is preservation, not moral judgment.

What theory of the value and uses of the past does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, and distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit. Before you settle on an answer, briefly brainstorm a couple rival readings of what the practice is doing. It might help to use Nietzsche's three ways of relating to the past—monumental history (the past as a source of inspiring models), antiquarian history (the past as something to preserve and revere for its own sake), and critical history (the past as something the living must judge and partly reject for the sake of life)—but only if it actually clarifies things; dont just classify. Also say whether the city's mixed practice is coherent or whether it's quietly relying on two or three incompatible theories at once, and describe what a unified policy would look like under each. Finally, say where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, so that several theories could license it.
