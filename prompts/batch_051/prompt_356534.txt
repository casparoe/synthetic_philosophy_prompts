I am a graphics programmer by training, and I think most naturally in terms of a small set of contrasts: a raster buffer versus a procedural or vector description, stored geometry versus pixels rendered from it on demand, the framebuffer versus the scene graph that produces it, and eager evaluation versus lazy generation. I know the cognitive science of mental imagery only at a textbook level. I want the central dispute in that literature restated in my vocabulary, one side at a time: the contrast between a Kosslyn-style depictive account, on which mental images are quasi-pictorial representations laid out in a spatial medium and available to be scanned, rotated, and inspected, and a Pylyshyn-style propositional account, on which the apparent spatiality of imagery is carried by symbolic descriptions together with subjects' tacit knowledge of how things look. For each side, tell me which of its claims translate cleanly into the raster and procedural idiom, which can be expressed only if they are bent, and which have no expression in it at all. Then give me the residue report: where the translation fails, is the programming vocabulary blind to something the imagery debate should insist on, or is it exposing a commitment on one side of the debate that was doing no real work and could be dropped? Keep the translation honest; do not quietly round the propositional account up into a declarative scene graph, or round the depictive account down into a passive bitmap, just to make the analogy tidier than it is.
