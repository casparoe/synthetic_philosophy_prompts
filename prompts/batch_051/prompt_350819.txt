I've been rereading Thomas Nagel's "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" and one early paragraph stuck with me. He imagines a Martian scientist who has a complete, perfect physical and behavioral account of bats, and then says the Martian still wouldn't know what it's like to be a bat, because the subjective character of experience is essentially tied to a point of view, and no amount of objective, viewpoint-free description can capture it. I keep noticing what feels like the same structure in cryptography. With information-theoretic secrecy, like a one-time pad, the ciphertext really contains no information about the plaintext; even an attacker with unbounded computation can't recover the secret from the public transcript, because the information just isn't there. Nagel's objective account seems like the ciphertext, and the "what it's like" side is the plaintext, and the gap isn't a merely practical limitation but something more principled. But maybe this is a stretch. On the cryptography side, the plaintext is just more objective information that someone somewhere has, and the key could in principle be handed over; Nagel's point seems stronger, that the subjective character isn't hidden from a third party but is a different kind of fact, not the sort of thing that exists as a string waiting to be decrypted. Or maybe the bat's point of view is exactly the key, and the hard problem is why there is a private key at all rather than just a longer public transcript. I cant tell if I'm onto a deep structural parallel about perspective-dependence and privacy, or just using "information" loosely. Could you walk through the mapping, say what would need to be true on each side for results to transfer, and name something the analogy predicts that comes out false?
