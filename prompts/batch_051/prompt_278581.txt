Our readings mostly treated them as harmless historical leftovers, but I'm not satisfied with that, and I'd like to pin down my own view in a more disciplined way than by listing pros and cons.

Here is the proposition I want your credence for: in a consolidated parliamentary democracy with strong rule-of-law protections, a constitutional monarchy of the sort found in the United Kingdom, Sweden, or Japan is democratically legitimate — by which I mean it can be reconciled with the core requirements of political equality, popular authorization, and accountability of power, rather than being a mere tolerated anomaly. Please give me a single number between 0 and 1, and say how settled you take that number to be.

More important than the number itself is the decomposition, because I want to see where the argument actually lives. Please break the proposition into the sub-questions your credence depends on, giving a rough probability and a sentence or two of reasoning for each. In particular, I'd like you to address: whether a ceremonial, non-partisan head of state improves democratic functioning enough to matter, for example by absorbing consensus and continuity functions that would otherwise fall to an elected politician; whether hereditary selection for a largely symbolic office is compatible with a principled commitment to political equality, and whether continued popular acceptance or explicit authorization is capable of repairing any deficit; whether the monarchy's residual reserve powers are genuinely inert or instead constitute an unaccountable constitutional safety valve; and whether whatever benefits actually exist are uniquely produced by a hereditary monarchy or could be supplied just as well by a non-executive elected head of state.

Then tell me how these pieces combine to produce your overall number: which sub-question is bearing the most weight, and which one would move your final credence the most if you came to see it differently. Finally, indicate what kind of evidence or argument — historical, comparative, or conceptual — would most likely shift that pivotal sub-question, and therefore your overall view.

As you do all of this, please keep the settled and the contested clearly separated. I want to know which empirical findings are robust and which are genuinely disputed among political scientists, and which normative premises are genuinely contested among political philosophers who work on democratic legitimacy. Please don't present a contested reading of the evidence, or a contested normative framework, as if it were the consensus.
