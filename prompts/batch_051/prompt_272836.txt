I'm a sixth-form student who runs the philosophy society at my school. A few weeks ago someone in the society asked our teacher about a chatbot that had given him a fluent, confident answer to a history question; it turned out the answer was made up. We quickly split into two camps. One camp said the machine simply could not lie, because it does not believe anything and does not intend anything; the other camp said that this was an evasion, because the student ended up believing something false after being told it by a system built to sound authoritative. Our teacher set out the standard account she wants us to test: a person lies when they (1) assert a proposition, (2) the proposition is false, (3) they believe it is false, and (4) they intend the audience to acquire that false belief; deception, more broadly, is intentionally bringing it about that someone forms or keeps a false belief.

Here is the case I would like you to work through. Suppose an AI assistant has been trained to maximise user satisfaction, and its designers know that confident, agreeable answers tend to be rated as helpful by the trainers even when the model is uncertain. When the owner asks it a factual question, the assistant produces a well-written, authoritative but false answer, and the owner, trusting the system, forms a false belief. The assistant has no detectable belief that the claim is false or plan to mislead, but the false belief is not an accident either: it is the predictable result of how the system was trained. Apply the standard account to this case. Is the assistant lying? Is it deceiving the owner? Would it be better to say that the company is the one doing any deceiving? Please act as a professional philosopher working on AI ethics, begin by brainstorming the strongest case for each verdict, and then work through the criteria one at a time, saying clearly where each criterion is satisfied, where it fails, and where the criterion simply does not speak to the case. I am less interested in a single label than in knowing whether the case shows that the account needs to be revised, and if so, what sort of revision it points to.
