I teach philosophy, but for the last couple years I've been the token humanities person embedded in a machine learning group that builds language-model assistants to summarize clinical notes and suggest triage levels for under-resourced clinics. The group is stuck on how to supervise their fine-tuning, and they've split into two camps. The outcome camp says raters should score only the final output: does the summary keep the medically important facts, does the triage suggestion match what a clinician would actually do. The process camp says raters should score the model's visible reasoning: did it weigh the right symptoms, did it avoid anchoring on the first diagnosis, did it treat missing information differently from a negative finding. The outcome camp says reasoning is just a means, and scoring it teaches models to produce tidy rationalizations for bad calls. The process camp says outcomes here are noisy, delayed, and sometimes never observed, so pure outcome supervision is just training on whatever the rater happens to notice.

My actual job is to turn this argument into a reusable procedure for the supervision leads, not settle it abstractly. I want a checklist or ordered decision rule they can apply each week when they draft the guidelines for a new batch of tasks: when should the scoring be process-based, when outcome-based, when some mix, and what should trigger switching from one to the other. Constrain it around their actual situation. They review about three batches a week, maybe a thousand outputs each. The raters are medical scribes and GPs who have fifteen seconds per item, with a bit more time for flagged cases. The sign-off body is a clinical safety board full of people who distrust anything they can't inspect. Work through two or three examples: at least one where the procedure says outcome-only and you think the team will hate it, one where it says process-based even though the outcome is checkable, and one where the procedure can't decide and a human has to make the call. And say plainly where the judgment can't be automated away. The philosopher in me keep thinking this is just the old act-vs-consequences question wearing a lab coat, but I need it operationalized, not a lecture.
