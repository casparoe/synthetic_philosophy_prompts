I have been reading a general-audience book about consciousness, and in the chapter on physicalism I hit a summary of the state of play that I suspect is doing a lot of work. The author writes:

"Physicalism is the doctrine that everything is physical, or at least that the physical facts determine all the facts. The trouble, known as Hempel's dilemma, is that physicalists cannot say what 'physical' means. If 'physical' means what current physics says exists, the doctrine is almost certainly false, since current physics is known to be incomplete and will be revised. If 'physical' means what some future, completed physics says exists, the doctrine is empty: we cannot say what that future physics will contain, so we cannot say what the doctrine rules out. For all we know, ideal physics could posit consciousness itself as a fundamental ingredient, and then the claim that everything is physical would stop being a substantive claim about the mind. The chapter concludes that physicalism cannot supply the definition its own formulation requires, and that the position is best treated as a promissory note — a hope about future science — rather than as a thesis with clear commitments."

I found the chapter's conclusion persuasive, which is exactly why I want to know how suspicious I should be of it. I would like an accuracy audit of that passage for a reader with no stake in the debate, not a brief for either side. Please go piece by piece and tell me: (1) what a careful philosopher of mind would accept as fair, (2) what they would call overstated or subtly wrong, and (3) anything that is just confused or rests on a conflation between two claims the position keeps apart. In particular, I want to know whether the chapter's conclusion — that physicalism is left as a mere promissory note — actually follows from the parts of the summary that survive correction, and what a sophisticated physicalist would say in response at that exact point. Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
