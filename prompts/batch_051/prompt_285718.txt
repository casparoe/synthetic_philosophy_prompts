I'm reading a pop science book about animal minds, and it gives this summary of the view people keep calling "sentience as a matter of degree":

"Consciousness isn't an on/off switch. Humans are more conscious than dogs, dogs more conscious than fish, and fish more conscious than insects. In fact, any system that processes information is at least a little bit conscious, because consciousness just is what integrated information feels like from the inside. So the question shouldn't be whether a creature is sentient, but how sentient it is, and it should recieve moral consideration in proportion to that. There's no sharp cut-off: insects still get a sliver of consideration, and on this view even a thermostat has a tiny nonzero amount of consciousness, though in practice it rounds to zero. Moral status is like income tax brackets—it tracks degree of consciousness on a sliding scale."

Something about this feels off to me, but I can't put my finger on where the mistake is. Could you go through it piece by piece and tell me which parts a careful defender of graded sentience would accept, which parts are overstatements, and whether the tax-bracket conclusion actually follows from the accurate parts?
