I'm a third-year undergraduate in a seminar that pairs Buddhist and Western treatments of the self, and I keep coming back to the fact that the two literatures seem unable to touch each other. The anatta side argues that the person is a conventional designation for five aggregates — material form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, and consciousness — none of which is a self, and that the felt sense of being a persisting owner of experience is precisely what generates clinging and suffering. The Western personal-identity side keeps asking under what conditions a person at one time is identical with a person at another time, and treats the special first-person concern each of us has for our own future as something a theory must respect. The striking thing is how little each side moves the other. The Buddhists seem to regard the Western question as itself a symptom — a request for metaphysical reassurance that a self exists — while the Westerners seem to regard the Buddhists as having changed the subject from identity to suffering and liberation. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are mostly answering different questions: one an ultimate-level inquiry into what exists, the other a conventional, practical inquiry into what makes someone responsible, concerned for the future, and the same person for purposes of promise and punishment. And the Buddhist side is, on top of that, doing something therapeutic with the question rather than trying to win a position. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a substantive disagreement underneath — for example, about whether conventional truth is a genuine level at which persons exist, or about whether the felt unity of consciousness is data or delusion? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the impasse rather than a verdict on who is right, and an honest account of what would count as progress given the diagnosis: a distinction, a change of question, a test, or an argument that none should be expected.
