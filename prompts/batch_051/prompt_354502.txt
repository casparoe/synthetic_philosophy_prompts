I am trying to think through a question about artificial motivation, and I have been reading Thomas Hobbes's *Leviathan* (1651). In the Introduction, he writes:

"Nature (the art whereby God hath made and governes the world) is by the art of man, as in many other things, so in this also imitated, that it can make an Artificial Animal. For seeing life is but a motion of Limbs, the begining whereof is in some principall part within; why may we not say, that all *Automata* (Engines that move themselves by springs and wheeles as doth a watch) have an artificiall life? For what is the *Heart*, but a *Spring*; and the *Nerves*, but so many *Strings*; and the *Joynts*, but so many *Wheeles*, giving motion to the whole Body, such as was intended by the Artificer?"

I want to understand what exactly Hobbes is claiming here. If life is just motion, and a watch has a heart-like spring and nerve-like strings, does it follow on his account that a sufficiently complex automaton could have something like desire or aversion in its own right? I know that later, in Chapter VI, Hobbes defines endeavour as small beginnings of motion and says these are called appetite or aversion. But I am not sure whether the Introduction's talk of artificial life is meant to extend that psychology to automata, or whether it is only a metaphor for organizational similarity. Can you work through the passage carefully and help me see what is doing the philosophical work? In particular, I want to know whether Hobbes could consistently say that an artificial animal is alive but has no motivations, and what he would have to add to confer something like motivation on an artifact. I am not asking for bibliography or for scholarly consensus; I just want your best interpretation. Please indicate how confident you are in the specific claims you make, and single out the point you are least sure of.
