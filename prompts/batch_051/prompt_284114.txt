The standard argument that honoring sunk costs is irrational goes like this: the time or money is gone either way, so it should not enter your comparison of future options; only forward-looking costs and benefits count. The argument quietly assumes the only way a past expenditure can reach your future situation is through your remaining budget. But suppose you live in a world where your decisions are logged and fed into models that other agents use to decide how to treat you—a rideshare app learning how long you'll wait before you cave on surge pricing, an employer inferring whether you're a quitter from how long you stuck with a failing project, a negotiation counterpart learning whether you'll walk away after you've sunk effort into a deal. In that world, honoring a sunk cost changes what a learner believes about you, and that belief becomes part of what future options you'll be offered. Nowdays the question is: does the anti-sunk-cost argument still go through, or does it need a patch, or is it dead in that setting? Say what the argument needs to be true and whether it holds in that environment. If it survives, show why the belief effects either wash out or are just future costs wearing a disguise. If it doesn't, name the exact premise that broke and say what principle is left standing for deciding whether to keep investing. I care about what happens to the argument, not about how to manage a stock portfolio.
