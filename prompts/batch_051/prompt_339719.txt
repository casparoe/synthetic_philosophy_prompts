The special composition question asks, roughly, when several things compose a further thing. Philosophers have divided for decades into stable camps: universalists, who hold that any plurality whatever composes something; nihilists, who hold that composition never occurs and that no composite objects exist; and restrictivists, who hold that composition occurs only under some further condition, such as the parts being caught up in a single life or being arranged object-wise. The striking feature of the debate is its resistance to resolution. The parties accept the same microphysical facts and the same ordinary observations about tables, sculptures, and scattered particles; each side has learned to absorb the others' counterexamples by reinterpreting a term, restricting a quantifier, or dismissing a disputed sentence as loose talk. Yet none of them regards the others as having changed the subject, and after decades of sophisticated work no decisive argument has emerged in either direction.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse may not be a first-order dispute about what exists at all, but a disagreement about what the special composition question is supposed to be: a question for fundamental metaphysics, a question about our practices of counting and referring, or a question about which descriptions are useful.
