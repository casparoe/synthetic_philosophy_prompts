I am a senior software engineer, and I think most comfortably in the vocabulary of systems design: interfaces and contracts, type systems and runtime checks, invariants, refactoring and technical debt, deprecation and backward-compatibility policies, and the difference between a system that conforms to its specification and a system that does what its users need. I have only a casual acquaintance with legal philosophy.

I keep encountering the dispute among Fuller, Raz, and Waldron about whether the rule of law is an intrinsically moral ideal or merely an instrumentally valuable property that a legal system can possess regardless of the ends it serves. Please restate that debate in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and then report what did not survive the translation.

Here is the rough picture I have, which you should correct and sharpen as you go. Fuller claims that a legal system must satisfy a set of internal requirements that I think of as scaffolding constraints: rules must be general, public, prospective rather than retroactive, intelligible, mutually consistent, possible to comply with, reasonably stable over time, and actually followed by officials. He seems to think that a system failing these constraints is not merely badly engineered but morally defective, because law of that sort stops treating its subjects as agents capable of guiding their own conduct. Raz replies that the rule of law is a virtue of law the way sharpness is a virtue of a knife: it makes law more effective at whatever it is used for, including bad ends, and so is not by itself a moral ideal. Waldron, as I understand him, shifts attention to courts and procedure, and claims that the point of rule-of-law institutions is to force power to be exercised through reasoned argument addressed to those it binds, which treats them as bearers of dignity.

Restate each position — Fuller's scaffolding constraints, Raz's sharp-knife point, Waldron's proceduralism — in terms of interfaces, contracts, type systems, runtime checks, invariants, versioning, and related concepts from my field. Be honest as you translate: do not quietly assume that a well-designed system is good in any moral sense, or that users are owed anything; call those out as commitments rather than translations.

Then give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly? Which translate only with distortion? Which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? And is the untranslatable remainder evidence that my vocabulary is missing something, or that the philosophers' disagreement is less substantive than it looks?
