I've been reading Plato's Laws lately—my first time through it, after years doing spectroscopy and mostly ignoring the Greeks—and I keep coming back to the bit in Book 1 where the Athenian stranger argues that wine is a kind of test of character. If I've got him right, his claim is that the drinking party provides a cheap, low-stakes way to reveal a person's underlying dispositions: shamelessness, boldness, inability to resist pleasure, or the opposite, all come out under intoxication, and you can observe them before the person is put in charge of anything important. He says something about how older men especially need wine to become confident enough to sing and show their natures, but my Greek is nonexistent and I'm relying on a translation anyway.

Anyway, I started wondering whether that idea could be turned into an actual institutional mechanism. Suppose a city in the present day, say a mid-sized town, decides to use a controlled version of this as part of its screening for public offices—not for all applicants, but for positions that require unusual self-command: police oversight board, animal control, code enforcement, something like that. The city sets up a supervised twice-yearly drinking exercise, with medical staff, paid participants, fixed amounts, and no external consequences for what anyone says while drunk. The stated aim is to elicit and record behavior under lowered inhibition; the recordings are then used by a hiring or reappointment panel.

What I'd like you to do is treat this as a mechanism, not a moral position: what does it incentivize among candidates, among the people who run it, and among the public? How would it be gamed? Does it actually select for self-command, or for something else—say, tolerance to alcohol, ability to fake drunkenness, pre-training, or simply willingness to perform for a panel? What second-order effects does it have on the culture of the city's bureaucracy? And are any of the failure modes fixable without gutting the scheme?
