I'm a novelist, mostly historical fiction, and I've developed a research habit that I keep defending but haven't really stress-tested. When I'm writing about a place or period and I can find an actual photograph of it (a street scene, a crowd, a boarding-house room), I treat the photo as if I'm not looking at a picture but through a window at the real thing. I catch myself saying I've "seen" the Lower East Side in 1912, when the truth is I looked at some old photographic prints. I have an unofficial policy: photographs get treated as my own direct contact with the scene, and if a photograph conflicts with a written description or a drawing, the photograph wins, almost as if it were my own memory. Paintings and drawings I treat as somebody's interpretation, but photographs feel categorically differnt: light from the actual street had to hit the film, so the scene itself seems to be present in the image in a way it just isn't in a sketch. A photographer friend says I'm fooling myself. He says a photograph is as much a made, selective object as any drawing: someone chose the lens, the frame, the moment, the exposure, possibly rearranged or staged things, and then it was developed and printed and cropped and captioned. I feel the force of that, but I also can't shake the causal point.

So my question: is my policy of treating photographs as transparent windows rather than as made representations defensible, and where does it stop being defensible? What am I actually entitled to claim I "saw" or know from a photograph, and should I keep letting a photograph overrule a conflicting written description?

Please answer as a professional philosopher working on perception, photography, and testimony. Start by brainstorming the strongest arguments on both sides before you commit, then work through the difference between what a photograph's causal history guarantees and what its framing and selection introduce. At the very end, after your full answer, give me your bottom line on a single line in this exact form: \answer{...}, where ... is one of: window, picture, or partly-window.
