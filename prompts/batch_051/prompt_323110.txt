I keep running into the same unproductive pattern in the democracy-versus-epistocracy debate: the parties disagree about what a theory of political authority even has to deliver, so they pass each other by. Before I read another proposal, I want a specification of the desiderata for an adequate theory of democratic authority once the epistocratic challenge has been taken seriously. By epistocracy I mean any arrangement that apportions political power by knowledge or competence—restricted suffrage, plural voting, an expert council with veto power—rather than by equal votes.

Write the list of requirements any such theory must meet if it is to adjudicate that debate, with the case or consideration behind each. At a minimum, the list should wrestle with the following material. (1) The authority-correctness gap: a binding decision can be substantively wrong, and a theory must say why it is still authoritative, or bite the bullet and say it is not. (2) The conditions under which large groups of nonexperts track the truth better than small groups of experts, and the conditions under which they do not: the independence, minimal competence, and common-aim assumptions behind jury-style arguments, and their failure under correlated bias, rational ignorance, and motivated reasoning. (3) The selectorate problem: any test of competence presupposes someone competent to write the test, and the history of literacy tests and poll taxes should be treated as a standing caution, not a cheap rhetorical point. (4) The distinction between epistemic and evaluative components of a decision: a question like whether to raise a minimum wage or impose a lockdown contains both technical forecasts and distributive choices, and a theory must say which component the franchise is supposed to track. (5) Equal standing and the expressive content of the franchise: whether excluding a person from the electorate wrongs them even when outcomes would improve. (6) Accountability and incentives: competence and insulation often trade off against responsiveness, and expert bodies can be knowledgeable and systematically self-interested at once. (7) Formal constraints on aggregation: whatever impossibility or indeterminacy results apply to aggregating preferences or judgments do not disappear merely because the franchise shrinks.

Then mark each desideratum as non-negotiable for any credible account, or as a preference of a particular camp—proceduralist, epistemic democrat, or epistocrat—and identify the two or three items that do the most work excluding candidate theories.

Next, say whether any subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable, or satisfiable only by smuggling in an idealization. I do not mean merely that there are trade-offs; I mean specific combinations that cannot be jointly met under stated conditions, and whether that result blocks an entire family of views or only their more ambitious versions.

Finally, give a short scorecard for the main families against the list: restricted-suffrage epistocracy; epistemic proceduralism, understood roughly as equal participation plus a standard of correctness independent of the procedure; and a purely procedural equal-standing view. Keep the scorecard brief—the criteria and the tensions among them are the point. No bibliography; the desiderata should stand or fall on their own.
