In a seminar on scientific realism I have been trying to get students to distinguish two things: whether a theory works, and whether its central posits are approximately true. The standard entry point is the pessimistic meta-induction associated with Laudan. Its form, as I present it, is this. The history of science contains a long list of theories that were regarded in their own time as empirically successful and that later generations came to regard as deeply false — the phlogiston account of combustion, the caloric theory of heat, the luminiferous aether, and many others. Our own best theories resemble those earlier theories in being empirically successful. By induction over that historical record, then, our own best theories are likely to come to be regarded as false as well. The realist's standard replies are also familiar: the discarded theories were not successful in the same way, they did not make the same kind of novel predictions, or their genuinely operative parts survived theory change.

What I have not seen discussed as clearly is how this argument is affected by the way a substantial part of successful science is now done. A great deal of current predictive success comes from deep-learning models that are deliberately not interpreted as candidate descriptions of the mechanisms that produce the data: systems that predict protein structures, weather, materials behaviour, and the like can be strikingly accurate while no one regards their internal structure as approximately true. Their success is truth-free from the outset. It is obvious enough how this puts pressure on the realist's no-miracles argument. My question is whether it also puts pressure on the pessimistic induction in the other direction. On one reading, the induction only had force because empirical success and truth-directed theoretical ambition ran together in its sample of past theories; if success can now be achieved without any truth-directed ambition, then the induction's sample may no longer be relevantly like the present truth-aiming theories to which the realist's hope attaches. On another reading, the new models simply give the anti-realist an especially clean case: they show that predictive success floats free of truth, which is exactly what the induction was driving at.

I would like a precise diagnosis. Does the pessimistic meta-induction survive the rise of black-box predictive models in science? Identify the specific premise or inductive similarity claim on which this development bears, and say whether the argument still goes through unchanged, requires repair, or should be re-described or abandoned in its present form. Your answer should be written in British English.
