For a decision-theory seminar, I have been asked to write down my current commitments about risk, uncertainty, and rational choice so that the rest of the group can audit whether they jointly hang together. Here they are.

1. I regard the Allais pattern as rational. I prefer a guaranteed $1 million to a gamble paying $1 million with 89 percent probability, $5 million with 10 percent probability, and nothing with 1 percent probability; and in the paired choice I prefer a 10 percent chance of $5 million to an 11 percent chance of $1 million. What should go, I think, is expected utility theory's independence axiom, not the preference.

2. I also regard the Ellsberg pattern as rational. Faced with a bet on an urn of known composition and a bet on an urn of unknown composition, I prefer the bet on the known urn whichever colour I am offered. Ambiguity aversion is a legitimate attitude, distinct from ordinary risk aversion.

3. But I hold that degrees of belief are always a single precise probability function over states. I do not want imprecise probabilities, sets of priors, or non-additive measures of belief.

4. The sure-thing principle in its unrestricted form seems to me nearly compulsory: if you would choose one act over another both on the supposition that some event obtains and on the supposition that it does not, then you should choose it unconditionally. I am reluctant to count violations of it as rational.

5. I am inclined to think that an agent's attitude to risk is exhausted by the shape of her utility function over outcomes. A separate device for weighting probabilities looks like extra machinery doing work that utility should do alone.

6. Yet I also think it can be fully rational for one person at one time and from the same financial position to buy both an actuarially unfair insurance policy and an actuarially unfair lottery ticket.

Go through these commitments and identify which pairs are in genuine tension and which pairs only appear to be in tension but can be reconciled at little cost. For each genuine tension, rank it by severity, say what the cheapest repair would be, and say what that repair gives up. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set coherent, and sketch the resulting position. Be definite rather than even-handed: give your own assessment and defend it.
