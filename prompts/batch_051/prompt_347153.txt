The course has covered deflationary approaches to ontological disagreement, including the view that the existential quantifier can vary in meaning across frameworks (often called quantifier variance), as well as the competing view that not all candidate meanings are on a par, because some better reflect the structure of the world than others. Exam question: Using the debate over whether there are tables as your running example, explain what it means to call a philosophical disagreement "merely verbal." Then evaluate the following claim: if a dispute is merely verbal, it is philosophically insubstantial and can safely be set aside. In your answer, be explicit about which steps are intended to follow from which.

Student's answer: A dispute is verbal when the parties attach different meanings to a key expression, so that their apparently contradictory claims can each come out true relative to their own uses. The debate over tables is a clear case. The universalist's sentence "there are tables" is true when "there are" ranges over composite objects as well as simples, while the nihilist's sentence "there are no tables" is true when "there are" ranges only over simples. Each side is therefore correct within its own linguistic framework, and neither is reporting a falsehood about the world as the speaker uses the relevant terms. Since the remaining disagreement is only about which meaning to adopt, and since no non-linguistic fact selects one meaning over the other, making the two meanings explicit dissolves the dispute. What is left is merely a choice of language, and choices of language are not themselves true or false. The debate is therefore philosophically insubstantial and can be set aside.
