I have been developing a principle about the ethics of using animal byproducts, and I would like an honest assessment of it. I call it the Closed-Causal-Chain Principle. It says the following. If an animal product is an incidental byproduct of a practice that was neither initiated nor sustained in order to obtain that product, and if the product will otherwise go to waste, then using that product does not make one complicit in any harm to the animal, even when one believes the practices that ordinarily produce such products are seriously wrong. The central case is a hen rescued from an egg operation who is kept in a sanctuary; she lays unfertilized eggs that cannot develop and that will simply decompose if left alone. The principle holds that eating those eggs is not wrong in virtue of any complicity in the harms of the egg industry, because the causal route from use back to the hen contains no demand signal and the egg is already wholly detached from the harmful transaction that produced the hen. The same reasoning would cover dairy products retrieved from a supermarket dumpster or leather taken from a discarded jacket.

I am aware of the obvious replies: that using such products expresses disrespect for animals, and that it may maintain tastes or habits that will later drive the very demand one disavows. I suspect those are objections to practicing the principle under imperfect self-knowledge rather than objections to the principle as a criterion of complicity, but I am prepared to be corrected. What I want to know is this. First, is the principle coherent as stated, or does it depend on a questionable distinction between an incidental byproduct and a product? Second, is it simply consequentialism about animal use under a new name, or is it substantially the existing freegan position, and if so what does it add? Third, give me the three strongest objections aimed at the principle itself rather than at its practical risks, and say honestly whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
