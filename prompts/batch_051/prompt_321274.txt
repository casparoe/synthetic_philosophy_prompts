Write the text of a deadpan, satirical "final determination" public notice from a fictional standards body, the Bureau of Canonical Designation, announcing that the symbol i may no longer be described as "the" square root of negative one. The notice should run 450–550 words and should be written in the formal register of a bureaucracy publishing a ruling: numbered sections, whereas-clauses, an implementation timetable, and a period for public comment. The target of the satire is not notation itself but the habit of treating a conventional choice as though it picked out a preexisting distinguished object — as if one of the two square roots of −1 came already wearing the label i before anyone did the labeling.

The humor should come from getting the algebra and its philosophy right, and the piece should stay straight-faced throughout; the narrator never announces the moral. The mathematical material to work with includes: complex conjugation is a field automorphism of C that fixes the real numbers while sending i to −i; the two roots satisfy exactly the same polynomial identities over R and are Galois conjugates over the reals; and adjectives such as "positive" or "clockwise" presuppose structure — an ordering, an orientation of the plane — that the complex field does not itself provide. Use these facts as the Bureau's sober institutional grounds: reject the notion of a "natural" or "principal" root, while regulating who may use the definite article in front of "square root of negative one," grandfathering existing textbooks under a compliance schedule, and licensing certain departments to "break the symmetry" informally. The notice should implicitly answer the question "does it matter how you label the roots?" with a two-sided answer: no, you cannot single out one root by intrinsic properties of the bare field; yes, an agreed label is exactly what lets a whole community refer unambiguously to one of them.

Include one short fictional quotation from a dissenting committee member who objects that the Bureau has discriminated against the "clockwise" root; the quotation should let the reader see that the member has mistaken a chosen orientation of the plane for an intrinsic property of one of the roots. End the notice with a bureaucratic footnote that accidentally concedes the useful point: a shared convention is precisely what makes the notation work, and the Bureau cannot tell you which root is which without already appealing to it.
