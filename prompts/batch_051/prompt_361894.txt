I've been reading about chartered companies as little states — the East India Company keeping armies, running courts, coining money — and got stuck on a hypothetical I want you to actually work through rather than wave at. Suppose the 1673 letters patent of the Meridian Company contain this clause: 'The said Company shall and may have, hold and exercise, within the Limits of its several Factories and Plantations, full Power and Authority to ordain, establish and put in execution such Laws, Ordinances and Constitutions, and to impose such Fines, Imprisonments and other Punishments, as shall be needful for the good Government of the said Factories and Plantations and the better Ordering of the Company's Servants and others residing therein.'

The Company itself went under in 1840, but its stock and privileges were bought up by a shell firm that survives. Today that successor runs an offshore server farm on a decommissioned oil platform in international waters, with a resident contractor workforce on six-month shifts and its own power, clinic, and security. The successor claims the clause gives it legislative and criminal jurisdiction over everyone on the platform, so it can write rules, levy fines, and run its own tribunal rather than call in any national authority. The 1673 grantors plainly never antcipated server farms, oil platforms, or contractors with laptops. Walk through what the clause actually licenses here under four approaches — plain words, the original purpose, what the 1673 grantors would have said if you could have asked them, and what a reasonable reader today would make of 'Factories and Plantations' and 'others residing therein' — and then tell me which approach should control and whether the company's claim survives.
