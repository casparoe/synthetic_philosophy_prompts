I am trying to reconstruct the principle that actually governs my decisions about tiny probabilities of enormous goods, and I would like you to say whether any single defensible view captures my verdicts or which of them I would have to revise. Stipulate throughout that the only thing at stake is how many lives are saved, that saving n lives is n times as valuable as saving one life for any finite n, that no special duties or side-constraints bear on the choices, and that the probabilities are well defined unless a case says otherwise. Each case pits a comparatively sure, moderate option against a risky, enormous option, at equal cost.

1. The manageable gamble. I choose a one-in-one-thousand chance of saving one million lives over the certainty of saving one hundred lives. The expectation is ten times larger, and the probability is one I can genuinely imagine being realized.

2. The stipulated astronomical gamble. I choose the certainty of saving one hundred lives over a one-in-10^30 chance of saving 10^40 lives, even though the process producing the chance is fully specified and I accept that the probability really is one in 10^30. The gamble has an expectation of 10^10 lives, vastly exceeding the certain option, yet the chance feels to me like something no different for practical purposes from zero.

3. The mugger. I choose the certainty of saving one hundred lives over a stranger's claim that, for the same money, he can deliver a one-in-10^10 chance of saving 10^20 lives, and no amount of raising the number or lowering the probability moves me. But my reason here is different from in case 2: I do not believe there is a well-defined probability to which I have access, and I take the offer to be exploiting the fact that any finite chance can be outweighed by a still larger prize.

4. The everyday small probability. I knowingly accept a one-in-one-hundred-thousand chance of a fatal crash in order to run a brief errand by car. So I cannot claim as a general principle that tiny probabilities are simply zero for me.

5. The portfolio case, on which I have no settled verdict. Suppose I could run the case-2 gamble not once but 10^31 times, each run probabilistically independent of the rest and each with its own one-in-10^30 chance, while the alternative remained one hundred certain lives per run. I find myself inclined to run the whole portfolio of gambles, since at least one success then becomes all but guaranteed, but I cannot see why I would accept the collection while refusing every individual member of it.

My working hunch is something like this: probabilities carry weight only once they cross a threshold of being a realizable, observable chance, and below that threshold I treat them as carrying no weight at all; alternatively, expected value governs choices only for risks that will be repeated enough for the expected outcome to show up in actual outcomes. Both formulations feel arbitrary to me, and I suspect they collapse under pressure.

So my question is this. Is there a defensible principle that captures verdicts 1 through 4 and yields a determinate verdict on case 5? If your best principle cannot capture all of them, please say exactly which of my verdicts must be abandoned, and explain why the remaining verdicts, as revised, are captured by the principle you give. I am especially interested in whether the trouble lies with unbounded value, with my treatment of very small probabilities, or with expected-value reasoning itself, and in what your preferred principle says about someone who keeps accepting ever-larger multiples of a sure thing in exchange for ever-smaller chances of ever-larger outcomes.
