You are a philosopher of language who works on truth in fiction and empty names, and who treats a definition as a precision instrument rather than a gesture. I want you to build a working definition of "true in the fiction" — that is, a definition of what it is for an ordinary sentence such as 'Sherlock Holmes is a detective' to be true according to a work of fiction.

Take Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes stories as the default fiction F. I will give you cases, some of which I have firm intuitions about and some of which I do not. Then I want the definition, not just a commentary on the cases.

Cases:

A. Clear positive. In F, 'Sherlock Holmes is a detective who lives in London and works with Dr Watson' is true.

B. Clear negative. In F, 'Sherlock Holmes is a Victorian lighthouse keeper in rural Wales' is false.

C. Silent background. In F, 'Sherlock Holmes has a great-grandmother' is never stated but I am strongly inclined to call it true. So is 'Sherlock Holmes is not a robot.' What licenses these verdicts, and where does the silent background stop?

D. Indeterminate detail. 'Sherlock Holmes had exactly 124,789 hairs on his head on 4 January 1889.' This looks neither true nor false in F. Must a good definition say that, or may it force a truth value?

E. Cross-canon. 'Sherlock Holmes uses a mobile phone' is false in F, but true in the BBC series Sherlock. Is the sentence true in the fiction, false in the fiction, or only true relative to a chosen body of text? Your definition should say what the "fiction" is relative to.

F. Internal inconsistency. Suppose a story states in one chapter that a character is left-handed and in a later chapter that the same character is right-handed. What should a good definition say is true in that fiction: both claims, neither, one of them, or something else?

G. Fan fiction. In an unpublished fan story, Holmes and Watson are married. Is that true in the fan fiction? Is it true in F? Does a new piece of writing automatically enlarge the fiction to which the definition applies?

H. Unwarranted inference. A reader concludes from F that Holmes was an only child, although the text neither states nor implies it. Is the conclusion true in the fiction, false in the fiction, or merely in the reader?

I. Existence talk. 'Sherlock Holmes does not exist' is true about the real world. Is it also true "in the fiction"? More generally, what does your definition say about claims about a fiction rather than claims made within it?

Then confront the empty-name problem directly. Since there is no Sherlock Holmes, what makes 'Sherlock Holmes is a detective' true according to F rather than meaningless or false? Does your definition require the name to refer after all? If not, explain how the truth conditions work without reference; if yes, say what the name refers to — a role, a property bundle, an inhabitant of some world, an abstract object, or something else.

Deliverable. Start by brainstorming the main candidate approaches and where they break. Then state your best single definition, in semi-formal terms if useful, and explain any technical vocabulary you introduce. Run the definition through A–I, saying for each case whether it returns the right verdict, a defensible but surprising verdict, a wrong verdict, or no verdict at all. Revise the definition if the cases expose defects. Finally, tell me which of A–I your definition handles least securely, and whether you think "true in fiction" is ultimately one notion or a loose family of notions.

This is a reasoning task: do not search for sources or cite literature; everything you need is in the cases above.
