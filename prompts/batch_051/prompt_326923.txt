I have spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and one argument keeps turning up in discussions of blame, resentment, and self-respect. I cannot accept its conclusion, but I have never been able to say exactly which step does the damage. Here is the argument as precisely as I can reconstruct it.

(1) There is nothing to forgive unless someone has actually wronged you.
(2) To forgive a wrongdoer is, at minimum, to set aside or substantially renounce the resentment one holds toward them for that wrong.
(3) If one renounces resentment toward a person who has not repented, offered amends, or committed to change, then one is treating their wrong as no longer counting against them.
(4) To treat a wrong as no longer counting against the person who committed it is to behave as though the wrong was not serious or should not be held to that person’s account.
(5) To behave that way toward a genuine wrong is to condone it or to excuse it, and doing either is a failure of self-respect.
(6) Therefore, forgiveness extended independently of the wrongdoer’s repentance, amends, or commitment to change is either not real forgiveness (it is condonation, excusing, or merely forgetting) or it is real forgiveness and morally objectionable.
(7) So unconditional forgiveness is either impossible or something no self-respecting person should give.

The conclusion seems far too strong, but each premise seems independently plausible. I want a diagnosis, not a name: identify the step at which the argument fails, explain why that step looked compulsory, and spell out what someone who rejects it is committed to—in particular, the exact distinction between forgiveness on one side and condoning, excusing, and forgetting on the other. I am inclined to keep (1) and (2), but I would rather learn where the argument genuinely goes wrong than have my preferred premises confirmed. I would also like to know whether any repaired version of the argument survives, for example one that concludes only that unconditional forgiveness can never be a strict duty.
