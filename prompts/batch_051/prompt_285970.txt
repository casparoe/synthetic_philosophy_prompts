Write a dialogue between two philosophers about Dennett’s two-bitser case—the cheap thermostat that we find it natural to describe as wanting the room warmer, or as believing it is too cold—and what that case shows about the distinction between original and derived intentionality. One character defends the Searlean view that a thermostat’s apparent intentionality is merely derived, borrowed from the purposes and interpretations of its designers and users, while the other defends the Dennettian view that all intentionality attribution is interpretation-relative and that the original/derived distinction marks no principled explanatory boundary. The characters should be equally competent and careful: neither may be a strawman, and each must respond to the other’s actual arguments rather than changing the subject or restating a script. They should press at least the following points: whether the two-bitser’s having beliefs is merely a harmless façon de parler, whether human beliefs are any less as-if once they are explained by evolutionary function or neurophysiology, and whether the original/derived distinction can be salvaged as a distinction about causal dependence rather than intrinsic nature. The dialogue should show genuine movement—each character should concede something when the other lands a point—but it should end without a forced synthesis, with each character stating the strongest unresolved consideration on the other side.
