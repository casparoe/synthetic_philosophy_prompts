I am on the planning committee of a housing-justice group that is protesting a city's abolition of its rental-assistance program, and we are trying to think through, before acting, whether a planned digital sit-in is covered by a city obstruction law. A city obstruction ordinance, enacted in 1962, reads:

No person shall obstruct, hinder, or unduly delay the ingress, egress, or free passage of persons or vehicles into, out of, or upon any public street, sidewalk, park, building, or other public facility.

Our plan is this. At a fixed hour, several hundred volunteers will run a script that repeatedly accesses the city's online portal for emergency housing assistance, opens an application, and abandons it. The portal is expected to fail, so that for hours no one can enter the virtual waiting room. We will not gather in any street or building, and no one will be physically blocked anywhere. We describe the action as a sit-in in the only public space left where the city meets its residents. The clause was drafted before the internet and says nothing about digital entry.

You are an expert in statutory interpretation and political philosophy. Please think through the problem carefully, begin by brainstorming several possible interpretations before settling on one, and give your answer in American English. Work through what each of the following approaches yields and where they diverge: (1) the plain text; (2) the purpose of the provision; (3) what the 1962 drafters would have said if asked; and (4) what a reasonable citizen today would understand the clause to cover. Then state which approach should control in this case and why. Finally, take up the political-obligation side. If the conduct falls outside the clause, can it still count as civil disobedience in the morally serious sense, or does disobedience require that a law actually be broken? If it falls inside the clause, does our insistence that the portal is a public facility express respect for law or the opposite? And does the justice of the underlying cause, even if real, affect the group's moral obligation to comply with the ordinance, or is that obligation the same regardless of the merits of the protest? You need not cite cases or scholarship; work from the clause and the concepts at stake.
