The view I want the case built around is this. Negative utilitarianism: only suffering has intrinsic negative value, and the right act minimizes it. Lexical threshold: there is a fixed intensity threshold T such that any episode of pain at or above T cannot be outweighed by any finite amount of pain below T, nor by any finite amount of pleasure; below T, pains and pleasures trade off against one another in an ordinary, roughly cardinal way (intensity-weighted duration, summed across persons). I want a pair of neighboring cases that isolates the discontinuity at T rather than merely illustrating the definition.

Construct two cases, identical except for one variable. In the base case, the view should require an action that lets one patient's pain peak just below T, because that averts a much larger aggregate of sub-threshold pain and lost pleasure spread across many other patients. In the variant case, the only change is that the same patient's pain would peak exactly at T — a clinically trivial difference — and the view should now forbid that same action and require its opposite, even though the aggregate of sub-threshold burden it allows is the same as in the base case and could be scaled up arbitrarily. Constraints: the setting must be medically realistic (an anesthetic protocol, a triage rule, or a formulary decision), everything must be finite, no utility monsters or infinite populations, and the lexical priority — not a stipulation that the severe pain is infinitely bad — has to do the work. After presenting the scenario, be explicit about how it meets each constraint, then derive the verdict in each case; say whether the threshold pain is being treated as comparable to the aggregate on a single scale with an infinite exchange rate or as incommensurable with it, and whether that distinction matters for the verdict; identify the precise feature of the case that produces the reversal and what happens if that feature is replaced with a steep but continuous weighting function; and finish with the strongest reply a defender of the lexical threshold could give and the strongest objection a critic could press about vague or noisy thresholds.
