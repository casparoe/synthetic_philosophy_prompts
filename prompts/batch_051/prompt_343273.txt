The scale argument for prioritizing insect welfare proceeds by aggregation: even if the probability that any given insect is sentient is low, the number of insects affected by human action is so vast that the expected number of sentient lives at stake dwarfs that of smaller animal populations. The standard form of the argument treats insect sentience as a single low-probability package, more or less uniform across the whole class. Now suppose the evidence develops lopsidedly: a few intensively studied taxa such as bees show enough flexible learning, motivated trade-offs, and injury-related behavioral modulation that they would move upward in any reasonable sentience-probability estimate, while the great majority of insect species remain behaviorally unstudied and the markers do not transfer cleanly across orders with very different nervous systems. Does the scale argument survive this development, and if so in what form? Which premise does it bear on, and is there a repaired version that replaces the single low probability with a distribution over taxa — and does that repair leave the practical conclusion stronger, weaker, or merely less calculable?
