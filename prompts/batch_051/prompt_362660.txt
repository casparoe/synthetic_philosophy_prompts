I am a novelist, and I recently had an exchange with my editor that has been bothering me for weeks. She read a draft chapter I considered the strongest in the book and called it "airless." When I asked what she meant, she waved the question away with the proverb "there's no accounting for taste," adding that her reaction was just her reaction and that I should not treat it as evidence about the chapter's actual quality. I accepted this at the time, but it has come to seem like a sweeping and quietly destructive claim: if one person's aesthetic response can never rationally bear on another person's aesthetic judgment, then the entire practice of soliciting feedback, and the institution of criticism itself, appears to be epistemically hollow.

I want you to take the proverb seriously and break it apart into its candidate meanings. What distinct, precise claims could "there's no accounting for taste" be used to assert, from the trivial to the radical? Candidate readings might include: that people in fact diverge in their aesthetic responses; that aesthetic disagreement cannot be settled by argument or evidence; that no one's taste is more authoritative than anyone else's; that aesthetic properties are not real features of the object; that sincere reports of aesthetic experience carry no evidential weight for others; and that aesthetic knowledge is somehow essentially first-personal. For each reading you identify, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false, and explain why. Finally, tell me which reading, if any, would actually license my editor's move of using the proverb to dismiss the idea that I could learn something about my chapter from her reaction, and which readings, even if true, would not get her there.
