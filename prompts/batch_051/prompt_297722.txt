I'm in a philosophy of mind and ethics class and I've landed on a computationalist view about moral status: what matters is which computations a system implements, not what it's made of. On my view a silicon system with the same causal-functional organization as a human brain would be just as sentient, and just as much a moral patient, and biological neurons matter only because of what they compute. I dont want the lazy objection that silicon cant feel; I want the best case that I'm wrong. What would actually have to be true for this view to be false? Give me the strongest defeaters—over-attribution cases (a giant lookup table, a whole economy wired up to mimic a brain, a rock with the right dispositional mappings), discoveries where biology does something the computational story can't capture, and the worry that no one can say what the right computation is without smuggling in consciousness. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a defender should honestly concede count against them, and tell me whether I've made the view so elastic it can't lose, and whether that matters.
