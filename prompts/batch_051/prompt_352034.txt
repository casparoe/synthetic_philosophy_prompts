I hold a broadly Ayerian view about survival after death, drawn from A. J. Ayer's discussion in Language, Truth and Logic, especially in the chapter The Self and the Common World. On this view, the claim that somebody survives bodily death is not an a priori claim about an immaterial soul but an empirical hypothesis: it is meaningful only if observations could count for or against it. Ayer's passage turns on the question of what difference such survival would make to experience; if the continuation cannot be cashed out in verifiable terms, talk of a soul's surviving is literally nonsensical, not false. The alternative to death is not a ghostly substance but some empirically specifiable continuation of experience or memory. I want an honest map of what would count against this position. Specifically, which developments in philosophy of language, philosophy of mind, or metaphysics since Ayer most seriously weaken it? Which exact commitments are load-bearing: the verification principle itself, the logical-construction account of the self, or the rejection of mental substance? Which defeaters would Ayer have conceded as counting against him, and which would he have dismissed as empty rather than merely unfavorable? Please rank the challenges from decisive to merely awkward, and say whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, whether that is a problem for a view whose entire point was to make survival an empirical question. Please answer in American English.
