Over the past few decades, two practices have grown up side by side in the same legal and therapeutic culture. Restorative justice programs, truth and reconciliation commissions, and victim-offender mediation routinely encourage victims to work toward forgiveness, sometimes before the offender has offered a full apology or shown any durable change, and practitioners often describe forgiveness as something the victim does primarily for their own sake: a release of resentment that aids the victim's healing regardless of what the offender does. At the same time, the surrounding criminal process treats remorse as deeply significant: judges sentence contrite defendants more leniently, parole boards scrutinize offenders for signs of genuine remorse, and a public apology offered before the offender has earned it through suffering or sustained change is widely dismissed as cheap or premature.

What theory of forgiveness, or what pair of theories, does this combination of practices presuppose? More specifically: does the restorative practice presuppose that forgiveness is unconditional and victim-directed, while the sentencing practice presupposes that what matters is a conditional, bilateral absolution the offender must earn? Can a single coherent theory reconcile them, perhaps by distinguishing forgiving as letting go of resentment from forgiving as restoring a relationship, or by distinguishing a private act of forgiveness from an interpersonal transaction? And does restorative justice, by treating forgiveness as part of a process victims are encouraged to undergo, quietly make forgiveness something like a duty, and would that conflict with the thought that forgiveness must be freely given if it is to count as forgiveness at all? Reconstruct the implicit theory or theories, mark where the practice is philosophically underdetermined rather than forced, and say what would have to change in either the restorative or the sentencing practice if we held instead that forgiveness is never owed and has no intrinsic conditions beyond the victim's free choice.
