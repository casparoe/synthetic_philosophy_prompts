I am the senior editor of a monthly history magazine. Every issue I have to sign off on feature essays and opening paragraphs that make single-cause claims about major historical events—sentences like "the printing press caused the Reformation" or "the assassination of Franz Ferdinand caused the First World War." These claims will be printed, and a later correction costs us credibility, so I need a procedure I can trust under deadline pressure. I have about twenty minutes per claim and cannot do new archival research. I must be able to explain my decision to the writer in plain language, and I must be able to point to the precise step at which a stronger claim was rejected.

Last week I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about that second example. He kept insisting that because the war had deep structural causes—alliance systems, mobilization timetables, imperial rivalry—no single spark could count as a cause, while I found myself defending the intuition that some events genuinely make a difference even when the stage is set. We both ran out of principled things to say. I want you to turn what philosophers have established about causation, overdetermination, and explanatory selection in history into a procedure I can run myself, not a one-off judgment about that particular argument.

You are an expert in the philosophy of causation and historical explanation. Please think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the distinctions and failure modes that a serious procedure would have to respect; then present the procedure itself.

The procedure should be a sequence of questions I ask in order. For each question, state what would count as a sufficient answer, and state whether the step results in: continue, rewrite the claim in a specified way, or stop and hand the decision to me as an ineliminable editorial judgment. It must take seriously the problem of overdetermination: an event can have multiple causes, each of which might have sufficed, so a naive counterfactual test ("would Y have happened without X?") returns "yes" even for causes we would ordinarily count. It should let me distinguish a genuine trigger from a standing condition, and distinguish claims of the forms "X was a cause of Y," "X was the cause of Y," "X triggered Y," "X was a necessary condition for Y," "X made Y more likely," and "X was the decisive factor in Y." Tell me which of these distinctions must survive the simplification and which can be dropped without corrupting the procedure.

Ground the procedure in the philosophical accounts you judge most relevant—counterfactual tests and their limits under overdetermination, INUS-style conditions, the contrastive structure of explanatory why-questions, the distinction between abnormal changes and normal background conditions, and anything else that earns its keep. Do not include a bibliography or reading list; the value lies entirely in the operating procedure.

After laying out the procedure, run it on the following three claims, showing enough of each step to make the final recommendation intelligible:

(a) "The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand caused the First World War."

(b) "The Black Death caused the end of serfdom in Western Europe."

(c) "Rosa Parks's arrest triggered the Montgomery bus boycott."

At least one of these should produce a recommendation I will find inconvenient—either forcing me to weaken a punchy claim or rejecting the strong claim on the grounds a writer would actually offer. Finally, tell me honestly what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, which cases it cannot settle, and where the remaining judgment calls really live.
