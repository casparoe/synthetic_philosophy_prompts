I'm a novelist, but this is me wearing my essayist hat. I keep coming back to The Truman Show for a piece about the simulation argument, specifically because it isn't The Matrix. Everyone uses The Matrix for that, and The Truman Show seems to me both closer to what it would actually feel like to be in a designed world and more obviously at odds with the actual argument. Truman's world is a giant physical set full of paid actors, not a computed world, and he's the only one being decieved; everyone else knows exactly what's going on. Yet the film still dramatises something real about how you'd catch a designed world from the inside — the seams, the too-timely product placements, the boat hitting the sky. I'd like a close reading of the film through Bostrom's simulation argument proper, the trilemma about ancestor-simulations, not simulation as a loose metaphor. Where does the film genuinely illustrate the argument, where does it trade on a confusion between a physical set and a computed simulation, or between deceiving one person and simulating billions of minds, and do you think the film knows the difference? Point me at specific scenes. I'd also like a line on what the film adds to the philosophical debate that the papers alone miss. And could you write the whole answer in British English — the place I'm writing it for is particular about that, and I'd rather not transpose the spellings myself later.
