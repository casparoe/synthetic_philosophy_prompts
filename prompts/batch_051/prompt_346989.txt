I am drawn to a Gödel-style modal ontological argument, for a reason I want to state clearly: it has the systematic virtues I normally trust. In outline, it defines God as a being that has all positive properties, takes any positive property to be possibly instantiated, and counts necessary existence as a positive property. Because such a being would exist necessarily if it existed at all, the argument moves from the mere possibility that God exists to actual existence — in S5, what is possibly necessary is necessary. The only live question seems to be whether the concept is coherent, and settling that asks for consistency rather than evidence.

Yet I cannot shake a firm conviction that no definition or modal axiom can settle whether something of that kind really exists. The proof turns the coherence of a concept into actual existence in every possible world, and that feels too cheap even before I can locate the bad step. My worry is not that any one premise is obviously false; it is that the argument is a machine for manufacturing existence out of definitions, and a derivation this easy should not resolve a substantive existential question.

I am not asking which side wins. Present both sides at their strongest and give me a negotiated verdict: what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what would it take for the argument to be the thing that goes, and is there a reading of 'possibility' or 'positive property' on which the argument establishes something real but less than God's existence?
