In my epistemology course we have been reading the Meno, and I have been puzzling over an inconsistency in how my own university evaluates work. On mathematics exams, a correct final answer with no shown reasoning receives little or no credit; the student must exhibit the derivation. The same institution runs multiple-choice and short-answer exams elsewhere in which a correct answer earns full credit regardless of whether it came from understanding or from a lucky guess. What view of why knowledge is better than true belief does the show-your-work requirement presuppose? Meno's challenge is that a true belief seems to guide action just as well as knowledge, so the extra demand for justification or understanding needs a special explanation. Does the grading practice presuppose that justification has value beyond the correctness of the output — perhaps reliability for future performance, or a kind of epistemic responsibility — and is that consistent with the multiple-choice practice? I want a reconstruction of the implicit epistemology behind each practice, a statement of whether they presuppose rival answers to the Meno problem or can be made coherent within a single view, and an account of what grading would have to change if the institution accepted a purely instrumental view on which true belief is worth exactly as much as knowledge.
