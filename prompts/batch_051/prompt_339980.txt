I have a friend who serves as an informal probability referee for our research group. When a member needs a defensible number for a singular claim—the probability that this patient has the condition, that this proposal will succeed, that this shipment is defective—she looks at the available reference classes and produces a judgment. I have kept a record of her rulings, all made under the same ground rules and all concerning the same medical condition. Case 1: a patient known only to be an adult in a city where 8 percent of adults have the condition; she gave 0.08. Case 2: a patient known to have the characteristic symptom pattern, a class with a 43 percent base rate; she gave 0.43 rather than falling back to the citywide 8 percent. Case 3: the same symptom pattern plus no recent travel to a particular region, a class with a 51 percent rate; she gave 0.51 rather than the broader symptom class. Case 4: symptoms plus fatigue, where the broad class has a 19 percent rate and the narrower class, adding a positive rapid test, has a 72 percent rate despite the smallest sample so far; she gave 0.72. Case 5: a patient in a broad clinical class with a 30 percent rate and a narrower subclass—adults whose postal code begins with 7—with a 10 percent rate; she gave 0.30, saying the subclass was gerrymandered. Case 6: a patient in a large, well-run registry class with a 78 percent rate and in a smaller clinical series with a 35 percent rate and wide confidence intervals; she gave 0.78, saying the narrower series was too noisy to override the registry.

Next week's patient falls under four reference classes at once: women aged 40 to 49, where a very large population dataset puts the rate at 12 percent; patients with a particular symptom profile, where a well-conducted clinical study of moderate size puts it at 61 percent; residents of a particular county, where the largest dataset of all puts the rate at 4 percent but the low figure is thought to reflect systematic underdiagnosis rather than lower true prevalence; and carriers of a genetic variant, the narrowest class, where a small but methodologically sound cohort study puts the rate at 33 percent with a confidence interval wider than the symptom study's but not obviously disqualifying. Predict the probability she will give if she is being consistent with her record. Identify the rule or rules she is implicitly running—for example, use the narrowest non-gerrymandered class unless its estimate is too noisy, use the class with the best bias-variance tradeoff, or use the most reliable unbiased data—say what each live interpretation predicts for the new patient, identify the earlier case that is the closest analog, and specify one further case whose verdict would cleanly separate the remaining interpretations.
