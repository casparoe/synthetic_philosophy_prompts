Rereading the "Lordship and Bondage" stretch of Hegel's Phenomenology of Mind, I hit a sentence that felt weirdly like something from formal epistemology, and I cant decide whether the resemblance is deep or just me pattern-matching.

Baillie's translation, §184:

"Each is the mediating term to the other, through which each mediates and unites itself with itself; and each is to itself and to the other an immediate self-existing reality, which, at the same time, exists thus for itself only through this mediation. They recognize themselves as mutually recognizing one another."

What it reminded me of is common knowledge in epistemic game theory: p is common knowledge iff everyone knows p, everyone knows everyone knows p, and so on. The Hegel line "they recognize themselves as mutually recognizing one another" looks like the same regress. Its not enough for me to recognize you and you to recognize me; the mutual recognition itself has to be recognized, and that seems to iterate — recognition of the recognition has to sit inside the recognition, or the thing bottoms out into two people treating each other as objects. A few paragraphs earlier (§182) he says action from one side alone would be useless, since the thing can only come about through both.

Is this an actual identity of structure, or does common knowledge only capture the iterated part while Hegel is after something more practical and risky? What would have to be true of Hegelian recognition for it to work like a common-knowledge fixed point, and conversely, what does the common-knowledge reading predict here that the text plainly rejects? My hunch is it would predict that transparent mutual attitudes are enough to constitute recognition, whereas Hegel thinks each has to stake its life to prove it — that recognition has to be enacted, not just known. But I want to know whether that difference kills the parallel or just limits how far it transfers.
