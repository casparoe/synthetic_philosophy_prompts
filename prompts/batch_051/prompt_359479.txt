I'm in an upper-level seminar on transitional justice, and we have spent several weeks on the familiar defense of amnesty in truth-commission settings. The argument, roughly, is this: in a fragile transition, attempting full criminal prosecutions against members of the outgoing regime may reignite conflict or invite a coup, and even where prosecutions are attempted they are likely to be selective or to fail for lack of admissible evidence. Conditional amnesty — full testimony in exchange for legal immunity — is then defended as a second-best arrangement because it purchases the one thing that would otherwise be lost: the truth about what happened, delivered in the perpetrators' own words, together with official acknowledgment of the victims. On standard readings, the argument gets much of its force from the assumption that perpetrator testimony is the scarce resource, and that without the incentive of amnesty the truth will simply never emerge.

I want to stress-test this argument against a development it was not built to handle. Suppose a future transition occurs in a place where the atrocities are documented extensively, though not completely, by means that do not require any perpetrator to cooperate: mobile-phone video, satellite imagery, leaked security-force and state records, border and identity databases, forensic DNA work, and the data trail left by the units and commanders who did the planning. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that a truth commission could reconstruct much of the pattern of killings, disappearances, and command responsibility from these materials alone, without offering a single amnesty in exchange for testimony. Does the amnesty-for-truth argument survive this change? Which premise is actually under pressure — the claim that truth is scarce, the claim that amnesty is the only feasible way to obtain it, or the claim that securing truth is the main justification for amnesty rather than securing a stable peace? Is there a repaired version of the argument that says something weaker but still important, for instance drawing a distinction between documenting events and eliciting acknowledgment from the perpetrators themselves? Or does the development remove the central philosophical rationale for amnesty and leave only a bare pragmatic argument about stability?

When you answer, I would also like you to say how confident you are in your verdict and to single out the point you are least sure of, so I can see where the argument might break the other way.
