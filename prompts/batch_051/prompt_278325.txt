I'm putting together a short handout for a reading group on practical rationality, and I keep tripping over the fact that 'choosing without a preference' covers several distinct claims that are easy to run together. Take the usual story: an ass sits exactly halfway between two identical bales of hay, with no reason to go left rather than right. Does it starve? Does it just pick one? Or is the example rigged from the start? I'd like you to build a decision tree that sorts someone into a position here, built from the logical space rather than from a list of -isms. The splitting questions should be things like whether a rationally complete tie is even possible, whether rationality has to settle the choice or may leave it indeterminate, whether an agent's choosing can itself reveal a preference, whether the will (or just agency) can close a gap that reasons cannot, and whether the setup is unfair because two real alternatives are never strictly identical. The questions should be answerable by someone who has only heard the Buridan example and hasn't read the decision-theory litterature, so don't use labels as the prompts. The leaves should include at least: a deflationary revealed-preference-ish view on which observed choice just is preference, so the tie dissolves; a view on which the ass is genuinely indifferent and any old tie-breaker (a coin, a habit, a twitch) is rationally fine; a reasons-based view on which the agent must find or create some relevant difference to act on; a brute/active-choice view on which the will can settle things without adding a reason; an incommensurability view on which the bales are not equal but incomparable; and, if you think it has earned a seat, a view on which the ass starving is the honest implication of a hard determinism about rational choice. Also flag where your tree can't seperate positions that people might want kept distinct.

Could you write the answer in British English, with these sections in this order: 'How to use the tree' (two or three sentences: audience and branching logic); 'The questions' (the tree itself, roughly six questions, each branch labelled by the claim that splits it and each question phrased without jargon); 'The leaves' (one neutral name per leaf, plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment); 'Empty boxes' (combinations the logic allows but almost nobody defends, with a one-sentence diagnosis: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored); 'What the tree cannot separate' (which positions collapse together given these questions, and whether that matters); and 'Confidence note' (a short paragraph saying how confident you are in the tree overall and singling out the single branch or diagnosis you are least sure of).
