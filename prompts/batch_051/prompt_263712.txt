I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so maybe I'm just pattern-matching, but I've been using a term I made up and I want it checked: the "narrative locus of control." The idea is that the Stoic dichotomy of control — the line between what's up to you and what isn't — isn't a fixed fact about human agency. It's something a story posits. The same event switches sides depending on the genre. In Greek tragedy, a hero's rage is basically a god-sent external; in a modern psychological novel the same rage is the character's own judgment and thus their responsibility. Loosing your fortune in a Victorian novel is a moral catastrophe the character gets blamed for; in a naturalist novel it's heredity and circumstance, entirely outside them; in a Stoic-flavored self-help story it's an indifferent that can't touch their virtue.

The stronger claim is that we don't first discover where the line falls and then write stories around it. Stories train us where to draw the line. The modern Stoicism revival treats the dichotomy as a timeless psychological fact; I think it's a genre convention — specifically, the convention of a story told so that the protagonist's only real stakes are their own judgments and choices. Even Epictetus's famous division, judgments and desires on the inside, body, property, reputation on the outside, looks to me like a choice of protagonist, the philosopher as hero, not a neutral discovery about the will.

I want an honest assessment, not encouragement. Is this a real distinction or just a metaphor? Has someone already worked it out under another name — I know about narrative identity, but I mean specifically the normative boundary between what's in and out of a person's control and how genre moves it. And where does it actually break? The obvious objection is that I'm confusing what authors choose to depict with what agents can really control: characters in a tragedy aren't actually unfree just because the genre says so. I don't know if that's fatal or just uncomfortable.
