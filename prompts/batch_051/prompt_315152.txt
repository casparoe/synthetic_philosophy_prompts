A friend and I have been going back and forth about the Great Filter: the idea that somewhere on the path from prebiotic chemistry to a galaxy-colonizing technological civilization there is at least one step so improbable that it explains why the sky looks empty. To sharpen the argument, I proposed four criteria for counting a particular step as a plausible candidate for the main filter. (i) Necessity: the step must sit on every, or nearly every, plausible route from nonliving chemistry to a spacefaring civilization, with no easy workaround that bypasses it while leaving the rest of the road intact. (ii) Principled rarity: there must be a structural, mechanistic, or energetic reason to think the step is genuinely improbable per habitable planet, not merely a one-off accident that our single data point tempts us to overread. (iii) Explanatory fit: conditional on the step's being rare, the observed absence of technosignatures should come out likely, even if earlier steps occur often. (iv) Independence from contrived coordination: the step's rarity should not depend on assuming that every later civilization does the same self-defeating thing.

The candidate case we have been fighting about is eukaryogenesis: the evolutionary transition from prokaryotic cells to eukaryotic cells — cells with a nucleus, an internal system of membranes, and mitochondria descended from an engulfed bacterium. On Earth the mitochondrial endosymbiosis appears to have happened once; all known eukaryotes descend from a single common ancestor that already had these features, and the transition may have taken a billion years or more after the first prokaryotes. Once it happened, it opened the space that complex multicellular life actually occupies: every large, tissue-differentiated organism is a eukaryote, and there is a well-known bioenergetic argument that prokaryotes cannot support the genome sizes and per-gene energy budgets that complex multicellular life requires, which would make the eukaryote package close to indispensable for intelligence. But the case is not clean. Maybe complex multicellularity, or technological intelligence, does not strictly require exactly this package; maybe a different cellular architecture would have worked; and maybe the fact that it happened only once on Earth is less impressive than it looks, since we would not know about extinct stem-eukaryote experiments or alternative routes that never got going.

Does eukaryogenesis qualify as the Great Filter under these four criteria? Go through the criteria one by one and say where the verdict is a clear yes, a clear no, or genuinely underdetermined. Where the answer is no or unclear, say whether that is a flaw in the criteria or a limitation of the case, and whether any of the four criteria should be revised in light of the case. Finally, say how confident you are in your overall assessment, and single out the point you are least sure of.
