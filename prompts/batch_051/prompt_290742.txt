Let's use the 1998 collapse of Long-Term Capital Management as a test case for Kelly betting and geometric rationality. The background: LTCM was a highly leveraged hedge fund run by people with serious quantitative chops—Myron Scholes and Robert Merton were involved, both Nobel laureates in economics—and it blew up when Russia defaulted and its convergence trades all moved the wrong way at once, before a rescue was brokered by the Federal Reserve. Treat the episode as a bet-sizing problem. Spell out the mapping: who or what is the bettor, what the repeated bets really were, what the Kelly criterion would have said about the fraction of wealth to stake, and how geometric rationality (maximizing expected log wealth, since fractions of wealth compound) would have handled the leverage and the fat tails differently. Then reverse the lens: which features of the episode does this story explain cleanly, which features does it miss or distort (liquidity, counterparty risk, the fact that they were managing other people's money), and does LTCM ultimately confirm Kelly-style reasoning about ruin or expose a limit of it? Use American English.
