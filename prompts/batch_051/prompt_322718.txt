I'm giving a paper next month defending the claim that the deserving/undeserving poor distinction can't do the work welfare policy asks of it. My argument, roughly: deliberate benefit fraud and voluntary worklessness are small in absolute terms; sanctions, workfare, and conditionality mostly catch bureaucratic errors and people too ill, disabled, or overburdened to comply; so out-of-work benefits should be near-unconditional, even for people who turn down available work. The Q&A audience is a workshop of social policy researchers and political philosophers, nearly all committed welfare-state supporters, who accept some reciprocity principle (able-bodied recievers owe something back) and who think the real problem is maintaining public support for the welfare state. Give me a prep sheet: the eight-ish objections I'll actually get from this room — not the ones I'd get from libertarians or UBI evangelists — ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased in the audience's own vocabulary, with a rough sense of who raises each, a two-minute reply to each, and a note on where my reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
