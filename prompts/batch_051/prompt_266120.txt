I have collected my own verdicts on seven cases about when a court or another institution may treat someone adversely on the strength of statistical evidence, and I want to know whether a single principle lies behind them. For the criminal cases, assume the standard is proof beyond a reasonable doubt; for the civil cases, the ordinary preponderance standard; for the non-court cases, whatever standard the institution itself uses.

1. The blue bus. A car is sideswiped by a bus that flees the scene. The only evidence is that the Blue Bus Company operates four out of every five buses on that route at that hour. I would not make the company pay.
2. The gatecrasher. A rodeo promoter has a list of the 1,000 people who attended, but only 499 paid; the rest climbed the fence. He sues one attendee picked at random. The odds that this attendee failed to pay are just over half, so the civil standard is met. I would still not make him pay.
3. The prison yard. A guard is beaten to death in view of 1,000 prisoners, 999 of whom joined the beating while one refused and sat apart. No one can tell which one refused. I would not convict a particular prisoner of the assault, even though any given prisoner is 99.9 percent likely to have participated.
4. The DNA match. A defendant's DNA matches the crime-scene sample, and the analyst testifies that the chance a randomly chosen person would match is one in three hundred million. I would convict, provided the sample was handled and tested properly.
5. The lone eyewitness. A single witness picks the defendant from a lineup, with no corroboration and only moderate confidence. I would convict, though less comfortably than in the DNA case.
6. The life table. An insurer prices a policy for a sixty-year-old applicant using nothing but actuarial tables for his age, sex, and smoking status. I would allow it.
7. The bail score. A judge sets bail mainly on the strength of a validated statistical risk score for failure to appear. I am genuinely torn.

Is there one principle that captures these verdicts, or a small set of principles that divides the cases cleanly? State it precisely, apply it to my list, and say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why. Then apply it to a further case I have not considered. Please also say explicitly what argument or additional case would most likely change your mind about the principle you propose, and where you think your own reasoning on this is most likely to fail.
