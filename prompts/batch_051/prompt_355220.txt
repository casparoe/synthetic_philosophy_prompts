I chair the clinical ethics committee at a mid-sized teaching hospital. Our institution permits licensed staff to decline participation in care to which they have a documented moral objection, provided the refusal does not delay, impair, or deprioritize any patient's care. Until now, the committee has dealt with a single clinician declining a single task. We recently received our first proposal for what the physicians involved call a "moral trade," and I expect it will not be the last. I need a standing procedure for evaluating proposals of this kind.

The case that prompted this request involves two hospitalists, Dr. Reyes and Dr. Salim, who both care for patients near the end of life. Dr. Reyes regards withdrawing mechanical ventilation as gravely wrong and withdrawing artificially provided nutrition and hydration as only mildly wrong; Dr. Salim holds the reverse pair of convictions. Under this week's on-call assignment, Dr. Reyes is responsible for a patient whose family and care team have agreed that ventilation should be withdrawn, and Dr. Salim is responsible for a patient whose family and care team have agreed that artificial nutrition should be withdrawn. Each has requested a conscientious accommodation. Back-up coverage is thin this week, so the two physicians propose to swap patients: Dr. Reyes would carry out the withdrawal of artificial nutrition, which she regards as mildly wrong, and Dr. Salim would carry out the withdrawal of ventilation, which he regards as mildly wrong. Each patient receives the same intervention as originally planned; each physician avoids performing the act he or she regards as gravely wrong, while still performing an act he or she regards as wrong to some degree.

The committee is uneasy about the proposal but has had difficulty articulating why. On one hand, the swap appears to honor each clinician's most serious moral commitments at no evident cost to patients. On the other hand, our objection policy treats each clinician's act as that clinician's own act: if Dr. Reyes withdraws artificial nutrition, she performs an act she judges wrong, and the fact that doing so spares Dr. Salim from a graver wrong is a benefit to Dr. Salim's conscience, not a feature of what Dr. Reyes herself does. There is also a worry about making conscientious scruples fungible, as though a serious objection were an item to be brokered rather than a limit on what one is willing to do.

Philosophically, I understand the issue as one about agent-relative constraints. In the kind of proposal I am describing, each agent performs an act he or she regards as less wrong in order to spare another agent from performing an act that other agent regards as more wrong. On the standard view of agent-relative constraints, each agent's constraint binds that agent regardless of what its violation would prevent others from doing: the fact that Dr. Reyes's violating a lesser constraint prevents Dr. Salim from violating a greater one does not, by itself, change the character of Dr. Reyes's act. The proposal's appeal is that the pair of violations looks collectively efficient, since each agent violates only the lesser of his or her own constraints and thereby spares the other from a greater violation. What I need is a translation of that point into policy: how much of this collective efficiency a conscientious-objection scheme may legitimately count, and which features of a proposed trade must, may, and must not enter the decision.

I need a reusable decision procedure our committee can apply to any proposed moral trade, not just a ruling on this one. It must be usable in thirty minutes by a committee of physicians, nurses, a social worker, a chaplain, and a lawyer, none of whom is a philosopher and none of whom will welcome a lecture on deontology. The stakes are high: patient care, staff retention, legal exposure, and the institutional meaning of conscience. The procedure should:

- identify which features of a proposed trade are morally relevant and which are distractions;
- distinguish what the underlying theory requires, permits, and forbids from what remains a judgment call;
- specify which proposals may be approved administratively, which must go to the full committee or the medical director, and which should be rejected outright;
- state what the procedure cannot do, and where it is most likely to yield an answer the committee finds uncomfortable.

Then apply the procedure to three cases:

(1) the ventilation-withdrawal-for-nutrition-withdrawal swap described above;

(2) a proposed swap in which a much-senior surgeon offers to take over a case he regards as mildly wrong in exchange for a first-year resident performing a case she regards as mildly wrong; the resident is visibly reluctant, appears to be agreeing only to avoid conflict with her attending, and could have declined the case outright;

(3) a proposed swap in which two clinicians each perform a procedure they regard as mildly wrong, but the counterpart they are trying to protect is not a colleague's graver constraint: it is the on-time completion of the operating list and the avoidance of an overtime payment.

For each case, state what a well-designed procedure would conclude, which step of the analysis does the real work, and what residual risk or discomfort the conclusion leaves unaddressed.
