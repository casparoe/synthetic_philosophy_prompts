I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I'm responsible for a content-classification taxonomy at my company. We have a single top-level "religion" category, and it produces recurring disputes. Three cases keep surfacing: a Theravada Buddhist meditation video, an instructional series on Confucian ancestor rites, and a local "secular congregation" that meets weekly for singing and shared moral reflection. Depending on which rule we apply, the three land in different places.

I've read around the conceptual debate as an amateur, and I believe I've collected the standard menu of boundary-drawing strategies:

1. Substantive: treat something as religion just in case it involves belief in supernatural beings, spirits, or gods.
2. Experiential: treat something as religion just in case the practice or tradition centers on cultivating and interpreting transformative first-person encounters with something taken to be sacred or higher.
3. Functional: treat something as religion just in case it marks off certain things as sacred or set apart and unites people around those things into a moral community (the Durkheimian line).
4. Symbolic: treat something as religion just in case a system of symbols supplies a picture of the general order of existence, carries a distinctive sense of unshakeable factuality, and shapes people's moods and motivations (the Geertzian line).
5. Family resemblance: give up on necessary and sufficient conditions; classify by whether an item shares enough of a shifting list of features—ritual, worship, scripture, clergy, moral code, founding narrative, institutional continuity.
6. Overriding commitment: treat something as religion just in case it functions as what a person or community holds as of unconditional and ultimate importance (the Tillichian line).
7. Cognitive kind: treat something as religion just in case the relevant representations issue from the ordinary cognitive machinery behind agency detection, mind-reading, and related sensitivities, so that "religion" tracks a rough natural kind of cognition rather than a definitional kind.
8. Deflationary: stop trying to draw a principled boundary, because "religion" as a folk category is a modern Western construction that gets projected onto traditions such as Buddhist meditation and Confucian rites even though they do not fit the model it was built for.

I believe this is close to the full standard menu, so I am not simply asking you to recall a well-known strategy I left out.

What I want is the strongest additional boundary-drawing strategy you can produce: one not on the list above and not merely a rewording, special case, or combination of items already on it, and one that could actually guide a classification decision in practice. Defend the claim that it is genuinely additional. If you conclude no such strategy exists, state that and defend it instead. Then (a) say whether your additional strategy, or the absence of one, changes the verdict about whether "religion" should remain a single top-level category, and (b) tell me which argument or consideration would most likely make you reverse that verdict, or where your reasoning about the category is least secure.
