I am a novelist. While gathering material for my work I keep a notebook of the striking coincidences people report to me, and I have learned that my own intuition about what such stories indicate is unreliable; I would rather have a calibrated probability, with the reasoning shown.

Consider this report. A friend of mine attended a dinner party of sixteen adults, all between their mid-thirties and fifty and fairly evenly spread across that range. Two guests who met that evening discovered they had been born on the same day and in the same year — not merely the same month and day. The story is being recounted to me, in good faith, as a near-impossible event that suggests some invisible connection between the two guests.

I would like your credence in the following proposition: nothing beyond ordinary chance and the ordinary ways in which rare events get noticed and retold is needed to explain the report of this shared full birthdate; neither a hidden link between those two particular guests nor a meaningful distortion of the memory is needed. Answer in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion:

1. Your estimate that a single dinner party with this size and age structure contains at least one pair sharing a full birthdate, with the one-line calculation behind that number.
2. Your estimate that, across the social events of an ordinary year or two of this friend's life, at least one anecdote of comparable strikingness would arise by chance.
3. Your adjustment for the fact that a shared birthdate is only one of many features whose recurrence would have been reported as a meaningful coincidence, such as a shared hometown, school, first name, or rare enthusiasm.
4. Your probability that the report has been sharpened or improved in memory despite being offered in good faith.
5. Your final credence in the proposition, a statement of how items 1 through 4 combine to produce it, and which single item would most move the final number if revised.
