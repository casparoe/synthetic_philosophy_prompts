I've been compiling the standard arguments for the conclusion that the linguistic data available to a child underdetermine the grammar the child actually acquires, and that acquisition therefore requires an innately constrained language faculty rather than domain-general learning from experience alone. So far I have the following six:

1. The structure-dependence argument. In forming yes/no questions, children generalize over hierarchical phrase structure rather than over linear order. From a statement such as The man who is tall is happy, a simpler linear rule would move the first auxiliary to the front and yield Is the man who tall is happy?; children instead converge on the hierarchical generalization that yields Is the man who is tall happy? The positive data do not by themselves force that choice.

2. The no-negative-evidence argument. Children receive little or no systematic correction of ungrammatical utterances, and what correction they do receive tends to target truthfulness, social appropriateness, or pronunciation rather than grammar. Nevertheless they converge on a grammar that excludes indefinitely many strings they have never heard marked as unacceptable.

3. The convergence argument. Acquisition is fast, uniform across children, and robust across wide differences in the quantity and quality of the input. The grammars children end up with are far more similar to one another than the variable, fragmentary input would predict.

4. The formal-learnability argument. Classical results in learning theory show that, for classes of languages rich enough to include the possibilities the child must choose among, the correct grammar cannot be identified from positive examples alone unless the hypothesis space is constrained in advance. The child's convergence therefore points to an innately restricted hypothesis space.

5. The argument from constraints that appear without instruction. Children respect subtle grammatical constraints — for example, on when a pronoun can take an earlier noun phrase as its antecedent, and on which embedded structures an interrogative element may be extracted from — although these constraints are not explicitly taught and the data that would reveal them are sparse in ordinary speech.

6. The creolization argument. When children are exposed to a pidgin, a rudimentary and unstable contact system without full grammatical structure, they go beyond that input and produce a creole with a systematic grammar. The grammatical knowledge they converge on cannot simply be read off the data they received.

I am looking for one more argument for the same conclusion — that the input is too impoverished to determine the grammar, so that innate structure must make up the difference — that is genuinely different from these six and is not a restatement, special case, strengthening, or combination of any of them. If you think I have missed a standard argument, give me that one and say what it adds. If you think the list already covers the standard arguments, give me the strongest new argument you can construct, distinguish it clearly from the six above, and be honest about how much work it actually does.
