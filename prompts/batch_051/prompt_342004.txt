A data scientist I work with keeps telling me the no-free-lunch theorem is basically Hume's problem of induction with the math done properly. Her version goes: Hume showed you can't get from past regularities to future ones without some principle like the future resembling the past, and Wolpert showed that, averaged uniformly over all possible target functions, every learning algorithm has the same expected error — so any edge you get comes from the inductive bias you brought in. Therefore, she says, induction isn't a philosophical problem; it's a design variable. Just chosse your priors and move on, and anyone still worrying about Hume is confused about what the theorem already settled. I buy that the analogy has some juice, but I doubt it can carry all that. Can you lay out exactly what maps onto what between the NFL machinery and the problem of induction, where the picture breaks down or quietly imports things Hume never said, which disanalogies matter for her conclusion and which don't, and whether the conclusion — that the theorem dissolves the problem of induction into choosing your priors — survives the disanalogies? If there's a better analogy for what NFL actually shows, I'd like that too.
