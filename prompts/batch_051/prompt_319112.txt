I have been putting together a discussion for a small reading group on the St Petersburg paradox, and I want to move past the stock response that Bernoulli's logarithmic utility function dissolves the puzzle. I would like a worked case in which that response is deliberately disabled, so that the group is forced to confront the difference between utility that is merely concave and unbounded and utility that is bounded above.

Please construct a St Petersburg-style gamble G as follows. A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads; if the first heads occurs on the nth toss (n = 1, 2, 3, ...), the player receives a finite payoff x_n, which occurs with probability 2^{-n}. Choose the payoff sequence x_n so that all three of the following hold. First, the expected monetary value of G is infinite, so that an expected-value maximiser will accept any finite entry fee. Second, with initial wealth w > 0, the expected utility of G under the logarithmic utility function u(x) = ln x, computed over final wealth, is infinite for every entry fee f < w, so that the standard Bernoulli resolution fails: the logarithmic player is, like the expected-money player, willing to pay any fee up to her current wealth. Third, for some utility function that is bounded above and strictly concave (for example, u(x) = 1 − exp(−x)), the expected utility of G is finite, and there is a finite entry-fee threshold above which the player rejects G.

After constructing G, demonstrate that it meets each specification by working through the verdicts of the three rules and showing that the relevant series behave as claimed. Then identify the precise structural feature of the payoff sequence x_n that is doing the work—the feature that pushes logarithmic expected utility to infinity while leaving the bounded utility expectation finite—and explain why it, rather than anything incidental, produces the split. Next, remove that feature: state what happens if the payoff sequence is changed to the ordinary doubling one, x_n = 2^n, and say whether the case then reverts to the textbook St Petersburg situation. End with one paragraph in which you act as counsel for the defence of the logarithmic-utility resolution: explain how a defender would most plausibly resist your example, and say whether that objection would also undermine the original paradox or only this variant. The answer should be written in British English.
