I'm a retired biochemist, and now that I've got time I've been reading the stuff I never made space for: philosophy of mind, behavioral psych, decision theory. A question that keeps nipping at me is whether training yourself to reason is more like training for a marathon or more like learning a martial art. The martial-art analogy says you drill katas that look nothing like a real fight, but the drilling builds components that are supposed to transfer to the fight anyway. So I've been kicking around the following argument, and I can't tell if it's sound or if I've built a trap for myself. Tell me which step, if any, fails.

(1) If rationality can be trained like a martial art, then the training has to work through transfer: the thing you build in practice has to show up later in real, unpracticed decision-making.
(2) Transfer has limits. A trained capacity carries over to a new setting only when the practice task and the real task share the same kinds of elements that the training actually modified: same underlying structure, not just a glancing resemblance.
(3) What standard rationality drills mostly modify is the ability to recognize and work with textbook formats: a clean base-rate vignette, a highlighted sunk-cost trap, a syllogism with the fallacy sitting in the second premise.
(4) Real decisions almost never arrive in those formats. They're messy, time-pressured, and tangled up with social stakes, so the elements that matter in them are not shared with the drills.
(5) Therefore the drills can't make you meaningfully better at real decisions, and the martial-art picture of rationality training is mostly a training illusion.

My lab instincts says the weak point is somewhere in the slide from (3) to (4): a format isn't the same as an element, and maybe what gets trained is something portable, like the habit of asking where the base rate is. But I cant make the objection precise, and I worry I'm just being defensive about a dead idea. Which step fails, and how would you reformulate the argument to make it valid? Or is it valid as it stands?
