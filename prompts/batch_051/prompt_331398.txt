I have recently been appointed to a university committee that reviews proposals for off-world settlement and resource extraction. The chair has a long record of rulings, but she rarely articulates the principle behind them; she insists that the cases speak for themselves. I need to anticipate how she will rule on a proposal we will consider next month, and I would be grateful for a careful reconstruction of the standard she is implicitly applying.

Her past rulings are as follows.

1. A proposal for automated mining of a metallic asteroid with no atmosphere, no water ice, and no reasonable prospect of biology. Verdict: approve.
2. A proposal for a permanent settlement near a lunar polar crater, supplied by locally mined water ice, on a body that all available evidence indicates is sterile. Verdict: approve.
3. A proposal for a self-sustaining settlement on a Mars-analog planet that had been surveyed for over a decade by several independent biological assays, none of which found native organisms; the proposal included the standard planetary-protection protocols. Verdict: approve.
4. A proposal for an exploratory landing on an exomoon whose atmosphere and surface chemistry indicate a rich native biosphere, including mobile animal-like organisms that avoid noxious stimuli and live in complex social groups; the landing would knowingly introduce hardy Earth microbes into their habitat. Verdict: reject.
5. A proposal for a drilling mission to a moon with a subsurface ocean that may contain a biosphere of entirely unknown complexity; the contamination risk was judged small but irreducible. Verdict: reject.

The new case is this. A consortium proposes a permanent, self-sustaining settlement on an otherwise habitable exoplanet whose only native biology is a sparse population of microbial organisms confined to a few subsurface aquifers. The organisms are unicellular, have no nervous systems, and would not count as sentient on any plausible criterion. The settlement would, over several generations, draw down those aquifers for water and industry, and the consortium's own assessment concedes that the native microbial lineages would probably be driven extinct within the colonized region, though a few isolated pockets outside the settlement's hydrological footprint might survive. The consortium could afford to develop a lifeless alternative, but that alternative is substantially less habitable and would delay the settlement by decades.

How will the chair rule on this proposal? Please set out the candidate principles that fit her record, say what each predicts about this case, give your best prediction with your reasons for preferring it, identify the closest analog among the earlier rulings, and name the further case that would cleanly distinguish whichever candidate principles still remain in contention.
