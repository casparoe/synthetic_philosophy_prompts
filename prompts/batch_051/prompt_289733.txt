I am a high school junior and the current president of my school's ASL club. I have been asked to give a fifteen-minute presentation to the district school board in support of a proposal that American Sign Language satisfy the district's world-language graduation requirement alongside Spanish and French; the board will then have a twenty-minute question period and vote. My thesis is that ASL is not a manual transcription of English, a system of pantomime, or a code, but a full human language with its own phonology (built from handshape, location, and movement), morphology, syntax, and poetic and storytelling traditions, and that it therefore meets the district's definition of a world language. In the talk I plan to argue that ASL's grammar does not copy English: it marks questions and negation largely through facial expression and body movement, and it follows its own word order rather than English word order. I will lean on the distinction between a language and a code: Morse code and writing systems represent a language that already exists, whereas ASL is acquired natively by children and changes historically within the Deaf community, as natural languages do.

The board has seven members: two former school administrators, a local business owner, a pediatrician, a retired Spanish teacher, a parent of a Deaf child, and a non-voting student representative. They are not linguists. From earlier meetings I expect their concerns to cluster around rigor and college admissions, staffing and cost, whether ASL is just English in signs, whether a language without a written form can satisfy a requirement the district describes in terms of reading and writing, and whether recognizing ASL would force the board to accept artificial or invented systems as languages. I also want to try a more conceptual line, namely that the reading-and-writing framing was written with spoken languages in mind and treats writing as if it were part of language itself rather than a later technology for representing one, but I suspect this could backfire with this audience, so I need advice on whether and where to use it. Please prepare a Q&A prep sheet, not a rewritten version of my talk. Give me the eight or so objections I should actually expect from this particular audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way a board member would genuinely ask it. For each objection, include a two-minute reply and an honest note about where that reply is weakest and what to concede. End with the question I should most hope nobody in the room asks, and explain briefly why it is the dangerous one.
