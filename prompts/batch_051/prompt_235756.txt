Here's one I've been going back and forth on. A friend who works on behavioral policy says a transparent nudge can't be paternalistic in any morally interesting sense. His example: employees are auto-enrolled in a retirement plan at 3 percent, the form tells them they've been enrolled, the opt-out is one checkbox, and the form even says the employer picked 3 percent because most people don't raise it. He thinks transparency plus easy opt-out settles it. I don't think it does, because transparency tells people what's happening without telling us whether staying in the default reflects what they actually wanted, as opposed to inertia or reading the default as a recommendation. So I want to see the autonomy part of that claim stress-tested. Design a concrete study: the intervention, the control, the outcome measure, and what pattern of results would count for and against the claim that a transparent default leaves autonomy intact. Make sure the design can tell apart 'people stick with the default and own the choice as theirs' from 'people stick with it but are just along for the ride.' Then say what a positive result would and wouldn't show, and which part of the disagreement stays conceptual no matter how clean the data are. Write in American English. Make the last line a one-sentence summary, and start it with 'Summary:'.
