I spent last night arguing with a friend about whether a person can rationally hold a view while also thinking their evidence does not quite support it, and we ended exactly where we started. The same question had come up at work earlier in the week, and I have not been able to put it down. I would like you to adjudicate the exchange I overheard.

One of my colleagues, Lena, is a physician. She had to decide whether a particular scan showed a rare condition; the decision could not be postponed and no one else was available to read the scan. After examining it carefully, she found herself strongly convinced the condition was present. But she had been awake for more than twenty-four hours, and she knows from her own history that when she is that sleep-deprived she tends to be overconfident about scan readings. In her note she wrote, in substance: my read is that the condition is present and my inclination is to trust that read; but because I know my judgment is degraded right now, I am setting my confidence noticeably lower than the read itself suggests.

Two of my other colleagues then argued about whether Lena was being rational or epistemically self-sabotaging. I have reconstructed their exchange as faithfully as I can.

Dana: Lena is doing what her higher-order evidence demands. She has the evidence of the scan, and she has further evidence about how reliable her scan-reading is likely to be right now. The rational response is not to report whichever conclusion feels most compelling from inside a degraded process, but to correct for what she knows about sleep-deprived readers generally and about herself in particular. If she did not lower her confidence, she would be treating her initial read as the output of a reliable instrument, when she knows it is not.

Sam: That conflates two things. Once Lena has taken her fatigue into account, there is just one question left: what confidence does her total evidence support, the scan and the fatigue and everything else together? Call that level c. If c is, say, 0.8, then 0.8 is the rational confidence; saying she will set it at 0.65 because she distrusts the process is not humility. It is knowingly adopting a view she takes to be less supported than an alternative she takes to be better supported. That is epistemic akrasia, and it is irrational for the same reason it is irrational to believe what you think your evidence tells you not to believe.

Dana: But that assumes Lena can compute c from inside the very state that is degrading her judgment. Sleep deprivation does not only bias her first read; it biases any attempt she makes to estimate the size of that bias. When you cannot estimate the correction from within the process, the best available policy is a blunt one: treat the output of the degraded process as less credible by some margin, even though you cannot specify the margin from the inside. That is a standing policy for handling one's own known unreliability, not a first-order belief that contradicts her evidence.

Sam: A blunt margin still has some particular size, and whichever size she picks is a substantive guess about how strong the bias is. If sleep deprivation prevents her from knowing that size, then the rational response is not to settle on a lower number whose only credential is that it is lower. It is to become less determinate: to hold the matter more tentatively, to suspend judgment, or to say she cannot form a reliable assessment at all. And if she really cannot assess the quality of her thinking, she should not first form a confident read and then discount it; she should report that she lacks the basis for a confident read in the first place.

I have gone back and forth, and my own argument last night with a friend reproduced the same impasse nearly move for move. Please go through the exchange step by step. For each move, say whether it is clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between, and justify that judgment. Then say who is more right overall, and what the two of them are both missing, if anything. If the disagreement as I have reported it rests on a false contrast, or if scoring it move by move is the wrong way to handle it, say so plainly rather than forcing a tidy verdict.
