Our engineering handbook contains a rule that reads: “No employee may use any shared build, test, or compute system in a manner that, if it became general practice within the company, would render that system unavailable or materially slower for other teams.” The clause was written when the main problem was people running enormous jobs on shared clusters during the workday. I recently watched an engineer on another team repeatedly re-run a flaky test suite by pushing empty commits until the build went green by chance, rather than fixing the flake. The cluster stayed fast and available; he ran mostly overnight when it was idle, and each run used no more resource than an ordinary build. He argues the clause plainly does not reach him, because a world in which re-running a build became general practice would be neither unavailable nor slower — it would just make the green-build signal less informative. A colleague replies that the clause is exactly a what-if-everyone-did-that test, that its target is the practice rather than the measured load, and that the informativeness of a green build is part of what the system exists to protect. I am less interested in whether to report him than in how the clause should be read. Work through it as text, as purpose, and as a guess about what its authors would have said if asked about this exact case; tell me which approach should govern, and whether the fact that no one was actually slowed down should make any difference.
