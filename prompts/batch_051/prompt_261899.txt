I've been roped into giving the opening remarks at a half-day workshop called something like "Emergency Measures After Covid: Law, Legitimacy, and the Long Argument." The room will be deliberately mixed: about half constitutional lawyers and judges, and the other half public health officials, mayors, and civil servants who actually ran the lockdowns, border controls, and contact-tracing operations in their regions, some of whom are still stung by having been blamed for doing too much and too little at once.

I spent two years in a regional government during the first wave, and I remember our legal office saying a border closure was clearly lawful under the emergency provisions while the phone lines showed that half the public thought it was an abuse and the other half thought it was cowardly. That gap is what I keep chewing on: lawfulness and legitimacy aren't the same thing, but in an emergency people keep talking as if they are.

Could you write the fifteen-minute opening remarks? I'd like the talk to keep legality and legitimacy seperate and then show why they won't stay seperate, with at least one example where something can be lawful but illegitimate and one where something can be legitimate but legally dubious. Please make the distinction do the structural work, not just decorate the speech; end by framing the day's sessions without me pretending I've settled whether the covid measures were justified on the whole. The lawyers should hear the problem stated precisely, and the public health people shouldn't feel they're being lectured about the difference between a court order and a difficult call.
