I am a novelist, and the book I am writing turns on the decades-long friendship between two moral philosophers who have spent their careers on opposite sides of the debate between moral particularism and moral generalism. Late in the novel, after agreeing to an adversarial collaboration, they produce a single joint statement of where they stand. I need that document written in full, because it will serve as the novel's centerpiece.

The document should read like a text two proud, careful people could each sign without flinching. First, include a numbered list of claims both sides could affirm: claims about the phenomenology of moral deliberation; about what each position does and does not entail; about the role of experience, examples, and narrative in moral thought; about the limits of codification in kindred domains such as case law, medicine, and the criticism of fiction; and about which standard objections each side regards as aimed at a straw man. These agreed claims should be neither vacuous nor slanted, and each should require a genuine concession from at least one side.

Second, include a disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, give the particularist's formulation and the generalist's formulation, each phrased so that a committed advocate on that side would accept the wording as their own rather than the other side's caricature. Then identify the crux: the sub-question whose answer would move at least one side. Then supply a concrete case or argument that both sides agree bears on that crux, and record in advance what each side predicts the case will show. I am especially interested in cruxes of the following kind: whether a non-vacuous principle concerning promises can survive the way reasons shift across contexts; whether the particularist's reliance on trained judgment secretly presupposes tacit principles; and whether general principles must be exceptionless in order to do real work. Where part of the dispute appears verbal, as it may if the two characters mean different things by "principle" or "codify," say so explicitly and explain why.

Do not include citations, footnotes, or named authors: the document should characterize positions rather than attribute them, and it should be written entirely in the voices of the two characters. Above all, neither character may be a straw man. The finished document should make clear where each has conceded ground and where they still, after all these years, cannot.
