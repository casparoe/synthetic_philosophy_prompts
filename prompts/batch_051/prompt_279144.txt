In a Platonia dilemma, twenty people who cannot communicate are each told that if exactly one telegram arrives at a given address by noon the next day, its sender wins a billion dollars; if none arrives or more than one arrives, nobody wins. In the noiseless version, the plain game-theoretic reading is that every profile in which at least one person sends is a weak Nash equilibrium (no unilateral deviation improves the deviator's own payoff, since any profile with zero or two-plus senders pays everyone zero), and the nobody-sends profile is not an equilibrium. Superrationality instead reasons that, because all players are symmetric, each should send with probability 1/20, the value that maximizes each player's expected winnings when all adopt the same mixed strategy. Now vary the case: each telegram is independently lost in transit with probability 1−q, so a telegram actually arrives with probability q (0<q<1), and this loss rate is common knowledge. Work through how the game-theoretic and superrational analyses change under this variation, and respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. The five items should cover: (1) the symmetric superrational sending probability as a function of q; (2) which of the original weak Nash equilibria survive and what the Nash prediction now is; (3) whether a player who believes the others are following the superrational strategy would prefer to deviate, and how this depends on q; (4) whether adding transmission loss makes the situation more or less cooperative — that is, whether it narrows or widens the gap between the Nash and superrational recommendations and raises or lowers the expected number of winners; and (5) your overall confidence in the analysis and the single point you are least sure of.
