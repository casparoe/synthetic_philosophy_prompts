Here's the situation. As a philosopher who works on artifact identity but somehow ended up on a museum collections-policy commitee, I've proposed something I'm calling a Theseus Clause. It works like this. Every composite object in the collection gets a material-identity score: the proportion of its original matter still present, but weighted by identity-relevance rather than raw mass. The weights are fixed when the object is accessioned, by an outside panel, before any argument about a specific restoration is on the table. Replacing a generic plank in a boat costs very little against the score; replacing a figurehead or an inscribed dedication costs a lot.

Once conservation brings an object below 0.5, the object must be re-accessioned under a new number and labelled "a reconstruction of X." And here's the part I actually like: if the removed original pieces are later reassembled into an object, that assembly takes over the original accession number — I call it the orphaned-parts rule. The old number cannot be held by two objects at once, so the maintained object becomes the reconstruction and the assembled fragments become, legally, the original.
