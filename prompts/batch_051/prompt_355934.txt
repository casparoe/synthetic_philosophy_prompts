I keep seeing the same two-step in arguments about meritocracy, and I can't tell whether it has a name or whether I'm just pattern-matching.

Two recent examples. A friend argued his company should hire by ranking candidates on a coding test: "we need the best engineers, anything else is leaving value on the table." When I pointed out the top scorer had three months to grind LeetCode because his parents covered his rent, he switched to "so what, he still did the grinding, he earned the spot." When I said plenty of people grind just as hard with more time pressure and less support, he switched back to "look, the test is the least bad predictor, this isnt about deserving anything." The second was a thread about college admissions where someone said a kid with a 1590 and a nonprofit his mom incorporated for him "deserves" his place, but once the test prep and parental logistics came up, the same person retreated to "the school needs students who can keep up; its not a reward for virtue."

What strikes me is that the first claim and the second claim aren't the same claim at all, but the word "merit" hides the jump. The first is forward-looking: this person will do the job best, the institution will get the most out of them. The second is backwards-looking: she's owed something because of what she did. And there's a third thing I also see in sports and competitions: "the rules were fair and she won, so she gets the prize" — that's not exactly deserving either, it's just what the rules say.

Is there an established name for this exact slide between merit-as-qualification and merit-as-desert, and for the third thing? I know meritocracy has its critics and I have a vague memory of a distinction between deserving something and merely being entitled to it, but I want the actual carving: are these three different concepts, which ones collapse into which, and does the forward-looking best-candidate claim ever actually ground the backwards-looking she-deserves-it claim?
