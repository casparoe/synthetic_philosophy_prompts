I write and edit for a magazine that covers science and intellectual life, and I oversee a section that runs essays tied to historical anniversaries, commemorations, and public acts of memory. I face a recurring editorial decision: when a date comes around — a disaster, a war, a political murder, a founding, a labor struggle — should the magazine mark it in a substantial way, pass over it, or actively argue that it is time to let it recede? I make roughly one such call a week, sometimes several in a cluster. The decision is not morally weightless: the magazine teaches its readers which pasts remain claims on the present, and either mistake has a cost, since a commemorative package can warm over old grievances while a pointed silence can function as a quiet amnesty. My conditions are those of ordinary deadline journalism. I typically have a thirty-minute editorial meeting and a few hours to read before committing; I cannot assign original research; and my editor-in-chief must be able to defend the final decision to readers, and to a publisher who cares about not appearing either maudlin or callous.

I have been reading two sets of arguments that pull in opposite directions. One side, which I associate with Avishai Margalit, holds that obligations to remember are owed primarily within thick relations — to families, communities, fellow citizens, former comrades — rather than as a universal duty toward everyone simply in virtue of shared humanity; on this view memory is what sustains the communities whose claims bind us, and it is a mistake to treat every distant atrocity as if it were a personal bereavement. The other side, which I associate with David Rieff, holds that the duty to remember has been inflated into a civic piety, that much collective memory is an edited, self-serving myth, and that some forgetting is not a failure of nerve but a condition of political decency. I want a procedure that keeps both of these thoughts in play and does not settle for averaging them.

The procedure must be usable by someone who has not read either author, must say explicitly at each step where the step is a judgment call rather than a mechanical test, and must handle the hard cases I keep meeting: an anniversary readers expect us to mark but to which my own institution has no living connection; a never-forget slogan that has become empty ritual; a recent atrocity where forgetting feels indecent but remembrance is being used to justify new violence; a request to let the past fade that comes from perpetrators or their heirs rather than from survivors; and the gap between private grief and public commemoration. Please give me exactly eight numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Items 1–5 are the steps of the procedure in the order I would run them. Item 6 is a worked application to a proposal the procedure accepts: a special issue marking the sixtieth anniversary of a textile-workers strike in the city where the magazine is based, requested by the workers' now-elderly children, with no living participants remaining. Item 7 is a worked application to the case I would most like to run and suspect the procedure will tell me not to: a package marking the thirtieth anniversary of a terrorist attack I covered as a young reporter and still feel personally bound to, though survivors' families are now divided about the annual commemoration and state officials have begun using it to recruit support for present policies. Item 8 says what the procedure cannot do.
