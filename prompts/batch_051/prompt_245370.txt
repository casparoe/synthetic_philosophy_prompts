Y'know the case: a three-judge panel has to decide liability, and liability requires two findings, P (breach of duty) and Q (causation). Judge 1 says P, not-Q. Judge 2 says not-P, Q. Judge 3 says P, Q. So a majority accepts P, a majority accepts Q, but a majority rejects liability. Everyone I've told this to has a different instinct about where the mistake is, and I want a tool to sort people's instincts before the discussion, so they don't just argue past each other.

Act as an expert on judgment aggregation, think carefully, and write me a decision tree or sorting instrument for this. Build it from the logical space rather than from the standard labels: start by actually brainstorming the genuinely separating questions — e.g., should we aggregate premises or conclusions, must the collective judgment set stay consistent, is the point to track the truth or to respect the votes, should the threshold change with the stakes, must individuals themselves be consistent before we aggregate them — and then place the actual positions in that space. The leaves should be named positions where they exist (premise-based, conclusion-based, distance-based, supermajoritarian, whatever you think genuinely earns a leaf) and labeled 'unnamed' where they don't, each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag combinations of answers that nobody occupies, and say briefly whether that's because the spot is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Also flag any positions your tree can't seperate, and tell me which extra question would pull them apart. I'm aiming for maybe six to ten leaves, for a mixed group of philosophy and political science undergrads, so keep the questions concrete and answerable without already knowing the labels.

Because this is getting turned into a handout, write in American English, and end the whole thing with a single line in this exact form: \answer{a short phrase naming the deepest split in your tree}.
