I just came home from a long argument with a friend about whether what we believe or expect can change how things literally look, not just how we interpret them afterwards. He had a chain of reasoning that seems to prove more than it should, and I couldnt find the hole in it. I'd be grateful for a second opinion.

His argument, as he gave it:

(1) The reason perception can serve as evidence for our beliefs is that how things look comes from the world, not from the beliefs themselves.
(2) Cognitive penetration is the claim that beliefs, desires, or expectations can affect perceptual experience itself — not just attention to, interpretation of, or memory for what we saw.
(3) If cognitive penetration is real, then in at least some ordinary cases, how the world looks is partly caused by what the perceiver already believes.
(4) If how the world looks is partly caused by what you already believe, you cannot appeal to how it looks to test or revise that belief; the evidence would be partly manufactured by the very hypothesis it is meant to check.
(5) And mixing in a little bit of world-driven input doesn't help: a partly circular source of evidence is still circular, and no amount of non-circular support makes the circular part okay to rely on.
(6) So any penetrated perception is epistemically disqualified from justifying beliefs.
(7) But the experimental work that motivates this whole debate is meant to show that penetration happens in exactly the humdrum perceptions we rely on every day — faces, distances, colors.
(8) So accepting the experiments forces you to say that plenty of ordinary perceptions cannot justify belief after all.
(9) Since that's absurd, perception must be cognitively impenetrable, and the studies must be finding changes in attention, judgment, or memory instead.

I want to resist this, because it seems to make the empirical debate impossible to have: any study that finds penetration gets reclassified as a study about something else. But he says that's not a bug; it's what the epistemology demands. Which step should I reject, and what do I pay for each option? I'd like to keep both the experiments about ordinary perception and the idea that perception is broadly world-driven; he insists that's the one combination the argument rules out. Is there a way for a little penetration to coexist with perception keeping its evidential role, or is the cost just higher than I think? And if he is right, what should I make of those experimental results?
