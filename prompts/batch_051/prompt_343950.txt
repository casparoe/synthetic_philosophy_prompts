So I spent all weekend arguing with a friend about what should and shouldn't count as genocide, and it spiraled into a much more annoying argument about what the word even means. We ended up agreeing on a rough list of criteria, pulled mostly from how the standard legal definition goes: the acts have to include killing members of the group, causing serious bodily or mental harm, deliberately imposing living conditions meant to physically destroy the group, trying to stop births within the group, or forcibly moving children to another group; and, crucially, all of this has to be done with the aim of destroying a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group as such, in whole or in part.

The case we couldn't settle was this. Imagine a state invades a region that's home to a distinct ethnic group. Over a couple of decades it takes most of their land, pushes the population into settlements, and bans the language in schools and courts. It runs a systematic program of taking children from their families and raising them in state boarding schools where they're educated in the majority language and religion. The official line is that the state isn't trying to destroy anybody; it says it's "integrating" the group into the nation and even improving their lives. Some internal planning documents talk about making the group's separate identity "disappear" within a generation or two, and they explicitly rule out mass killing—and in fact there's no large-scale killing campaign. My friend says this has to count as genocide; he kept going back to Lemkin, the guy who coined the term, and saying Lemkin cared at least as much about destroying a group as a social and cultural unit as about physically exterminating it. I said that if you call this genocide, the category loses all its edges, because then basically every forced assimilation policy in history counts, and the word stops doing the special moral and legal work it's supposed to do.

So: does the hypothetical qualify under our list of criteria? Go through it criterion by criterion, and be explicit about which verdicts come from the criteria themselves and which come from extra moral intuitions we didn't write down. If the list is ambiguous or just doesn't determine the answer at some step, say that, and say whether that's a reason to redraw the criteria. At the end, tell me how confident you are in the overall call and flag the single point you're least sure about.
