I am a member of a philosophy department currently under an academic program review, and our university's planning committee has circulated a consultation document that rests its case for restructuring the department on a single comparison: philosophy is to the sciences what alchemy was to chemistry. On their telling, alchemy made progress only by handing its tractable problems over to chemistry and leaving behind a residue of unfalsifiable speculation. Philosophy, they argue, has done the same—it spun off physics, psychology, logic, linguistics, and computer science—and what remains is a stagnant collection of non-empirical questions that have never demonstrably been settled. Their conclusion is that the useful parts of philosophy should be absorbed by the relevant sciences and the remainder cut.

I must prepare a written reply, and I want the analogy itself audited rather than merely disputed. Please produce a rigorous audit that includes: an explicit correspondence table showing what stands in for the alchemist, the transmutation project, the surviving techniques, chemistry, and the residue; the respects in which the analogy genuinely holds; the disanalogies that matter for the committee's intended use, distinguished from the ones that are superficial; the features that the alchemy source domain quietly imports into philosophy—for instance, assumptions about what a discipline's aim is, what it means for work to be residual, and whether a question can be settled without being empirical; and a verdict on whether the committee's conclusion, that philosophy's only form of progress is self-liquidation into the sciences so the remainder can be cut, survives once those disanalogies are on the table. If a better analogy would do more honest work, name it and say exactly what it improves.
