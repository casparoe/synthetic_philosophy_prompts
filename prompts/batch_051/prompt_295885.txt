I'm putting together a mini-syllabus on the last chapter of Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics, the one where he lands on the dualism of practical reason. If I'm remembering the gist, he concludes that practical reason leaves us with two apparently rational methods that can conflict, and the orderly system of duties turns into a kind of anarchy because he can't prove that egoism and utilitarianism have to converge. I want to rank the standard-ish escape routes from this by how well they actually dissolve the dualism on Sidgwick's own terms, rather than just dodging it. Here are the candidates:

A. Kant-style reconciliation: posit God or a moral order to guarantee that self-interest and the general good coincide in the long run.

B. Deflationary rationalism: say egoism isn't a genuine method of ethics after all, just a background constraint, so there is no dualism.

C. Impartialist self: say the conflict only arises from an overly thick conception of 'me'; a fully rational agent has no separate ends from everyone else's.

D. Disunity of reason: accept that practical reason is genuinely incoherent, and treat moral judgments as commitments rather than dictates.

E. Utilitarian dominance: keep both methods but make egoism subordinate, allowed only as a tie-breaker among utilitarianly equivalent options.

Please tier these from A-tier down to D-tier for how well they solve the problem Sidgwick actually poses, with one sentence for each. Also tell me which one Sidgwick himself seems closest to endorsing in that final chapter, and what he would demand before signing off on it.
