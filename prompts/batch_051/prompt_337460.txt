I've been trying to sort out my views on the boredom objection to immortality, using Bernard Williams's essay The Makropulos Case as a starting point. As I understand him, Williams thinks Elina Makropulos, after 342 years of life, refuses the elixir because she has run out of categorical desires, and that any endless human life would eventually lead to a kind of unbearable tedium. My own verdicts on nearby cases are messier than his, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them.

Here are my cases:

- A person with normal memory and a fairly stable personality takes the elixir and lives for many centuries. I think Williams is basically right about her: eventually her interests run dry and she becomes bored.

- A person lives forever, but every few decades the elixir generates a genuinely new set of interests and desires. I don't get the same boredom intuition, but I also feel much less confident that this is still one person living one life.

- A person lives forever in a permanent beatific state that doesn't involve temporal desires at all. I don't find boredom intelligible here, because boredom seems to require wanting something and being unable to care.

- An immortal lives forever with full memory and keeps forming new attachments to new projects, people, and art. I suspect this eventually feels less like one life and more like a museum of successive selves, and that itself is a new kind of tedium.

- A person lives forever but her memory periodically compresses or resets, so she genuinely cares about old things as if new. I'm inclined to say this might stay interesting, but I'm not sure why it should count as the same person living forever.

Is there a single principle about categorical desires, personal identity, and the sources of boredom that captures all these verdicts? Which ones would such a principle force me to drop, and what would it say about a case where an immortal has a potentially endless sequence of desires that are themselves time-indexed, so that she wants each new experience partly because it comes later in the sequence?
