Last month, in a graduate reading group on material constitution, we spent a long time on the question whether a statue is identical with the lump of clay that makes it up or merely coincides with it. When someone pressed the group to say what sort of evidence could settle the matter, another member cut in and said the debate was "just verbal" — that we were only disagreeing about which words to attach to a single agreed-upon situation, and that there was no further fact out there to get right. Several people nodded, and the conversation moved on. I can't shake the feeling that the dismissal was doing more work than anyone acknowledged.

I would like to know exactly what "that's just a verbal dispute" could be claiming when it is used to shut down a debate like this. Does it mean the two sides are using a key term — say, "identical" or "statue" — with different meanings? Does it mean the parties agree on every non-linguistic fact but still prefer different descriptions? Does it mean the original question is defective or meaningless? Does it mean that, once each side is translated into the other's vocabulary, no genuine disagreement remains? Or does it mean something more ambitious about the world itself — for example, that there is no fact of the matter about which of two equally good conceptual schemes is correct, or that there are many equally real ways of carving up what is there?

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses someone might be asserting when they call a philosophical dispute merely verbal, and give a verdict on each: which readings are truistic, which are substantive and defensible, and which — if any — would actually license dismissing the statue/lump question the way my fellow group member did? I am less interested in a final answer to the statue puzzle itself than in understanding what the dismissal would have to be claiming in order to be justified, and how plausible those claims are.
