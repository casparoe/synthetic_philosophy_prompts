I have been thinking about how much of our everyday willingness to accept risk is shaped by the absence of a running tally, rather than by the underlying numbers themselves. Here is a concrete intervention for you to reason through. Suppose that, beginning in 2030, the Netherlands requires every newly registered private car to display, continuously while the vehicle is in motion, an estimate of the cumulative micromorts of the current trip; a micromort is defined as a one-in-a-million probability of death. The estimate updates with speed, road type, traffic density, weather, time of day, and distance travelled, and it sits next to a reference line showing the average micromort count per hour for a driver on the same road network at the same time, so that the number is not free-floating. The trip total resets only when the engine is switched off. Drivers may shrink the display to a corner of the instrument panel but cannot turn it off while the car can move, and for the first decade insurers and regulators are barred from accessing any individual car's trip totals.

Please predict, as an explicit chain of reasoning about mechanisms, incentives, and adaptation, how this would play out over the following ten years for driving behaviour, crash rates, the composition of the vehicle fleet, insurance pricing, and public discourse about risk. I am less interested in a bare yes-or-no than in which effects are robust and which are fragile: where would a small change in an assumption flip your prediction? In particular, address whether drivers would habituate to the number, whether they would treat cumulative risk differently from momentary risk, how social comparison of trip totals would shape behaviour, and whether the main effect would simply be to shift exposure onto unmeasured modes such as cycling or onto older, unequipped vehicles. Finish by identifying the single belief doing the most work in your overall prediction and saying what observation or experimental result would most change it.
