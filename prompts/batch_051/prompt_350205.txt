I teach an upper-level undergraduate course in philosophy of law, and I am writing the final examination. The course covers the rule of law, legal interpretation, and the constitutional constraints on criminal statutes, with a substantial unit on the void-for-vagueness doctrine. I want a single essay question, to be answered in 75 minutes, that tests whether students can do more than recite the doctrine's two standard prongs — fair notice and protection against arbitrary enforcement. A student whose only move is to say that a law is vague when ordinary people cannot tell what it prohibits should not be able to pass the question.

Lately I have been thinking about a criminal statute as an interface between a citizen and the state: a person with full knowledge of the facts ought to be able to determine whether the statute reaches their conduct, and an officer ought not to be able to settle the same question by unarticulated judgment. I want the question to make students treat under-specification itself as the constitutional defect, rather than treating vagueness as a mere proxy for unfair outcomes.

Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme.

For the question, use the following invented municipal ordinance as the object of analysis:

Section 1. It is a misdemeanor for any person, while on transit property, to behave in a manner that a reasonable passenger would find alarming or seriously annoying.

Section 2. A transit officer may issue a citation under Section 1 whenever, in the officer's judgment, the behavior is alarming or seriously annoying. No finding that any particular passenger was actually alarmed or annoyed is required.

The question should present the ordinance and then give a city attorney's defense of it: the terms alarming and seriously annoying have ordinary, widely understood meanings; the ordinance has a clear core of covered conduct; and it requires the behavior to be such that a reasonable passenger would be disturbed, so it does not punish harmless conduct. The students' task should be to evaluate that defense under the void-for-vagueness doctrine.

The difficulty should come from a specific crux. The city attorney's argument sounds like a fair-notice argument, and in an easy case it might suffice. Students need to see that a statute can have a perfectly clear core and still be void because its periphery is standardless, and that a reasonable-person standard does not by itself constrain enforcement discretion when the operative terms are evaluative, no mens rea is required, and the officer need not identify any actual disturbance. They should also see that the ordinance sweeps in expressive conduct, so the vagueness analysis operates with special strictness, and they should be able to say why that is a vagueness problem rather than merely an overbreadth problem.

The model answer should make the following five moves, in whatever order is most natural: (1) state the doctrine's two prongs and identify which prong the city attorney's argument actually addresses; (2) explain why a clear core of prohibited conduct does not resolve a vagueness challenge when the boundary is unconstrained; (3) analyze Section 2 as a delegation of standardless discretion, including the significance of the missing actual-effect requirement and the missing mens rea; (4) explain how the expressive-conduct dimension triggers heightened vagueness scrutiny and distinguish vagueness from overbreadth; and (5) say what, if anything, would save the ordinance — for example, a specific-intent requirement, a requirement of an actual disturbance, or a narrowing construction to fighting words or true threats — and note what each fix would and would not cure.

The marking scheme should assign 20 marks across the five moves above, four marks each, with a one-line note for each point saying how students typically get it wrong, for instance treating the city attorney's core-of-clear-cases argument as a complete answer or conflating vagueness with overbreadth. It should also separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific move a B answer omits.

The question and model answer must be self-contained. Do not ask students to cite cases by name, do not make any part of the answer depend on a particular case, treatise, or quotation, and do not include a bibliography or reading list. The exercise should reward understanding of the doctrine as a constraint on rule-design, not recall of its history.
