I teach a course on the philosophy of computation. One unit is for advanced philosophy undergraduates who have had exactly one introductory programming course: they can write small Python programs but have never examined programming constructs as objects of philosophical analysis. The unit's organizing question is why mutation is hard to think about, and what that difficulty reveals about state. I do not want the explanation itself; I want a plan for teaching it. Please prescribe the order in which the component ideas must be introduced so the difficulty presents itself as a discovery rather than a lecture point. I need to know what must already be in place before the distinction between value equality and object identity can land — for instance, how firmly they must distinguish a name from a value, and understand assignment as an event that changes a binding rather than as a definition — and what only becomes intelligible after that distinction is in place. Choose one carrying example to thread through the unit (I have doubts about relying on box-and-arrow diagrams for identity, but I will accept a defense of them) and specify what that example must contain and what it must avoid if it is to do its work. Then give me the misconception map: which mistakes students predictably import or form — I expect, among others, reading an expression such as x = x + 1 as a contradiction, treating a variable as a permanent label for a value, using the word equal loosely across value and identity, assuming a function's behavior is determined by its argument's value, and taking the current state to be all that matters to what happens next — and identify where in the sequence each should be preempted or dismantled, plus what would show it has been dismantled rather than merely suppressed. I would also like you to address the structural problem that makes the usual instruments fail: why interchangeability of expressions with the same value stops being safe once evaluation can occur before or after an intervening update, and why the burden shifts to tracking what stays true across a change. End with a single check for understanding that would distinguish grasping the concept from using the vocabulary, and state plainly what should be set aside at this level. Throughout, mark the boundary between what is settled in mainstream programming-language semantics and what is genuinely contested philosophically — such as what identity through time consists in, or whether state is a real feature of a system or only a useful way of describing it — and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
