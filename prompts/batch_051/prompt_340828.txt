You are a statistically sophisticated philosopher of science advising a three-year, conceptually oriented research program on the replication crisis. The program's mandate is to clarify whether and how the frequentist–Bayesian debate bears on why published findings fail to replicate and on what would make the scientific literature more trustworthy. Funding is limited: the program can pursue only two or three questions seriously.

Begin by brainstorming a broad list of candidate questions at the interface of the frequentist–Bayesian debate and the replication crisis, including at least one question you think the mainstream methods literature underrates. Possible starting points include: whether null-hypothesis significance testing tempts researchers into base-rate neglect; whether replacing p-values with posterior probabilities or Bayes factors would change publication decisions or mostly relabel the same incentives; whether the concept of a successful replication needs a decision-theoretic or predictive-accuracy definition; and whether multiplicity corrections and preregistration are frequentist patches for problems that Bayesian updating might handle more naturally.

Then rank the candidates by how much they matter for scientific practice, making your criteria explicit: importance, tractability within three years of conceptual work, neglectedness, and how many downstream decisions turn on the answer. For each of the top two or three, explain what practical change the answer would make for journals, funders, individual researchers, or training; what would have to be true for the question to be overrated; and what empirical finding would most move it in your ranking. Conclude with the two or three questions you would fund, with a one-sentence justification for each.
