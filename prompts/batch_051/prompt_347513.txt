I've been stuck on whether you can trust your own sense of what's going on in your head. The other day I snapped at my brother, and right after, I "noticed" that I snapped because he was talking down to me. But then I got worried: did I actually have that reason before I snapped, or did I just stick a reason on afterwards and it felt like it had been there all along? So the bigger question I keep circling is whether introspection is a reliable way to know your own mind, or whether it's more like a story you tell yourself after the fact.

Write a dialogue between two people arguing this out. One person thinks introspection is basically trustworthy for knowing your current mental states and even some of your reasons—that if you look carefully, you can sort real reasons from later rationalizations. The other person thinks introspection is a kind of construction or guesswork, and the feeling of "seeing" your own reasons is often the same storytelling machinery running in reverse, so your introspective reports should be treated as hypotheses rather than direct observations. Dont make either of them a strawman or easy to knock over. They should actually respond to each other's points, and I'd like each of them to give a little ground or sharpen their view under pressure, not just repeat themselves.

Give them a concrete situation, like the brother case or something similar, and let them dig into whether the person in the case could tell the difference between noticing a reason and inventing one after the fact. Let them also get into whether that distinction is even usable without already assuming the answer to the question they are fighting about. Keep it a plain conversation, no sources or citations, and no jargon for its own sake. At the very end, after the dialogue, write one final line that begins with "Summary:" and gives a one-sentence summary of where their disagreement ends up.
