For a reading group on practical rationality and constitutional theory, a colleague and I tried to reconstruct a half-remembered challenge to the very idea of precommitment, and the argument we built is now more convincing than either of us wanted it to be. We cannot find the step to reject, and the conclusion strikes us as unacceptable, but each premise seemed forced. I would like you to identify where the reasoning fails, or, if it does not fail, to say which premise is least costly to abandon and what the main alternatives give up.

The argument runs as follows. (1) Ulysses' arrangement with the mast works only because, at the moment the Sirens sing, he is physically unable to untie himself and physically unable to issue new orders to his crew; his present self has not merely made temptation more expensive but has deprived his future self of the relevant power. (2) Therefore, a present self genuinely binds a future self only if the future self is actually deprived, at the time of temptation, of the power to act on its then-current preferences. (3) A constitutional provision does not deprive later majorities of anything analogous: what one sovereign majority enacts, a later majority can repeal, and what one generation entrenches, a later generation can amend away, because the power to change the law is the same power that made it. (4) Procedural obstacles such as supermajority thresholds, bicameral assent, or ratification do not change this, since the rules for amending the constitution are themselves legal rules that the same ongoing sovereign can revise. (5) Every constitutional constraint is therefore either revisable or not. If it is revisable, it is not a precommitment but merely an expression of present preferences that a later majority may discard. If it is not revisable, then a past majority has permanently disabled its successors, which is not a people governing itself but the dead governing the living. (6) Hence constitutional precommitment — and, by parity of reasoning, the Ulysses contract — is either vacuous or a disguised form of domination, and the idea of a sovereign binding itself is incoherent.

Where does the argument go wrong? I would like to keep the Ulysses case as a genuine, non-coercive instance of self-binding, and I would like to keep constitutional entrenchment as more than a cheap promise, but I do not know which premise that combination forces out, or what the standard ways of resisting the argument would have to sacrifice. Please diagnose the argument rather than merely rejecting its conclusion.
