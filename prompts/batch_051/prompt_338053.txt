You are a senior epistemologist and philosopher of science with a specialism in Bayesian confirmation theory, known for giving explicit, carefully decomposed credences rather than hedging. Please write your answer in British English.

I want your honest numerical credence in a proposition, together with the reasoning that produces it. The proposition is this. Take the classic raven situation. An observer's background knowledge consists only of the following: the predicates "raven" and "black" are well-defined; ravens exist; and non-black things exist. She has no information about how numerous ravens or non-black things are, and no information about how the object she observes was selected. She then sees a single object and learns only that it is neither black nor a raven — a white shoe, for definiteness. The hypothesis "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens", so the shoe is a positive instance of the second generalisation. The proposition on which I want your credence is: that observation provides at least some positive confirmation of the hypothesis that all ravens are black — where "positive confirmation" means that the evidence rationally warrants a strictly greater posterior credence in the hypothesis than the prior, even if the increment is arbitrarily small.

Give your credence in that proposition. Then decompose it. Assign a numerical probability and one or two sentences of justification to each of the following, and show explicitly how they combine into your final figure, stating any independence assumptions you make:

(a) Nicod's instance condition (Nicod's criterion), or a suitably restricted version of it, is correct: in the absence of defeating background information, an observed object that is both F and G genuinely confirms "all Fs are Gs".
(b) The equivalence condition is correct: if evidence confirms a hypothesis, it confirms to the same degree any logically equivalent formulation, so the shoe's standing with respect to the contrapositive transfers to "all ravens are black".
(c) No defeating feature is present in the described setup — no biased selection, no relevant base-rate fact, no other background element that cancels or nullifies the support that (a) and (b) would otherwise provide.
(d) Even if at least one of (a), (b), and (c) fails, some other route — for example, a direct Bayesian treatment of the likelihoods — still yields a strictly positive increment rather than exactly zero.

Then tell me which of these sub-questions your final credence is most sensitive to — that is, where shifting one sub-probability would move your final number most — and what evidence or argument would most shift that sub-question. Finally, suppose a colleague reports 0.9 for the same proposition and another reports 0.15. In your decomposition, where is the most likely locus of their disagreement?
