I spent my scientific career using laws of nature without ever needing to say what a law is; since retiring I have been reading the Humean/anti-Humean literature, and I find the two sides usually begin arguing before either has stated what a theory of laws is supposed to do.

For a handout for my reading group, set out the desiderata: the list of requirements an adequate account of laws of nature must satisfy, each accompanied by the phenomenon, case, or use of the notion that makes it binding. At a minimum, address the following, and add anything central that I have missed: the distinction between lawlike and merely accidental regularities — the familiar contrast between a large sphere of gold and a large sphere of enriched uranium is the sort of case I mean; the way laws underwrite counterfactuals, predictions, and inductive practice; the feeling that laws govern or constrain the course of events rather than merely record it; the status of ceteris-paribus laws in the special sciences; and the problem of identifying a law from finite data that can be gerrymandered into alternative regularities. For each requirement, say whether it is a demand any account must meet or a commitment peculiar to one side of the debate, and note what a theory loses if the requirement is relaxed. Then take the step I rarely see taken together: identify any subset of these requirements that is jointly unsatisfiable or satisfiable only at a real cost, and say which single requirement does the most to exclude familiar positions. Present this as a list with commentary rather than a narrative essay, and keep it under 700 words.
