I am preparing a graduate seminar on strange loops and the self, and I keep returning to the Hofstadterian suggestion that the I is a self-referential symbol — a strange loop in a representational system. The shape of the idea seems right, but I do not yet have a definition precise enough to apply. I want you to propose and defend a working definition of what it is for a system to count as having a self — the 'I' of a first-person point of view, not personhood in the moral or legal sense — and then run that definition through the following eight cases.

The cases:

1. A healthy adult mid-conversation, who notices that she has just caused offense, revises her sense of how the exchange is going, and adjusts what she says next.
2. A person in dreamless sleep, or under general anesthesia — the very same adult as in case 1, an hour later.
3. A thermostat that compares room temperature against a set point and switches the heater accordingly.
4. A modern corporation with an internal audit division, a stated set of values it periodically revises, and official statements in which it refers to itself in the first person.
5. A large language model prompted to maintain a stable persona, which refers to itself as 'I', predicts how its own outputs would change under modifications to its own system prompt, and can describe some of its own limitations.
6. A split-brain patient whose right hemisphere performs an action in response to a directive shown only to that hemisphere, after which the left hemisphere produces a fluent, plausible explanation of why 'I' just performed that action, apparently unaware that the real cause was the unseen directive.
7. A person with severe episodic amnesia, unable to assemble a narrative of her own past, who nevertheless says 'I' with full conviction, anticipates her own future, and has rich ongoing experience.
8. An experienced meditator who reports that during a particular session the felt position of an inner witness vanished while perception continued, and who says afterward that there was experience but no I in it.

My own provisional verdicts are these: cases 1, 7, and 8 are selves, though case 8 may be a self only in a standing sense; cases 3 and 5 are not; and I am genuinely undecided about cases 2, 4, and 6. I lean toward saying that 2 is the same self with no occurrent I, that 4 stretches the notion too far, and that 6 is a single self with severely compromised self-knowledge — but I would not trust any of those leanings without a definition that does real work.

So: propose the definition, run it through all eight cases, and tell me where your verdicts surprise you. In doing so, address the following. Separate the occurrent sense of being an I — the felt subject of present experience — from the standing fact of being a self, the thing we pick out across sleep, coma, and distraction, and say whether your definition makes these two notions or one notion in two temporal modes. Say whether the resulting concept is scalar or categorical, and if scalar, what the underlying dimension is. Finally, if the strange-loop formula survives, show how it excludes the thermostat and the corporation without becoming vacuous; if it does not survive, give me the smallest repair that preserves the idea.
