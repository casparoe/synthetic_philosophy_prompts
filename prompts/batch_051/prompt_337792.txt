So my friend Dan is a pretty careful guy with money, but he has this habit that drives our other friend Priya up the wall. Dan pays for renters insurance, extended warranties, travel insurance—the works—and then also buys a couple lottery tickets whenever the jackpot gets big. Priya says this is incoherent: if he's willing to pay real money for a tiny chance at a huge gain, he should also be willing to accept a tiny chance of a huge loss when the expected value is in his favor, but he won't. She also caught him taking a guaranteed $900 over a 90% chance at $1,000, while at the same time paying $10 for a 1% chance at $1,000. Her line is that expected value is expected value, and any deviation is just innumeracy dressed up as prudence.

I get that Dan violates simple expected-value reasoning, but it also feels to me like there's something rational in being extremely sensitive to worst-case scenarios while still taking small fliers on best-case scenarios. It's like the probabilities aren't the only thing doing the work—the shape of the risk matters too, and a 1% chance at the very bottom of the possible outcomes might reasonably loom larger than a 1% chance at the very top. Who's right here? Is there a coherent, formally defensible way of thinking about decisions where a rational agent can weight the same small probability differently depending on where it sits in the ranking of possible outcomes, or is that just a story Dan tells himself to cover for a math mistake?
