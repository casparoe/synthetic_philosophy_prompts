ok so for my Chinese philosophy class I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think about wu-wei, and I'd like a consistency check before I write my paper. Here's where I've landed:

(1) wu-wei is best understood as a kind of absorbed, skilled action — think the butcher Cook Ding carving an ox, or a jazz musician improvising — where you aren't forcing or monitoring yourself. It's not just sitting around doing nothing.

(2) You can't get there by directly trying to be wu-wei; trying to be spontaneous kills the spontaneity. That's the whole paradox.

(3) But it can be cultivated indirectly: you practice a craft or a ritual until the moves become second nature, and at some point the effort drops out and wu-wei shows up as a byproduct.

(4) I think the Daodejing and the Zhuangzi are basically giving the same account here — it's all about emptying the self and unlearning, not about acquiring more technique.

(5) I also think wu-wei is an ethical ideal, not just a performance ideal: it tells you how to be a good person, not just how to carve an ox well.

(6) And I'm inclined to say the state itself is the same across all these cases — the butcher's flow is the same kind of thing as the sage's effortless responsiveness, just aimed at different objects.

(7) Finally, I don't think any of this commits me to a special metaphysics of qi or the Dao; wu-wei is just a fact about how human skill, habit, and attention work.

Does this set hang together, or am I quietely holding something that conflicts? Go through the pairs and tell me which are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and rank the real tensions by how severe they are. Then tell me what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost.
