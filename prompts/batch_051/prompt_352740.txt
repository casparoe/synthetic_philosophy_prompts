I spent most of my career in chemistry and only started reading philosophy after I retired, so forgive me if I don't have the vocabularly. Here's the thing I keep getting stuck on. The scientific communities I belonged to spent a lot of energy on two practices that seem to point in different directions. On one hand, we named results after individuals — Avogadro's number, the Haber process, that sort of thing. On the other hand, everyone knows these names are often not the first person, or even the main person: the Pythagorean theorem was known long before Pythagoras, Halley didn't first see the comet that carries his name, and Stigler's law has it that no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer — and the law itself is a case, since Stigler credits Merton for the idea. Meanwhile the same community treats priority as a race with a single winner and gets genuinely upset about scoops and disputes about who got there first.

So my question is: what does this whole set of practices presuppose about what a discovery is and who it belongs to? Does it commit us to the idea that discoveries are discrete events with identifiable first owners, and that credit tracks causal contribution? Or is that too strong? And is it in tension with the way the same people, including me, talk about science as a collective, incremental enterprise where multiple discovery is the norm? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of credit and discovery, say what the practice strictly needs versus what just happens to fit with it, and what would actually have to change if we took the collectivist view seriously.
