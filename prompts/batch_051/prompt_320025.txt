I cover the science-and-ideas desk at a general-interest magazine, and I am putting together an explainer about the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness. The piece will begin from the result that a risk score used across groups with different base rates cannot satisfy all three of the following at once: calibration, where a given score means roughly the same risk in each group; equalized false-positive rates; and equalized false-negative rates. Each of the three sounds, on a first hearing, like something fairness plainly requires, and yet the math says an imperfect predictor cannot deliver all three. I do not want the piece to stop at the impasse; I want to give readers a decision tree that guides them, question by question, to the view of algorithmic fairness they actually hold once they are forced to choose.

Please construct that decision tree. It should run through eight to ten diagnostic questions, each posed around a concrete deployment — a pretrial detention score, a hospital readmission model, a credit or insurance algorithm, a school early-warning dropout predictor, and similar cases — and each phrased so a reader can answer without knowing the academic labels or any formal machinery. The tree should be built from the logical space rather than from a list of named camps: first the questions, then the combinations of answers they permit, then the substantive positions located at the leaves, with combinations that are logically possible but unoccupied marked as incoherent, unattractive, or simply under-explored. Each branch should be labeled with the question or claim that does the splitting, never with the destination position.

At minimum, the leaves should include: a calibration-first view; an error-rate-parity view; a contextualist view on which no criterion is globally primary and the right trade-off depends on the decision context; a restrictive or proceduralist view on which the impossibility is a reason to remove risk scores from certain high-stakes decisions rather than tune them; a skeptical view on which the whole metrics program fails to capture what fairness means; and an approximationist view that treats the criteria as unsatisfiable ideals to be approached and audited rather than satisfied. Where the tree cannot separate two positions, say so explicitly and state what further question would separate them. The audience is a scientifically literate but non-technical readership, so keep the mathematics to proportions and simple comparisons, but do not flatten the logical structure of the impossibility into a vague cautionary tale.
