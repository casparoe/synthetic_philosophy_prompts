I am a journalist who has spent years covering physics and the philosophy of science, and I have watched the reductionism–emergence debate recur with an almost ritual predictability. One side holds that higher-level phenomena — a turbulent flow, a phase transition, the coordinated activity of a cell — are in principle nothing over and above their lower-level constituents, so that higher-level talk is at best a compact summary of lower-level facts. The other side holds that at least some higher-level patterns are genuinely autonomous, doing explanatory work that no microphysical story, however complete, could replace. The standard exchanges now feel scripted. The reductionist points out that turbulence is governed by the Navier–Stokes equations and concludes that the apparent novelty of an eddy is only our failure to solve them; the emergentist replies that this concedes the point, since the explanations we actually use are statistical and higher-level rather than read off the equations. The reductionist then asks for a criterion of genuine novelty that does not collapse into computational convenience; the emergentist asks for an actual derivation rather than an "in principle" promise. Both remain unmoved.

I want a diagnosis of why this dispute has stalled, not a verdict on who is right. What kind of disagreement is it? Is it verbal, turning on different uses of "reduction" and "emergence"? Is it a disagreement about what would count as a successful reduction — for example, whether a numerical simulation or an equation counts, or whether explanatory relevance should settle the matter? Is there a hidden empirical bet about what future science will be able to derive, dressed in metaphysical vocabulary? Or are the two sides answering different questions, one about what exists and one about what explains, under a single heading? My working hypothesis is that the impasse rests on two largely unargued intuitions about when a higher-level account has "added something" to the microphysical story, and that neither side has formulated a test that could move the other. Assess that hypothesis, and say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction that splits the readings, a concrete case that would decide between them, a formal result, a change of question, or a reason to expect no progress at all.
