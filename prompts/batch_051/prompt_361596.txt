I'm a grad student working on the proof paradoxes, and I'm collecting accounts of why naked statistical evidence—the blue bus case where the only evidence tying the company to the accident is that it owns 80% of the blue buses in town, the gatecrasher where a rodeo has 1,000 attendees but only 499 paid, and the prison-yard case where 99 of 100 prisoners joined the fatal beating of a guard and one didn't—feels insufficient for liability or conviction even when the chance of wrongdoing clears 50% in the civil cases or sits at 99% in the criminal one. So far I have:

(1) The knowledge account: a verdict asserts that this particular defendant did it, assertion requires knowledge or something close to it, and bare statistics can only give you knowledge about a reference class, not about this individual.
(2) The sensitivity account: evidence is good enough only if your verdict would likely have been different had the defendant been innocent; market-share or base-rate evidence stays the same whether or not the defendant did it, so it doesn't track the actual facts.
(3) The normic support account: proof requires evidence that makes the defendant's innocence a special, abnormal occurance in need of explanation, whereas a statistic makes innocence just one of the cases the statistic itself predicted.
(4) The explanationist account: factfinders settle cases by selecting the best explanation of the evidence, and a naked statistic doesn't explain anything about how this particular deed happened—it's not a story about the defendant's conduct at all.

I have a feeling I'm missing at least one account that's genuinely different from these—not a rewording, special case, or mashup of them—maybe something about the state's duty to justify coercion to each person, or about incentives, or something else entirely. Act as an epistemologist of legal proof: brainstorm a few candidate accounts first, then give me the strongest one not on my list. Say exactly how it differs from (1)–(4), whether it handles all three cases uniformly, and what it predicts for a case where the statistic is about the defendant's own prior conduct (say, a driver with dashcam evidence that they ran the red on 80 of their last 100 left turns) rather than about a reference class they merely belong to.
