Our lab has a little software-licensing review committee that decides when a piece of closed-source code has to be GPL'd because it counts as a derivative of GPL'd code, and I've been trying to reverse-engineer what rule they actually run. Their old rulings look suprisingly inconsistent to me:

- An app that statically linked a GPL computer-vision library, where the app was basically just a wrapper around the library: derivative, must be GPL.
- A data-processing script that shelled out to several unmodified GPL command-line tools as child processes; the tools were installed separately and the user could swap in non-GPL alternatives: fine, no obligations.
- A closed-source plugin that a GPL editor loads into its own process through the plugin API, and that's useless without that editor: derivative, must be GPL.
- A phone app that talks to our GPL-licensed server over ordinary HTTPS; it links no GPL code, and would work fine against any server that speaks the same protocol: fine.

Now they have to rule on this: a closed-source desktop "companion" program, distributed separately, that runs in its own process and only exists to work with one specific GPL application. It reads and writes the GPL app's files, drives it through its command-line interface, and the marketing and docs present the two as one integrated system. Without that particular GPL app it does literally nothing. Should the committee say it's derivative (and has to be GPL), or is it fine like the shell-script case?

More specifically: lay out the two or three plausible rules that are consistent with the four old rulings, say what each predicts for the companion program, give your best guess about how the committee would actually rule, and then describe a further case that would cleanly settle which rule they're really using. Don't just tell me the law is unsettled — I want the prediction and the reasoning.
