I am a retired physical scientist, and only recently have I begun reading philosophy in any systematic way. Lately I have been working through ancient and modern discussions of equanimity and apatheia—whether freedom from emotional disturbance is a good thing, and what it might cost. This has made me reconsider a norm I observed for decades in the teaching hospitals where I did various kinds of laboratory work.

When a patient was dying or a child had just been badly injured, a resident who stayed composed and continued working was praised as mature and professional. A resident who wept at the bedside, or had to excuse herself, was not treated as someone who had been appropriately moved; she was quietly marked down for lacking professional distance. At the same time, the same institution's mission was full of talk of compassion and empathy, and its patient-satisfaction surveys asked whether the physician seemed to care.

I would like to know what theory of the emotions, and of the value of being disturbed by another person's suffering, this practice presupposes. I can see several candidate commitments: that one can care without being disturbed; that visible distress is a failure of attention to the patient; that the patient needs the clinician's composure more than the clinician's grief; that there is a stable trait, equanimity, distinct both from callousness and from distress, and that this trait is worth cultivating. But I do not know which of these the practice strictly presupposes, which it merely fits well with, and whether its parts presuppose consistent things. Reconstruct the implicit theory, and say what a hospital would have to change if it instead held that being disturbed is an integral part of caring rather than a lapse from it. Is the practice's ideal really distinct from apatheia, or is it only a professionalized version of the same thing?
