In my epistemology seminar we have been working through Sellars's attack on the given—the idea that empirical knowledge rests on a foundation of self-justifying, non-inferential awareness. I have been trying to reconstruct a contemporary foundationalism that replaces sense-data with seemings. The position, as I would state it, runs as follows:

(1) If empirical justification is to avoid an infinite regress, it must terminate in states that can justify beliefs without themselves standing in need of justification.

(2) Seemings are the natural candidates for such terminating states: when the page seems white to me, I thereby have prima facie justification for believing that the page is white.

(3) Seemings are not beliefs. A person who knows she is hallucinating can still be appeared to whitely; the seeming occurs without being endorsed, so seemings are non-doxastic.

(4) Because seemings are non-doxastic, the demand for justification that applies to beliefs does not arise for them. Asking whether a mere seeming is justified or unjustified would be a category mistake.

(5) Therefore the chain of empirical justification can legitimately stop at seemings. Sellars's claim that the given is a myth applies, at most, to sense-data theories, not to this more modest given.

I have a nagging sense that a Sellarsian could accept most of the premises while rejecting the conclusion, but I cannot locate the exact step where the argument goes wrong. If the argument fails, identify the first step at which it fails and diagnose the failure precisely—a false premise, an invalid move, or a term that means different things in different premises—and say how the argument would fare on each disambiguated reading.
