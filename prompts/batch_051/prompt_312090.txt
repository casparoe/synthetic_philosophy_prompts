I'm in an ethics seminar and we just did a couple weeks on supererogation — obligatory vs good-but-optional vs the moral minimum. Before I start writing my term paper I want to nail down what an account of supererogation actually has to do, so I don't end up defending a view that just happens to match my favorite cases.

So: write me the desiderata. Not a survey of the literature and not a reading list — a spec that any adequate theory of supererogation has to meet, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Make sure it covers at least these, and flag anything I'm leaving out:

- The easy rescue / kidney donation / falling-on-a-grenade spread. A theory has to keep these from collapsing into each other, and also has to distinguish the genuinely supererogatory from the merely costly-but-required and the saintly-but-foolish. Where exactly does each fall, and what distinction does the work?
- The saint vs hero distinction: a whole life lived above the minimum vs one-off heroic acts. Do we actually need the theory to treat these differently, or is that a distinction people think matters more than it does?
- The fact that being good, being better than a permissible alternative, and being beyond duty are three different things. If a theory fuses the second and third, you get the paradox of supererogation: if the act is better and you ought always to do the better thing, it can't be optional. Which pairs of desiderata are in tension, or can't be jointly satisfed? This is the part I most want to see — say which pairs and what has to give.
- An all-or-nothing case I'll probably use in my paper: you can save one stranger or two strangers at roughly the same small cost to yourself once you're already wading in, but you're also permitted to just stay home and not save anyone. If you're going to do anything, you ought to save both; but doing nothing is fine. What does a theory have to say about whether the one-person rescue is wrong while the no-rescue is fine, and whether the two-person rescue is supererogatory?
- Suberogation: acting within your rights but still morally ugly. Does a theory of the positive 'beyond duty' category have to handle the negative 'permissible but bad' category too, or can it stay silent?
- Luck and intention: a risky rescue that only works if you're lucky, and the person who performs a supererogatory-looking act with a lousy motive. Do we evaluate the act, the attempt, or the character? Which distinctions are non-negotiable here?

For each requirement, mark whether it's a hard constraint any theory must meet or more like a preference of a particular camp. Then, very briefly, how do the usual contenders — threshold views, agent-relative permission views, and views that say supererogation isn't a real deontic category at all — do against the list? And tell me which single requirement does the most work excluding popular views, and which requirement you suspect is just one camp's prejudice dressed up as a desideratum.
