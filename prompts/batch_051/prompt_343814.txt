Next month I'm presenting a paper at a political theory workshop, and I just got back from arguing about it with a friend for two hours, so my head is full of the objections in there street version rather than the seminar-room version, and I need help translating. The paper defends constitutional precommitment: the idea that a democracy can legitimately bind its own future hands—entrench rights, require supermajorities, insulate constitutional courts—in something like the way Ulysses had himself tied to the mast because he knew future-him, listening to the Sirens, would be a worse judge than present-him. My positive claim is that precommitment isn't a limitation on democratic self-rule but a way of making self-rule possible across time, because a polity that can always rewrite anything at any moment by simple majority can't reliably sustain the conditions that make majority rule meaningful in the first place. I defend the individual-to-collective inference by saying the individual case need not be a case of weakness of will: a person can have stable second-order preferences and use a binding but revocable procedure to enforce them, and a constitution works the same way—it binds only through the slower, more demanding procedure, and that's exactly what distinguishes it from mere prediction about the future.

They won't care whether the Ulysses analogy gets the Greek details right; they'll press on whether the analogy earns its keep once you move from one person to millions of people who never actually agreed to the ropes.
