A Malthus discussion in my 19th-century social theory seminar got weirdly heated last week, and I still can't sort out who was right. The assigned reading was Thomas Malthus's 1798 Essay on the Principle of Population, and one of the two kept coming back to a sentence from the preface: "If he should succeed in drawing the attention of more able men to what he conceives to be the principal difficulty in the way to the improvement of society and should, in consequence, see this difficulty removed, even in theory, he will gladly retract his present opinions and rejoice in a conviction of his error."

Dana said the last two centuries basically amount to the difficulty being removed, or at least dodged: in the countries Malthus was writing about, food output per person went up and birthrates came down without first running through the famines and vice he predicted. So his theory was falsified, and calling it "right but early" is just protecting it from refutation.

Marco said no—the move to a newer "check" is still exercising the theory's central mechanism. The question was whether something would check population, not whether the check would be nasty. Lower birthrates are a check, so that isn't a refutation; it's the theory's own machinery operating. He wasn't wrong, just early about which checks would matter.

Dana: If every outcome counts as confirmation—famines check it, prosperity checks it, voluntary contraception checks it—then the theory can't lose. A theory that fits the Malthusian trap and its opposite is a framing, not a prediction. And the "in theory" line from the preface shows you're moving the goalposts: Malthus said he'd retract, and modern defenders won't retract.

Marco: A refutation is available. Show population increasing indefinitely with no check whatever, or show food growing geometrically for a very long time while fertility stays high. Nobody has shown that. What happened was a contest between exponential tendencies, and one side stayed ahead partly because people chose to have fewer kids—that is the check. That's "falsifiable but not refuted," which is exactly what a long-run prediction can be.

Dana: That's still rescue. The specific misery mechanism got junked, and the wording was stretched so the abstract mechanism survived. If the prediction only becomes testable over centuries, then in practice we're stuck with an unfalsifiable restatement, and that's a reason to treat it as sociology, not science.

Marco: That's too strict even for science. Nothing says a theory has to be testable within one generation. If you're forecasting the interaction of two exponential processes in a finite system, "not finished yet" is a legitimate epistemic status, not a dodge. His actual mistake was smaller: he read the near-term equilibrium as the permanent one, but his dynamics were fine.

I honestly couldn't tell whether Marco is rescuing a falsified theory or Dana is dismissing a theory because its test hasn't arrived. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move—say who is right about which specific point, rate the strength of each move roughly, and say who is more right overall.
