I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics and I keep going back and forth on the logic of Bell's theorem. Someone in our group laid out the argument this way:

(1) Suppose there's a local hidden-variable theory: each particle in an entangled pair has a definite but unknown state λ at the source, and the outcome on each wing is fixed by λ plus the local detector setting, so once λ and the two settings are known, the two outcomes are independent — nothing travels from one wing to the other.

(2) Bell's theorem shows that any theory like that, given also the assumption that λ isn't correlated with the choices of measurement settings, must satisfy the CHSH inequality.

(3) Quantum mechanics predicts CHSH violations for some settings, and experiments confirm them.

(4) So no theory with both features — the factorizability in (1) and the no-correlation-of-settings assumption in (2) — can reproduce quantum mechanics.

(5) But the no-correlation-of-settings assumption is just the harmless thought that experiments aren't rigged, and the factorizability condition just is what locality means. So the only serious assumption being dropped is locality.

(6) Therefore the measurement on one side has to be causally influencing the measurement on the other side — quantum mechanics is committed to faster-than-light causation.

I get suspicious around (5) and (6), but I cant articulate what a defender of locality should say. Walk me through each step and tell me whether the argument is valid, and if not, exactly which step fails and what the corrected version of that step would be. If more than one step is bad, say which one is the main culprit.
