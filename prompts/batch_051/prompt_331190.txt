I'm a philosophy professor at a land-grant university, and I've been talked into giving a colloquium at the entomology department — applied people, mostly, working on corn, soybean, and vegetable pests, plus extension folks who spend their summers telling growers when to spray. My thesis, in one sentence: integrated pest management is already doing welfare economics for insects under a different name, but it carries a hidden zero in the ledger, and once you assign any positive probability to pest insects feeling something, the economic-injury-level and action-threshold reasoning these folks already accept commits them to counting expected insect suffering the way they already count defoliation and dollars. My punchline is deliberately moderate. I'm not saying don't spray, and I'm not saying go organic. I'm saying two things: (a) when two products are genuinely equivalent on efficacy, cost, resistance risk, and off-target effects, pick the one with the quicker or less drawn-out mode of action; and (b) in a handful of high-value, low-pressure cases — spraying vegetables for cosmetic damage, or spraying on a hunch because the calendar says so — raise the action threshold a notch even when the yield-loss arithmetic says spray.

The audience is not philosophers. They're IPM entomologists who think in life tables, degree-day models, and resistance allele frequencies; a couple of them think "sentience" is a word they last heard in a freshman seminar. There's one theoretical ecologist who will push me on whether killing invasive pest insects counts as a welfare improvement or just a smaller harm, and there are two or three grad students who've been reading the new insect-welfare literature and will either be my only allies or my most annoying critics, because they'll know alot of details I don't.

Give me a prep sheet for the Q and A. I want the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way these particular people would phrase them, not how a philosopher would tidy them up. For each objection, tell me what to concede on the spot, what to hold, and where my reply is weakest — including the objections where the right move is "yes, you're right, and here's why it doesn't sink me." Then flag the one objection I'm secretly praying nobody asks, and tell me the single claim in my talk that has to land clean for any of the rest to survive. I should admit I'm not a bug person myself and may be off on some of the pest-specific details.
