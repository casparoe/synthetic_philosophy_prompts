Okay, I keep using a distinction in my head when I teach a unit on forgiveness and I want someone who actually works on Strawson's reactive attitudes to check it before I put it in a paper. The term I've been kicking around is "the half-life of resentment." Here's the observation: if someone wrongs me and then literally nothing about the wrong changes — no apology, no repair, no new information — my resentment still tends to fade over time. Not to zero and not smoothly, but there's a decay curve. Yet when philosophers argue about whether resentment is fitting, they mostly treat fittingness as atemporal: the wrong either grounds the resentment or it doesn't, and if it grounded it at t1 it still grounds it at t10. So either ordinary people are systematically defective (resentment ought to stay at full strength until the demand behind it is answered, and the fading is just weakness), or fittingness has a temporal dimension that's being ignored. I lean toward the second, and I want to defend something stronger than that: the decay isn't just a psychological accident layered on top of the attitude, it's partly constitutive of it — resentment is a demand with a built-in statute of limitations. But I worry this collapses into saying forgiveness is the default and resentment needs constant justification to keep existing, which also feels wrong. An example: a colleague takes credit for my work in a meeting; that day I'm genuinely furious; two years later, still no acknowledgment, I don't trust them, but what I feel is more like a low-grade wariness than the original resentment. Is that wariness the same attitude decayed, or a different attitude that replaced it?

Could you play the role of a philosopher who works on the reactive attitudes and moral responsibility and give me an honest assessment? Before you poke holes in it, start by steelmanning the strongest version of the "half-life" idea. Then: is this already a thing in the literature under some name I'm not remembering (it feels too obvious not to be)? Is the strong claim — that expiration is internal to the attitude, not just a contingent fact about how our emotions happen to work — coherent, or does it confuse the attitude with its typical causes? And please seperate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested here; I don't want to present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
