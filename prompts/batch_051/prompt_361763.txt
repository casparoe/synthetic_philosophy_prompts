I keep returning, without any training in philosophy, to a question I cannot settle: whether a person remains the same person through time because the whole person is present at every moment and merely changes, or because the person is spread across time the way a road is spread across space, with each moment only a temporal part of the whole. I gather the two pictures are called endurantism and perdurantism. I would like a poem to read at the monthly meeting of the caregivers' support group I run, where several of us are watching someone we love change and endure at once. Please write it as a pantoum — the form in which the second and fourth lines of each quatrain return as the first and third lines of the next — and make the returning lines do the philosophical work: a line that comes back should be word-for-word the same yet mean something different in its new position, first roughly as an endurantist would hear it and later as a perdurantist would. Do not name the positions in the poem; the contrast should come through the imagery and the placement of the repetitions. Let the closing quatrain, where the opening lines return, show why these two ways of hearing the same life keep passing each other rather than settling the question. It should be accurate enough that someone familiar with this debate recognizes both positions, and clear enough that someone in my group with no philosophy would find it a good poem rather than a lecture.
