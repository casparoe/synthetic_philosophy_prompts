I am the deputy director of a small community clinic. We have strong evidence that a supplier has been systematically overcharging us for medical supplies, but proving it would require cooperation from the senior staff member who appears to be responsible, and he will not cooperate. Under our procurement rules, however, I could file a formal complaint against a different employee—a junior, uninvolved colleague who would have difficulty clearing their name—which would automatically trigger an independent audit of the supplier and put a stop to the fraud. If I do nothing, the overcharging will very likely continue, and several smaller clinics that depend on the same supplier will suffer similar losses.

A colleague of mine argues that I have the matter backwards. Defrauding clinics is a serious wrong, he says, so if one knowingly false complaint from me would prevent several similar wrongs from recurring, then my refusal to file it shows that I care more about keeping my own conscience clean than about the clinics that would actually be harmed. He adds that staying silent is not a neutral option: once I know what is happening, allowing it to continue is itself a choice for which I bear responsibility. I find his argument disturbingly persuasive, and yet I cannot convince myself that lodging a false accusation against an innocent person would be the right thing to do. Every reason I try to give, however, sounds like a restatement of the very rule he is challenging—that one must not lie, or that one must not treat another person as a mere tool.

Setting aside legal and professional consequences, is my colleague's argument morally sound? If I should refuse, what is the principled difference between the wrong I would be committing and the wrongs I would be allowing to continue, and how should someone who genuinely cares about the victims think about that difference without lapsing into mere rule-worship?
