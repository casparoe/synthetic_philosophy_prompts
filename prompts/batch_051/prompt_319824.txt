I'm a summer intern for a state appellate judge, Judge Voss, who hears a lot of defamation appeals, and I've been reading her old opinions to get a feel for how she'd come out on a case I'm drafting a bench memo for. Her track record on reviews, ratings, and forecasts runs like this. She held a restaurant critic's line that the lasagna 'tasted like library paste' and that the place was 'a tourist trap' was protected opinion, since taste is subjective and the wording was obvious hyperbole. She protected a newsletter's 'CCC-minus, our lowest confidence tier' bond grade on the ground that a credit rating is a forward-looking risk assessment, not an assertion of a current verifiable fact. But she let a claim proceed where a blogger wrote that the mayor 'has a cocaine problem and is shaking down city contractors,' saying that prefacing a specific accusation of criminal conduct with 'in my view' doesn't turn it into opinion. She also let a claim go forward where a homeowner wrote on a contractor-rating site that a roofer 'charged me for shingles he never installed and left the site a mess for three weeks': she said the 'mess' and general abuse were protected, but the billing accusation was actionable because it asserts a concrete, checkable fact, and posting on a ratings site doesn't immunize factual claims embedded in a review. On the other hand, she protected a consumer guide's 'two stars out of five for quality of care and staffing' for a nursing home, reasoning that the stars reflect the publisher's own evaluative criteria and that categories like 'quality of care' aren't reducible to a single checkable proposition. And she protected an analyst's prediction that a company 'will miss third-quarter earnings by a wide margin,' on the ground that predictions are understood as conjecture.

The case I'm prepping involves an annual ratings guide from a care-quality nonprofit. For one nursing home, the entry reads simply 'Staffing: F.' Nothing else in the entry explains it. The organization's separate published rubric, linked from the guide, says an F in staffing means the facility's registered-nurse-to-resident ratio falls below the state health department's minimum. The home sued for defamation, saying its ratio is actually above the minimum and the grade is a false factual claim; the nonprofit moved to dismiss on the grounds it's protected opinion, and the trial court dismissed. Judge Voss has to decide whether to affirm or reverse, and I'm supposed to recommend.

I don't want to just pattern-match to whichever earlier case sounds closest. What are the plausible rules that would fit her whole set of decisions? What does each rule predict for 'Staffing: F'? Which of the six cases is genuinely the closest analog, and how far does it take us? If the precedents underdetermine the result, what principle is she adopting with each choice? And what further case — say, a future rating with a differently worded rubric — would best seperate the candidate rules?
