A research institute's Code of Conduct contains this clause:

"No employee shall accept, directly or indirectly, any gift, favour, hospitality, loan, or other thing of value from any person with whom the employee has official dealings, or from a member of that person's family, where the thing could reasonably be regarded as likely to place the employee under a sense of personal obligation."

I am a researcher at the institute. During fieldwork in a society I shall call Talai, I have been interviewing a senior official in the municipal government and members of his staff. The official's family has now asked me, as a mark of respect, to stand as sponsor at his daughter's naming ceremony. In the local practice, a sponsor becomes a kind of kin to the family for life: each side is thereafter expected to exchange gifts, labour, and help on request, and the bond brings heavy duties as well as advantages on both sides. It is not, to local eyes, a present or a bribe; if I refuse, the family will take it as a serious insult and I shall lose my research access. No one there would think my accepting made me compromised.

What do the words of the clause require? What does its evident purpose require? What would the drafters have said if they had been asked about ceremonial kin-sponsorship, and what would a reasonable reader understand the clause to forbid? Set out what each approach yields, then say which approach should govern this case and why. Finally, say what your answer implies about the wider question: can a rule built on a picture of the impartial official be exported unchanged to a society in which trust and public cooperation are sustained through exactly such standing obligations?
