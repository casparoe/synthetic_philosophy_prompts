Two coworkers of mine got into it at lunch over charity and expected value. I'll recount it and then ask you to referee.

(1) Nate said it's irrational to give only to charities with a track record, if you actually believe in expected value. A bednet charity might save one life per $5,000, but a group working to prevent a global catastrophe might have, on an honest estimate, a one-in-100,000 chance of preventing a billion deaths. That's an expected 10,000 lives saved for the same $5,000, so the math says fund the speculative one.

(2) Rachel replied that this is how you end up wiring money to an online stranger who says his one-man project has a one-in-a-trillion chance of saving quadrillions of future lives. Once you allow tiny probabilities times huge payoffs, there is always a bigger payoff to make the expectation say whatever the stranger wants.

(3) Nate said the stranger's probability isn't a real credence at all. The catastrophe group has a model and evidence; the stranger's claim is unsupported, and an unsupported probability deserves zero weight, not a small but positive weight.

(4) Rachel said that just pushes the problem around: "unsupported" is a judgment you get to make after you've already decided the stranger's conclusion is absurd, and if you round tiny probabilities to zero whenever you dislike the conclusion, you've stopped doing expected-value reasoning and started doing something you won't name.

(5) Nate answered that she's left with a theory under which no decision is safe. Whatever you'd otherwise do, the mugger just names a payoff big enough to outweigh it, so a theory that can be gamed by naming arbitrarily big payoffs can't be a theory of rational choice. One of Rachel's premises has to give way, he said.

Who's right about what? Go move by move: say what holds up, what doesn't, and what either of them would need to add to make their position defensible. Also tell me how confident you are overall and which single point you're least sure about.
