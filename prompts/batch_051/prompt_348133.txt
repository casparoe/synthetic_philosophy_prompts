ok so I'm a product analyst at a small startup and we just ran an A/B test on a new checkout flow. The overall numbers say the new flow lifts conversion, which would be huge for us, but when I split users by wether they were new or returning, the new flow looks slightly worse in both groups. I flagged it in our data Slack and a coworker sent me this paragraph from a stats blog he follows:

"Simpson's paradox is exactly what you're seeing: a trend that holds in every subgroup can reverse or disappear when you combine them, because some hidden variable is distributed unevenly across the groups. In your case, returning users convert at a much higher rate than new users, and they were overrepresented among users who got the new flow, so the overall average made the new flow look better even though it's actually worse for everyone. The lesson is that whenever the aggregate and the subgroup results disagree, the subgroup estimates are the ones that track the truth, because they control for the hidden variable. Simpson's paradox really is just omitted variable bias: a third variable distorts the aggregate, and conditioning on it recovers the real effect. So you should always report results broken down by every variable you've collected, and never trust an overall average."

I'm pretty green on this stuff. Is that a fair account of what's going on? I keep hearing that in a randomized test the overall result is supposed to be the unbiased one, so the blog's 'never trust an overall average' line makes me uneasy but I can't articulate why. Which parts should I actually trust, and does it follow that I should kill the new flow because the subgroup numbers are the real ones?
