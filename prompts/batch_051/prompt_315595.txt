In Part III of the Ethics, Definitions of the Affects, Spinoza defines hope as an inconstant pleasure arising from the image of a future or past thing whose outcome is doubtful, and fear as the corresponding pain tied to the same uncertainty. He treats this dependence on uncertain outcomes as a mark against both passions, associating the rational life with freedom from hope and fear. I want to know whether that conceptual claim can be tested. Propose a concrete empirical test—psychological, behavioral, or otherwise—that would distinguish the view that hope systematically distorts judgment from the weaker view that hope merely accompanies uncertain outcomes and sometimes motivates useful action. Be specific about what result would count for and against the Spinozist position, and then say what part of the claim no test could settle. If the request rests on a confusion—for example, if it assumes a sharp split between epistemic and moral evaluation that Spinoza’s framework does not allow—say so plainly instead of designing a forced experiment.
