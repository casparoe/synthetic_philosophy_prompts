Someone in my philosophy of physics seminar keeps shutting down debates with "science only ever gives us structure, not the nature of things." The other week they used it to dismiss a question about whether quarks are real objects with some intrinsic character, or just placeholders that do whatever the standard model's equations need them to. I honestly can't tell whether their making a boring point about our models, a Kantian point about the limits of knowledge, or a much stronger claim about what exists. Could you pull apart the different precise things that one-liner might be claiming—a claim about what we can know, a claim about what there is, a claim about how theoretical terms get their meaning, maybe a deflationary claim about what scientific theories assert—and go through which readings are platitudes, which are substantive but defensible, and which are just false? Then say which reading they were actually leaning on when they dismissed the quark question, and whether it really supports that move or proves too much. One more thing: I vaguely remember an old objection that if "structure" just means "satisfying the same formal relations," then the view either goes empty or commits you to saying the world is nothing but abstract relations, and I'd like to know which reading of the slogan, if any, avoids that.
