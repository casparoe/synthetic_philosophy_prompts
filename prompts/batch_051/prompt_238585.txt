I'm a software engineer — my background is in programming-language implementation and formal verification, not philosophy — and I've been working up notes for a short essay about the Lucas–Penrose claim that the human mind cannot be a machine. I'd like comments on the notes rather than a polished essay from you, but the notes are fairly complete. I'm especially interested in whether my computational restatement is faithful, and where it most needs repair before I'd show it to someone with real philosophical training.

Notes for the essay:

1. I treat the mind as a program P that enumerates the mathematical statements a particular person would sign off on; call this P's theorem list. Assume P is consistent (it never lists a falsehood) and strong enough to talk about its own enumeration, the way a programming language can introspect its own source.

2. Gödel's first incompleteness theorem gives a sentence G_P that asserts, in P's own language, that G_P is not on P's theorem list. If P is consistent, G_P is true but P cannot list it. This has the same shape as a diagonal argument in computing: a halting analyzer cannot decide its own halting behavior on a self-referential input.

3. Lucas and Penrose then want to say: the person in question can see that G_P is true, while P cannot list it; since P was arbitrary, no program can capture the person's output; therefore the mind is not a program. That conclusion seems enormous, and my engineering instinct is that this is exactly where the argument overreaches.

4. The step from accepting that P is consistent to accepting that G_P is true is short and mechanical once the consistency premise is granted — one conditional inference, essentially. So the only non-mechanical ingredient is the assertion that P is consistent. But a consistent-looking system cannot in general prove its own consistency; from the inside, a claim of self-consistency is the kind of self-certification I would not accept in code review unless it came with a proof or a test. So I doubt that the human is exercising a non-algorithmic ability here; at most, she is accepting a premise that P cannot prove.

5. A program can print its own Gödel sentence as a string — a quine-like construction — it just cannot add it to its theorem list while remaining that exact enumerator. So what is limited is proof, not output or behavior. The theorem therefore does not constrain what a machine can say or do; it constrains only what an enumerator can certify.

6. I can see the standard escape route: the mind is not a fixed P, because it can add G_P as a new axiom and move on. But the rule — given any P, add G_P — is itself mechanical; fold that rule in and you get a new program Q, and Gödel gives you G_Q, and the treadmill repeats. I do not yet see why this iteration ever becomes non-algorithmic, and I would like to know whether that is a real gap in my understanding or the standard position.
