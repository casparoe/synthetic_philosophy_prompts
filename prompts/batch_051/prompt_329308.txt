Two people in my study group are stuck on this version of the two-envelope paradox. Setup: one envolope has twice as much money as the other, you pick one blindly, and then you may look inside before deciding whether to switch.

Maya: Once you open and see an amount, say $20, the other is either $10 or $40. Nothing in the setup gives odds on those two possibilities, so we should treat them as equally likely. Then switching has an expected value of $25, beating $20. Same reasoning for any amount, so you should switch no matter what.

Dan: That can't be right, because before you open anything symmetry guarantees switching has no expected advantage — the two envelopes are on equal footing — and what you see can't make switching favorable at every possible amount. So the 50/50 after seeing the amount has to be wrong at least sometimes.

Maya: But all that shows is the problem is ill-posed without a prior over the pair. Without a prior there's no conditional probability at all, so "always switch" isn't valid — but you also can't say the posterior isn't 50/50. With a real prior, say a fixed upper limit, you'd sometimes switch and sometimes not, which is just what Dan wants.

Dan: The pre-opening symmetry isn't prior-dependent, so "missing a prior" isn't the real story. The real story is that a uniform 50/50 split between half and double can't be maintained for all observed amounts under any actual distribution; the mistake is conditionalizing on the observed amount while ignoring how the pair got filled.

Neither budged. Adjudicate point by point: who's right about which specific claim, who's wrong about what, and what are they both missing? Start by separating the claims that are actually doing the work.
