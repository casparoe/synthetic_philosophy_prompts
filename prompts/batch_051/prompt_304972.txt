I teach an advanced undergraduate philosophy of economics seminar to about eighteen students, and I want to spend one seventy-five-minute meeting on ergodicity economics without opening with a lecture. Please design a single classroom activity—a game or structured simulation with a debrief, not a talk—that makes the students themselves generate and then recognize the difference between an ensemble average and a time average. Here is the conceptual core I want it to embody. Consider a repeated multiplicative bet: wealth is multiplied by 1.5 on heads and by 0.6 on tails. Across many players each playing a single round, the arithmetic mean wealth is multiplied by 1.05, so the ensemble average rises. But a single player who every round stakes the entire balance has a time-average per-round growth rate of 0.5 ln 1.5 + 0.5 ln 0.6, which is about −0.051, so the typical individual balance decays while the positive cross-sectional average is carried by a few very lucky paths. The students should run into that divergence through their own choices and visible results, and the terms ensemble average, time average, and non-ergodic should be introduced only in the debrief. The activity should last about fifty minutes, use no real money, and leave a running record of individual balances alongside the group average.

Commit to one definite design rather than offering alternatives: specify the number of players and rounds, how the coin is run, whether participants choose their fraction staked each round or are assigned one, and why those choices serve the lesson. Make the debrief map concrete moments in the play back to the formal distinction, say what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run and how the facilitator should handle it, and be explicit about which part of the concept cannot be reproduced in the room and must be supplied.

Please present the answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introductory or concluding text. The five items should cover: the setup and sequence of play; the precise divergence the activity is built to produce and how the record-keeping makes it visible; the debrief mapping from the play to the ensemble average, the time-average growth rate, and non-ergodicity; the likeliest failure modes and how to fix them; and the feature the classroom cannot reproduce.
