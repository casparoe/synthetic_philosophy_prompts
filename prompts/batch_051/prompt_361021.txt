I’ve been teaching a philosophy of mind seminar built around a thought experiment in which one imagines reading a dialogue with Einstein’s brain — not as a metaphor for intelligence, but as a literal record of a brain’s causal activity. A sharp version of the thesis came up in discussion: if a book contained not just a description of a brain’s states but a fully explicit encoding of its state transitions, written so that the text itself could in principle be used to run the process forward, then the book could be conscious even if no one ever opened it. A student objected that this turns a conceptual claim about consciousness into something suspiciously close to an empirical claim about a physical object. Another student replied that any claim about what could be conscious has empirically testable shape, even if we can’t yet build the thing.

I want to push the class past plausibility intuitions and toward evidence. Please propose how one could test the claim that a suitably encoded book could be conscious. Your response should specify a concrete experimental or observational design, state what result would favor the claim and what result would count against it, and then identify what the test could not settle. Give the response as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
