I got pulled into this by a dumb fight about my daughter's math homework, and now I keep seeing the same question everywhere. Here are my gut calls. I want someone to tell me what principle, if any, is actually behind them, and which of my gut calls I have to give up.

1. The homework asked: solve x^2 = 4. My daughter wrote "-2 and 2." The teacher took a point off because the answer key said "2 and -2." I think that's nonsense. It's the same two roots; the order can't matter. They're a set, not a sequence.

2. But I do think it's fine to write sqrt(4) = 2, even though -2 also squares to 4. We just picked the positive one. My gut says that choice is arbitrary but harmless. Then I catch myself: one root is positive and the other is negative, so maybe it isn't arbitrary at all. That bugs me.

3. A guy at work who took a lot of math says it's different with the square roots of -1. Call one of them i and the other is -i. He claims there's no fact about the numbers themselves that says which one really deserves the name i: any true sentence stays true if you swap i and -i everywhere and leave the ordinary real numbers alone. I think I believe him. This feels deeper than the homework case, because with 2 and -2 there's a real difference (positive vs negative), but with i and -i there isn't. Except I also think i and -i are still two different numbers, not one number with two names. Is that even coherent?

4. Before my sister's twins were born, they were labeled A and B on the scan by who was closer to the cervix. The babies moved around, the labels flipped, and the measurements attached to each label started getting mixed up. Which one got called A at the start felt completely arbitrary, but the label staying stuck to the right baby mattered enormously. So: the original pick was arbitrary, but keeping it fixed was not.

5. At work somebody wanted to renumber our customer IDs so there'd be no gaps. I argued against it. The IDs are meaningless labels, but old invoices and spreadsheets point to them; changing them breaks references. So here I'm saying even meaningless labels can matter once they've been used.

6. The fire marshal made us label the exits A, B, C going clockwise from the main door. Clockwise felt arbitrary to me, but I also get that a firefighter coming through the north door needs to know which one is A. So the shared rule matters even though the exact rule is arbitrary.

My working theory is something like: a choice of label doesn't matter when the thing you're choosing between are genuinely interchangeable, and it matters when the label becomes a hook that other stuff hangs on, like records, references, or agreements between people. But I can't make that precise, and cases 2 and 3 already seem to poke holes in it.

So: what principle best captures all these, or gets as close as possible? Then test it on a case I haven't settled. The software we use at work solves a quadratic and prints the two roots in whatever order the solver happened to spit them out. I go back and forth on whether we should force it to always print them, say, smaller root first. What does the principle say about that? And be honest about which of my gut calls I have to drop.
