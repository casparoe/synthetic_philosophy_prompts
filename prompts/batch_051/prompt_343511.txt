I'm an observability engineer. I reason fluently in terms of services and execution paths, instrumentation, log and trace pipelines, metric aggregation and sampling, dashboards, and the distinction between the state a system is actually in and the state its telemetry reports. I also work constantly with the difference between a monitor that samples a system from outside and a service's own self-report, where the reporter and the thing reported on share a substrate. Restate illusionism about phenomenal consciousness in exactly this vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. By illusionism I mean the position that there are no phenomenal properties — no qualia, no fact of there being something it is like for the subject — and that introspection systematically presents mental states as having such properties even though they do not. Translate the whole position: the concession that conscious states exist in a functional or access sense, the denial of a further phenomenal layer, the error theory about why introspection gets this wrong, and the explanation of why the illusion is so resistantly compelling. Then give the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly, which translate only by stretching my terms, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all? For each failure, say whether my vocabulary is missing something the illusionist is entitled to, or whether the illusionist's claim depended on a distinction that evaporates once the translation is made. Be strict about one point in particular: in my world a dashboard is a representation rendered for an operator who looks at it, and the dashboard itself does not undergo anything. If what it is like gets translated as what the dashboard displays, does the subject of experience become an internal operator reading the display? If so, does illusionism owe us an account of that operator that an observability vocabulary cannot supply without smuggling the phenomenal back in?
