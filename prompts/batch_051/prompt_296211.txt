Take the standard bystander-at-the-switch trolley case: a trolley heads toward five track workers, and a bystander could divert it onto a side track with one worker. Change one feature: the switch is stochastic. If thrown, it diverts the trolley with probability p and otherwise fails, leaving the trolley on the main track; the outcome is settled immediately, no second intervention is possible, and the bystander knows p. Analyze this chancy case through the lens of the doing/allowing and killing/letting die distinctions. Address the following. In the original deterministic case, is throwing best classified as killing the one and refraining as letting the five die? In the stochastic case, is the ex ante act itself a killing, a mere risking, or a probability-weighted mixture of the two possible outcomes, and does the classification change ex post? Finally, what does the distinction, with its special burden on doing over allowing, imply about which values of p leave the standard permissive verdict intact? Do not simply compute expected lives saved; the question is how the distinction itself handles an intervention that is only probabilistically connected to each death.
