I've been poking at the philosophy of chance and credence lately, and after a long argument with a friend who does statistics I've come around to something like the Principal Principle: when you know the objective chance of an event is p, your rational credence should be p, with the usual caveat that your other evidence has to be admissable — roughly, no crystal balls, no newspapers from the future. But I've started to state the view a bit differently than the standard version. I want to say deference to chance isn't a one-off constraint; it's a standing rule about what kinds of evidence can move you. If you already know the chance of X is p, then information that isn't about the chance itself — information that's just about how the world will turn out — shouldn't shift your credence at all. The only thing that licenses moving off p is evidence that the chance is different from what you thought, or evidence that you were wrong about the actual chances.

What I actually want is a map of the position's exposure: what would count against it? I don't just mean the stock objections; I want the cases, intuitions, and formal results that should make a defender genuinely worried, sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to say which ones a committed defender should accept as real counterevidence versus which ones they should dismiss as falling under the admissibility caveat or as attacks on a strawman. For instance, undermining-future cases (where the history already guarantees a different outcome) and the Sleeping Beauty setup both seem to put pressure on the view, but in pretty different ways. And there's the expert worry: a forecaster with a great track record who isn't latching onto chances at all, just very good at reading situations. Does the view have to call it irrational to defer to them? I'm especially curious whether the admissibility clause has been doing too much work — whether I've protected the principle into unfalsifiability, and whether that's even a defect for a norm of rationality of this kind.
