I’m teaching an adult-education course on community and belonging, and I want to use Frank Capra’s It’s a Wonderful Life as a case study for a session on what is lost when thick communal ties dissolve. I keep thinking of a passage near the end of John Dewey’s The Public and Its Problems where Dewey says something roughly like this: the modern age has produced a Great Society, an enormous web of interdependence created by steam, electricity, and mass communication, but it has not produced a Great Community; the machine age has given us a large-scale society, but not a community in which shared experience is actually felt, understood, and communicated across that scale. I won’t trust my memory enough to quote him exactly, but that is the thought I want to test against the film.

Read the film through that Deweyan lens. On the surface, Bedford Falls looks like exactly the kind of face-to-face community Dewey worried was being eclipsed, and Pottersville looks like the Great Society stripped of communal ties: anonymous transactions, commercial noise, atomized pleasure, and no settled mutual obligations. But I suspect that reading is too neat. Does the film actually reveal the costs and exclusions of small-town community as well—the parochialism, the way George’s life is consumed by others’ needs, the quiet social control—and does it show Bedford Falls as already dependent on wider economic forces that nearly destroy it? I want a critical reading that is faithful in both directions: one that does not reduce the film to an illustration of Dewey, and does not distort Dewey to fit the film. In particular, what does the film’s resolution—the community literally showing up with cash to save the Building and Loan—add to, or miss about, Dewey’s claim that a Great Community would require shared inquiry and communication, not just sentiment, charity, or emergency solidarity? And what would Dewey’s distinction lead us to notice in the film that a casual viewer would overlook?
