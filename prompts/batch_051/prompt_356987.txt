Early in 2003, during the run-up to the Iraq War, the Bush administration made a public claim, sourced to British intelligence, that Saddam Hussein had recently sought significant quantities of uranium from Africa. The claim later failed verification and became a case study in how official public statements can shape what a nation treats as settled. I want you to analyze that episode through David Lewis’s scorekeeping model in his paper Scorekeeping in a Language Game. Keep your focus on the mechanics of presupposition and accommodation, not on the broader politics except where the politics exposes a limitation in the model.

Please organize the response into the following sections, in this order:

1. The Initial Scoreboard (about 250 words)  
Reconstruct the public conversation about Iraq and weapons of mass destruction in early 2003. What was already presupposed, what was treated as at-issue, and what sorts of moves were permissible for different participants—the administration, Congress, the press, foreign governments, and United Nations inspectors? Identify the most important components of the conversational scoreboard before the claim was made.

2. The Claim as a Conversational Move (about 400 words)  
Reconstruct the claim’s presuppositional structure. Distinguish what was asserted from what was presupposed, and identify what a hearer who did not object would have had to accommodate. Pay special attention to the attribution to a foreign intelligence service: how does that framing affect what gets added to the scoreboard, and why might it make the move harder to reject than a direct assertion of the same content?

3. Accommodation and Its Costs (about 400 words)  
Use Lewis’s idea that the conversational score tends to adjust to make a move acceptable, but only if the required adjustment is not too disruptive. Show which components of the scoreboard shifted, which presuppositions were accommodated, and which were challenged. Explain how media repetition altered the record, and what the model predicts about the later retraction: should acknowledging that the claim should not have been included have reset the scoreboard to its earlier state?

4. Where the Scorekeeping Model Fails (about 300 words)  
Identify what the model misses or misdescribes in this episode. Consider strategic speakers who exploit cooperative norms, audiences that do not share a single scoreboard, mass media as a repetition mechanism that is not a cooperative conversationalist, and the asymmetry between adding and removing presuppositions. Be precise about which of these are contingent political facts and which are principled limits of the framework.

5. Verdict (about 150 words)  
State whether the episode confirms, extends, or refutes Lewis’s scorekeeping model, and say what a defender of the model would need to add in order to handle official misstatement, non-cooperative speech, and the social life of a claim after it enters the public record.
