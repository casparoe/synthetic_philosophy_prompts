ok so we read that lewis carroll bit where achilles and the tortoise go in circles, and ive been stuck on it since. the reading i keep wanting to defend is something like this: you can always take a rule like modus ponens and treat it as one more premise, and then the tortoise just asks what licenses the move from that new premise plus the others to the conclusion, and off we go again. so rules of inference arent the kind of thing that can compel a conclusion — theres no proposition you can add that forces anyone to take the step, and deduction bottoms out in something we do, a practice of going on, not in a hidden extra fact. i like this because it stops us treating the 'must' of logic as a spooky fact about the world and makes it a feature of how we actually reason. but then i hit this wall: when the tortoise grants the premises and still refuses the conclusion, i have a very firm intuition that the tortoise is wrong. not just declining to play our game, not just being uncooperative, but genuinely in error — same family as someone who infers the wrong thing from the premises. and i cant see how a mere practice can underwrite a genuine error unless the practice is already normative, which feels like it sneaks back in the very thing the reading was trying to avoid. so: does the practice-based reading have room for the tortoise being wrong, or is 'wrong' just 'violates the norms of a practice we happen to stand inside,' and if the latter, should i give up the intuition or give up the reading? give me the strongest version of both sides, and then a real verdict on whether theres a third option — a practice account that keeps genuine normativity without making logic a body of force-giving propositions — and say what would actually justify each move (dropping the intuition, revising the reading, or whatever you land on).
