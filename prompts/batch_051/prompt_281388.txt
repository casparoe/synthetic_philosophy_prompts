I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on the history of Protestant hermeneutics this term, and I also sit on my church's adult-education committee. In our planning meetings, it happens roughly once a month that two small-group leaders disagree about what a passage means for the topic we are about to teach: whether a command directed at a first-century congregation transfers without qualification to a practice that did not exist then, or whether a passage one leader treats as a literal directive is read by another as a figure of speech. The stakes are not disciplinary, but they are real in a quieter way, since the decision shapes what gets taught for the next month and ad hoc rulings have begun to wear people out. The committee operates on a loose Protestant commitment to sola scriptura, and none of us wants to settle these disputes by simply deferring to the pastor or a denominational statement, since that seems to reintroduce the very problem the principle is supposed to avoid: a fallible human interpreter with final say. At the same time, when two people each claim to be giving what the text plainly says, appealing back to the text does not say which of the two readings is the plain one, and invoking the community's discernment seems only to move the same regress one level up. Our pastor has asked me to draft a reusable procedure the committee can run in about an hour whenever this happens, something a thoughtful layperson could follow without having taken my seminar. It should be an ordered set of questions with, for each, a statement of what would count as a sufficient answer, along with clear stop conditions for when the procedure yields a conclusion and when it should instead hand the dispute to the pastor or to the congregation as a matter of theological judgment rather than something the text itself can settle.

I would like the procedure to take the regress problem seriously rather than paper over it. In particular, it should force us to distinguish questions about what the words most plausibly meant in context, what kind of speech act or genre the passage is, and how its normative force carries over to a present-day question, and it should flag every point at which the procedure itself is making a theological or hermeneutical choice it has not made explicit. Please spell out the procedure in a form we could adopt at a committee meeting, and then run it on two recurring test cases: first, deciding whether a biblical prohibition aimed at a specific ancient practice extends to a structurally similar modern practice; and second, deciding whether a disagreement about literal versus figurative reading is the kind of dispute the procedure can resolve, or one it should refuse to settle. I don't need the history of interpretation or citations; I need a working instrument.
