Here's an asymmetry that's been bugging me since I retired from chemistry and started reading moral philosophy. Ann pays Bruno to kill someone. Ann never touches a weapon and never sees the victim; Bruno does the killing. Most people, and the law, treat Ann as a murderer—often worse than Bruno, because he was just her instrument. Now take Carla, who runs a small machine shop. A customer she's supplied for years orders a batch of parts, and Carla knows in a general way that this customer outfits a security force that tortures people. She doesn't know which end product her parts go into, or which detainee they'll be used on, or even whether this particular batch is for that side of the business. Most people shrug at Carla: she's just running a business, the chain is too long, she doesn't make the decisions. But Ann's connection to the killing is also entirely through someone else's action. Both contribute to a wrong through another person's hands; neither does the wrong.

The obvious difference is intending a specific wrong versus merely foreseeing a diffuse one. But when I test that distinction it goes wobbly. A bank clerk who knowingly wires the payment that secures a torture contract doesn't intend the torture either, yet people mostly don't let him off as easily as Carla. So the intuition is tracking something finer than intention-versus-foresight, and I can't pin down what its actually tracking.

Act as a philosopher who works on complicity and moral responsibility. Think carefully, start by brainstorming two or three candidate principles that could generate both the Ann and Carla judgments, pressure-test them, and then give me your honest verdict: is there a defensible principle here, or are we just living with an inconsistency (or with the psychology of blaming the person we can picture at the scene)? Should I keep both judgments, revise one, or give one up? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning; no preamble and don't restate my question.
