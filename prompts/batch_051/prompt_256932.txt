Max Black’s spheres thought experiment is usually read as a counterexample to the principle of the identity of indiscernibles: a possible world contains nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres two miles apart, and any purely qualitative predicate true of one is true of the other, yet there are two objects rather than one. Suppose now that the underlying physics of such a world is quantum mechanics and the two spheres are two bosons in exactly the same single-particle state. On the standard quantum-statistical treatment, swapping the labels on the two bosons does not produce a distinct physical possibility; the state is symmetrized, and the labeled descriptions are not treated as corresponding to two different individuals. Does Black’s argument survive this change? In particular, which premise is actually under pressure: the assumption that there are two distinguishable objects at all, the assumption that qualitative sameness is the relevant kind of sameness, or the assumption that indiscernibility entails identity only if one has already smuggled in haecceities? Say whether a repaired version of Black’s argument could make the same point in a quantum setting and, if so, what it would show. Also say what would change your mind: what result or conceptual argument about quantum individuation or about the identity of indiscernibles would lead you to the opposite verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
