In the Theravada monastic code a monk may eat meat only if it is pure in three ways: he has not seen, heard, or suspected that the animal was killed for his sake. The argument behind the rule, as I understand it, is that the monk's volition is clean — he is not killing and is not asking anyone to kill for him. That was workable when a monk took alms from a village and could know the food's history. Now suppose a monk in a city buys a packaged chicken breast from a supermarket. No particular chicken was killed for him; the supply chain kills to a demand forecast, and his purchase is a tiny part of the signal that determines how many chickens are killed next month. Is the meat still pure in the three ways? If it is, has the rule lost what made it matter — and if it is not, what does suspected even mean now, and does the expanded rule end up forbidding almost any food bought in a system that kills animals as a side effect? A consequentialist Buddhist might say the rule was only ever a proxy for not contributing to death, and this is the case where the proxy comes apart from the thing it tracked. Is that right, or is the rule tracking something else that survives the supply chain intact? I am asking whether the old argument survives the supermarket, not what the official rule says.
