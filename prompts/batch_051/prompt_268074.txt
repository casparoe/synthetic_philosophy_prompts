Last weekend a group of us were talking about our friend Rosa, who is generous and warm and also reliably twenty minutes late to everything, and who has a habit of saying "I'll send you that thing tonight" and then sending it a week later or never. Maya and Dev got into an argument about it and I mostly just listened. I found myself nodding along with both of them at different points, and honestly couldnt referee the thing, so I'm bringing it to you.

Maya started: "Small promises are promises. When Rosa says she'll be somewhere at 7 and shows up at 7:25 without texting, she's broken a commitment. The fact that no single instance is a big deal is exactly what hides it. People act like big betrayals are the moral crises, but trust is actually built and eroded in the small stuff. If everyone treated 'I'll be there at 7' and 'I'll send it tonight' as optional whenever it got inconvenient, promising would stop working as a practice. So each instance is a real wrong, just a small one."

Dev pushed back: "That move from 'everyone' to 'this instance' is too fast. Rosa isn't claiming nobody should keep promises; she's bad at estimating time and bad at saying no. And 'promise' is doing alot of work. Most of these are loose social forecasts, not vows. The harm of someone scrolling their phone for twenty minutes, or getting a link on Thursday instead of Tuesday, is annoyance, not a rights violation. If we moralize every scheduling wobble, friendships turn into compliance monitoring, and people stop making casual plans at all."

Maya: "The 'bad at estimating' excuse works for the first twenty times. After years of data, Rosa knows her base rate. If you know you're basically always late and still say '7:00, definitely,' you're issuing a forecast you don't believe. That's a form of misrepresentation, not innocent optimism. And even if each instance were too small to count, the pattern does the actual damage: friends stop inviting her, make backup plans, or file her under 'can't be counted on.' Being someone others can count on is a moral description, not just a scheduling convenience."

Dev: "But now you've shifted from 'each instance is a wrong' to 'the pattern is the wrong.' Those aren't the same claim. And if the wrong only lives in the pattern, then we can't blame her for any particular lateness, because no particular lateness crosses the line. Also, context matters: being twenty-five minutes late to a crowded party where nobody's waiting on you is not the same as leaving one person alone at a restaurant. And in a friend group that has already adjusted to Rosa-time, she isn't deceiving anyone. The problematic case is strangers and new people, and there I'd agree she should disclose."

Maya: "Your relational point has a weird consequence: the exact same act, showing up 25 minutes late without a heads-up, is wrong when the person waiting is a punctual stranger and fine when it's a forgiving friend, purely because the friend won't complain. But someone's tolerance shouldn't be doing the moral work. Friends who've 'adjusted' are often just absorbing a cost, and the fact that they don't make a fuss doesn't mean there's nothing to make a fuss about. Also, the convention wasn't negotiated, it was adapted around. That's not consent to a different meaning of '7:00.'"

Dev: "I'll grant that she should be upfront with new people, and that knowingly committing to a time you'll almost certainly miss is a real failing. But that's much narrower than your original 'small promises are promises' line. What you've actually arrived at is a calibration requirement: make commitments that match your real reliability. I partly accept that. But I still wouldn't describe the original wrong as a broken promise each time. It's closer to a failure of self-knowledge that becomes other-regarding once it wastes people's time. And I'd add that some of this is capacity, not choice — for some people the difference between 7 and 7:25 just doesn't stick the same way, and a blanket rule about punctuality quietly favors people whose brains run on clock time."

Maya: "The calibration point isn't a retreat, it's what the promissory point was always about. A promise's content includes its timing. If you can't commit to 7:00, say 'closer to 7:20,' or 'I'll text before I leave.' I'm not demanding clock-worship or identical capacities. I'm saying don't make commitments you know you'll misfire on. That's the part I can universalize without turning friendship into a contract."

Dev: "Then I think we partly agree: she should stop making precise-sounding commitments she can't keep. But I'd still say the gap between us is about moral description. You want to call each miss a lie or a breach; I think the standing facts of a friendship can include forgiveness for known flakiness, and what you call 'absorbing a cost' can just be what it is to love a slightly chaotic person. The wrong you've located is in continuing to make promises she won't keep, not in being late itself."

At that point somebody changed the subject to ordering food, and I genuinely couldn't tell whether they'd converged or just run out of steam. So please adjudicate the actual back-and-forth. Specifically: who is right about (a) whether each lateness is a promise-breach, (b) whether the wrong can reside in the pattern rather than the individual instances, (c) whether the loose-plan-versus-promise distinction matters, (d) whether a friend's non-complaint changes the act's status, (e) the capacity point, and (f) the calibration point. Say what, if anything, both of them are missing. And if you had to pick one side as more right overall, which one?
