Ok so I just got back from a three-hour argument with a friend about whether God can suffer, and he layed out an argument that felt airtight in the moment and now I can't find the wrong step. It goes:

(1) A perfect being can't be acted on from outside, because being acted on would mean something else had power over it and it wasn't fully actual.
(2) Suffering just is being acted on from outside in a way that diminishes you — it's a passion, something that happens to you, not something you do.
(3) So a perfect being can't suffer.
(4) The God Christians worship is supposed to be a perfect being.
(5) The same God is also supposed to be love.
(6) But love, at least the kind that isn't just a word, means suffering with the beloved. If you can watch someone you love suffer and not be affected, that's not love, that's just benevolence or good management.
(7) So the Christian God both can't suffer and must suffer.

He pushed this all the way: even the incarnation doesn't help, because it just moves the problem to Christ's human nature while leaving the divine nature locked outside. He also brought up Anselm's old move where God does compassionate things without feeling compassion, and said it sounds like a king who pays for a hospital but has never been sad. I want to say the flaw is in (6), but I don't know what I'm then defending. Does love really require the lover to suffer, or is that just modern sentimentality? If the flaw is in (1), then what does "perfection" even mean for classical theism? Which premise should go, and what does rejecting it cost? If the whole argument is misstated or my friend's set-up is a strawman, tell me that too; I'd rather get the real position than win the argument.
