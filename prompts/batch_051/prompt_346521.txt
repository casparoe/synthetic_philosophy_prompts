I have been reconstructing an argument that appears in medieval Islamic philosophy, and I cannot figure out whether it is valid or where exactly it goes wrong. The thought experiment is this: a person is created all at once, fully grown and intellectually normal, but suspended in empty air with their eyes veiled, their limbs spread so that they touch nothing, and with no sensations of any kind—no sight, sound, smell, taste, or touch, and no memories. The argument then runs as follows. (1) Such a person would still affirm, immediately and with certainty, that they themselves exist. (2) They would not affirm that they have a body, a face, hands, or anything corporeal, because nothing in their state supplies any such idea. (3) So the self they grasp can be grasped as existing without grasping any body or bodily part. (4) If one thing is identical with another thing, then an awareness that is complete with respect to the first cannot leave the second out entirely; if you are fully aware of a thing as it is, and that thing is nothing other than some body, you cannot be wholly unaware of that body while your awareness remains complete. (5) The flying person's awareness of themselves is complete in the relevant sense: they are certain that they exist, and there is nothing missing or mistaken in their apprehension of the thing whose existence they affirm. (6) Therefore the self they grasp is not identical with any body or bodily part, and since the argument used no sensory premise, the self can exist and be known in independence from the body.

I suspect the fault lies somewhere near (4) or in the word "complete" in (5), because it seems possible to be perfectly certain that you exist while being ignorant or mistaken about what you are. But the flying man case may be special, since there is nothing else in his mind to confuse him. I would like to know: is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, under what reading of it, and what is the strongest conclusion the argument can actually support? In particular, does it establish only that self-awareness is distinct from bodily awareness, rather than that the self is actually distinct from the body?
