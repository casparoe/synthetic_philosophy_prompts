I work as a software engineer, so my background is mostly in formal systems and program semantics rather than theology or academic philosophy. Recently, though, I came across David Lewis's short discussion of the doctrine of penal substitution, and it has been occupying my thoughts. As I understand his argument, he contends that the official doctrine of penal substitution—the view that on the cross Christ bore the very punishment that human sin had made deserved, and that God's justice was satisfied by inflicting that punishment on a substitute—is almost never believed in its full form by the people who profess it. Instead, according to Lewis, what most of them actually accept is a weaker package of claims: that Christ suffered and died for us, that his suffering somehow removes or cancels our guilt, and that God can therefore forgive without being unjust. But they do not, if pressed, accept all the components that make penal substitution distinctively penal and substitutionary.

I find this distinction compelling, but I am not convinced it has the force Lewis seems to think it has. One could respond that the reduced version is the real doctrine, properly understood; or that full penal substitution is coherent even if psychologically rare; or that the reduced version collapses into a different model of atonement altogether. I want to know what the strongest overall position is.

Please write an essay on whether the full doctrine of penal substitution is a position one should hold, or whether the weaker view Lewis describes as the common belief is actually the more defensible version of the doctrine. Weigh the main arguments on both sides, but do not end on a non-committal note: defend a specific verdict about which version, if either, deserves to be called the real doctrine of penal substitution, and justify that verdict.
