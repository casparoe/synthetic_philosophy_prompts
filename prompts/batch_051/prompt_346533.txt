I am a historical sociologist, and a colleague has been pressing an argument that he says shows the distinction between path-dependent and robust historical outcomes is empty. I want to keep the distinction — it does real work when we argue about whether the Industrial Revolution, the rise of Christianity, or the outcome of a particular war was "locked in" or could easily have gone differently — but I cannot find the flaw in his steps. Here is the argument as he gives it:

(1) Any large historical outcome has a causal ancestry: a chain of events and conditions that brought it about.
(2) In any such ancestry, no matter how robust the outcome looks, there is some point at which the outcome hung on a detail that could easily have been otherwise — a storm before a battle, a minister's illness, a delayed letter, an accidental discovery, a decision that was nearly made differently.
(3) If an outcome hangs on a detail that could easily have been otherwise, then the outcome itself could easily have been otherwise.
(4) So every large historical outcome could easily have been otherwise.
(5) But "path-dependent" just means that the outcome could easily have been otherwise, while "robust" means that the outcome would have come about anyway across a wide range of nearby possible histories.
(6) By (4), the class of robust outcomes is empty, so every outcome is path-dependent.
(7) If every outcome is path-dependent, then the label "path-dependent" sorts nothing and rules nothing out; it does no descriptive or explanatory work.
(8) Therefore the path-dependence/robustness distinction is empty, and counterfactual history — the practice of asking what would have happened without this or that event — rests on a distinction that cannot be drawn.

I have a strong wish to keep the distinction, but every objection I try gets pushed back. When I say some outcomes are overdetermined, he asks whether the overdetermining causes are not themselves preceded by contingencies; when I say "robust" means higher probability rather than certainty, he replies that this is a difference of degree, not a distinction of kind, and that it does not restore the contrast the field actually uses. His standing challenge is: name a robust outcome that survives inspection, or admit that "robust" is just path-dependence we have not traced far enough back yet.

Which step in this argument should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, what has to be true of the "nearby possible histories" appealed to in (5) for the distinction to survive an argument like this, and is that notion defensible? Please tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the point you are least sure of.
