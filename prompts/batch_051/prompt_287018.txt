I got into this argument after a late-night discussion with a friend who thinks Bernoulli settled the St. Petersburg game in 1738 and there's nothing left to say. I keep coming back to it feeling like the standard fix misses what is actually strange. The usual move is to say the expected value is infinite only because we treated dollars as having constant value; make utility concave — log of wealth, say — and the game suddenly has a finite price. Case closed. But I want to go the other way and defend the claim that the paradox is not really about the shape of the utility function. It is about what happens to a decision rule when you get exactly one draw and the arithmetic mean is carried almost entirely by a tail of outcomes whose probabilities are smaller than any number you care to name.

Please make the strongest case you can for the following position: a risk-neutral agent with linear utility is not rationally required to accept the St. Petersburg game at every finite price, because expected value is simply the wrong aggregator for one-shot, heavy-tailed gambles. On this view, concavity "solves" the original game only by changing the stakes rather than addressing the actual defect, and bounding utility is an ad hoc patch — the same difficulty reappears in any setting where the utility scale is allowed to be unbounded. The real lesson of the paradox is that a single-shot decision maker should not be optimizing an expectation over an infinity of possible worlds they will never get to sample; the iterated version of the game, where the long-run average is an observable quantity, is where the arithmetic mean genuinely applies. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
