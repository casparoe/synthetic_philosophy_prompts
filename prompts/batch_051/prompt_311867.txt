In a seminar on reproductive ethics I ended up stuck on a question about an old argument meeting a new technology, and I want a careful treatment. The old argument is the principle of procreative beneficence, roughly: if a couple has already decided to have a child and is choosing among several possible children they could bring into existence—for example, among IVF embryos already created—they have reason, and on a strong reading an obligation, to select the child with the best expected life, where "best" is judged by the ordinary standards parents already use when they treat a disease, move for better schools, or choose a partner: health, capacities, opportunities, and expected well-being. The principle deliberately applies to selection among possible children rather than to creating a child at all, and that restriction does important work in the usual defense of the position.

The new development is polygenic embryo screening. It is now possible to genotype a few cells from each embryo and rank the embryos using polygenic scores: weighted sums of hundreds or thousands of common genetic variants that predict, imperfectly, a person's risk for diseases such as diabetes or schizophrenia, and also continuous traits such as height, cognitive performance, and educational attainment. The scores are explicitly probabilistic and population-level; they explain only part of the variance, they can perform differently depending on ancestry and environment, and for non-disease traits especially an individual score is a weak and unstable guide to any particular child's actual life. This also puts pressure on the wider position sometimes called liberal eugenics, whose defenders distinguish it from coercive state eugenics by appeal to parental choice, value pluralism, and a line between treating disease and enhancing traits; polygenic scores for continuous traits make that line hard to locate. Yet if procreative beneficence is right, such scores look like precisely the information parents ought to use once selection goes beyond single-gene disorders.

I want to know whether the procreative beneficence argument survives this development, and in what form. Which premise is actually under pressure—the premise that we can compare expected lives, the premise that polygenic scores track the relevant sense of "best," the premise that the costs of failing to select are morally significant, or the premise that selection benefits the selected child? Is there a repaired version that still supports screening for serious disease risk without turning into a mandate for trait selection, or does the development expose a flaw in the original argument that was there all along? I am asking about the fate of the philosophical argument, not about what regulation would be sensible. In your answer, please separate what is settled among people working in reproductive ethics from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading—for instance, whether the principle is best read as a permission or as an obligation—as the consensus.
