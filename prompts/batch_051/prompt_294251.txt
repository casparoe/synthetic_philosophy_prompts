I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and one move I keep running into is the empire-retirement spreadsheet. You know the genre: somebody wants to say how much longer the current American-led order will last, so they open a file of historical great powers — Rome, Han, the Ottomans, the Abbasids, the British, maybe the Soviet Union if they're feeling inclusive — code each one with a start date, an end date, and a cause of death, take a median or a mean, and then announce something like "the base rate gives unipolarity until roughly 2040; after that it's anyone's guess." I've definately repeated that move myself in print, and a friend at a forecasting shop has a whole spreadsheet with a column labeled "is this really an empire?" that everyone argues about for hours.

What I've never gotten anyone to spell out is the philosophy underneath this. What do you have to believe about history for the spreadsheet to be more than numerology — that great powers form a natural kind, that a particular historically unique entity can be slotted into a reference class of entities it doesn't much resemble, that there are lawlike regularities in how states rise and fall rather than history being a sequence of unique, non-repeating events? I genuinely can't tell whether the practice strictly presupposes those commitments or just floats on them because they feel right. So: reconstruct the implicit philosophy of history and induction that this "outside view" forecasting-by-reference-class presupposes. Be precise about what it needs (without which the move isn't licensed) versus what it's merely compatible with. And tell me what the forecast would look like if someone kept the base-rate machinery but dropped the strongest of those commitments — does anything survive, or does the method collapse into "we found a pattern because we went looking for one"? End with a one-sentence summary as the final line, beginning "Summary:" with nothing after it.
