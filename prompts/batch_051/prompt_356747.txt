I'm a journalist writing a feature on why the Anthropocene has turned out to be so difficult to date, and I want to organize the arguments before I begin reporting. The candidate starting points I keep hearing are: late-Pleistocene megafauna extinctions tied to human dispersal; the early-agriculture "early Anthropocene" greenhouse-gas signal beginning several thousand years ago; the 1610 Orbis spike, when post-contact depopulation in the Americas shows up as a sharp dip in atmospheric CO2; the Industrial Revolution and its shift to fossil energy in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries; the mid-twentieth-century Great Acceleration; and the mid-twentieth-century plutonium-239 fallout horizon from nuclear testing, which was the formal candidate for a stratigraphic golden spike. Could you sort these into tiers by how defensible they are as the onset of the Anthropocene, with a sentence or two of reasoning for each? Make explicit the criteria you are rating against—for example, global synchroneity, detectability in a lasting physical record, causal link to a durable shift in the Earth system, and fit with what a geological epoch is supposed to mark. Then, given the recent disputed rejection of the effort to ratify the Anthropocene as a formal geological unit, say who you think should decide when it starts—and whether a single boundary date is even the right way to represent the concept at all.
