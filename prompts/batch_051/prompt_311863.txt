I spent most of my career in fluid dynamics before I retired, and I've only recently started reading philosophy of physics properly, so please excuse any mangled jargon. The thing I can't stop chewing on is Laplace's demon.

The standard setup: the demon knows the exact positions and momenta of every particle, plus all the laws, and therefore the whole future and past are laid out for it like an open book. My old physics self never trusted that, because we spent decades learning not to trust exact initial conditions. In a chaotic system, two states that agree to a hundred decimal places diverge on the Lyapunov timescale; a small error in the initial data grows like e^λt, and after a few Lyapunov times you are just guessing. That is the weather-forecast horizon all over again.

So here is the variation I want to run by you. Give the demon perfect knowledge of the dynamics, but supose the initial state it is handed is specified only to within a fixed nonzero error ε — as small as you like, but fixed. Don't read this as human ignorance; read it as: the demon's input is a ball of radius ε, not a point. For a chaotic system it can then predict out to roughly (1/λ) ln(1/ε), and beyond that its predictions are no better than chance. For a non-chaotic system the errors grow at worst polynomially, so the horizon is much gentler. (I'm glossing over local Lyapunov exponents; that's fine for present purposes.)

Now the actual question. The usual line is that determinism is true but predictability is a separate matter, and the original demon brought the two together only by stipulating omniscience. With this finite-ε demon, which conclusion should I land on: (a) determinism still guarantees predictability in principle, because "in principle" just means allowing exact values after all and ε is only a practical handicap; (b) the finite-ε version shows the omniscience was doing all the work, and determinism leaves us with no predictability worth wanting; or (c) the determinism/predictability distinction needs redrawing, because a deterministic system can be predictable-for-a-while without being predictable, full stop? Two follow-ups. First, does it matter whether ε is read as a practical limit versus a physical one — say the demon is a finite physical thing that must store its digits somewhere, so infinite precision is not available inside the world being described? Second, if the demon lives inside the world it is predicting and its own simulation has to be part of the simulation, does that add anything beyond the precision point, or is it just a self-reference garnish?
