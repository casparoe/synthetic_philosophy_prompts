I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, and I've ended up deep in a problem that started as an engineering one. I've been trying to evaluate systems that learn a concept from a handful of examples — the kind of thing Bongard problems were designed to test: two panels of six simple boxes each, where every box on the left shares some property that none on the right has, and the task is to say what the property is. The hard part, I've come to think, is not the classifier. For any finite set of examples there are indefinitely many properties that separate left from right, and yet people reliably settle on one as the rule. The intended rule is sometimes a local feature (every figure contains a circle), sometimes relational (dots equal sides), sometimes disjunctive, sometimes negative, and sometimes opaque to a model that sees only pixels. So the real question looks to me like what it means to learn the concept, as opposed to merely fitting a decision boundary.

So I want to stop and ask the prior question: what should any adequate theory of concept induction from a handful of examples be able to do, assuming the theory is meant to guide an implementation and not just a post hoc rational reconstruction? Please write out the desiderata. For each requirement, give the case that motivates it: one-shot generalization without overfitting, learning from negative examples, disjunctive concepts, cases where a human judge and a model pick different rules for the same images — and whether "who is right" is even the right question — and a child learning a new word from one exposure. Mark which distinctions a theory must respect, such as concept versus decision boundary, generalization versus curve-fitting, and the intended rule versus any separating surface, and say which requirements are negotiable and which are not. Where two requirements are in tension — matching human judgments of what is natural while remaining data-driven, or preferring the simplest rule while still learning contrived disjunctive concepts — flag the tension and say whether you think the pair can be jointly satisfied. Then score, no more than a few sentences each, how symbolic program synthesis, Bayesian accounts with inductive priors, similarity-based accounts, and deep few-shot learning do against the list.

One constraint from my side of the table: in my work, a model that cannot say why it generalized as it did is not shippable. I genuinely do not know whether an adequate theory of concept induction also owes us an account of how the learned concept is represented and available for inspection, or whether that is a separate engineering problem to bolt on afterward. Place that one wherever you think it belongs, and tell me whether treating it as a desideratum would rule out otherwise serious contenders.

When you answer, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, and don't restate the question.
