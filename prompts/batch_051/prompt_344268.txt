ive been chewing on a decision rule for total ignorance for a while and I'd like an honest read on whether it's coherent, whether it already exists under some name I should know, and where it breaks. I'm prepared to be told I reinvented something or smuggled in an assumption. The setup is the usual one: known acts, known states, known payoff for every act-state pair, but no probabilities over the states at all. My gripe is that maximin only looks at one cell per act and ignores everything else, while minimax regret looks at every cell but then squashes the whole profile into a single number, so an act with one huge regret and no medium regrets comes out exactly the same as an act with a dozen medium regrets. I dont think those are the same, so ive been calling my fix 'floored leximin regret.' You first delete any act whose worst-case payoff would be genuinely catastrophic for you, because no amount of later regret-minimizing should be allowed to buy back ruin. Then for each surviving act you compute Savage regret in each state: how much better the best act in that state would have done. Then you sort each act's regrets from largest to smallest and compare the sorted lists lexicographically, minimizing the largest regret first and using the next largest only to break ties. I compute regret against the best act in the original menu, even the ones I've deleted, because otherwise the floor quietly rewrites the comparison and lets a rule hide its own violence; but I go back and forth on that. My example is three jobs and three states of the economy. A trade job pays 90, 80, 70 across the states, a startup pays 150, 60, 20, and civil service pays 75, 75, 75. The regret profiles sorted largest first are 60, 5, 0 for trade; 55, 20, 0 for startup; and 75, 5, 0 for civil service. Minimax regret alone picks the startup. Maximin alone picks civil service. My rule picks the startup unless the 20 in the bad state is below your catastrophe floor, in which case it picks the trade job. I keep telling people this is just Rawls's difference principle pointed at your possible future selves instead of at citizens, and that Harsanyi's equal-probability move is fine when you actually have probabilities but question-begging here, since the whole problem is that you dont have any. What I want is a candid assessment, not encouragement. Is the rule coherent? Is this just lexicographic minimax regret or some other named thing? Does the Rawls gloss do any real work? Where does the rule give silly or unstable answers, for example when I partition one state into several trivial sub-states and the sorted list gets longer so the tie-breaking starts caring about how finely I chopped the world? And please say what would actually change your mind about whether the rule is worth using, and where your own reasoning is least secure. Also, write the whole thing in continuous prose like an email; no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
