I'm a PhD student in political theory working on international climate governance, and in a recent seminar someone floated a proposal for governing stratospheric aerosol injection that I would like you to analyze as a mechanism rather than to assess as a moral position.

Under the proposal, before any state or coalition may begin injecting sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere, it must post a termination bond with an independent trustee, recalculated every year to equal the estimated monetized damage of the warming that would follow if injection were stopped abruptly within two years at the then-current aerosol burden. The bond is released over a planned thirty-year phasedown, and only once the deployer has satisfied the trustee that reductions in greenhouse-gas concentrations have durably replaced the cooling the aerosols had been providing. If deployment ends faster than the schedule allows, the bond is paid out as adaptation finance to the countries most exposed to the resulting warming surge. Separately, any state—deployer or not—may unilaterally start the thirty-year phasedown at any time, without a vote, by paying a fee into a global adaptation fund, with the fee capped low enough to be affordable for a medium-sized state.

Walk through what this actually does: who deploys under such a rule; how it interacts with the moral-hazard worry that the availability of a cheap planetary fix will weaken other states' incentives to cut emissions; whether it makes abrupt termination more or less likely; who in practice gains control over the decision to stay in or get out; and where the scheme gets gamed first. If it fails, explain whether the failure can be fixed without recreating the very problem the mechanism was meant to solve. Please keep the analysis under 900 words.
