Since I retired from the lab I've been working through the philosophy of science literature, and theres one dispute I keep coming back to because, from a working scientist's point of view, it doesn't seem to track how thought experiments actually function. The debate over whether thought experiments in science can yield genuine knowledge has been running for decades, and from where I sit nobody has budged. I'd like you to diagnose what kind of disagreement this is, not give me a verdict on who's right.

Let me lay out the two positions as I've come to understand them. On one side, people argue that a good thought experiment can produce new knowledge about the world even though no experiment has been run and no new observations have been made. The thought experiment works by forcing you to make explicit the commitments already buried in your concepts or your theory, and by exposing a conflict between principles you hadn't noticed were in tension — think of Galileo dropping balls in his head, Einstein riding alongside a light beam, Maxwell's demon. On the other side, people argue that a thought experiment is just an empirical argument in disguise: whatever it seems to deliver was already paid for by past experience, and if it looks like it gives something new, that's because hidden assumptions got smuggled in. On that view a thought experiment can clarify, but it cannot generate.

Here's my scientist's frustration. When I was actually in the lab, we used thought experiments in at least two seperate ways. Sometimes they were demolition tools: push a theory until it yields an absurd result and you've shown something is wrong, no new physics required. Sometimes they were exploratory: vary a parameter beyond what the equipment can reach and see what the theory says is possible. It seems to me the philosophers might be talking past each other because the first side is mostly focused on the exploratory kind and the second side is mostly focused on the demolition kind. But it's also possible I'm flattening a real epistemological disagreement into a rough lab-bench taxonomy because that's what I'm comfortable with.

So my question is this: is this a substantive disagreement about whether a priori justification exists, a verbal one about the word knowledge, a difference about which examples count as the data to be explained, or are the two sides answering different questions under one heading — one about justification, one about the psychology of discovery? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here: a sharper taxonomy, a formal result, an unexamined case study, or an argument that the question itself is ill-posed and we should stop asking it?
