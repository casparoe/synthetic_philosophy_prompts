I've just been called for jury service in a county where the district attorney has announced she will begin seeking capital sentences again after a long moratorium, and I've been rereading Beccaria's *On Crimes and Punishments*, especially the chapter "Of the Punishment of Death." There Beccaria gives a contractarian argument against capital punishment: he claims the sovereign can possess no right to kill because no rational person would surrender to the state a power over his own life; he also argues that the penalty is irremediable, so that any error cannot be corrected after the fact. I'm particularly interested in that second strand, because Beccaria treats the possibility of error as a reason to reject the penalty even without any empirical evidence about how often capital trials go wrong.

Since Beccaria, post-conviction DNA testing and systematic review of capital cases have produced a nontrivial list of exonerations from death row, and researchers have attempted to estimate the base rate of wrongful convictions among persons sentenced to death. Assume for the sake of argument that the credible estimates are in the low single digits: a small but real fraction of those sentenced to death are innocent. Does Beccaria's argument survive this development, and which premise does the new information actually put under pressure? Is the contractarian premise independent of how accurate the later proceedings are, or does the empirical error rate change its force? And if the argument does survive, is it now best understood as an actuarial argument about intolerable risk rather than as a principled denial of the sovereign's right to kill? I want to know whether the argument needs repair and what weakened version remains defensible — not what the policy should be.
