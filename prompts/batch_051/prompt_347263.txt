I ended up in an argument about original sin recently and can't shake it. The strong Augustinian version says we inherit Adam's guilt itself, not just a bent toward sinning, and the standard philosophical objection is that guilt is a moral relation between a person and what that person has done — you can inherit a disease but not a crime. But I've been reading about epigenetic inheritance, the way severe stress or famine (the Dutch Hunger Winter studies, for instance) seems to leave a biological mark that can be passed down a generation or two, so that a child arrives with its body already carrying traces of what its grandparents went through. That isnt guilt, obviously, but it struck me as structurally the thing the doctrine needs: a burden you get by descent rather than by deed, already there before any choice of your own. So does the classical objection still go through, or does epigenetic inheritance give the Augustinian picture a way to survive? Or maybe it cuts the other way — it shows that what gets inherited is a vulnerability, not a culpability, which is exactly what inherited guilt is supposed to require, so the doctrine ends up hollow anyway. I want an honest assessment: does the old argument against inherited guilt survive this changed picture of biological inheritance, does it need repair, or is it just dead? And if it does survive, what does the epigenetic inheritance point actually add, if anything?
