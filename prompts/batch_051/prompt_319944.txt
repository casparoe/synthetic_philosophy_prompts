Two students in my philosophy of language seminar fell into a disagreement last week over a sentence the instructor had put on the board: 'Dana is the first person to have swum the length of Lake Ondine,' which we were told to treat as true in the actual world. One argued that both designators pick out the same individual, that the sentence therefore expresses an identity, and that identities are necessary; since its truth could be known only from the historical record, it is a necessary a posteriori truth. The other replied that 'the first person to have swum the length of Lake Ondine' is a definite description, not a rigid designator: in a possible world where Dana stayed home and someone else made the crossing first, the description picks out that other person and the sentence is false, so the original proposition is contingent. The first rejoined that the description could be rigidified—'Dana is the actual first person to have swum the length of Lake Ondine' designates the same individual in every possible world, because 'actual' fixes the description to our world, so there is a necessary a posteriori truth after all. The second answered that this changes the proposition: the original sentence attributes to Dana an achievement she could have lacked, whereas the actualized sentence attributes only identity with the person who in fact did it, so the move does not make the original claim necessary. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: assign each of the four moves a verdict from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each verdict, then say which specific points each side has right and what distinction both are missing.
