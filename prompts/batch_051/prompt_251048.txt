Lately I've been tinkering with Van Fraassen's Reflection principle—the idea that your current credence in a proposition should equal your expected future credence in it, so if you're certain that tomorrow you'll be .7 confident in P, you should be .7 confident now. Obviously nobody thinks that's unconditional: you might know you'll be drunk, exhausted, amnestic, or under pressure. So here's my working criteria for when you genuinely ought to defer to your future self about P: (i) your future self has at least as much relevant evidence as you have now, (ii) you don't expect your future reasoning to be cognitively impaired or biased relative to your present reasoning, and (iii) you don't currently possess some piece of evidence your future self lacks. Now the borderline case I keep waffling on: you deliberately schedule a psilocybin trip for next week because, from a lot of sober experience, you suspect your sober confidence in the proposition 'my current career is the right one for me' is inflated, and you predict that while tripping you'll assign that same body of evidence a much lower credence—say .3—before swinging back afterward. You set this up as a self-check; you expect the .3; you currently hold .8. Should Reflection force you to adopt .3 now? Does the tripping self pass my three criteria, or does this case show that one or more of them needs to be added, deleted, or revised? Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion.
