ok so in my econ class we were doing public goods and someone brought up whether taxpayers should fund cosmology — like mapping the cosmic microwave background or looking for dark energy — when theres basically zero chance it ever becomes a product or fixes anything. Someone else joked that if applied stuff can get money from companies anyway, maybe the government should fund the most useless stuff the most. I want to take that joke seriously and think through a specific rule — not whether we should do it, but what it would actually do if we did.

Here's the rule. A country takes part of its science budget and runs it like this. Every grant proposal gets scored 0 to 1 on two things. Q is scientific quality, normal peer review stuff. U is expected usefulness: the panel's guess at the probability the research leads to some commercial or practical application within 30 years. Then a proposal's priority score is Q times (1 minus U). So a high-quality proposal with a 5% usefulness score gets 0.95 times its quality, and an excellent battery project with a 90% usefulness score gets 0.10 times its quality — meaning a pretty mediocre useless project outranks an excellent useful one. The pot gets spent down the ranked list until the money runs out. Pure cosmology basically always wins; near-term engineering basically never does from this pot.

Walk me through how this actually plays out once people have a few years to adapt. What does it incentivize researchers to do, both in what they study and in how they write proposals? What happens to in-between fields like quantum computing or genetics where the usefulness score could go either way? How do universities and labs game it — spinning off applied labs, rebranding projects, hiding possible applications, coaching the U panels? Does 'usefulness' turn into a wierd status thing where everyone competes to look as useless as possible, and what does that do to science overall? And can you fix the obvious failure modes — audits, a cap on the multiplier, a separate fund that catches stuff when it later turns useful — without just turning it back into normal peer review?
