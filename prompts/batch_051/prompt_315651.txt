In his essay Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren, John Maynard Keynes writes: "For I predict that both of the two opposed errors of pessimism which now make so much noise in the world will be proved wrong in our own time – the pessimism of the revolutionaries who think that things are so bad that nothing can save us but violent change, and the pessimism of the reactionaries who consider the balance of our economic and social life so precarious that we must risk no experiments." Please make an argument that this double prediction is not a side remark about interwar politics but an implication of Keynes's account of the good life: the claim that once the economic problem is solved, the central human problem becomes learning to use leisure well rather than continuing to treat work and accumulation as ends. Then address the strongest objection to that argument, namely that Keynes's vision of the good life presupposes a shared standard of what counts as a good use of leisure, and that the essay never supplies one—so the prediction may be stable economically while still leaving the good life radically underdetermined. Keep the focus on the normative question of what Keynes thought the good life requires, not on the technical macroeconomics of his forecast.
