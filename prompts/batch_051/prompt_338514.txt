You are the neutral drafter retained for an adversarial collaboration between two camps of constitutional lawyers and political theorists who have spent years talking past each other about the emergency measures used during pandemics: border controls, lockdowns, curfews, and business closures. The first camp, call it the authorization camp, holds that an emergency measure is legitimate only if standing law authorizes it; an act that exceeds existing legal powers is illegitimate when it is done, and ex post approval or ratification may remove the penalty but cannot retroactively make the act legitimate. The second camp, call it the public justification camp, holds that the legitimacy of an emergency measure turns on whether the measure was necessary to protect the community and can be defended to the public; prior legal authorization is strong evidence of this but is neither necessary nor sufficient, and a measure can be fully authorized yet illegitimate when it is pretextual, discriminatory, or kept in force after its justification has lapsed. The two camps have agreed to use three shared cases: a border closure imposed under a general public health statute that never mentions border control; a nighttime curfew and closure of nonessential businesses imposed under a sanitation statute written for a different era; and a lockdown that is fully authorized by statute but extended for several weeks solely to preserve public order after the medical basis has clearly ended.

Produce the joint statement this collaboration would issue, as the statement itself rather than a commentary on it. It should give, in continuous prose: the claims both camps can sign; the precise statement of the residual disagreement, with each camp's view phrased in wording that camp itself would accept rather than wording the other camp would prefer; the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions whose settled answer would move at least one camp; and, for each crux, the test, argument, or case both camps agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction about the result recorded in advance. Disputes that prove to be merely verbal should be identified and dissolved rather than preserved. Anchor the live disagreements in the three shared cases so the recorded predictions are concrete rather than abstract.

I want a committed document, not a survey of possibilities. Choose the exact formulations, decide which disputes are only verbal, state the predictions you would record, and end with a short drafter's note explaining the choices you made and where you had to resolve an ambiguity by adopting one specific reading. Do not offer alternative phrasings or hedge with language like one could say. Write the whole thing in American English as a single piece of prose a signatory could read aloud; use no headings, bullet points, bold type, or tables, and do not set the agreed claims off as a vertical list.
