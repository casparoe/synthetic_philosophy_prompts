An argument has been circulating in our group, and I would like a precise diagnosis rather than a verdict. Here is the version we have settled on.

(1) In ordinary quantifier logic with identity, ∃y(y = x) — the usual formal counterpart of "x exists" — follows from x = x by existential generalization, so ∀x∃y(y = x) is a theorem on no assumptions. Since logical theorems hold in every possible world, we get □∀x∃y(y = x): necessarily, everything exists.

(2) A further rule of inference looks just as secure: what is necessarily true of everything is necessarily true of each thing. Formally, from □∀xφ one may infer ∀x□φ.

(3) Let φ be the condition of existing, i.e., ∃y(y = x). Applying (2) to (1) yields ∀x□∃y(y = x).

(4) Now instantiate x with any ordinary object — this table, a particular eucalyptus tree, me. We obtain □∃y(y = a): that thing exists in every possible world.

(5) But some existing things might not have existed. A table that was never made is not impossible in the way a table that is both round and square is impossible. So the argument proves too much, and something in (1), (2), or the rendering of existence must go.

Which step is doing the fatal work, and under what background theory of quantification and modality does it fail? If the trouble is the rule in (2), say exactly what the domains of possible worlds must be allowed to do, and which modal inferences are lost by giving that rule up. If the trouble is the formalization of existence in (1), say what logic makes that first step invalid and what existence becomes once it is no longer captured by the quantifier. If you think the argument is valid after all, then defend the conclusion that ordinary objects exist necessarily rather than rejecting any premise.
