I work as a climate adaptation planner for a county government, and several times a year I must produce cost and time estimates for infrastructure and restoration projects — raising a levee, restoring a wetland, relocating a water intake — before the board votes on funding. For each project I have two kinds of input: an inside view built up from the specific features of the project (our engineers' detailed design, the site's particular soil and flood history, the contractor we expect to use), and an outside view drawn from a reference class of broadly similar projects completed by other counties (their median cost overrun and typical delay). I have noticed that our inside-view estimates are systematically more optimistic than what the outside view predicts, and I want to stop overruling the base rates by default without becoming a slave to statistics that ignore what we actually know about this site.

Write me a reusable procedure — a checklist or an ordered set of tests, in words — for deciding, on any given project, how to combine the inside view with the outside view in the number I sign off on. Constraints: I usually have about a week per estimate; my staff have no formal training in probability, so the procedure must not require anything beyond simple averages and ranges; and the final number must be one I can honestly defend to an elected board that punishes both cost overruns and budgets that look padded. Ground the procedure in the actual inside/outside view distinction and in the idea of epistemic modesty, but translate both into steps a non-philosopher can follow, and say which distinctions must survive the simplification and which can be dropped. Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a routine culvert replacement with a rich reference class; (2) a novel wetland restoration for which no other county has done anything close enough to form a reference class; and (3) a project in which our inside view genuinely knows something the outside view cannot see, such as a recently introduced construction method that has not yet appeared in other counties' records. Make at least one of the three cases produce a number I will not like, and explain why the procedure forces that number on me. End by stating explicitly what the procedure cannot do — the residual judgment calls it cannot eliminate and the conditions under which I should distrust it.
