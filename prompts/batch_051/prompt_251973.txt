Here is an asymmetry in ordinary practice that is rarely stated in this form. A nurse who deviates from a clinical protocol is treated as having done something wrong even when the patient does fine; the deviation itself is the offense. A junior employee who skips a step in a compliance checklist is disciplined regardless of whether any harm followed. Yet the same society treats a founder who ignores industry rules, a general who disobeys doctrine in the field, or a politician who promises to break the rules that constrain everyone else as doing exactly what those roles are for. In the first kind of role, rule-following is a virtue and deviation is misconduct before any outcome is known; in the second, rule-following is treated as a failure of imagination and deviation as evidence of judgment. So the same act-type, departing from an established rule, is condemned in some roles and demanded in others, and the difference is not the outcome, since both judgments are made before the outcome arrives.

Is there a principle that justifies this, or is it a status-based double standard we have learned to live with? The strongest justification I can see is that rules and checklists are calibrated for recurring situations: a nurse faces roughly the same procedure thousands of times, so a deviation is almost always an error, whereas a founder or a general faces a novel, non-recurring situation for which existing rules were never calibrated, so deviation is how the society gathers information about which rules to write next. That would make the right amount of rule-following depend on how routine the situation is, not on rank. But that principle has to survive hard cases. A junior employee who can see that a specific rule is misfiring and breaks it to prevent a loss: is she a hero or insubordinate, and what does your principle say? A leader who breaks a safety rule to hit a target: is that the same novelty-driven deviation, and if not, what exactly distinguishes it? And a society that demands punctual, exact compliance from the poor while tolerating constant rule-bending by the rich: is that the efficient allocation of tightness and looseness across different kinds of situations, or evidence that the asymmetry is really about power?

Give the strongest statement of the principle you can find, test it against those cases, and end with an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
