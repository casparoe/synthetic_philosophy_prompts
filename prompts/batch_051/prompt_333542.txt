I have been moderating a long exchange between two theologians who have finally agreed to stop debating and instead produce a document recording what their disagreement actually consists in. One is a Protestant theologian in the Reformation tradition committed to classical sola scriptura: Scripture alone is the infallible rule of faith and practice; it is perspicuous in everything necessary for salvation; and it interprets itself through the analogy of Scripture under the guidance of the Holy Spirit, so that no merely human teaching office may bind the conscience. The other stands in the Catholic tradition and holds that Scripture can function as an authority only within the living interpretive community of the Church: the text cannot secure its own canon, validate its own transmission, or adjudicate among its own competing readings, and making private judgment the final court of appeal generates a regress of interpreters in which no reading can be distinguished from a misreading except by another contested reading.

I would like you to draft the adversarial-collaboration document they should have produced: a joint statement both could sign, not a survey of positions or a commentary on them. Make it genuinely constructive. Do not present a menu of acceptable formulations; in each section make a definite choice about the strongest wording both parties could accept, and where the choice is non-obvious, say briefly why you think both would sign it. Organize it in the following named sections, with approximate lengths: Preamble (100–150 words) stating the aim of the document and what each party grants about the other's good faith and about the normative authority of Scripture; Agreed claims (a numbered list, 250–300 words total); The disagreement, stated exactly (200–250 words, with each position phrased so that its own defender recognizes it as fair and the other recognizes it as accurate); Cruxes and predictions (300–350 words, as a table with columns for the crux stated neutrally, why it matters, a concrete case or test both sides agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction); and Where the dispute may be verbal (100–150 words) identifying any residual disagreement that is largely an artifact of different uses of key terms such as authority, clarity, or the Church.

In the cruxes section, I suspect the live sub-questions include: whether perspicuity extends only to what is necessary for salvation or to the whole of doctrine; whether the formation of the canon can be accounted for on the text-alone view; whether the regress of readers is a defect in principle or only a consequence of sin, ignorance, and poor scholarship; and whether the Spirit's guidance is best understood as a private charism or as an ecclesial promise. Treat these only as leads: improve them, discard them, or replace them if the document you are drafting calls for it.
