I am a doctoral student working in the epistemology of mathematics, and I have been trying to formulate a version of Benacerraf's dilemma that is strong enough to force a choice between Platonism about mathematics and the causal constraint on knowledge. The argument I produced now appears to me to prove too much, and I would appreciate a careful diagnosis.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) A subject knows that p only if the fact that p stands in some causal-explanatory relation to the subject's believing that p.
(2) If mathematical Platonism is true, then true mathematical statements are true in virtue of facts about abstract, acausal, non-spatiotemporal objects.
(3) No fact about abstract, acausal, non-spatiotemporal objects can stand in a causal-explanatory relation to any human belief.
(4) We do know some mathematical truths, for example that there are infinitely many primes.
(5) Therefore, from (1) to (4), at least one of the following must be abandoned: the causal constraint, mathematical Platonism, or the claim that we know the mathematical truths in question.

So far this is familiar territory. The difficulty arises when I observe that the same reasoning seems to extend to logic.

(6) Logical truths, such as the truth that modus ponens is truth-preserving, also appear to be true, if they are true at all, in virtue of facts about abstract, acausal, non-spatiotemporal entities or structures.
(7) By parity of reasoning with (1) to (3), if such logical facts are abstract and acausal, then they cannot stand in causal-explanatory relations to our beliefs; hence, under the causal constraint, no one knows any logical truth.
(8) But the reasoning from (1) to (7) is itself a piece of logical reasoning; if (7) is correct, then one cannot know that this argument is any good.
(9) The argument therefore no longer yields a clean refutation of Platonism. It leaves an uncomfortable trilemma: reject the causal constraint, deny that logical truths are made true by abstract facts in the relevant sense, or accept that we do not even know the logical truths by means of which we reasoned.

I find the causal constraint in (1) initially very plausible, and I should like to retain (4) and avoid scepticism about logic. But I cannot see how to hold the line against mathematics without thereby losing logic. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? If it does not go wrong, which of the three options in (9) is the least costly, and why?
