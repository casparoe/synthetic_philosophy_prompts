I teach a second-year undergrad seminar on democratic theory, around 20 students, 80 minutes. I want an activity for the lottocracy unit that makes them actually feel why someone would replace elections with lotteries, instead of just hearing the arguments. The thing I especially want them to feel is the self-selection problem: elections reliably hand power to people who are comfortable wanting it, good at campaigning, or just pushy, while sortition hands power to an average slice. Design a game where the class runs the same budgeting decision twice — once with representatives elected after brief pitches, once with representatives drawn by lot with no campaigning allowed — for a real but small stake (like points that decide whether we read another Rousseau excerpt or a citizens-assembly reading next week). The rules and payoffs should be arranged so the difference shows up in who sits at the table and how the deliberation goes, not in my debrief. Give me the setup, exact timeline, materials, the debrief that maps what happened to descriptive representation, the self-selection objection, and the accountability tradeoff, plus what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and an honest note on which features of real lottocracy the activity can't capture (expert testimony, random sampling from a whole polity, repeated service, etc.).
