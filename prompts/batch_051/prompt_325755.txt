As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I keep encountering one analogy doing a great deal of work in disputes about monuments and street names. Opponents of removal often argue that taking down a statue or renaming a street is like tearing pages out of a history textbook: the public record is being thinned, so later generations will be less able to learn what happened. I want to test how far that comparison actually holds. What maps onto what — the monument, the inscription, the choice of site, the original commissioners, the approving officials, the audience, the later public? Where does the textbook analogy break down once we consider how monuments are authorized, selected, maintained, read, ignored, and contested, as opposed to how textbooks are written, adopted, taught, revised, and argued over? Which of those disanalogies matter for the specific conclusion that removal causes a loss of historical knowledge, and which matter only for the different claim that removal changes whom a polity chooses to honor or hold up as exemplary? If the textbook analogy fails precisely where its rhetorical force is supposed to come from, is there a better comparison — a portrait gallery, a syllabus, a legal precedent, a commemorative speech — that more honestly captures both what is lost and what is not? I am less interested in whether removal is right or wrong overall than in knowing exactly what argumentative weight this analogy can bear.
