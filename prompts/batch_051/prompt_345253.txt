Okay, here's a mechanism I want you to tear apart, as someone who knows both philosophy of mind and how institutions get gamed. A government research agency is considering a sentience registry for large compute. Any lab that runs a training run or simulation above a certain compute threshold has to file a moral-status self-assesment beforehand, saying which features the system is expected to have by the end: a self-model, reward signals, anything that looks like even a rough analogue of suffering, and so on. A standing committee turns that filing into a moral-weight score from 0 to 1, and the score sets the lab's legal duties. Below 0.1, nothing. Between 0.1 and 0.6, you can't freely delete checkpoints without review. Between 0.6 and 0.9, shutdown requires independent oversight. Above 0.9, running the system at all is treated like animal testing. I want this analyzed as a mechanism, not as a question of whether the underlying worry is right. What does it incentivize labs to report and to build? Where does it get gamed first — strategically low self-scores, lobbying the committee, relabeling systems as something else — and which of those are fixable? Is a continuous moral-weight score better than a coarse protected/not-protected binary here, or does precision just create more room to fudge?
