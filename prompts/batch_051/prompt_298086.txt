During the State of the Union the U.S. deliberately keeps one cabinet member at an undisclosed location, so that if everyone at the Capitol were killed at once, there'd still be a person legally in the line of succession alive to become president immediately. Weirder still, the line of succession can put someone in the Oval Office who was never elected to anything—just confirmed for, say, running the Department of Commerce—and the country is supposed to treat that person as the fully legitimate president, not a caretaker. Nobody seems to think this creates a new state or a coup. What does this whole arrangement presuppose about what a state actually is, and about where a government's right to rule comes from? Reconstruct the implicit theory, and say whether it's consistent with the same system's insistence, during ordinary transitions, that legitimacy comes from elections and the consent of the governed. I want you to seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits or leaves open—could a consent-based theorist of legitimacy still sign off on the designated survivor for purely practical reasons, or does the practice only make sense if you already think of the state as a continuous office-holding machine that survives its people? Finally, tell me what in the law or the practice would have to change if we replaced that presupposition with the view that legitimate rule requires being chosen by the people, with at most a short, strictly limited caretaker exception.
