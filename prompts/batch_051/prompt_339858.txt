I realised recently that I keep trying to convince myself luck egalitarianism is basically right, whilst also failing to actually accept what it seems to say about people who make bad choices. The attraction, for me, is that the view gives a single elegant test for when an inequality is unjust: it's unjust when it traces to brute luck — your genes, your parents, where you were born — but not when it traces to option luck, the risks you knowingly took. That one distinction seems to sort so many cases at once. The person born into poverty has a claim; the person who chooses not to train has less of one. It also rescues egalitarianism from the charge that it treats everyone as a passive victim of circumstance, and it dovetails with how I actually think about desert: it feels right that people should bear the costs of their own choices and not the costs of their unchosen circumstances.

But then I think about the gambler who loses everything, or the person who drops out of school and later wants help, or the motorcyclist who rides without a helmet and ends up with a head injury, and my intuition is rock solid: a decent society doesn't leave them to rot, and 'you chose it' isn't a full answer to someone in need. Maybe that intuition is just tracking the fact that real choice is murky — addiction, bad foresight, social pressure — so the theory can be amended rather than abandoned. Or maybe it's tracking something the theory leaves out entirely: that people can have claims on one another that don't depend on how they came to be in difficulty. I honestly can't tell. So present the strongest version of the luck egalitarian reply and the strongest version of the objection, and then give me a negotiated verdict. Should the intuition be debunked, and what would justify that rather than making it a convenient dodge? Should the theory be amended or abandoned, and what would that cost? Or is their a reading of 'choice' or 'claim' on which both survive? I want to know what would justify each move, not just which side you'd pick.
