I'm in a reading group on Kierkegaard and we've spent two sessions stuck on his attacks on the daily press and the thing he calls "the public." As best I can reconstruct it, he doesn't mean the public in the sense of an audience at a theater or the citizenry of a state. He means a kind of ghostly social object that the press conjures into being: a standing cloud of opinion that no particular person authors, where readers get the feeling of participating in public life without ever having to commit, identify themselves, or do anything with consequences. He links this to what our translation calls leveling, the process by which distinctions of competence and character stop mattering and every anonymous voice counts the same. My group tried to turn this into a checklist for when something counts as this kind of public rather than an actual crowd or association: (a) it exists only through a communication medium that has a stake in keeping the talk going; (b) participation is anonymous or low-cost, with nothing really at risk; (c) opinion is aggregated so that all contributions are equalized regardless of knowledge or character; (d) the spectacle of discussion substitutes for action and gives people the sense of being involved; and (e) responsibility evaporates because the resulting "view" belongs to everyone and therefore to no one.

A friend in the group insist this is just a description of a certain kind of online platform. Imagine a site, call it Agora, where all accounts are pseudonymous, an algorithm ranks comments by engagement, every take is displayed with the same typographic weight whether it comes from an expert or a crank, and the companys whole business model is to maximize scrolling and arguing. Users spend hours a day debating social issues, and some say the discussions changed their minds or moved them to donate to a cause, but nobody is ever made to own a position; the community never meets face to face; contradictions and bad arguments carry no cost; and the site's about page literally describes it as a public square. Is Agora an instance of Kierkegaard's phantom public, or does the move from print to algorithmic media change something that matters? Go through the criteria one by one and tell me where the analogy is honest and where it is forced, and whether any one of the criteria is doing all the work, or whether the checklist itself needs revision.
