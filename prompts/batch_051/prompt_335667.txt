I have been collecting my own judgments about a deceptively simple question: when should we say that someone intentionally brought about a foreseen side effect of an action, rather than merely knew it would happen or allowed it to happen? I want to know whether a single principle captures the way I actually draw the line, or whether some of my judgments have to be revised. Here are my cases and my verdicts.

1. A citrus grower sprays a fungicide to save her orchard. She has been told that the runoff will kill the fish in a downstream pond. She would rather that not happen, but saving the orchard without spraying is not a live option, so she sprays and the fish die. I judge that she intentionally killed the fish.

2. The same grower installs a drip-irrigation system to cut water use. She has been told that the overflow will form a small depression where a rare orchid will establish itself. The orchid means nothing to her either way. The work proceeds and the orchid thrives. I judge that she did not intentionally cause the orchid to thrive.

3. A physician gives a hospitalized patient a strong antibiotic for sepsis. She knows it will also wipe out the patient's gut flora and cause severe colitis. She expects the colitis, regrets it, and would use a gentler drug if one were available; none is. The patient survives but suffers the colitis. I judge that the physician did not intentionally cause the colitis.

4. A city council votes to install a protected bike lane to ease congestion. It has been told that the lane will displace food trucks from a popular curb, which will incidentally increase foot traffic to struggling cafés on the same block. The councillors are pleased by this and later advertise the change as having helped local cafés. I judge that the council intentionally helped the cafés, even though that benefit did not motivate the vote.

5. A dermatologist prescribes a drug for mild acne. She knows it carries a 1-in-10,000 chance of causing liver failure. A patient's liver fails. I judge that the dermatologist did not intentionally cause the liver failure.

6. The same dermatologist prescribes the same drug, knowing it carries a 1-in-10,000 chance of clearing psoriasis in patients who have it. A patient's psoriasis clears. I judge that the dermatologist did not intentionally clear the psoriasis.

7. A commuter takes a shortcut through a park at night. A posted sign says the path is closed and that pedestrians trample the nests of a ground-nesting bird. She reads the sign, does not care, and walks through anyway, crushing a nest. I judge that she intentionally crushed the nest.

8. A landlord replaces a boiler so his tenants will have reliable heat. The new exhaust pipe must run along a wall where a tenant has tended a wisteria vine for years, and the work will kill it. The landlord knows this and does not care about the vine. He could reroute the pipe to a less convenient wall at moderate extra cost, but he sees no reason to spend that money for a vine he does not care about. The work proceeds and the vine dies. I go back and forth on whether he intentionally killed the wisteria.

Notice that cases 1 and 3 are both harmful side effects and yet my verdicts differ, and cases 2 and 4 are both helpful side effects and yet my verdicts differ. Cases 5 and 6 make me think that probability matters, at least at the extremes. I have one failed candidate. I was drawn to a test built on avoidance: an agent intentionally brings about the side effect just when she would not have paid a meaningful cost to avoid it while still performing the main action. This fits the harmful cases well enough—the grower and the commuter would not pay to avoid the fish or the nests, while the physician and the dermatologist in case 5 would pay to avoid the colitis or the liver failure. But it mishandles the helpful cases: in case 2 the grower would not pay to keep the orchid from thriving, and in case 6 the dermatologist would not pay to prevent the psoriasis from clearing, yet I deny intentionality in both. And something beyond this test must separate case 2 from case 4, since in each the agent would not pay to prevent the good outcome, and my verdicts still differ.

What principle best captures these verdicts? State it precisely, and tie each clause to the case or pair of cases that forces it. Then apply it to the wisteria case, and to two or three further cases of your own construction chosen to distinguish your principle from nearby alternatives. Finally, say plainly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether those are the verdicts I should revise.
