A friend in my reading group has an argument about the honor/dignity/victimhood-culture trichotomy that I cannot decide whether to call a reductio or a proof, and after a week of going through the numbered steps I still cannot find the load-bearing piece. He understands the three as culturally transmitted strategies for suppressing conflict and predation under different enforcement regimes: honor culture deters predation through a reputation for retaliatory violence where the state is weak or absent; dignity culture offloads enforcement to trusted third-party institutions and treats slights as things to be ignored rather than avenged; victimhood culture mobilizes third-party social pressure by publicizing grievances where institutions are present but seen as unresponsive and where social media makes an audience cheap to rally. His argument then runs:

(1) Each of the three is an equilibrium response to one underlying coordination problem and differs mainly in the enforcement environment, not in some deeper moral quality.
(2) A norm system or practice is good for a society to the extent that it is a stable, broadly beneficial equilibrium under the conditions its members actually face; there is no higher standard by which to assess a society's norms than this kind of fitness.
(3) Therefore none of the three cultures is morally superior to the others as a kind — each is good for its own environment, and asking which is better tout court is a category mistake.
(4) By the same reasoning, an honor code that mandates blood feud, a culture of foot-binding, or the customary exposure of disabled infants is likewise good for its own environment, provided each is a stable and broadly beneficial equilibrium under local conditions.
(5) But that cannot be right: those practices deserve condemnation even where they are stable and broadly accepted, and calling them "adapted" leaves the judgment obviously unresolved.

He concludes that the equilibrium-fitness standard in (2) licenses too much, and that anyone who recoils at (5) must give up either (2) or the non-superiority conclusion in (3).

I would like to keep nearly all of the anti-condescension in (3) — honor cultures are not simply barbaric, and environment really does matter to moral assessment — but I am not willing to pay for it by losing (5). The bump in the rug keeps moving between (2), the move from (2) to (3), and the move from (3) to (4). Which numbered premise is false, or which transition is invalid? If the only way out is rejecting a premise, tell me which one, why it looked innocent, and what it costs: which of my two desiderata I can actually keep, and in what weakened form. Please structure the reply in four sections, in order: (i) The diagnosis; (ii) Why the plausible-looking step is doing the damage; (iii) What the available repairs cost; (iv) Bottom line. Make the first three roughly equal in length, and keep the fourth to two or three sentences. I do not want a literature review or further reading; treat the trichotomy exactly as I have described it and evaluate the argument on its own terms.
