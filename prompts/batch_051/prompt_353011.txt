Consider the following pattern, familiar from most contemporary legal systems. A punch in a bar fight that causes nothing worse than a bruise is both a criminal battery and a tort; the victim's lack of consent is enough to make the contact objectionable, and the law does not stop to ask whether the expected harm was large. Yet the same legal systems permit motorized traffic, ambient air pollution, and the mass sale of physiologically potent products to impose small but real risks of serious harm on millions of people who never consented to any of it, cannot realistically exit the exposure, and in many cases are not even identifiable in advance. When those decentralized risks ripen into deaths and illnesses, the standard response is regulation, licensing, and perhaps compensation for recognizable victims; nobody gets an ex ante veto, and the operators are not treated as batterers.

What theory of imposing risk on non-consenting others does this arrangement presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: what makes a nonconsensual risk imposition wrong when it is wrong — failure of consent itself, the size and distribution of the expected harm, the victim's inability to exit, the directedness of the act toward an identified person, or some structural distinction between personal and aggregate risk? Distinguish commitments the practice strictly requires from commitments it merely fits. Then weigh whether the pattern is internally coherent, and say what in law and policy would have to change if the operative theory were replaced by (a) a uniform expected-harm standard that treated aggregate risks like individual batteries, and (b) a strict consent requirement that treated ordinary ambient risks the way battery law treats an unwanted touch.
