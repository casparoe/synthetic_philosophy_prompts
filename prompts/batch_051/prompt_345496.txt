I have been reading Kant's Lectures on Ethics, specifically the section on duties towards animals and spirits. He claims there that animals are not self-conscious, so we do not owe them anything directly; every duty we seem to have toward them is really an indirect duty to humanity. He says that cruelty to a dog, for example, is not wrong because of what it does to the dog, but because it damages the feeling for the dog's suffering in the person who does it, and that this same feeling is needed for our duties toward other human beings. In the same discussion, he allows that we may kill animals for food and use them for work, so long as the killing is not drawn out and the work is not beyond their strength. I find myself stuck on the asymmetry this view produces. A person who shoots a healthy dog because the dog is no longer useful is condemned as inhuman, even though the act is quick. A person who eats a cutlet is not condemned, even though the calf he ate endured a worse life and a more systematic kind of killing. The difference seems to be that the first act damages the agent's character, while the second is supposed to be compatible with a properly sympathetic character. Is that a real difference, or does it just enshrine local attachments to pets? I want the strongest version of the indirect-duty justification for this asymmetry, then a test against nearby cases: a stray dog put down quickly by a shelter, a pet pig kept as a companion, a working horse worn out through years of labor, a man who tortures a dog but is otherwise gentle to people. Does the indirect-duty view give the right verdicts? And should the asymmetry between companion animals and livestock be kept, revised, or given up once the view is pressed?
