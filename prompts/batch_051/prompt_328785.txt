I'm a journalist who covers science and philosophy — I do a little column on ideas and how they play out in ordinary life — and I'm going to run a 50-minute session at a local adult-ed night, maybe 18 to 24 people, mostly smart non-specialists. I want them to leave with a gut-level feel for a distinction that took me forever to get: you can be complicit in a wrongdoing without having caused it. Not just the weak sense that you didn't personally do the worst part, but the stronger case where your particular act made no causal difference at all — the decision would've gone through without your vote, the crowd would have been big enough anyway, the memo you forwarded changed nobody's mind — and still, its partly yours.

Design me a runnable activity for that session. I'm picturing something like a role-play or small-stakes game, not a lecture and not a discussion question. The rules or roles should be set up so participants actually find themselves contributing to a jointly produced bad outcome while each one can truthfully say "it would have happened without me." Maybe a threshold vote, an anonymous contribution, a distributed task with one redundant stage — whatever, up to you, but the complicity should come from the structure, not from the facilitator announcing it. Then give me a debrief that moves step by step from "did you cause it?" to "were you nonetheless in it?" and that maps what happened onto something like the philosophical idea that moral involvement can flow from what we join and intend, not just from what our actions bring about. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (people refusing to play, someone seeing the setup and spoiling it, the group explaining away there discomfort, etc.) and be honest about which parts of real complicity the exercise can't reproduce and I'll have to supply in my own words. American English please, and keep materials simple — nothing that needs money or preprinted cards if it can be helped.
