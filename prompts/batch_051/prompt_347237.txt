Two people in my building were arguing in the laundry room last night and I keep going back and forth on who's right. Dan said he can't take his friend Mia's lectures about ghosting people seriously anymore, because Mia once ghosted someone for three weeks and only ended it with a text. Jo said that's a cop-out: Mia's point is that ghosting is cowardly and unkind, and whether she once did it doesn't change whether it's true. Dan said its not about truth, it's about standing — she doesn't have the standing to blame anybody for this, the way a burglar doesn't get to lecture people on shoplifting. Jo said the burglar's lecture would still be true, and if the shoplifter's comeback is just "you did it too," that's a deflection, the tu quoque fallacy. Dan said no, because blaming isn't just stating a fact, it's a kind of demand — it says "I get to hold you to this" — and from a hypocrite that demand is hollow or even manipulative, because she's claiming a position she's already shown she doesn't actually hold. Jo said if that's right, then the worse someone's own record is, the more shielded from criticism everyone around them becomes, and that can't be right: bad people can make true accusations and sometimes they're the only ones who saw what happened. Dan said fine, then she can make the accusation as a witness, like reporting a fact, but not as a moral judge — the thing he objects to is the judgmental tone, the taking up of a role she forfeited. Jo said that distinction between reporting and blaming is fake: calling an act cowardly is blaming, and it stands or falls on the act, not on the speaker's biography. They turned to me and I just shrugged. Can you walk through this move by move? For each step, who has the better of it, where exactly each one goes wrong or overstates, and what are they both missing about when someone does or doesn't have standing to blame?
