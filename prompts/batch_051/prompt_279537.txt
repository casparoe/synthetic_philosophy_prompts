I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I run into this on every book. A huge amount of my job is inside a character's head — narrated feelings, free indirect style, someone explaining why she did something — and my actual method for deciding whether those passages are true to a mind is to introspect and trust whatever "rings true." I suspect that's just taste. I'd like you to turn whatever is actually known about the reliability of introspection into a revision procedure I can run on a scene. The decision it has to help with is: when is an introspective claim (mine, the narrator's, a character's) licensed as a report about a mind, and when is it just a story being told? I know people confabulate reasons for their choices and so on, but I've never seen that distilled into something usable for a working writer. Constraints: it has to run in about half an hour per scene, no psych training, no looking anything up, and it has to be usable on my own draft, where I can't interrogate a subject — I only have the sentences on the page and my own head.

Give me the questions in order, what counts as a pass on each, where the procedure says "trust this," where it says "no, don't," where it hands the call to me because it's an artistic choice rather than a fact about minds, and what the procedure can't catch. Then run it on three spots from a hypothetical draft: (1) a paragraph of present-tense felt experience — a man hearing his father has died, the texture of the first minute; (2) a scene where a character explains to her sister, two years later, why she really left her husband; (3) a line I'm attached to where the narration says she'd known, deep down, all along, that the marriage was already over. Say which steps in your procedure are solid and which are just me wanting a checklist to dress up taste. And if the premise is the problem — treating introspection as one thing you can trust or not — tell me that and give me the version that's actually usable; dont politely comply if the task cant be done.
