A friend of mine has been running a little reading group on definitions of life, and she's been keeping a running list of how she classifies the usual hard cases. Her verdicts so far are:

- E. coli in log phase: alive.
- A healthy adult mule: alive.
- A dessicated bacterial endospore that can sit dormant for decades and then revive when rehydrated: alive.
- A virus particle sitting outside any host cell: not alive.
- A prion: not alive.
- A forest fire moving through dry brush: not alive.
- A growing crystal in a saturated solution: not alive.

She says she's pretty confident these all hang together, and they're not just gut calls. But when I try to pull a single rule out of her list, it looks to me like at least two different principles fit all seven verdicts, and they'd come apart on a case we were arguing about. The case is a synthetic protocell: a lipid vesicle with a handful of enzymes inside it that take up precursor molecules from the surrounding medium, use them to make new membrane lipid, and patch holes in the vesicle. The thing maintains a stable internal pH and keeps its own membrane intact for a couple of weeks, as long as precursors and an energy source are supplied. But it has no nucleic acids, no genes, and it can't copy most of its own parts or divide; when an enzyme denatures there's no way to replace it, so each one eventually winds down and the researchers have to build new ones from scratch.

What should she say about the protocell, if she wants to be consistent with her own list? Is her list enough to settle it, or does it underdetermine the answer? If it underdetermines it, which of her earlier cases is the closest analog (is the protocell more like the endospore, more like the virus, or something else entirely), what criterion of life is she implicitly adopting with each possible verdict, and what additional case would force a choice between them?
