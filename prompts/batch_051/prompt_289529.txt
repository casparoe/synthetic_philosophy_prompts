I am a novelist, and I recently joined the editorial board of a literary magazine whose most widely shared feature is a monthly column called Small World, made up of reader-submitted accounts of astonishing coincidence: a passenger on a long flight discovers that the stranger beside her grew up on the same cul-de-sac; a man's new colleague turns out to be the person who found and returned his lost passport a decade earlier; two guests at a wedding learn they were born in adjacent rooms of the same hospital. The current policy is deliberately light. A contributor signs a statement that the account is true, an editor verifies whatever is cheaply checkable, and the piece is printed as a true coincidence. At last month's meeting, three board members argued that this is harmless entertainment and that adding any skepticism would be pedantic, since no reader mistakes the column for a survey.

My difficulty comes from thinking about coincidence as a novelist. In fiction, a coincidence is supposed to earn its place: it must pay for itself in meaning or plot, and a writer who imports a too-convenient collision is rightly accused of cheating, because the reader knows the author staged it. Small World quietly inverts that discipline. We receive roughly a thousand submissions a month and print six, drawn from a readership whose lives generate an enormous number of opportunities for near matches—same city, same year, same employer, same rare surname—and the boundary of what counts as the same is drawn after the fact. The birthday paradox is the clean version of my worry: with enough pairs and a forgiving standard of resemblance, a collision with tiny individual probability becomes ordinary in the aggregate, and the fact that this particular reported coincidence was unlikely tells us almost nothing once we recall how many other coincidences could have been reported instead.

Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before next Tuesday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete policy for the column—continue as is, reframe each piece with context, tighten the verification standard, suspend the feature, or some combination—with criteria the editors can actually apply to a submission, and it should engage the strongest form of the objection that all of this is merely entertainment.
