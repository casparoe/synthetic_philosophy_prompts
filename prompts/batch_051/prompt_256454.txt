I am a novelist, and I have spent the better part of two decades trying to write characters who are not merely functions of plot. Over that time I have settled into a set of convictions about what a self is and about what I am doing when I write a character. I would like an audit of whether these convictions hang together, because I suspect the fault line is somewhere I am not looking.

Here is where I have landed.

1. I take the self to be a strange loop, in roughly the sense Hofstadter gives the term: an abstract, self-referential pattern whose existence does not depend on any particular physical substrate, only on there being some substrate rich enough to support the right kind of symbolic feedback.

2. I believe that a well-made fictional character can become a self in precisely this sense. The character is a loop that runs across the text, my imagination, and the minds of readers; it is not reducible to the sentences that describe it.

3. I also believe that the author's 'I' is not a fixed thing that precedes the work. The authorial self is another loop, and in writing a character the two loops partly implement each other. My novels have literally changed who I am, not merely my opinions or my memories.

4. I do not think a character needs narrative continuity, a single point of view, or a coherent biography in order to be a self. What matters is the self-referential structure of the loop, not linearity or consistency.

5. And yet I cannot bring myself to say that a computer program, however intricate, could be a self. At most it would simulate selfhood, because there is no felt interior, nothing it is like to be the program itself.

6. Finally, I believe that if a character has become a self, I as its author am in a privileged position to know it. No reader can know the character as I do.

I would like to know whether this set is jointly tenable. Which commitments pull against which? Which pairs are only apparently in tension, and which tensions are the serious ones? If something has to give, what is the cheapest repair, and what does it cost? And I would ask you to say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict about where the fault line lies, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
