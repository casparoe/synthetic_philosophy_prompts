Last weekend I watched two of my friends argue for most of an hour about a small decision a mutual acquaintance had made, and I still do not know how I would referee it. I am a graduate student in an unrelated field, so I would appreciate an expert decision theorist's help. The acquaintance, Jayden, had paid $40 to a website running a "post-singularity reconstruction register." The site's offer, as Jayden summarized it, is this: it keeps a list of names on a server; if a future superintelligence ever gains the power to reconstruct past people (the site grants that this is extraordinarily unlikely), registered names get priority, and the fee pays for the server and the curation of the list. Jayden reasoned that even if the probability any of this works is only about one in a billion, the payoff — an indefinitely long future life of well-being — is so enormous that the expected value dwarfs $40, so the one-time payment was reasonable.

Here is the argument I overheard. (1) Mara called Jayden irrational: the real probability is not one in a billion but a number no one is in a position to name, and once you allow the possibility of large enough rewards, any story with a non-zero probability can be made to dominate any finite alternative — which shows the calculation is broken, not that the website is a good bet. (2) Daniel replied that expected-value reasoning is exactly how sensible people buy insurance and take long-shot bets; refusing to multiply a tiny probability by a large payoff is just probability neglect dressed up as common sense, and if Jayden's probability was wrong, the remedy is a better probability, not abandoning the calculation. (3) Mara answered that for claims about future superintelligences and privately held lists there is no better probability to be had; where the probability is unknowable, expected value offers no guidance, and the sane move is to decline the gamble rather than invent a number. (4) Daniel said that even granting an unknowably small chance, Jayden's choice is defensible as a cheap lottery ticket: $40 is trivial, people spend more on an ordinary evening out, and the upside is unbounded. Mara countered that this is the exact shape of being mugged — pay now, receive nothing, because the story was engineered to exploit the gap between zero evidence and infinite promise — and added that Pascal's wager has the same structure and fails in the same way. (5) Daniel then said the mugging worry only works if utilities are unbounded; place a finite ceiling on how good an outcome can be for a person, and a sufficiently tiny probability can no longer dominate a certain loss. Mara replied that a ceiling we cannot locate or justify does no practical work and merely relocates the invented number.

The argument stalled there, and both of them pressed me to referee, which I could not do. Please take the role of a philosopher of decision theory and practical rationality, begin by distinguishing the issues you think are actually doing the work, and then adjudicate the exchange move by move: which of Mara's and Daniel's specific claims stand, which need qualification, and where each is stronger. In the process, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers and decision theorists, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Also distinguish Pascal's mugging as a technical problem for expected-utility theory from its use as practical advice about a real choice, and say what the most defensible overall position on Jayden's payment is.
