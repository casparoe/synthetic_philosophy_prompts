I have spent enough years reading rationalist blogs and forums that I now reach almost automatically for a credence, a decomposition, and an account of what would change my mind. I want exactly that for a question on which my own number keeps shifting: whether Malthus is best classified as right but early, as falsified, or as rescued into unfalsifiability.

Here is the proposition I want you to put a probability on, stated so that it has a truth value in principle. I take Malthus's core structural claim to have been roughly this: a human population can multiply at a rate that the resources required to sustain it cannot match, so unless something else changes, some limiting pressure must eventually bind. By "right but early" I mean that this structural mechanism is broadly correct for human populations over the long run, and that the recognized failure of Malthus's specific predictions is best explained by parameter error — chiefly the rate of sustained technological improvement, institutional change, and the demographic transition — rather than by a structural mistake in the argument. By "falsified" I mean that the structural mechanism itself was wrong enough that the later history is not just a matter of recalibrating parameters. By "rescued into unfalsifiability" I mean that the claim has been adjusted, consciously or not, so that no actual trajectory of population and resources could count against it.

Give me one number between 0 and 1, representing your actual credence that "right but early" is the correct classification of Malthus's core claim today. I do not want "it depends on the time horizon" as a final answer; if you need a time horizon, choose one and say which it is.

Then decompose that number. I want at least the following four terms addressed, with a probability and one line of justification for each:

1. The mechanism. The probability that, over a genuinely long horizon — say the next thousand years, unless a binding constraint arrives sooner — human population and material consumption cannot grow multiplicatively forever, and that some limiting pressure (food, energy, land, phosphorus, atmospheric capacity, or another resource) must eventually bind in the absence of deliberate transformation.
2. The failure mode. Conditional on the mechanism being broadly right, the probability that Malthus's actual predictive failures were parameter errors rather than structural errors.
3. The category. The probability that "right but early" is a distinguishable epistemic category rather than a polite name for unfalsifiability — that is, that there is some observation or intervention which, had it occurred, would have counted against the claim even after the parameter corrections are made.
4. The classification. Conditional on the first three, the probability that applying the standards you endorsed in (3) to the actual history yields "right but early" rather than "falsified" or "rescued."

Add any further terms you think are carrying real weight. If any of the terms are not independent, say how the dependence runs and adjust the combination accordingly rather than multiplying through blindly. Then show explicitly how you combine the terms into your final credence.

Finally, tell me which term you are least confident in, and what evidence or argument — realistic or unrealistic — would move your final number the most, and through which term it would act. For calibration: I currently sit around 0.55, and a friend of mine sits around 0.9. If your number parts company with either of us, say where in the decomposition the disagreement most likely lives.
