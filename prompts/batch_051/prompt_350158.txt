I should say at the start that I have no academic background in philosophy or linguistics, so I have arrived at this view by observing conversations rather than by reading scholarly work. I would like you to engage with the view itself, not to place it in the literature.

The view I hold is this. A conversation has something like a public scoreboard, on which are recorded the propositions that the conversation is currently treating as settled. When a speaker makes a presupposing remark — using a noun phrase such as Sam's sister or the mayor, a verb such as manage or stop, a particle such as too, or a construction such as the fact that Sam was late — the speaker's main business is the point explicitly asserted, but underneath that the speaker is also putting a further proposition forward for the scoreboard. The scoreboard operates by a default rule: if nobody objects promptly, the proposition is written onto it. I think of this default as the whole of what gets called accommodation. On my view, it is not a change in what the listeners privately believe. It is a bookkeeping move on the public record. Listeners can leave a proposition on the scoreboard while privately doubting or even rejecting it, and in many cases that is exactly what they should do.

The picture appeals to me because it explains how presuppositions can slip claims into a conversation without anyone asserting them. The question Have you stopped cheating? cannot really be met with a bare yes or no, because by the time one answers, the proposition that one used to cheat is already sitting in the conversational record. The scoreboard view also explains how one speaker's presupposition binds later speakers. Once Sam's sister has been put on the board, someone can ask whether she is arriving by car, and everyone treats that follow-up as licensed even if no one would have endorsed the claim Sam has a sister beforehand. And the view predicts a familiar kind of delayed realization: a person can leave an exchange feeling that something has been conceded in the bookkeeping even though they never agreed to it and still do not believe it.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this view. Do not give me a reading list or a history of the idea. Tell me which observations, examples, distinctions, or arguments would most strongly push toward the conclusion that the scoreboard picture is false, or so incomplete that it cannot do the explanatory work I am claiming for it. Please rank these from the ones that would be fatal down to the ones that would merely be awkward. For each, say what a defender of the scoreboard view could reply, and whether that reply looks like a genuine refinement of the view or like a maneuver that protects the view from any possible disconfirmation. Then end with an honest assessment of whether the view, stated as I have stated it, still risks anything — and if it does not, whether that fact should count against the view or whether a claim about conversation is allowed to be that difficult to refute.
