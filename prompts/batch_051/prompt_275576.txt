I keep saying in seminar that a community is worth more than the sum of its members, but the students aren't feeling it, so I want an activity that makes it click before I give it a name. It's an undergrad political philosophy course (18 people, one 50-minute session, mixed majors). The topic is community and belonging as goods: specifically whats lost, and hard to replace, when a tight-knit community dissolves.

Design a structured simulation or role-play, runnable in 50 minutes, where students start off in small communities with some shared history and reputational accountability — repeated favors, a public ledger, insurance against random shocks, something like that — and then half the membership gets reassigned so the old communities dissolve. Keep the same people, same formal rules, and same payoffs after the reshuffle, so any change is down to the dissolution itself. I want the collapse (or at least the loss) to show up in their choices, not in a lecture afterwards.

Give me rules and a low-materials setup that doesn't need real money (points, choosing next week's case study, whatever), a payoff scheme with real-ish stakes, a minute-by-minute schedule, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the abstract goods: trust, being known, informal insurance, local accountability, shared memory. Also tell me what'll predictably go wrong the first time I run it (free-rding, last-round defection, groups rebuilding trust too fast) and how to fix or debrief each one. Be honest about which part of real community loss the game just can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself, and which feature of the design is doing most of the work.
