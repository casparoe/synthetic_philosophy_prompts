I am revising an upper-level unit on consciousness for my philosophy of mind course, and I keep returning to a meeting point between Dennett's attack on the Cartesian theater and an experiment students half-remember from introductory psychology: Benjamin Libet's free-will experiments from the early 1980s. My students can usually recall the headline result—that a readiness potential appears in the brain before subjects report being aware of their intention to move, and that Libet took this to show that the brain "decides" before the conscious self does—but they rarely ask what the experiment must already assume about consciousness for that headline to make sense. I want the assignment to force that question.

Here is the episode as I would present it. In Libet's setup, subjects watched a rapidly rotating spot on an oscilloscope and flexed a wrist whenever they chose, reporting the spot's position at the moment they first became aware of the urge or intention to move. Scalp EEG showed a readiness potential beginning roughly half a second before the movement, while the reported time of first awareness, W, fell only around two tenths of a second before it. Libet read the gap as evidence that the brain initiates action before conscious willing enters the picture, though he allowed that consciousness might still exercise a late veto by blocking an already-initiated action in the final fraction of a second before execution. Dennett's multiple drafts model rejects the architecture that gives this comparison its force: there is no single place in the brain where an intention arrives and gets time-stamped as conscious, and no definite moment at which the conscious decision occurs. Reports of when one became aware are not neutral readouts of an inner clock; they are themselves constructed drafts, sensitive to how the question is asked and to what the subject is doing.

Using the Libet episode as a case study, analyze it through the lens of the Cartesian theater/multiple drafts distinction. Your answer should (a) state exactly what the Cartesian theater would have to be true for the gap between the readiness potential and W to carry the metaphysical weight Libet assigned to it; (b) show how the multiple drafts model would redescribe the situation—what becomes of the readiness potential, the reported W time, and the supposed veto window once the theater is dropped; (c) identify which features of the episode the multiple drafts model handles cleanly, which it leaves underdescribed, and which it may distort; and (d) close by saying what the episode, so interpreted, shows about the theory itself—whether it amounts to a confirmation, a merely congenial illustration that does little independent work, or something else. Where the model is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
