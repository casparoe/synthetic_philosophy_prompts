I keep running into this argument at a reading group on Dennett's three stances and real patterns, and I cant find the exit. It's meant to show the three stances arent really three, and it goes like this.

(1) All three stances are just strategies for finding patterns in a system's behavior: physical patterns, functional patterns, intentional patterns.

(2) A pattern is real only if it buys you compression, meaning the patterned description is shorter or more predictive than a brute list of what the system does.

(3) For any system and any finite chunk of behavior, there's some assignment of beliefs and desires that makes that behavior come out rational, if I'm allowed to tinker with the contents.

(4) Nothing in the intentional stance stops me from tinkering, so it licenses post hoc fixes whenever a prediction fails.

(5) Therefore an intentional pattern is no more or less real than any other compressing description; the only difference between the stances is vocabulary and what I find easy to track.

(6) So the three stances collapse into one stance, find a pattern any pattern, and calling one system an agent and another a mere physical or functional system is just taste.

(7) But that conclusion seems too strong: the intentional stance is supposed to be licensed for people, maybe chess computers and some animals, but not rocks or piles of sand, and giving that licensing up would mean we cant say some things literally have beliefs.

I want to keep the hierarchy of stances and reject something around (3) or (4), but I can't see how to do that without making compression a mysterious non-pattern fact. Which step should I reject, and what does it cost? If the real answer is that the three stances do collapse, tell me what that leaves.
