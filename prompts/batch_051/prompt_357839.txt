You are a philosopher of language with a working knowledge of how literary fiction is written and revised. I am a novelist, fluent in the practical vocabulary of my craft: voice, register, point of view, free indirect discourse, dialogue and dialect, the copyedit as distinct from the story edit, and the workshop question of whether an odd sentence is an error or a choice. I want you to restate the debate between prescriptivism and descriptivism about grammar — roughly, whether there is such a thing as bad grammar, or whether "bad grammar" is only a social judgment or a category mistake — in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, concept by concept: the prescriptivist's claim that some constructions are incorrect, the descriptivist's claim that nonstandard varieties are internally rule-governed, the charge that prescriptive rules are just the prestige dialect enforced by institutions, and the worry that some sentences are genuinely unclear or ungrammatical rather than merely nonstandard. For each element, tell me what translates cleanly, what must be distorted to fit, and what has no expression at all in a novelist's terms. Then give me the residue report: which side of the debate loses most in translation, and whether what fails to carry over is a defect in my vocabulary or a sign that the original claim was less precise than it looked. Think through the mapping before you answer, and keep the translation honest rather than treating either framework's assumptions as neutral.
