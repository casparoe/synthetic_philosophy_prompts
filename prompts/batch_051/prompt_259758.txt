I have been reading about rational irrationality, and I reconstructed Derek Parfit's hitchhiker example this way. A hitchhiker is stranded somewhere dangerous; a passing driver will rescue him only if he sincerely promises to pay a large sum once he is safe, and the driver is very good at detecting insincere promises. The hitchhiker knows that, once safe, he will have strong self-interested reasons not to pay and will renege, and he cannot make himself sincere now simply by deciding to be. But suppose he has a pill that would give him a disposition to keep his promise even when keeping it is contrary to his later interests. Taking the pill seems rational now, since it is his only way to be rescued and its cost is smaller than staying stranded, even though the disposition it induces would later lead him to act against his own interests. I find this a compelling case that it can be rational to bring about your own future irrationality.

Even so, the reasoning behind the case seems to generalize in a way I distrust, and I built an argument that now strikes me as proving too much. (1) In the pill case, it is rational to cause a mental state in yourself solely because of its downstream benefits, even though that state is not one that rationality would otherwise recommend. (2) What rationalizes the pill is just that it brings about a useful mental state; the state's fit with the agent's evidence, or its intrinsic reasonableness, is doing no independent work. (3) This rationale does not depend on whether the useful state is conative or cognitive, a desire, a disposition, or a belief. (4) So by parity, if a useful but evidentially unsupported belief could be induced by a similar pill, it would be rational to induce it: for instance, taking a pill that made you believe your struggling business would succeed, simply because the confidence would make you work harder, or one that made you believe your marriage was better than your evidence suggests, simply because the belief would keep you committed. (5) But that cannot be right; the mere usefulness of a belief gives no rational license to hold it against the evidence. (6) Therefore either the original hitchhiker verdict is wrong, or at least one of the premises misdescribes the principle at work.

I would like to keep (1), because the hitchhiker case seems decisive, and (5), because I do not want to abandon evidential norms for the sake of useful delusion. So my suspicion falls somewhere in (2) through (4), but I cannot see exactly where, and I worry that my reservations are themselves question-begging. Where does this argument fail, what is the least costly repair, and what am I committed to if I keep both (1) and (5) and reject whichever step must go?
