I'm putting together a zine on decision theory and common sense for friends who never took a class in either, and I want one piece of sustained satire aimed at Pascal's mugging: the move where a stranger asks you for, say, ten dollars, and promises that unless you pay, an astronomically large number of people (think up-arrow notation) will suffer, at only a tiny but nonzero probability — so a flat expected-utility calculation seems to say you should hand over the money. I'd like it to be a parody of a startup landing page plus FAQ plus terms of service for a subscription service (call it MuggerBlock or whatever you like) that claims to protect you from ever getting Pascal-mugged again, but is itself structured as a Pascal's mugging. Keep it deadpan and corporately cheerful. The humor has to come from the actual mechanics of the problem, not just from making it absurd: the dance between an astronomically small probability and an astronomically large payoff, whether you're allowed to put any upper bound on utility, the idea that any nonzero credence in the threat seems to commit you to paying some finite amount, and the way each payment can set up a slightly bigger threat next week. Aim for around 700 words, formatted as landing-page copy plus numbered clauses. It should be funny to someone who knows the puzzle cold and still readable to someone who has never heard of it.

After the piece, add a short author's note, maybe 150 words, saying where the satire leans on a contested reading of the mugging — for instance, whether the real disagreement is about unbounded utility, about priors, or about the difference between a precommitment you can make ahead of time and a one-off mugging — and name the argument from someone who genuinely takes the mugging seriously that would most make you rewrite one of the jokes. Dont worry about making the note itself funny.
