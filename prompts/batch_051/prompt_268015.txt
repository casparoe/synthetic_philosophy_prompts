You're a scholar of midcentury British political writing, especially Orwell's essay Politics and the English Language and the Newspeak material in Nineteen Eighty-Four. Before you write, actually think through what Orwell argued, because the humor has to come from getting him right rather than from vaguely gesturing at big brother. Brainstorm at least four specific habits he attacked — dying metaphors, pretentious Latinate diction, verbal false limbs and operators, meaningless words, euphemism, the bit where he translates Ecclesiastes into flabby modern English — and note what he thought those habits do politically: fog up thought, let power hide ugly acts, make lies sound dreary and respectable.

Then write the piece: a deadpan bureaucratic parody, a leaked internal style guide from the Ministry of Truth (or a front body you invent) issued to junior writers and editors who produce Party pamphlets and telescreen copy, dated arround 1984. Each rule should take one of Orwells actual complaints and turn it into sensible-sounding official policy. So writers get told, as a matter of style, to prefer the inflated phrase over the concrete one, to reach for the prefabricated metaphor, to avoid fresh wording on security grounds, to use words like 'free' only inside Ministry-approved fixed phrases. That kind of thing. Stay helpful and managerial the whole time — never wink, never break character, never name Newspeak in the piece itself. A reader who knows Orwell should recognize exactly which lecture each clause is drawn from; a reader who doesn't should still feel the thought-control leaking through. No direct quotations from Orwell anywhere, and no citations or reading list.

Format: exactly five numbered clauses, each a short paragraph of about 100–130 words, for a total in the 550–650 range. No introduction, no conclusion, no heading — rule 1 is the first thing on the page and rule 5 the last.
