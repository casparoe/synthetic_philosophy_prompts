A friend and I spent most of the evening arguing about a claim I made: that we owe each other a certain respect and recognition in attitude, not only in action — that how one privately regards another person can itself be a way of wronging them. He held that only actions and speech can wrong a person, and that a hidden attitude, however contemptuous, is not morally assessable unless it leaks into behavior. I resisted, but my cases pull in different directions, and I could not state the rule underlying my own responses. I am hoping there is a defensible principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that clarifies the matter.

My verdicts on particular cases:

1. A hospice nurse gives a demanding patient flawless care and never lets her contempt affect her work, though privately she despises him and wishes he would die soon. I think she wrongs the patient, though less than she would by mistreating him.

2. A donor funds the local food bank generously but regards its clients as lazy and enjoys feeling superior to them; no recipient ever notices. I think his contempt is morally bad, but I hesitate to say he wrongs any particular person.

3. A manager promotes a candidate who is plainly the best-qualified, but privately thinks of her as a diversity hire and resents the decision. I am fairly confident the manager wrongs her, even though the action itself was right.

4. A server must smile and apologize to a customer who has just abused her; privately she loathes him. I do not think her loathing is wrong in the least.

5. After betraying a friend, a person apologizes only because the friendship is useful, feels no remorse, and privately thinks the friend overreacted; the apology is convincing. I think the absence of remorse adds a second wrong, but I cannot tell whether it wrongs the friend or is only a defect in the apologizer.

6. A colleague publicly credits my idea to me because the professional costs of not doing so would be high, but privately is certain that someone with my background could not have produced it and assumes I got lucky. This feels worse than the donor's general contempt, even though no outward act differs from a respectful colleague's.

Cases 1 and 4 are the pair I need the account to separate: private contempt coexisting with correct outward behavior, yet opposite verdicts. My friend's proposed test was to look at what the attitude would lead the person to do if the costs of acting on it dropped. That handles the donor and the insincere apology, but it lets the nurse and the colleague count as fine so long as circumstances never change, which I cannot accept.

So my question is: what principle, if any, captures these verdicts? Is the wrong-making feature located in the attitude itself, in the mismatch between attitude and performance, or in what the attitude would license under changed circumstances? Which of my verdicts does the best principle force me to give up? And how does that principle come out on the case I keep flipping over: a politician who votes for a minority-rights law because it will help his re-election, while privately hoping the courts strike it down.
