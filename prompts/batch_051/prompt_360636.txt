I'm a retired geochemist — spent thirty years looking at isotope ratios and fluid inclusions — and in the last couple of years I've fallen into political philosophy instead. I now sit on the collections and restitution committee of a mid-sized natural-history museum, and we've got a case that's got to many strands, and I'd like help from someone who actually works on this stuff.

Here's the case. In 1904 our founder bought a small ceremonial bronze plaque at a London auction; the lot came from the estate of a British colonial officer, and the sale catalogue says he carried it off during a punitive expedition in 1896, after the kingdom's royal treasury was dispersed. No one disputes that original taking — it was looted at gunpoint, more or less. The museum bought in good faith and has held and displayed the piece ever since; it's become one of our signature objects. Now the national museum of the modern country is asking for it back, claiming to act for the descendant community. Half the committee says stolen is stolen, end of story. The other half says we bought honestly, looked after it for over a century, and returning it would just put it in the hands of whatever regime happens to be in power.

I've recently worked through Robert Nozick's entitlement theory, and I've been trying to get the committee to use it: justice in acquisition, justice in transfer, and rectification of past injustice. The Wilt Chamberlain argument keeps coming up. As I understand it, if the starting holdings are just, and lots of people each freely pay a little extra to watch Chamberlain play, the lopsided pile of money he ends up with is also just, even though it wrecks whatever pattern — equality, desert, need — you'd imposed. One colleague says that by the same logic our title is clean if every transfer after the looting was voluntary: the officer's estate sold the piece, our founder paid for it, done. To me that feels wrong, because the chain starts with force. But Nozick is maddeningly vague about how rectification actually works, and if we unwind every 19th-century wrong, I dont see the stopping rule.

So write me the committee's decision memo. You're a professional philosopher who works on property, historical injustice, and museum ethics. Please think through the options carefully before you settle on a recommendation. Start by brainstorming how each of Nozick's three principles would actually apply to these facts, and where the framework runs out or underdetermines the answer. Then add the non-Nozick considerations the committee would need to make a defensible decision in the real world, and land on a clear bottom line. Make the memo actionable, and state the strongest argument on the losing side as fairly as you can. Also tell me which features of the case are doing the real work: if the museum had known the piece was looted when it bought, if the descendant community didn't actually support the claimant's request, or if the object had no ongoing cultural role for any living group, would your recommendation flip, and why?
