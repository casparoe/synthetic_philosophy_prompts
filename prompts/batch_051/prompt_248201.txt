I'm a retired chemist, and over the last couple years I've gotten into philosophy of language, mostly the debates about whether the meaning of a text is fixed by what its author intended. I used to think in terms of operational definitions, so I like working from concrete cases. I've collected my gut verdicts on a bunch of them, and I'd like you to help me find the principle behind them — or tell me which verdicts I have to drop.

Here are the cases.

1. A friend who was passed over for a promotion texts me "I'm just thrilled for you" right after I get it. I'm sure he means the opposite. I'd say the message means he's not thrilled; the literal words are being used ironically.

2. Someone emails me "I'll meat you at noon." I'd say it means they'll meet me at noon. The typo doesn't change the meaning because the intended word is obvious.

3. Two students agree that "the library is open" will mean "the exam answers are online." When one texts it to the other, I'd say it means the exam answers are online — but only for them. A teacher who saw the text would be right to take it as meaning the literal thing.

4. My granddaughter has those magnetic word tiles on the fridge. One morning they happen to spell "the blue cat sleeps." I don't think anyone intended anything by it, but I'd still say it means that a blue cat is sleeping.

5. I read a poem from around 1700 with a line about the morning star "preventing" the dawn. In that period "prevent" could mean "come before." I'd say the line means the star precedes the dawn, not that it stops the dawn, even though a modern reader would naturally hear "stops."

6. A novelist has a character say "the sea is a cruel mistress." The author later said he privately meant it as a comment on his own marriage. I don't think that private intention changes what the sentence means in the novel; it means what it means in the story.

7. A city council passes a resolution saying "the park shall be closed after dusk." The members had privately agreed that "dusk" would mean 9 p.m. in the summer, but the text doesn't say that. I'd say the resolution means dusk in the ordinary public sense, not their private gloss.

My working rule was: the meaning of a text is what the author intended to communicate. But cases 4 through 7 all apperently push toward convention or public meaning, while 1, 2 and 3 push toward intention. I can't tell whether I need one principle, two seperate notions of meaning, or a repair of my gut verdicts.

Please think carefully — you can brainstorm a few candidate principles before settling on one — and, as an expert in philosophy of language, tell me: is there a single account that delivers all seven verdicts? If not, which should I overturn, and why? And what does your account say about a case I keep going back and forth on: a politician reads a speech a staffer wrote, and a line about "a bridge to the future" is meant by the staffer as a nod to a specific infrastructure bill, meant by the politician as a vague slogan, and heard by the audience as a promise to build an actual bridge in their town. What does the line mean, or is the question badly posed?
