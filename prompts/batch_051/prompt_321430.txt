I'm writing the final exam for an upper-level philosophy of physics course whose enrolment is split between physics students and philosophy students who came to the material through the decision-theory side of the rationalist world rather than through quantum foundations. We have spent several weeks on Everettian quantum mechanics, and I want one essay question on the Born rule problem that cannot be answered well by reciting the standard package — the wavefunction branches, every outcome occurs, and rational agents weight branches by squared amplitude — because that package is exactly what I want them to prise apart. The exam gives them 75 minutes and the question is worth 20 marks. Please write the question itself, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth four marks each, with a one-line note on how candidates typically get each point wrong. Please keep the whole thing in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as though you were writing it up for me in an email. And because this literature is a minefield, I would ask you to separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this — the bare architecture of many-worlds, the determinism of the unitary dynamics, the fact that all decohered outcomes exist as branches in the standard Everettian picture — from what is live and contested, such as whether probabilities are incoherent or merely in need of a measure, whether self-locating uncertainty is coherent, and whether the decision-theoretic derivations buy anything rather than smuggling the Born weights into their axioms. Do not present a contested line as the field's consensus.

For the question itself, I have in mind an exchange to evaluate rather than an open essay asking students to discuss the Born rule problem in general. An objector argues as follows. In an Everettian universe the state before a spin measurement is a superposition of up and down, both decohered branches will exist, and the dynamics is deterministic; so the up outcome occurs with probability one, the down outcome also occurs with probability one, and the agent before the measurement cannot be uncertain about which outcome will happen, since both happen. Therefore, the objector concludes, talk of probabilities for outcomes loses its subject matter, and no derivation of the Born rule within many-worlds can even get started. A sophisticated Everettian replies that the objector has confused a third-person fact about the branching structure with a first-person question about what I should expect: I should be uncertain about which branch I will experience, and I should proportion that uncertainty to the squared amplitude, because larger-amplitude branches are the ones I should expect to find myself in. The exam question should ask students to evaluate this exchange by, first, separating the question whether self-locating uncertainty is coherent at all from the question what measure such uncertainty should have; second, saying what work the appeal to squared amplitude does in the Everettian reply and whether it is being assumed or earned there; and third, giving a reasoned verdict on what, if anything, of the objector's challenge survives after the best version of the reply. Please put the exam question in your own words and make it self-contained for someone who has not seen my sketch above.
