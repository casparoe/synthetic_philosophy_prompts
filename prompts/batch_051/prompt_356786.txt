I've been turning over a passage in Jeremy Bentham's An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, the footnote where he says, roughly, that the day may come when people recognize that the number of legs, the hairiness of the skin, or the shape of the tailbone are just as irrelevant to whether a being should be mistreated as skin colour is. The test he cares about is whether the being can suffer, not what the being looks like or how its skeleton is arranged.

Here's the thing I keep getting stuck on. A lot of people now say insects probably aren't sentient, or that each individual insect has only a small chance of being sentient, because their nervous systems are so simple and their behaviour is mostly reflexive. But there are something like a billion billion insects alive at any given time, and we kill or harm staggering numbers of them through agriculture, pesticides, heating, and just going about our lives. So even if the chance that some random beetle or caterpillar can suffer is one in ten thousand, the expected number of suffering beings from, say, a crop spray is still enormous. The expected value calculation doesn't care that your confidence is low per individual; it multiplies by the population.

I want to know what Bentham's principle actually commits him to here. Is the "can they suffer?" test a binary thing, where you need sufficient evidence of sentience in the individual before it counts at all? Or does it work like a probability-weighted utilitarian calculation, where a tiny chance of sentience across a huge population generates real obligations? And if Bentham's principle does push us toward worrying about insect suffering on scale grounds, does that mean his footnote was far more radical than he could have realized, given that he had no idea how many insects there were or how much we'd end up killing them? Or would he have drawn some line about certainty or about observable responsiveness that blocks the scale argument? I'm less interested in the practical answer than in whether the scale argument is a natural extension of Bentham's own test or an abuse of it.
