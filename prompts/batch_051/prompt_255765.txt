Financial regulators and tax authorities in several jurisdictions run whistleblower reward programs that pay an employee a share of the penalties recovered when the employee brings original, non-public evidence of their own employer's fraud directly to the agency. To collect, the tipster generally must supply information that has not merely been taken from the news or public filings; internal reporting is encouraged but not required; and the employee may collect while the agency shields their identity from the employer. The reward is thus paid for informing on one's own firm, yet the informing is kept confidential, and public disclosure of the same evidence is precisely what the program does not pay for. What view of loyalty and betrayal does this design presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of loyalty the practice seems committed to, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say whether its parts presuppose consistent things. In particular, address whether it presupposes that an employer forfeits any claim to an employee's loyalty by breaking the law, that loyalty is ultimately owed to the political community rather than to one's associates, or only that the public interest in detection outweighs a genuine betrayal the whistleblower still commits. Then say what would have to change in the design of such programs if one held instead that loyalty is a virtue of persons rather than an entitlement of institutions, or that a loyal employee owes candor to the firm before anyone else.
