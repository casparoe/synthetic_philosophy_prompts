In Book III, Chapter II of An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations, Adam Smith observes that the law of primogeniture rests on a supposition he regards as the most absurd of all: that each successive generation has no equal right to the earth and all it possesses, and that the property of the present generation should be constrained and regulated according to the inclinations of those who died hundreds of years earlier. An inheritance-tax advocate in my reading group has been using this passage as the normative core of her argument. She says the analogy is straightforward: behind Smith’s attack on entailed landed estates lies a general principle that each birth cohort has an equal claim on society’s resources, so allowing financial wealth to descend lightly taxed across generations is just a modern form of primogeniture — a legal privilege that gives later generations no real starting gate and lets the long dead rule the living. I want to know how far this analogy actually holds. Can you lay out what maps onto what between Smith’s target, entail and primogeniture in a land-based economy, and modern inheritable financial wealth; where the mapping breaks or quietly imports assumptions; and whether her conclusion — that a substantial inheritance tax is required by the equal-right-of-generations principle, not merely by welfarist or revenue considerations — survives once the disanalogies are on the table? I am especially interested in which features of landed estates do the real argumentative work for Smith and whether those features carry over to a world where the largest fortunes are diversified, liquid, and managed by intermediaries.
