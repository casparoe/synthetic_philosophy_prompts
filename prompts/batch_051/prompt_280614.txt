As a philosophy professor who teaches decision theory, I have spent years collecting the rulings of a colleague who works on Dutch book arguments, because he issues only verdicts and never states the principle behind them. The cases all concern $1 tickets on events in a fixed partition: a ticket on event E priced at \(p\) costs \(p\) now and pays $1 if E obtains, and $0 otherwise. Among the rulings I have collected are these. For a student who assigns probability .5 to each side of a coin toss, he deems irrational the portfolio consisting of heads at $0.60 and tails at $0.60, but deems rational heads at $0.40 and tails at $0.50. For a student whose credences over a partition \(\{A,B,C\}\) are .30, .30, and .40, he deems irrational the portfolio of A at $0.45, B at $0.45, and C at $0.55, but deems rational A at $0.20, B at $0.20, and C at $0.30. Notice that the two portfolios he calls irrational each guarantee a net loss and each contain tickets priced above the student's credence, while the two he calls rational guarantee a net gain and contain no ticket priced above the student's credence.

Next week he must rule on a new case I have not yet shown him. A student arrives with credences .45, .45, and .45 over \(\{A,B,C\}\) — credences that do not sum to one — and accepts the package A at $0.45, B at $0.45, and C at $0.45. The package costs $1.35 and pays $1.00 for certain, a guaranteed loss of $0.35; yet each individual ticket is priced exactly at the student's own stated credence. If my colleague is to be consistent with his earlier verdicts, what should I predict he will say? Please identify the rule he seems to be running, state which other rule or rules fit his record equally well, and work through what each leading candidate rule predicts for this new case. Give your best prediction with the reasons for preferring it, and name one further portfolio that would decide among the remaining candidates. Finally, tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail and which consideration would change your mind.
