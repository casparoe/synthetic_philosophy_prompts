I teach a second-year undergrad ethics seminar (18 students, 70 minutes), and next week we hit the inefficacy objection: the thought that since my one flight, one burger, or one vote never makes a detectable difference, I'm not really on the hook for the collective bad. I want to run a game that makes them feel the force of that before I name it. Design a repeated commons dilemma with small real stakes (participation points or a shared snack fund). Each round, players choose whether to take a small private benefit; there's a collective bad that gets triggered once the total benefits taken cross a threshold, and the threshold is unknown or set so that in most rounds no single player's choice is what pushes it over. After the game they should be able to honestly say "my choice didn't cause that," and feel the pull of it, before the debrief complicates things. Include at least one round where a player's contribution provably cannot be the difference-maker (a sharp threshold they're not within one unit of), and one round where each choice shifts an invisible probability of the bad, so the debrief can seperate causal inefficacy from imperceptible expected difference. The debrief should walk them through: no-exception rules vs. imperceptible contributions, expected value vs. actually causing the outcome, being the threshold player, complicity in a practice, and the difference between a negligible effect and a zero effect. Give me full rules, materials, a timeline, teed-up debrief questions, and what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm especially worried about a couple of students going full charity and breaking the dilemma, and others getting realy annoyed that the game feels rigged. Also be honest about which feature of the real inefficacy objection (climate, meat, voting) the game can't capture, and what I'll have to supply in discussion.
