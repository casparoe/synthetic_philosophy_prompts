You are an analytic philosopher who works on the metaphysics of causation and is used to drawing distinctions that are easy to blur. I would like you to think slowly and carefully about a phenomenon I keep noticing in a reading group on whether causation is fundamental, reducible, or eliminable from a mature picture of the world. Some of us are sympathetic to the old Russellian complaint that the notion of cause is a piece of common sense with no clear place in finished physics; others want to keep it. I am not asking you to settle that question. What interests me is a dialectical pattern that the defenders of causal talk keep falling into, and whether it has been identified and named.

The pattern goes like this. When challenged, a defender first glosses cause in counterfactual terms: the cause of an event is whatever made the difference, in the sense that if that item had not occurred, or had been suitably different, the effect would not have occurred, or would have been different. Then, when someone presents a case in which that test picks out the wrong thing — for example, a back-up process that would have brought about the same effect if the actual cause had failed, so that the effect does not really depend on the actual cause — the same defender switches to a different gloss: the cause is what actually produced the effect, what the effect issued from, the thing that did the work. But when this second gloss is pressed with cases in which the only plausible candidate is an absence, an omission, or something that never touches the effect, they quietly slide back to the counterfactual gloss. The two criteria are never acknowledged together, and no one says which one governs when they conflict.

I am not asking which account of causation is correct, and I am not asking for a reading list or references. I want to know whether this particular back-and-forth — the use of two quite different tests for a single concept, each wheeled in to absorb counterexamples aimed at the other — has an established name of its own. Please begin by reconstructing the pattern in your own words, then distinguish it as precisely as you can from (i) ordinary equivocation between two senses of a word, (ii) an honestly held pluralism according to which causation really is two related things, and (iii) mere vagueness or family resemblance. If no established label fits exactly, propose one, define it, and say what would count as a boundary case of the pattern. Please write your answer in British English.
