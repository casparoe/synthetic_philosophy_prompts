Okay so I've been lurking in the life-extension/longevity corner of the rationalist internet for years now, and one thing that keeps bugging me is how stuck the whole deathism debate seems to be. The same handful of points get recycled — an indefinitely long life would get boring, death is what gives life its shape, the first people to get the tech would hoard it, wanting to live forever is just a failure to accept your own death, etc. But almost nobody has sorted out which of these are actually load-bearing. Some are claims about how an extended life would go for the person living it (boredom, identity drift across centuries), some are distributional worries about who gets access first, some are claims about what the fact of universal death does to the rest of the human psyche, and some are pretty obviously post-hoc rationalizations for a feature of the world that nobody can currently change.

What I want is a ranking, not a survey. Suppose I have about a year and a small budget (enough to read broadly, talk to a few people, maybe run an informal survey or two) to write one longform piece aimed at open-minded people who currently lean deathist — the goal is to actually change minds, not to dunk on the dumbest versions. Which two or three open questions in this area should I spend that year on, and in what order? For example, is the keystone the boredom/identity argument (not just "would you get bored," but "would the person 500 years from now even be you in the sense that matters"), so that if it fails most of the secular case against extension collapses? Or is the real load-bearing thing the distributional worry, so that even if extended life would go well for the individuals who got it, intragenerational inequality or moral hazard means we should be cautious by default? Or is the highest-leverage target actually the psychological layer — pro-aging trance, terror management, status quo bias — on the theory that the explicit arguments will just get swapped out and re-rationalized until the underlying thing is named?

Defend the ordering with something like importance-times-tractability-times-upstreamness: for each candidate, say why getting it right would change the shape of the rest of the debate or people's actual bottom line, how much real progress a non-expert with a year could realistically make, and which ones are traps that feel deep but wouldn't change anyone's mind if you solved them. I care less about the list than the reasoning about what would do the most downstream work.
