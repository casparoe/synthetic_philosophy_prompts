I am a high school student, and for my student-government class I have been asked to analyze a proposal my school is considering. The proposal is a dispute system built around the idea that envy and resentment can be read as signals: sometimes they point to a real injustice, and sometimes they are merely spiteful. Any student may file a "disparity claim" against another student, alleging that the other student's advantage — a grade, a team captaincy, a club presidency, a summer opportunity — was gained through unfair background conditions rather than merit, such as paid tutors, family connections, or access the claimant never had. A panel of two students and two teachers investigates. If the panel upholds the claim, the school compensates the claimant with a benefit of comparable kind: extra tutoring hours, a reserved study room, a chance to re-compete for the role, or similar. If the panel rejects the claim as untethered to any genuine unfairness, the claimant must publish a short retraction and loses the right to file any further claims for one semester. To discourage casual filings, submitting a claim costs the claimant two hours of school service; the hours are refunded only if the claim is upheld. All claims, rulings, and retractions are published with names included.

I am not asking whether the underlying moral idea is right. Treat this as a mechanism and walk through how it would actually behave. What would it incentivize among students, among the students who are accused, among teachers on the panel, and among parents? How would it be gamed, and by whom — for example, through performative resentment, strategic accusations against talented peers, or students cultivating visible disadvantages? What happens to friendships, reputations, and academic competition if every advantage can be publicly hauled before a panel? How would the panel's own social standing and incentives distort its rulings? And can any of the failure modes be fixed — for instance, by making claims anonymous, raising the deposit, or changing who sits on the panel — without gutting what the scheme is trying to do?
