Ever since I retired from physical chemistry (reaction kinetics, mostly) I've been reading epistemology, and I keep noticing a policy I apparently ran in the lab and still run now. I trust my own conclusion fully when I've checked every step myself: re-derived the equation, re-plotted the data, traced the argument line by line. If a genuinely smart peer disagrees with me about that kind of thing, I don't budge at all until they can show me the exact step where I went wrong. But for murkier judgements — how much confidence to put in an effect size from a noisy study, whether a sub-field has a systematic bias, whether some objector is just changing the subject — I automatically move halfway to the other person's view, often before they've even stated their reasons. I can't tell whether this two-track policy is defensible, or wether it's just me being conciliationist when I have little at stake and steadfast when my own careful work is on the line. Does the fact that a long derivation has a checkable route make disagreement about it rationally different from disagreement about a holistic judgement? Is the halfway rule itself even right for the murky cases — should I move all the way, or not at all, or at least wait for their reasons first? And can someone coherently be both a conciliationist about judgements and a steadfast person about derivations, or does one attitude eventually force the other once I'm honest about how thoroughly "checked" any piece of my own reasoning really is?
