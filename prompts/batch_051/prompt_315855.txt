I'm a novelist, not a lawyer, and my current novel turns on a point of inheritance law I'd rather get right than fudge — a beta reader's already told me the legal subplot feels hand-wavy. The scene: April 2020, first lockdown. An elderly man in a care home, Arthur, wants to change his will before an operation. No visitors allowed. His solicitor arranges a video call, and Arthur signs the new will on paper at his desk while two witnesses — a care-home nurse and the solicitor's assistant — watch from different rooms on tablets. The same document is then put in a sealed envelope and left outside Arthur's door; each witness signs it separately, half an hour apart, in the staff office down the corridor, with Arthur not present in person or on camera. The will is later challenged by a niece who would inherit more if Arthur had died intestate.

Assume Parliament has not amended the statute. The provision in question (first enacted in 1849, long before anyone imagined a video call) reads: "A will shall be valid only if the testator signs it in the presence of two witnesses who are present at the same time, and each witness signs it in the presence of the testator." On the plain words the will clearly fails. My question is whether a judge in England and Wales could plausibly save it by using the absurdity doctrine — the idea that a court may depart from the plain words where following them would produce an absurd or unreasonable result, and the statute can't have meant the thing it appears to mean. I'd like you to work through the different approaches: the ordinary meaning of the text, the purpose of the witnessing formalities, what a legislator in 1849 would have said if asked about remote witnessing, and what a reasonable person now would take the provision to require. Then tell me which approach should win here, and wether the absurdity doctrine is really doing the work or being stretched past its limits. Please answer in British English.
