Before I retired I worked as an experimental physicist, and I have spent my retirement reading philosophy of mind and cognitive science. The topic I keep returning to is concept learning, and more specifically Bongard problems: each problem presents six simple diagrams on the left and six on the right, where the six on the left share an organizing property that the six on the right lack, and the solver must induce, from those twelve examples alone, the rule that separates them. The easy cases are triangles versus squares. The hard cases involve abstract, relational, or context-dependent distinctions, and it is the hard cases that interest me.

What puzzles me is that the standard theories of concept acquisition seem to have settled into a long standoff over these problems. Prototype and exemplar accounts handle graded typicality and family resemblance, but they have trouble explaining how a solver arrives at the single, often disjunctive or relational rule behind a difficult Bongard problem from one positive set and one negative set. Rule-based accounts explain the decisive left/right classification, but critics find them brittle and uninformative when the rule is non-obvious or shifts with context. Bayesian accounts model the induction cleanly once a hypothesis space and a prior are given, but critics reply that choosing those is just the original problem relocated. Each literature seems aware of the others, and none of them has moved.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is less an empirical dispute than a disagreement about what the word "concept" is being used to denote: a recognitional capacity, a stored representation, or a principle for partitioning a space. If that is right, the theories may not be competing answers to one question at all. But I may be wrong; there may be a hidden factual question about what human solvers actually do, or about what Bongard problems actually measure, that could settle matters. I would like a diagnosis of why this debate has stalled and what kind of disagreement it really is, rather than a survey of the theories, together with an honest account of what, if anything, would count as genuine progress.
