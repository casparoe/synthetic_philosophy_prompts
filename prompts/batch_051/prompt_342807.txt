Our school district's curriculum policy, adopted in 1998, includes this clause: "When instruction concerns historical events over which professional historians hold differing interpretations, teachers shall present the principal competing interpretations fairly and shall not advocate for any one interpretation as correct." This year my U.S. history teacher assigned an excerpt from the 1619 Project as the framing for our unit on the American Revolution; the rest of the unit used our standard textbook, which takes the more traditional founding-era view, and the 1776 Report was not assigned at all. One parent has filed a complaint arguing that the clause requires the 1776 Report to be assigned as the competing interpretation, while a second parent argues that making the 1619 Project the frame for the unit itself violated the clause because it amounts to the teacher endorsing one interpretation. The policy predates both documents. I would like an analysis of what the clause actually requires in this case: work through the text, the purpose of the clause, and what the board that adopted it would most plausibly have said if asked about a case like this, then say which reading should control, which of the two complaints (if either) succeeds, and what the teacher would need to do to comply. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on education law and history curriculum, and do not present one side of the underlying historical debate as the consensus.
