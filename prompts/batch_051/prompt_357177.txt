I'm on the collections committee of a city museum and we're now looking at a ten-year strategic plan for the so-called difficult parts of the collection. The central issue is this. The founding trust deed (1921) contains the following clause, which I'm quoting exactly:

"The Trustees shall hold the several objects enumerated in the Second Schedule upon trust for the benefit of the inhabitants of the City in perpetuity and shall not sell, exchange, make away with, or suffer the permanent removal of any object in the Permanent Collection, save only that a Trustee may, by a two-thirds majority, authorise the sale or exchange of any object deemed a duplicate, the proceeds to be applied solely to the care and enlargement of the Permanent Collection."

We hold in the Permanent Collection a group of the Benin Bronzes — bronze plaques and carved ivories that documentation now shows were taken in 1897 during the British punitive expedition against Benin City. The Royal Court of Benin has requested their unconditional return. The city's legal counsel has advised that the trust deed is the only real obstacle; there is no other law barring return. The escape hatch (duplicates) plainly doesn't apply, since none of the pieces are duplicates. So everything turns on what the clause requires or permits in a case its drafter never contemplated. The drafter was a shipping magnate who bought the pieces at auction in 1902, endowed the museum, and died in 1934; he was plainly worried the trustees would quietly sell off the bequest to patch a budget hole. The clause was not aimed at restitution to a foreign state, and the word restitution appears nowhere in the deed.

I need a paper for the board that works through the options without trying to be a law-review article, so no citations needed, just reasoning. Please structure it in exactly this order:

1. "The words" — 180–220 words on what the clause, read in its ordinary meaning, gives.
2. "The purpose" — 180–220 words on what reading it in light of its evident purpose gives.
3. "The drafter's probable view" — 120–160 words on what, if the drafter could be asked, he would most likely say, and how much that should matter.
4. "The reasonable reader" — 150–180 words on what a reasonable trustee, or a reasonable member of the public, would understand the deed to mean today.
5. "My recommendation" — 120–160 words on what the board should actually do, and wether reframing the return as a long-term co-stewardship agreement, a loan in reverse, or a digital return (keeping the objects but transferring the scans and reproduction rights) changes the analysis.
