So I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether a language could in principle do without pronouns and indexicals — no I, no you, no here, no now, no this, none of it. His line is that you could swap in names, dates, places, and definite descriptions and lose nothing but convenience. I dont buy it, and the reason is something like this: to make the swap you already have to know who and where and when you are, and the whole point of words like I and now is that they let you act from inside a situation without that detour. You can know every description of the person leaving a sugar trail in a supermarket and still never catch on that the mess-maker is you; all those descriptions never add up to the belief you actually needed. (That's the idea sometimes called the essential indexical, though I dont want to fight about the label.)

Anyway, I want to test the intuition with a poem, because I think the form can do the arguing. Write a 14-line sonnet — doesnt have to rhyme, but I do want a real turn, an octave building something up and a sestet or closing couplet breaking it — from the point of view of someone stranded at a particular place and time, needing to act, and trying to state their situation without using any pronouns or indexicals. Ban these in the poem: I, me, my, mine, we, us, our, ours, you, your, yours, he, him, his, she, her, hers, it, its, they, them, their, theirs, this, that, these, those, here, there, now, then, today, tomorrow, yesterday. The octave should be the paraphrase strategy: the speaker describes the place, the time, and the agent from the outside, the way my friend says can be done. The turn should be the moment that strategy visibly strains or fails, where the reader senses exactly what the banned words were for. Someone who knows the indexical debate should see the point, and someone who doesn't should still get a poem that earns its constraint. American English please.
