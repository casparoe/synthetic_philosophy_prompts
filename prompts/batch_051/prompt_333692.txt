After thirty years in behavioral ecology, most of it with insects, I've only recently started reading philosophy of mind, and the thing I keep tripping over is the scale argument for insect sentience. A philosopher friend puts it like this: you don't need to be sure that a bee feels pain; if the chance is even one percent, the number of insects affected by a pesticide program or a farm is so huge that the expected amount of suffering is enormous, so we should treat insects as if they were sentient. I'm used to uncertainty that an experiment can shrink, and I dont see what experiment shrinks this one.

So here's the question. Take the claim that honeybees are sentient -- that there is something it is like to be a bee, with valenced states such as pain and pleasure. Design the best test you can of that claim, and be concrete about what you would measure, what a positive result would look like, what a negative result would look like, and what a positive result would actually entitle you to say. I can imagine, for instance, a bee trading off noxious heat against higher sugar concentration, or changing its behavior after an analgesic in a way that suggests the noxiousness is doing real representational work. But I can also imagine a nociceptive reflex, or a robot, doing all of that with nobody home. So say plainly what even a battery of perfect tests could never settle. Then tell me what the scale argument should do with the outcome: if the tests come back as positive as one could hope, does the low-probability-plus-huge-numbers move go through, and does it go through anyway if the tests stay ambiguous?
