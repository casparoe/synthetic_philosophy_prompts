For a class on the philosophy of information law I have to write a short analytical memo about a made-up statute, and I keep going in circles. The rule in question is this (again, it's made up for the course, so don't try to check it anywhere):

"Section 9(1). A natural person owns all personal data concerning the person. Section 9(2). No person may process personal data concerning another person without that person's consent. Section 9(3). 'Personal data' means any recorded information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person."

The case our professor gave us: a company, call it Veritas, has no dealings with me at all. It buys grocery loyalty-card records and public property-tax files — supposedly de-identified but re-identifiable — and uses them to train a model that assigns to every address a set of inferred traits: probable political leanings, a stress score, estimated chronic-illness risk, maybe predicted credit strain. Veritas sells these inferred profiles to insurers and political campaigns, and none of the people profiled ever consent or even learn there profile exists. To make it harder: the model is frequently wrong, its outputs are probabilities rather than facts anyone observed, and a good chunk of each prediction comes from other people's behavior in the same zip code rather than from anything the person did.

My question is whether these profile outputs count as personal data that I own under Section 9, and whether Veritas processing them without consent violates Section 9(2). I've been told to argue it from the plain text, from the purpose of the statute (written well before anyone imagined machine inference from aggregate data), and from what the drafters would have said if asked, and then to say which approach should win and why. I want you to act as an expert on the philosophy of property and information; think carefully, and maybe start by brainstorming the strongest version of each reading before committing. The professor cares less about the practical legal outcome than about what the statute has to assume about property in information for Section 9 to reach or not reach these inferences, so lean into that.
