I'm a CS minor taking a moral and political philosophy course, and we just did Rawls's method of reflective equilibrium. I can't shake the feeling that it's exactly fixed-point iteration from numerical methods. You start with some pair (the particular cases you're sure about, the candidate principles), apply a revision operator — tweak the principles until they fit the cases you won't give up, then re-judge some cases in light of the new principles — and repeat until the pair stops moving. That stopping point is literally a fixed point of the operator. In numerics we know this can converge, oscillate, or diverge; convergence can depend on the starting point, and you mostly get guarantees only when the operator has something like a contraction property. Rawls seems to assume that for a given person the process settles into a stable equilibrium, and he's relaxed about the fact that different people land at different equilibria.

The obvious snag is that Rawls never actually writes down the revision operator; he just says you go back and forth, adjusting things until it feels coherent. In numerics, without a specified operator the whole convergence question doesn't even get started. So I cant tell whether this is a real structural analogy or just me pattern-matching from two departments at once. If the mapping is real, what on the moral-reasoning side could do the work of the contraction condition? Should we inherit worries about non-convergence, cycles, and path-dependence as genuinely live possibilities for someone doing the method? And what's one thing the analogy seems to predict that probably comes out false for actual moral reasoning, cause I suspect that's where the interesting difference is.
