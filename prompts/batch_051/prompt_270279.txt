I'm putting together a handout for an upper-level seminar on infinity and the metaphysics of supertasks, and I've noticed the same handful of moves keep turning up whenever people argue about Thomson's lamp. Rather than teach the obvious stuff yet again, I'd like you to act as a specialist on supertasks—someone comfortable with both the metaphysical side and the relevant mathematics of limits and divergent series—and to think carefully before answering. Here are the five replies to the usual question "at t = 2 minutes, after all the toggles, is the lamp on or off?" that I've collected so far. (1) The Benacerraf-style reply: the story fixes the lamp's state at every moment strictly before t = 2, but says nothing at all about t = 2 itself, so the "final" state is simply unspecified—an incomplete description is not a contradiction. To make matters worse, the natural way to fill that gap, extending the state by taking a limit, fails because the sequence on, off, on, off, … doesn't converge; it's Grandi's series in disguise, and the fact that run-of-the-mill summation methods assign that series a value of 1/2 doesn't tell us what it would even mean for a lamp to be half-on. (2) Thomson's own reply: he thought the case exposed a real contradiction—the lamp can't be on, because every time it was switched on it was immediately switched off, and it can't be off, because every time it was switched off it was immediately switched on—and he took that to show that the very idea of completing infinitely many discrete operations in a finite time is impossible. (3) The physical-realizability reply: whatever the concept allows, no actual lamp could toggle infinitely many times in a finite span; the switching rate would have to increase without bound, and any physical switch has a maximum rate. (4) The bad-idealization reply: the paradox is an artifact of forcing a two-valued on/off description. Replace the lamp with a continuously variable state, like a dimmer whose brightness changes continuously, and you can have a perfectly consistent supertask that ends in a determinate limiting state—half-bright, say. So the real problem is the crude idealization, not infinity. (5) The fictionalist reply: the story just doesn't determine the final state, and either completion of the story is consistent with everything before t = 2; a "paradox" that can be dissolved by stipulating an ending was never really a paradox in the first place.

Now the actual question. Is there a sixth reply that is genuinely different in kind from these five—not a restatement, not a special case, and not some combination of them? I'd especially like one that does'nt dissolve the puzzle by declaring it underdetermined or physically unrealizable, but instead grants the setup is intelligible as stated and argues that there is a determinate, non-arbitrary answer at t = 2 (on, off, or something else entirely). If you think no such sixth reply exists, say so directly, and then tell me which of the five you would defend and why. Either way, also say how the new reply—or the strongest old one—bears on the broader question of whether supertasks are possible at all. Before you settle on an answer, brainstorm a few candidate replies and test each one against the five above to make sure it is'nt secretly one of them wearing a different hat.
