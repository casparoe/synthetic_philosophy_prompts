I want you to treat the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster as a case study in causation by omission. Here's the factual skeleton I'm working from: the night before the launch, Morton Thiokol engineers recommended delaying because they feared the O-rings would fail in unusually cold weather; that recommendation was overruled; Challenger broke apart after launch in 1986; the Rogers Commission later blamed a flawed decision-making process. The philosophical detective work I want is a mapping of the omissions, not a retelling of the event. Whose failure to act was a genuine cause of the disaster, and whose was just background? Run two tests on each candidate: a counterfactual test (if this person had refused to sign off, or had actually halted the process, would the launch have been stopped?) and a norm-based test (an omission counts as a cause only where the person had a duty or expectation to act). Then push the theory. Can a normal-vs-abnormal conditions distinction handle the way the shuttle program had normalized O-ring erosion as an accepted risk, or does that distinction collapse? Does the omission framework strain once you realize nearly every manager in the chain could have spoken up — do they all become causes? End by saying what the case shows about whether causation by omission ultimately needs a norm, a counterfactual, or something else.
