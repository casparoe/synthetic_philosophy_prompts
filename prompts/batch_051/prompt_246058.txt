My mate Dave won't stop going on about privacy being basically just property. If your location history or your private messages are yours, then when a company collects the lot and sells it on, that isn't some fuzzy "privacy violation" — it's theft. His line is: treat data like a car. You wouldn't let a stranger borrow your car just because the paperwork had some small print, and you'd call the police if someone nicked it. So stop agonising about the philosophy; just give people proper property rights over their data, and then they can decide whether to keep it or flog it.

I can't shake the feeling that the analogy is doing a lot of hidden work, and that Dave's conclusion feels obvious mainly because the word "property" is already doing the arguing for him. The bit I keep tripping over is this: if someone nicks my car, I've got less car afterwards; if someone peeks through my window and memorises what I look like undressed, I've still got everything I started with, but it feels like a violation all the same.

I want to know how far the car/property analogy actually holds. Please audit it. In particular: what maps onto what in Dave's analogy; where the mapping genuinely holds; where it breaks; which features of property the analogy quietly imports into privacy that maybe aren't there; and whether the conclusions Dave draws — that collecting your data without asking is just theft, and that the fix is proper property rights plus a market in personal data — survive once the disanalogies are on the table. If there's a better analogy that does the useful work without the dodgy bits, I'd definately want that too.

Please organise the answer under these headings, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. The mapping (roughly 150–250 words). Lay out what corresponds to what as explicitly as you can; a table is fine.

2. Where it holds (roughly 150–250 words). The genuine insights the analogy captures.

3. Where it breaks (roughly 250–350 words). The important disanalogies, especially ones that bear on Dave's argument.

4. What the analogy smuggles in (roughly 150–250 words). Features of property that get assumed for personal data without argument.

5. Does the conclusion survive? (roughly 200–300 words). Whether the theft framing and the property-rights-and-a-market fix stand after all that, and what better analogy, if any, could replace Dave's.
