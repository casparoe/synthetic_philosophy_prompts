In my epistemology seminar we've been going over the problem of induction, and whenever somebody tries to defend it they fall back on the line that "the future will resemble the past." I get the intuitive appeal, but I genuinely don't think anyone has said what that means. So I want to know what precise claims that slogan could be making. Is it a claim about the laws of nature staying the same, about observed regularities continuing, about our methods staying reliable, or something else? And do all of those actually get you induction, or do some of them just restate the problem in a fancier way?

What's making me suspicious is a thing our instructor mentioned in passing: Goodman's "grue" trick. Define grue this way: an object is grue if it is first observed before next year and is green, or if it is first observed next year or later and is blue. Every emerald we've seen so far is green, so by this definition every emerald we've seen so far is also grue. But "all emeralds are grue" predicts that emeralds first seen next year will be blue, while "all emeralds are green" predicts they'll be green, and both fit our evidence equally. So if "the future resembles the past" is supposed to rule out grue, it must be doing more than saying the pattern continues — because the pattern can be described in green terms or grue terms.

Could you lay out the different precise principles "the future will resemble the past" might stand for — same laws, same regularities, same natural kinds or "projectible" predicates, same statistical frequencies, whatever else you can think of — and say for each one whether it's plausible, whether it actually rules out grue and supports induction, and which version, if any, a defender of induction actually needs? I'd rather have it as a menu of readings than have you pick one.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
