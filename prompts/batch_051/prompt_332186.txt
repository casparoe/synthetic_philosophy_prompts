I spent forty years in a lab where a claim you couldn't conceivably falsify wasn't a claim, it was a mood. So when I retired and started reading philosophy, I expected to be irritated, but an old colleague of mine has a thesis about sadness that I genuinely can't decide is profound or just unfalsifiable.

Her line, as near as I can reconstruct it: grief and sadness aren't simply the price of caring. They do a job. After a loss, the felt badness is what forces a slow re-mapping of your expectations and routines — it makes you stop expecting the person to walk through the door, makes you notice that half your daily scaffolding is gone. Her stronger claim is that if you could take a drug that stripped out the suffering but left the factual memory intact, you'd be worse off in the long run: you'd keep planning around a world that no longer exists, and you'd miss out on learning what you actually value. She even floats the line that a life without sadness would be worse by the person's own later judgment, not just from some outside moralizing standpoint.

I want to know whether any part of that can be brought under an empircal lens and what the test would actually settle. Design the strongest test or small battery you can — don't worry about budget or ethics boards for now, but do worry about confounds. I'm a physicist, not an emotion researcher, so I want it concrete: who are the subjects, what gets manipulated or compared (a drug that blunts the sting of grief while leaving the memory intact, a natural group with blunted affect, experience sampling, the old depressive-realism finding — use whatever helps), what gets measured, over what timescale, and what numbers you'd compare. Then tell me which pattern would count for her view, which would count against, and what an honest ambiguous result would look like.

And the part I care most about: after all the data are in, what's left standing no matter what? Her 'worse off' looks like a value claim, and 'doing a job' smells like the teleology we stopped allowing in biology. If my framing is muddled — if I'm asking for a test of something that can't be tested, or I've loaded the dice by the way I set up the example — say so plainly instead of politely building a test for a bad question.

Format: sections in this order, with rough lengths. (1) The steelmanned claim (~150 words): restate her thesis in its strongest genuinely testable form, and say which bits you're setting aside as untestable. (2) The test (~350 words): subjects, manipulation or comparison, measures, timeline, and the pattern predicted under the 'sadness does a job' hypothesis. (3) Outcomes (~200 words): the result that favors her, the result that counts against, and an ambiguous pattern. (4) The residue (~150 words): what no experiment reaches, and whether her evaluative claim could survive even a clean positive result.
