The exam question: Kripke argues that some truths are necessary yet knowable only a posteriori. Explain how this is supposed to work for (a) true identity statements involving ordinary proper names and (b) theoretical identification statements such as "water is H2O." Your essay should do all of the following: define rigid designation and explain how a rigid designator differs from a typical definite description; explain why the necessity of a true identity statement does not make it knowable a priori; discuss the sense in which "water is H2O" might be said to have turned out false, and say what that sense is and is not; and answer whether recognizing necessary a posteriori truths forces us to distinguish two kinds of necessity. Defend your answer.

One student wrote: Kripke's main insight is that the way a term gets its reference and the way it behaves in modal contexts are two different things. A term is a rigid designator if it picks out the same object in every possible world in which that object exists, and it never picks out something else instead. Proper names are rigid, whereas most definite descriptions are not, because a description such as "the first Postmaster General" picks out whichever person satisfies the description in the world in question, and different people satisfy it in different worlds. Since "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" both rigidly pick out the planet Venus, the sentence "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is true in every possible world in which Venus exists, and so it is necessary. It is nonetheless a posteriori, because it took empirical observation to learn that the two names pick out the same object, and this could not have been known just by understanding the names. So a necessary truth can be a posteriori.

Natural kind terms work the same way on Kripke's view. "Water" and "H2O" are both rigid designators, so if the statement "water is H2O" is true, it is a true identity statement between two rigid designators and is therefore necessarily true. Chemistry discovered empirically that water is H2O, which is what makes the statement a posteriori. The intuition that it could have been otherwise comes from imagining a possible world in which the clear drinkable liquid in the lakes and rivers is XYZ instead. But in that world the term "water" would pick out XYZ, because that is the liquid that satisfies the reference-fixing description there; so the imagined world is not one in which water, the actual substance, is not H2O. It only shows that a word spelled "water" can be used for a different substance. The imagined world therefore is not a case in which water is XYZ.

This diagnosis also answers the final question. Since "water is H2O" could have turned out false, there must be a sense of possibility in which water is not H2O, since otherwise our pre-scientific ignorance could not be made sense of. But that sense of possibility cannot be the metaphysical sense, because the sentence is necessary. Kripke therefore needs two distinct kinds of necessity: metaphysical necessity, which concerns the way things are in all possible worlds, and epistemic necessity, which concerns what can be established a priori. That is why his account is described as distinguishing the semantic notion of rigidity from the epistemic notion of apriority.
