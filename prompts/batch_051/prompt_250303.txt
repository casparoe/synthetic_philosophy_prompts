I was recently elected chair of the general assembly of a housing cooperative with about 120 member-households. Under our bylaws, decisions of the assembly bind every member, and the board is expected to enforce them even against members who voted against a measure or stayed away. A contested case is now before us: a special assessment to replace the roof, which a clear majority supports but which falls disproportionately on a few lower-income households, and some members argue that it is merely an aggregation of private interests rather than a genuine common interest, so enforcing it against them would be illegitimate. I have been reading Rousseau, and I keep returning to the distinction between the general will and the will of all, and to the claim that someone who refuses to obey the general will may be compelled to comply by the whole body, and that this compulsion is not a violation of the person's freedom but what preserves it — the doctrine often paraphrased in English as being forced to be free. I want to turn that distinction and that doctrine into a procedure I can actually run as chair.

Please write a decision procedure I can use before each contested enforcement decision. Ground it in the actual structure of Rousseau's position, but translate it for a lay board: I have no formal philosophical training and perhaps an hour before a meeting. The procedure should consist of questions to ask in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of judgment rather than fact. In particular, it should distinguish: (a) whether a proposal is general in its object and its source rather than directed at a private interest; (b) whether a majority vote is a reliable expression of the common interest or only a sum of private interests, the will of all; and (c) whether coercing a dissenting member into compliance is the kind of compulsion the doctrine can justify, rather than majority tyranny.

The decisions come up monthly and are mostly low-to-moderate stakes, but enforcement against a member can end in a lien or, in the worst case, eviction proceedings, so the stakes for the dissenter are high. Then run the procedure on two cases: (1) the roof assessment, where a clear majority favours it, the burden is uneven, and some members say it is mere aggregation; and (2) a proposal to ban short-term subletting, which a majority of owner-occupiers support but which several members who depend on sublet income say is only the majority's private interest in quiet and property values. Make sure at least one application yields an answer I will not like, and identify the step of the procedure that produces it.

Finally, be explicit about where applying a concept built for a sovereign people to a 120-household cooperative strains the theory, and mark the points at which the procedure is no longer Rousseau's own view but a pragmatic adaptation. Also state how confident you are in the reading of Rousseau on which the procedure rests, and single out the one point in that reading or in the procedure you are least sure of.
