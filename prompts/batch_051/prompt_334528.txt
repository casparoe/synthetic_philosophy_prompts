I sit on the General Education Committee at a university that is redesigning its required first-year seminar in critical thinking. A faction on the committee proposes to organize the course around W. K. Clifford's claim in "The Ethics of Belief" (1877) that how a belief is arrived at is itself morally assessable. They want the course's first stated learning outcome to be that students can distinguish convictions honestly earned through patient inquiry from convictions acquired by silencing doubt, and that students learn to regard the second kind as an ethical failing, not merely an intellectual defect. In their draft language, the course would present critical thinking as a moral value and doubt as a duty owed to other people.

The passage they lean on is from section I, "The Duty of Inquiry":

"Their sincere convictions, instead of being honestly earned by patient inquiring, were stolen by listening to the voice of prejudice and passion."

Opponents on the committee reply that this turns an academic skill into a moral crusade, invites instructors to grade students on how they formed beliefs rather than how they reason, and stacks the deck against students whose deepest commitments—religious, political, or familial—were acquired through upbringing and trust rather than documented investigation. They also argue that a duty to doubt is unworkable: nobody can audit every belief at once, and a course demanding it tends to produce either performative skepticism about trivial matters or a double standard about the beliefs students actually live by.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before next Thursday's meeting. It should give a concrete recommendation—adopt the proposal as written, reject it, or adopt it with specified revisions—and provide reasons both sides can recognize as fair. Engage candidly with the strongest version of the losing position rather than softening it. If you recommend a revision, state what the revised learning outcome should and should not require, and identify the classroom practices that would mark the difference between a defensible version of the course and an indefensible one. In doing so, test whether the contrast Clifford draws in the quoted passage can bear the weight the proposal places on it, and whether it can be applied to first-year students without treating ordinary reliance on other people's word as morally suspect.

Please write in American English. At the end, state how confident you are in your central recommendation, and identify the point you are least sure of.
