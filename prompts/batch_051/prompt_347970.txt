In my philosophy course on how thought experiments persuade and mislead, I keep noticing two patterns, and I do not know whether either is recognized or named. The first: someone offers a vivid counterexample to a claim; an objection is raised that the case is under-described, or that it fails to satisfy the claim's own conditions; everyone, including the person who offered it, accepts the objection; and then, minutes later, the same counterexample is back doing the same work, cited as though the objection had never been made. The second: the central stipulation of a familiar thought experiment is shown to be incoherent or impossible, so the discussion quietly shifts to a 'revised' version of the case whose only apparent function is to preserve the original intuitive verdict, even though no one can say concretely what the revised scenario involves. I am trying to determine whether there is an established term for this persistence of a thought experiment's persuasive force after its argumentative force has been withdrawn — and a separate term, if there is one, for the move where a broken stipulation is replaced by an empty placeholder that inherits the old verdict. If these are two distinct phenomena, I would like to understand the boundary between them. A bare label will not help me: what I need is an account of which nearby concepts, from the study of how thought experiments persuade and mislead, fit each pattern, which fit only in part, and in what respects.
