Ordinary democratic practice treats a binding national referendum as an authoritative expression of the will of the people, even when it passes by a single percentage point. The same practice treats a well-run survey of the entire electorate — asking the same question on the same day and recording the same distribution of answers — as merely informational: no one is bound by it, and no one calls it the people's decision. If the will of the people names a real feature of the public, it is puzzling that the same underlying answers constitute that will under one procedure and not under the other. If it does not name a real feature, then much popular-sovereignty rhetoric is ascribing authority to something that only the procedure itself brings into being.

I want a definite position on whether anything justifies this asymmetry, not a survey of options. Give me the strongest principle that separates a vote from a survey — prior authorization, common knowledge of implementation, the act of casting a ballot, legal consequences, or something else — and test it against the hard case of a survey conducted under a pre-announced, legally effective rule that the majority's answer will be implemented as law. If that survey would be a referendum in all but name, say exactly what the name and the surrounding institution add. If it would not be, say what is still missing. Then deliver your verdict: should the asymmetry be kept, revised, or given up?

Write in American English. On the final line, begin with 'Verdict:' and give your bottom line in one sentence.
