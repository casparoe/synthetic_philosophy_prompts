I help run a small community gallery, and this year the admissions committee has deadlocked over a submission that has made me realize I don’t actually know what principle we should be using to choose works. The submission is a generative piece: the artist wrote a program that produces an effectively endless sequence of images from a seed and displayed it on a monitor. She says she chose the seed, the constraints, and the stopping rule, but that she does not control or feel strongly about any particular frame. One committee member argues that this is plainly art, since it has conceptual content, provokes reflection on authorship and chance, and holds the viewer’s attention. Another insists it fails a more basic test: on his account, art properly so-called is the transmission of the maker’s feeling to an audience through the work, and a work succeeds to the extent that the audience is infected by the maker’s sincere feeling. By that standard, he says, the generative piece is at best a clever artifact, and we should instead admit the technically uneven but plainly heartfelt paintings that narrowly lost out.

I have some sympathy with both positions. I can think of works whose value seems wholly independent of anything the artist felt, but I also distrust treating artistic value as a free-floating property that no one can state or apply. Normally I am the person on a committee who wants to taboo the disputed word and show that everyone is agreeing about the facts while using different definitions, but here the two accounts really do seem to recommend different ballots. Can you help me sort out who is right, or at least state what the actual disagreement should be about, so I can explain it to a committee that votes on Friday?
