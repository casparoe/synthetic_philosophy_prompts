Just got out of a long argument with a friend about a scheme someone floated in the forecasting group we're both in, and I want someone to actually think through the mechanism instead of just taking sides like we did. The scheme: every time you submit a probability on the platform, you also have to submit a second number, which is your current best guess about what your own probability for that same question will be one week later. The platform penalizes the gap between the two numbers, say the squared difference, in addition to its normal truth-based scoring. So if I say 70% today but I admit I expect to be at 40% next week, I get dinged immediately for that 30-point gap, before the week is up and before the question even resolves.

The stated idea is to enforce a principal my friend swears by — I think it's called Reflection — that your current credence shouldn't diverge from what you expect your future credence to be. My friend thinks it's a clean way to make forecasters honest about the updates they know are coming. I think it's a machine for rewarding people who are extremely sure of themselves and plan to stay that way, because a forecaster who's at 97% and expects to stay at 97% pays no gap penalty, while someone who expects to learn from new evidence gets punished for being honest about that. He says you can fix it by also penalizing people later for the gap between the future number they predicted and the future number they actually submit, but I think that just punishes people for being surprised.

Treat it as a mechanism, not as a rule of good reasoning. What does it do to the forecasts people submit and to who does well on the platform? How would people game it? What second-order effects does it have — for example, does it push people toward extreme credences, toward submitting fake expected-future numbers, toward not gathering evidence they expect would move them? If the platform lets people choose the evaluation interval, what intervals does everyone pick and why? What happens right before an election or any time everybody expects a big update? And can any of the failure modes actually be fixed without turning the whole thing into either a ban on updating or a box-ticking exercise nobody takes seriously?

Oh, and when you sort out the philosophy from the mechanism, please seperate what's actually settled among people who work on this stuff from what's still genuinely contested — because my friend talks as if every serious person accepts the principle, and I'm not convinced that's true. If the platform is implementing some bureaucratic, watered-down version of the actual principle and not the real thing, say so.
