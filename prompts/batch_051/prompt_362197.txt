I'm compiling a list of reasons for thinking that, even if nature has a final theory—a finite, self-consistent scheme from which all physical behavior in principle follows—we could never know we had found it. So far I have these five:

1. Underdetermination by finite evidence: any finite body of observations is compatible with indefinitely many rival laws, so no quantity of data can rule out a competitor that diverges only in regimes we have not yet managed to measure.
2. The pessimistic induction: every framework that once looked close to complete was later demoted to a special case or approximation of something deeper, and by induction any present candidate should be expected to meet the same fate, so a feeling of finality is never evidence of actual finality.
3. The effective-field-theory regress: our best physics treats theories as scale-relative effective descriptions, and there is no in-principle guarantee that the tower of effective descriptions bottoms out; if it does not, the phrase "final theory" may not even pick out a level that exists.
4. Computational irreducibility: if the true theory has computationally irreducible consequences, a finite reasoner cannot derive enough of them to check the theory against its own full set of predictions, so the claim that "this theory covers everything" is one we could never fully verify.
5. The framework-relativity of completion: any criterion of completeness must be stated from within a current theoretical framework and its standards of explanation, and there is no external standpoint from which to certify those standards as the ones a genuinely final theory ought to meet.

I believe these are the standard moves. I want a sixth that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or combination of any of them—in particular, not another skeptical induction from past theory change and not another underdetermination-by-data argument. Tell me what the new reason is, what it adds that these five do not, and whether you think it is any good. Then say what would change your mind about that verdict: which objection, if developed well, would show that the proposed reason either collapses into one of the five or fails on its own terms.
