An asymmetry in my readers' judgments has been bothering me. Two of my characters act on the same backward-looking reason—an unwillingness to waste what they have already invested. The first keeps a failing restaurant open through two further years of loss solely because he cannot bear to write off the money and years already sunk; there is no prospect of recovery, and my readers are sure he is committing a fallacy. The second stays in a marriage that has been empty for years solely because she cannot bear to declare the decades she has already invested worthless; there is no prospect of recovery, and the same readers are sure she is showing loyalty. Is there a principle that genuinely distinguishes the two verdicts—one that does not simply change the subject by supplying obligations or an identity I did not give the second character? If there is, give me its strongest form and test it against a nearby case you devise. If there is not, say so, and tell me which judgment should be corrected. End with a definite verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or abandoned.
