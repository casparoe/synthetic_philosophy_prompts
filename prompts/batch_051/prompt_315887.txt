I've been turning a question over for weeks and I think it'd help me to see it played out as a dialogue rather than get a straight answer. The image stuck in my head is Escher's Drawing Hands: each hand draws the other, so neither is really "below" the other, and the whole thing sustains itself without a fixed ground. In the Hofstadter-ish framing, that's a tangled hierarchy. What I can't settle is whether a tangled hierarchy still has to bottom out somewhere. One side of me thinks there's always an inviolate level — the paper fibers, the neurons, the silicon, the axioms, something that the loop never actually touches. The other side thinks that's exactly the illusion a strange loop produces: any level you nominate as the ground can get pulled up into the loop and reinterpreted from above, so the "bottom" is just a level you've temporarily agreed not to look at too hard.

Write me a dialogue between two people, one taking each side. Set it somewhere mundane — a coffee shop after a conference talk, maybe, or a pub. Make them equally sharp: neither gets to be a strawman, they should respond to thier actual arguments rather than giving position statements. One character should be sort of deflationary, always reaching for a substrate that stays fixed while the loop runs on top of it; the other should keep showing how that substrate, once you inspect it, turns out to be doing interpretive work too. Ground the exchange in concrete examples — a court interpreting the constitution that created it, a program reading its own source code, a person trying to locate the "I" doing the locating — but don't let it become a lecture. End without a clean resolution; it should feel like each character has sharpened the other's problem.
