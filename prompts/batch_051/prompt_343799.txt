I was rereading the opening chapter of Gilbert Ryle's The Concept of Mind, where he introduces the idea of a category mistake. He gives a few examples: a foreigner visiting Oxford who is shown the colleges, libraries, and administrative buildings but then asks where the university is; a child watching a marching division and asking when the division itself will appear after the battalions, batteries, and squadrons have gone by. Then he says something like: the mistake is to represent the facts as if they belonged to one logical type or category, when they actually belong to another. Ryle puts this in quotation marks as a kind of official formulation, but I don't have the book with me, so I won't pretend to quote it exactly.

My question is about how far this idea is actually supposed to go. Ryle's main target is mind-body dualism: someone who treats 'the mind' as an extra thing alongside the body, rather than as a way of talking about what people do and how they are disposed to do it. But I met someone at a discussion group who pushed back, and I couldn't settle the argument. She said Ryle's own examples don't really support the anti-dualist conclusion. A foreigner asking where the university is after seeing the colleges is confused, yes, but that's because 'university' is an institution made up of those buildings and their functions. A person asking where the mind is after examining the brain is not obviously making the same kind of error, because it is genuinely controversial whether the mind just is the organised behaviour and dispositions of a living body, or whether it is a further something that explains them. She said Ryle is basically smuggling in the conclusion by choosing cases where the reductionist account is uncontroversial, and then hoping the label 'category mistake' does the work in the controversial case. Her example was: a builder's foreman asks where the university is, because she has a contract to paper all its walls; a philosopher asks where the mind is, because she wants to know whether it is the brain, the body, or a nonphysical substance. The foreman's question is settled by reminding her of how the word 'university' is used. The philosopher's question is not settled by reminding her of how the word 'mind' is used, because that is precisely what is being argued about. So, she said, calling the dualist's question a category mistake is either question-begging or just a rhetorical way of insisting on one side of the debate.

I'd like a careful adjudication of this. Which parts of her criticism are right, and which parts miss what Ryle is attempting? Could Ryle reply that his task is not to prove the mind is not a nonphysical something, but to diagnose the conceptual picture that makes that hypothesis look compulsory in the first place? And if so, does the foreign visitor example actually show what he needs it to show? I'm interested in the details here, not a summary of the book: where exactly the analogy between the university case and the mind case holds, and where it breaks down.
