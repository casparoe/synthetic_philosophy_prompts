At a game night, two friends of mine, Mara and Dev, got into an argument about a game Dev had written. The game is in the style of Nomic: the entire activity consists of proposing and voting on changes to the rules, and the rules themselves include the procedure for changing them. The amendment rule provided that any rule may be changed by a proposal receiving a simple majority. Dev proposed to amend that very rule so that all future rule changes would require a two-thirds supermajority. His proposal received a simple majority, which would not have been enough under the rule it would create.

Mara insisted the move should not count as valid. The only thing that made a simple majority sufficient, she said, was the old amendment rule, so the proposal used the old rule to abolish the old rule; an act cannot draw its validity from the very rule it destroys. Dev replied that this is no more paradoxical than a program modifying its own source: validity is judged under the rules in force at the time of the change, and the new rule applies only afterward. Mara answered that, by that logic, a simple majority could change the amendment rule to require unanimity, or pass a rule making itself unamendable; then the old rule would authorize either its own destruction or its own immunity, and the act would have no stable source of validity, since the rule that validates the act is the same rule the act replaces. Dev responded that every system must allow its procedures to govern their own modification at some point, since otherwise you need an endless stack of higher-order rules, and that the game's whole point is that no rule stands outside the game.

They asked me to referee. I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so the self-modifying-program analogy feels natural, but I can also see that real self-modifying code usually runs on a fixed runtime underneath, and I cannot tell what would play that role here. Please adjudicate move by move: which of their specific claims are right or wrong, what either of them is missing, and whether applying the rule for changing rules to itself generates a genuine paradox or only an ordinary time-indexed change in which the new rule takes effect after the old one authorized it.
