For the past several months I have been keeping a journal through a bereavement, and I keep oscillating between reading my grief as a form of knowledge and tribute and reading it as an affliction I would be better off without. The question has become practical rather than academic for me. Two friends of mine have argued for years about exactly this. One holds a broadly Stoic view: turbulent emotions are disturbances of judgment, and the good life is one of reasoned equanimity, though certain calm rational affects may remain compatible with it. The other holds a broadly Romantic view: emotional experience is itself a non-instrumental constituent of a good life and a mode of apprehension calm cognition cannot replace. I have persuaded them to attempt an adversarial collaboration, and I want you to draft the document they should produce.

Produce the document itself, with three sections.

Section 1: a numbered list of claims both sides can accept. Each claim should be one a scrupulous member of either camp would sign without wincing, and none should be a tautology. Include at least one claim about how grief or anger interacts with practical reasoning, and at least one about the limits of treating an emotion as good merely because it is natural, spontaneous, or strongly felt.

Section 2: a disagreements table. Each row names one point of disagreement; gives the Stoic wording and the Romantic wording, each phrased so that its own side would sign it; and states whether the disagreement is substantive or mainly verbal (for example, about how broadly the word 'emotion' is being used). The Stoic side may distinguish turbulent passions from calm rational affects; where that distinction does work, the Romantic side should say whether it rejects the distinction itself or only its normative use.

Section 3: the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, state the sub-question; attach a concrete case both sides agree bears on it; and record, in advance, what each side predicts about that case. I suspect three cruxes (do not force exactly three if the table supports others): whether the evaluative information in grief or anger is available through calm cognition alone; whether attenuating a negative emotion also attenuates the attachment or positive emotion entangled with it; and whether the felt badness of a painful emotion is itself a cost, or only a cost when it impairs agency. For each crux, include a short 'conditions for revision' note: the specific first-person report, longitudinal observation, or argument each side would accept as counting against it, and where each side says its own reasoning is most likely to fail.

Write this as a constructive text both friends could put their names to, not as a verdict and not as a dramatized dialogue.
