You are a political philosopher working in the social contract tradition. Work carefully; before settling on an answer, briefly lay out the standard view in your own words and record your initial hunches.

Recall Rawls's original position: parties behind a veil of ignorance choose principles of justice for the basic structure of society. They do not know their class, natural talents, conception of the good, generation, or eventual place in the distribution of life-prospects, but they know general facts about society. Rawls argues that, under this uncertainty, they follow the maximin rule and so choose the difference principle: inequalities are permissible only insofar as they benefit the least advantaged.

Now modify the construction in one respect. The parties are told before choosing that each of them will live not one life under the chosen principles but a sequence of K lives, drawn uniformly without replacement from the N positions in the society's final distribution; each party cares about the average well-being of their K lives. Run the analysis for K=1, K=2, and K=N. Distinguish what happens if the parties maximize expected well-being from what happens if they follow Rawls's maximin rule. Which principles are selected in each case, and as K grows, what family of principles interpolates between the difference principle and average utilitarianism? Finally, assess whether this variant is a fair extension of Rawls's argument or whether it smuggles in a probabilistic interpretation of the veil that Rawls rejected, and say what the exercise reveals about how the original position's conclusions depend on its assumptions about how many positions each party occupies.
