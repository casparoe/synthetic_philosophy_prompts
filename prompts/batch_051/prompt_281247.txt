At a dinner last week, two friends of mine, Elena and Marcus, got into a long argument about a proposed city ordinance that would make it a misdemeanor "to act in a public place in a way that gives a reasonable onlooker reasonable grounds for unease." Elena said the ordinance would be void for vagueness; Marcus said it is imprecise but not so vague as to be invalid. Their exchange went roughly as follows. Elena argued that an ordinary person cannot know in advance which conduct will strike a reasonable onlooker as giving grounds for unease, so the law fails to give fair notice and invites arbitrary enforcement. Marcus replied that all statutes have borderline cases, and that the reasonable onlooker is an objective standard of the sort juries apply every day; requiring total precision would make criminal law impossible. Elena answered that objective standards like reasonable care work only when a shared practice gives them content, whereas there is no shared practice governing "grounds for unease," so an inchoate onlooker reaction is being made into an element of a crime. Marcus said the statute criminalizes conduct, not feeling, and that the reasonable-person filter exists precisely to screen out the edgy and the idiosyncratic; if Elena's objection succeeded, breach-of-the-peace laws would fall too. Elena said breach of the peace has a long interpretive history that this brand-new ordinance lacks, so people cannot know whether, say, standing silently outside a bank in a hooded coat is criminal. Marcus accused her of confusing novelty with indeterminacy: the remedy for a new and uninterpreted statute is a narrowing construction by courts, not invalidation before anyone has interpreted it. Elena said that offer comes too late for the person prosecuted before the construction arrives, since fair notice is supposed to protect people on the day they act. Marcus said that proves too much, because the first person charged under any genuinely new statute always acts without judicial gloss, and no one thinks legislatures must never criminalize new conduct. Elena said the problem is not that the conduct is new but that the standard is structurally open-ended: "reasonable grounds for unease" does not pick out a class of actions so much as a diffuse onlooker reaction, and that invites exactly the after-the-fact judgment the vagueness doctrine polices. Marcus replied that the law still has obvious core cases, such as harassing passersby or following someone while making threats, and a workable vagueness test requires a core of clear applications plus a neutral standard, both of which this law provides. Elena said a few worst-case scenarios cannot anchor a law that leaves everything else to enforcement discretion; the statute must be judged as written, not by its easiest applications. Marcus asked her to state the test for how much precision a criminal law needs, noting that a ban on vehicles in a park has fuzzy edges too yet would not automatically be void. Elena said the difference is that "vehicle" has ordinary, shared criteria of application even if bicycles are contested, whereas "grounds for unease" has no shared criteria at all—her complaint is not about fuzzy edges but about a missing middle. They asked me to referee and I could not. Please go through the exchange move by move and say who is right about which specific points, what distinctions or tests both of them are missing, and who is more right overall.
