I've been going back and forth on the propensity interpretation of fitness, and I'm stuck on something that feels like it should be obvious. The tautology objection says that if fitness just means actual reproductive success, then the claim that the fit survive is circular — the survivors are the fittest by definition. The standard fix is to treat fitness as a propensity, like a coin's bias toward heads: a coin can be biased 0.6 and still land tails four times in a row, so a fitter organism can leave fewer offspring than a less fit one because of drift or plain luck. That all makes sense. But the more I think about it, the less I see what a propensity is, ontologically, other than a long-run frequency. You'd only call a coin biased 0.6 because in a long run of tosses it comes up heads about 60 percent of the time. So isn't fitness-as-propensity still ultimately defined in terms of actual reproductive outcomes, just averaged over hypothetical repetitions? And if so, hasn't the tautology just been pushed back a step — organisms with higher fitness are ones that would, on average, leave more offspring, and leaving more offspring on average is still just a fancier way of saying the same thing? Honestly it feels like the propensity view swaps a circular definition for an unobservable something that does no explanitory work. Can someone walk me through why this isn't just the tautology with extra steps? And could you answer in American English?
