I am preparing a ninety-minute graduate seminar session on the problem of old evidence in Bayesian confirmation theory, and I would like you to plan the pedagogy rather than rehearse the material, which I already know well. The students are master's students in philosophy who have completed one course in formal epistemology; they can apply Bayes' theorem fluently but have never met this problem. What I want from you is a teaching plan: the order in which the components must be introduced and why (what depends on what), where the formal triviality should be stated relative to the motivating scientific example, and whether the example should come before, after, or alongside the formalism. I am inclined to carry the session with the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion as evidence for general relativity. Tell me whether that is the right carrying example: what a good example for this problem must have and must lack, what would go wrong if I instead used a case where the evidence was only vaguely known or where the hypothesis was constructed to fit the evidence after the fact, and whether I need a second, artificial toy case on the board to isolate the probabilistic structure.

On misconceptions, I expect several but want you to correct and extend the list and locate each item in the sequence: students conflate evidence known before the hypothesis was proposed with evidence that is merely old; they think the obvious solution is to conditionalize on a reconstructed historical background from which the evidence has been removed, without noticing what that reconstruction requires or presupposes; they read the claim that the evidence has probability one as a psychological point about certainty or memory rather than an idealization internal to the formalism; and they conclude too quickly that the problem refutes Bayesianism rather than that it exposes a tension in how the formalism represents known facts over time. For each, say at which point in the sequence it is likely to form and exactly how I should head it off without derailing the session. Finally, give me a single question I can ask in the last ten minutes that would distinguish students who have merely reproduced the formal triviality from students who have understood why the problem is genuinely difficult, and tell me what I should deliberately leave out at this level and how to say that I am leaving it out.
