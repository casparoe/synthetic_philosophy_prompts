Consider an asymmetry in the way we judge two kinds of corporate body. When a chartered corporation with perpetual succession, a governing board, and the power to hold property and make binding rules also levies fees, resolves disputes among its members, and keeps an armed guard, we call it a private actor and insist that any coercion it exercises must be delegated and supervised by a state. Yet the state itself is often described, in the legal idiom inherited from early modern Europe, as a body politic and corporate: a chartered entity with perpetual existence, named offices, and the capacity to own property, sue, and issue rules. Its taxes, courts, and police are not called private overreach. The contrast becomes sharper in the case of the old chartered companies that governed territory. The East India Company is the standard example: over a long period it collected taxes, ran courts, and commanded troops across large parts of India, and it is now usually described as a private corporation that had illegitimately acquired sovereign powers. But when a body we call a sovereign state collects taxes, runs courts, and commands troops over the same territory, we call it legitimate government.

Your task is to determine whether anything justifies this asymmetry. Consider at least these candidate criteria and test each against the hard cases that follow: (a) the state acts for a public purpose while a corporation acts for private profit; (b) the state is accountable to the governed through elections and public law while a corporation is accountable only to its shareholders; (c) the state's charter grants a general jurisdiction while a corporation's charter is limited to enumerated purposes; (d) the state is recognized by other states and claims exclusive authority within a territory. Hard cases: a public university with its own sworn police force; a city operating as a municipal corporation; a private military contractor running a detention facility under a state contract; a homeowners association that fines its members and can foreclose on their homes; a nineteenth-century company town; and a chartered company that governs a whole territory with its own courts and taxes. For each candidate, identify where it succeeds and where it breaks down; then state whether the everyday asymmetry between the sovereign state and the private corporation should be kept, reversed, or given up, and why. Write your answer in American English.
