Over the weekend a classmate in my pragmatism seminar tried to condense Rorty's antirepresentationalism for me, because I admitted I had not really followed the primary reading. His reconstruction went roughly as follows. Rorty's central claim is that language does not represent the world: vocabularies are tools for coping with the world rather than media that depict it, so sentences and beliefs are not made true by reality in any substantive sense. All we can ever ask of a claim is whether the people we answer to let us get away with asserting it, and once we grant that, the difference between being right and being accepted by some audience collapses. It follows, he said, that science and poetry are equally non-objective vocabularies — science is no closer to how things really are, it just has more institutional prestige — and that a whole vocabulary cannot be criticized for failing to fit the facts, because there are no facts outside a vocabulary for it to fit. The conclusion he drew is that vocabulary choice is ultimately arbitrary, so one large-scale description of the world (say, physics plus ordinary objects) is no better, except by local and circular standards, than another (say, astrology or mythology).

I have a feeling this is the sort of thing Rorty is accused of and resists, but I cannot tell where the slide happens. Could you audit the reconstruction piece by piece? I'd like to know which parts a careful reader of Rorty would accept as fair, which parts are conflation or overreach, what distinctions the reconstruction quietly erases (for example, truth versus justification, or whole vocabularies versus particular claims made within a vocabulary), and whether the final conclusion — that no vocabulary can be better than another in any non-arbitrary sense — actually follows from the parts Rorty would accept.
