I am a novelist, and I have lately been turning over a disagreement with a critic about my present manuscript. The novel centres on a woman I shall call Margit. The text tells the reader a good deal about her: her age, the town in which she grew up, the timbre of her voice, the fact that she once broke her left wrist in a fall from a bicycle. It is, however, entirely silent about whether she has a small scar on her left wrist. I have always taken this to mean that there is simply no fact of the matter: the manuscript neither says nor implies that she has such a scar, and that is the end of it. A critic with whom I discussed the matter went one step further and declared flatly that the bare sentence "Margit has a small scar on her left wrist" is neither true nor false.

That remark seemed right to me at first, but it has led me into an argument I cannot find my way out of. Here it is, set down as plainly as I can.

(1) Every meaningful declarative sentence is either true or false, and not both.
(2) "Margit has a small scar on her left wrist" is a meaningful declarative sentence; the sentence "Margit does not have a small scar on her left wrist" is its negation and is equally meaningful.
(3) Given (1) and (2), exactly one of those two sentences is true.
(4) If the first sentence is true, then Margit has a small scar on her left wrist. If the second is true, then Margit has no such scar.
(5) Yet neither of those states of affairs is stated, implied, or in any way fixed by the manuscript. I never decided the matter, and no reader could settle it from anything I wrote.
(6) Therefore one of two states of affairs about Margit obtains — she has the scar, or she lacks it — despite its being entirely absent from the text and from my intentions.
(7) The same reasoning runs for every character and every determinate feature: every unmentioned cousin, childhood pet, and freckle. On this picture, my finite manuscript determines a fictional world as complete as the actual world.
(8) That cannot be right. A novel is a selection, not a complete inventory; where it is silent, there is nothing to be right or wrong about.
(9) Hence something earlier must fail, and the conclusion seems unavoidable that at least some meaningful declarative sentences — those concerning fictional characters, if nothing else — are neither true nor false.

I should like to keep both (1) and (8), yet I cannot see where the chain gives way. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would particularly like to know whether the damage can be confined to talk about fiction or whether it spreads to ordinary, straightforward statements about the world. Please answer in British English.
