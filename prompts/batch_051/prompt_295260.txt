I have a policy for any market where I have no special information, and I am no longer sure whether it is rigorous or just a rationalization for inaction. The policy is this. If someone voluntarily offers to trade with me—selling me a car, a stock, a currency, a collectible—and I cannot point to a specific non-informational reason why a sensible person would want the opposite side, I treat the offer itself as evidence that my valuation is wrong, in the direction that favors the counterparty, and I refuse. I only accept when I can supply such a reason: they need liquidity, they are hedging an exposure I don't have, they face a different tax treatment or time horizon, or they simply value the thing differently for non-epistemic reasons. Last month a friend-of-a-friend offered to sell me a used car at just under blue-book value, and another acquaintance offered to buy a collectible from me above the highest price I could find anywhere. I declined both because I could not name the reason they were willing to trade, and I took their offers as a sign that I was the one who was ignorant.

I know this is close to a formal result sometimes called the no-trade theorem: under common priors and common knowledge of rationality, purely informational differences should not produce trade, because each side would read the bad news out of the other side's willingness to deal. But actual markets are full of trade, so the result must rest on assumptions that seldom hold. I want to know whether my policy is defensible anyway, and under exactly which assumptions. When is treating someone's willingness to trade as evidence about my own mispricing a sound epistemic habit, and when does it fail? Is there a restated version that keeps the benefit of treating others' actions as information while still allowing trades that are genuinely good for both of us? And how can I tell, from the inside, whether I am applying this rule because the logic is sound or because I am risk- and regret-averse and have found a flattering name for it?
