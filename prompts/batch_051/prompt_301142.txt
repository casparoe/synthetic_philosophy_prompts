I’m a journalist who covers science and ideas, not a specialist in East Asian philosophy. I’ve been reading about the Korean Four–Seven debate of the Chosŏn dynasty—the exchange among Yi Hwang (Toegye), Ki Taesung (Gobong), and later Yi I (Yulgok) over how the Four Beginnings and the Seven Emotions relate to the Neo-Confucian pair of principle (li) and material force (qi). I also have a working journalist’s familiarity with contemporary affective science and moral psychology: core affect, valence and arousal, psychological construction, appraisal, basic emotion, and moral emotions. I want you to do a translation, not an explanation pitched at a lay reader.

The central question, as I understand it, is whether the Four Beginnings—commiseration, shame and dislike, modesty and yielding, and the sense of right and wrong—are a distinct kind of emotional issuance, anchored in principle, or whether they are better understood as the morally well-ordered instances of the Seven Emotions (joy, anger, sorrow, fear, love, hatred, and desire), which as a class are anchored in material force and can be good or bad depending on circumstances.

Please restate the central disagreement of the debate in the vocabulary of contemporary affective science as faithfully as you can, distinguishing at least the positions of Toegye and Yulgok, with Gobong’s mediating moves included where they matter. Then give me a residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in the target vocabulary at all. Conclude by telling me whether the residue points to a defect in the affective-science vocabulary, a sign that the original Neo-Confucian claim was less clear than it looks, or both.

Use these sections, in this order, with rough lengths:

1. The Terms of the Debate (150–200 words): State the relevant Neo-Confucian terms and the disagreement without evaluative comment.
2. The Translation (450–550 words): Restate Toegye’s and Yulgok’s positions, and Gobong’s intervention where necessary, in the target vocabulary.
3. Friction Points (200–300 words): Identify what had to be stretched, simplified, or subtly altered for the translation to work.
4. Residue Report (250–350 words): Name what no longer appears in the translation, and assess whether the loss reveals a gap in the affective-science vocabulary or an ambiguity in the original positions.
5. Bottom Line for My Article (100–150 words): Say what a responsible science journalist should and should not claim when connecting this debate to modern emotion research.
