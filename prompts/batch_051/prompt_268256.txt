I am caught between a principle I find hard to reject and a set of judgments I find hard to abandon, and I want a negotiated verdict rather than a one-sided resolution. The principle is broadly sentientist: if a creature can suffer, its suffering counts morally, and species membership is no more relevant to how much it counts than race or sex; on this principle, the ordinary use of insecticides — spraying aphids off a rose bush, fumigating a house for bedbugs, protecting a crop by killing thousands of insects that die slowly or by paralysis — is not a minor matter but a serious, recurring harm of a kind we would not tolerate if the victims were mice or sparrows. The principle has real virtues: it declines to draw an arbitrary line at vertebrates, at fur, or at creatures whose faces we can read; it extends to insects a standard most people already apply to the animals they care about; and it explains why the wrongness of causing suffering does not depend on the victim's being able to complain. Yet when I face an actual mosquito in the bedroom or an aphid infestation on a plant, I do not feel the weight the principle predicts: I swat, I spray, and I treat it as about as morally serious as weeding. I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what would have to be true for the intuition to be the thing that gives way, what would have to be true for the principle to give way, and whether there is a reading of 'counts morally' on which both survive — for instance, a reading on which insects have genuine moral status but the garden-spraying remains permissible, because the intuition is tracking something about uncertainty in insect sentience, about the aggregation of very small harms across very short lives, or about the limits of what it is reasonable to demand of an agent. Give the strongest version of each position, and tell me which you would give up and why, or exactly how the two could be reconciled.
