A friend and I spent most of a long evening arguing about Frankfurt-style cases — the kind with a counterfactual intervener who will step in only if the agent shows signs of choosing differently — and whether they actually refute the principle that an agent is morally responsible for an action only if he could have done otherwise. The specific point we kept circling was the flicker-of-freedom reply: even when the intervener would have forced the final action, the agent still could have done something smaller, such as hesitate, show a tell, or form a different decision before being overridden, and perhaps that smaller alternative is enough to keep the principle alive. My friend thinks it is enough; I suspect it either trivializes the principle or quietly relocates responsibility to what actually happened. Re-describing the case to each other did not settle it.

I teach a moral-psychology seminar of about 18 students, and I have one 75-minute session. Design a runnable classroom activity, with rules, materials, and a point-based payoff scheme that does not use real money, that makes the participants run into this problem themselves before any of the philosophical vocabulary is introduced. The activity should involve real small-stakes choices, and it should be arranged so that in one condition an agent is forced into an action by an intervener, while in another condition the agent does the same action on their own even though the intervener would have stepped in had things gone differently. Have the participants make responsibility judgments about the agent in each condition, and structure the rules or roles so that the difference between the two conditions and the pull of the responsibility judgment show up in what they do and say before it is named.

Then provide a debrief that maps the moments of the activity onto the formal structure of a Frankfurt-style case: the agent, the counterfactual intervener, the actual sequence, the alternative sequence, the principle of alternative possibilities, and the flicker-of-freedom reply. The debrief should first let participants articulate their own judgments and then connect them to that structure, so that by the end they have seen from their own responses that responsibility can come apart from the ability to do otherwise, and that the live dispute is whether the smaller alternatives remain and do the necessary work. Be explicit about what the live activity cannot reproduce and what the facilitator must supply — for instance, in the classroom the intervener's reliability is merely stipulated or acted out, while the philosophical case depends on the coherence of infallible or near-infallible intervention, and the stakes cannot match the moral seriousness of the original examples. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, including the possibility that the agent does not choose the target action on their own and the key condition fails to occur.
