In 1758, d'Alembert published an article called "Genève" in the seventh volume of the *Encyclopédie*. Jean-Jacques Rousseau responded with his *Letter to M. d'Alembert on the Theatre*. In the Letter, Rousseau quotes the passage that provoked his reply, in which d'Alembert argues that Geneva should permit a theater and that the theater would improve the citizens. The relevant part reads:

«On ne souffre point de Comédie à Geneve: ce n'est pas qu'on y désapprouve les spectacles en eux-mêmes; mais on craint, dit-on, le goût de parure, de dissipation & de libertinage que les troupes de Comédiens répandent parmi la jeunesse. Cependant ne seroit-il pas possible de remédier à cet inconvénient par des loix séveres & bien exécutées sur la conduite des Comédiens? Par ce moyen Geneve auroit des spectacles & des mœurs, & jouiroit de l’avantage des uns & des autres; les représentations théatrales formeroient le goût des Citoyens, & leur donneroient une finesse de tact, une délicatesse de sentiment qu'il est très-difficile d'acquérir sans ce secours; la littérature en profiteroit sans que le libertinage fit des progrès, & Geneve réuniroit la sagesse de Lacédémone à la politesse d'Athenes.»

The part I want to focus on is the claim that theatrical performances would "formeroient le goût des Citoyens, & leur donneroient une finesse de tact, une délicatesse de sentiment." Rousseau spends much of the Letter denying that this is what theater actually does. He says, in effect, that theater does not improve moral sensibility; it only exercises it harmlessly in a fictional space, so that spectators leave the theater satisfied with having felt pity without having done anything.

I want you to act as an expert on Rousseau and on the philosophy of moral education. Think carefully, brainstorm alternatives, and don't rush to a conclusion. My question is this: suppose someone wanted to test the disagreement between d'Alembert and Rousseau about whether theater actually produces something like real moral sensibility — not just refined taste, not just knowing how to talk about feelings, but a change in how people actually respond to others' suffering. Design the best concrete test or study you can, using a real or realistic setting. Say exactly what observations would count in favor of d'Alembert, what observations would count in favor of Rousseau, and what result would be ambiguous. Then be explicit about what even a positive result for either side would and would not settle. In particular, would a test showing that theatergoers become more generous or more emotionally perceptive answer Rousseau's deeper worry that this feeling is morally inert, or just move the question somewhere else?
