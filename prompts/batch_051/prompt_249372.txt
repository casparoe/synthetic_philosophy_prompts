I sit on the conduct committee of a professional association, and we have a case that has pushed me into reading about the norm of assertion — the debate over whether knowledge, truth, or belief is what licenses a factual assertion. Our code of conduct says: 'Members shall not assert as fact any statement that is false, or that they do not believe to be true.' A member gave a public lecture in which he asserted a statistical claim that is in fact true and that he sincerely believed, but he believed it only because he had misremembered a study that actually supported the opposite conclusion; he had not checked the study before speaking, and he got the right answer by luck. The drafters plainly had in mind false statements and insincere assertion, not a true statement held on bad grounds. Please answer as a philosopher of language and epistemology with a feel for how professional codes are interpreted. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the plausible readings of the clause before settling on one. Then work through what each approach to interpretation — the plain words, the clause's purpose, what the drafters would most likely have said if asked, and what a reasonable reader of the code would take it to mean — would require in this case; say which norm of assertion each reading encodes, and which reading is most defensible. Is he in breach on that reading? Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
