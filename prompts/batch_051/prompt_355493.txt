Two friends of mine were working through Peirce's 1878 paper "Deduction, Induction, and Hypothesis" and got stuck on one sentence. Maya read it out:

"Hypothesis is where we find some very curious circumstance, which would be explained by the supposition that it was a case of a certain general rule, and thereupon adopt that supposition."

She said the upshot of the sentence is adoption: when a surprising fact would be explained by treating it as a case of a general rule, Peirce says you don't just toy with the supposition, you adopt it. So if she's reading him right, inference-to-the-best-explanation-style reasoning is a rule for accepting hypotheses, not merely generating them.

Dario said Maya was overreading a single verb. In the example Peirce gives in the same paper, he says he infers which bag a handful of beans came from only as a probability, or as a fair guess — and a fair guess isn't acceptance; it's a hedged "worth taking seriously." So the passage licenses pursuit, not belief.

Maya shot back that "fair guess" is just Peirce being appropriately modest about that particular inference, not a thesis about the form of hypothesis in general; the sentence names adoption as what follows, and if you strip adoption out you've collapsed hypothesis into a brainstorming device instead of the third kind of inference Peirce says he is classfying.

Dario replied that even if Maya is right about 1878 Peirce, so much the worse for Peirce: lots of false suppositions would explain any curious circumstance, so "would be explained by" can't by itself warrant adopting one of them; later Peirce seems to agree when he treats abduction as something that merely suggests hypotheses for testing.

I honestly couldnt sort out who had the better of it. Can you go through their exchange and adjudicate it — who's right about which specific point, what the sentence actually licenses, and who has the stronger case overall?
