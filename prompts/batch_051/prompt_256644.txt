I am a novelist, and the ending of the novel I am drafting depends on a scene whose intellectual honesty matters to me more than its drama. Two siblings, Mara and Elio, have spent the book reconstructing whether their late father knowingly suppressed documents in a lawsuit that ruined a neighboring family. By the final chapter they have jointly assembled and shared everything: the same bank records, the same letters, the same witness recollections, the same timeline, and each has walked the other through every inference they drew. Mara concludes that their father suppressed the documents; Elio concludes that he did not. Each fully understands the other's assessment; neither withholds anything; neither is stupid, careless, or operating in bad faith. The disagreement is the core of the ending, and I wrote it as a disagreement between two rational people.

A friend who read the chapters took issue with this. He walked me through a line of reasoning inspired, he said, by Robert Aumann's agreement theorem, which he glosses as the result that rational people cannot agree to disagree. His argument, as I recorded it from our conversation:

(1) Both siblings update their beliefs the way any rational inquirer must: by conditioning on their total evidence.

(2) By the final scene, each has shared all the evidence in her or his possession with the other, and each knows that the other has done so. Their evidence is common knowledge between them; neither possesses any private fact, memory, or document relevant to the question.

(3) If Mara and Elio still assign different probabilities to the claim that their father suppressed the documents, that difference cannot be explained by their evidence, since their evidence is the same. Given step (1), the difference must therefore lie entirely in their prior probabilities, the beliefs they each held before any of this evidence arrived.

(4) The agreement theorem says that two ideal reasoners who share a prior and have common knowledge of each other's posterior probabilities for a proposition cannot assign different values to it; even when their private information initially differs, their posterior probabilities must coincide. This is why the result is glossed as showing that rational agents cannot agree to disagree.

(5) The common prior is not an optional extra; it is supplied by rationality itself. Before any evidence has been gathered, every honest inquirer is in the same state of complete ignorance, and complete ignorance is the same state for everyone: with no reason to favor one hypothesis over another, the rational inquirer begins by treating the possibilities symmetrically. Any departure from that shared starting point is, by definition, a bias, an arbitrary leaning not warranted by anything. But rational disagreement cannot be grounded in an arbitrary difference in starting points.

(6) Therefore, if Mara and Elio are rational, fully transparent with each other, and neither withholds evidence, they cannot in the final scene arrive at different conclusions about the same proposition. If my ending is to be intellectually serious, at least one of the siblings must be irrational, biased, or secretly holding something back.

I am not trained in formal epistemology, so I cannot tell whether my friend's argument is a proof or a piece of quietly loaded language. I suspect the trouble is buried somewhere around the phrase common prior, but I could not say whether it is a false premise, an equivocation, or an overstatement, and I do not know whether the theorem, properly stated, leaves room for two rational people with shared evidence and full transparency to disagree. Please go through the argument step by step: on the strongest reading of each premise, which steps are true and which are false; whether the conclusion follows from the true steps; and, once the corrections are made, what the agreement theorem actually licenses me, as a novelist, to write about rational disagreement.
