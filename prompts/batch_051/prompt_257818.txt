An adversarial collaboration is being convened between two parties who have long argued past one another about the moral force of everyday conventions such as queueing, tipping, and respecting the pedestrian signal. The first party — call it the practice-based view — holds that an established convention can itself generate a pro tanto obligation for those within the practice, including short-term visitors, because participants form reasonable expectations and rely on one another's compliance in ways that carry moral weight over and above the convention's content. The second party — call it the content-based view — holds that a convention is never more than a fallible, local guide to independently existing moral considerations such as fairness, harm, reliance, and respect; the fact that a practice is established adds no further reason, and a visitor should follow a local convention only when, and because, the underlying considerations already favour doing so.

Draft the joint statement these two parties could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should include:

(1) a numbered list of claims both parties accept, stated precisely enough to be non-vacuous — for example, about what conventions do, about which cases are clearly not binding (duelling, bribery), and about what a visitor can reasonably be expected to know;

(2) a disagreements table giving the exact residual disagreements, each column worded so that its own party would accept it, and distinguishing wherever possible between a disagreement about the existence of a reason, its weight, its content, and its explanation;

(3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party — including at least these three: the opaque convention (a visitor violates a local queueing or tipping norm they could not reasonably have known); the arbitrary-but-benign convention (which side of the pavement to walk on, or the point at which a crowd becomes a queue); and the objectionable convention (a settled local practice whose content the visitor regards as unjust, such as a queueing priority given to one group over another);

(4) for each crux, a concrete case or argument that both parties accept bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance; and

(5) a closing note identifying any part of the dispute that the collaboration revealed to be merely verbal.

Where the two parties' predictions about a crux would not differ, say so, and explain why that crux fails to discriminate between them.
