You are a professional philosopher whose research focuses on the philosophy of perception and epistemology. Think through the dispute carefully and do not rush to a verdict. Begin by brainstorming the dialectical structure: what each side needs to establish, where the burden of proof lies, and which premises are doing the real work.

I am a philosophy professor, and at a reception after a colloquium I overheard two colleagues of mine arguing about the argument from illusion and disjunctivism. I could not adjudicate the dispute on the spot, and I would like you to referee it. Here is the exchange as I remember it.

Daniel began with the argument from illusion. A straight stick half-submerged in water looks bent. In that situation, he said, you are directly aware of something bent, yet the stick itself is not bent. So what you are directly aware of cannot be the stick; it must be a bent item of another kind—an appearance or sense-datum. He then added the case of hallucination: a vivid hallucination of a dagger can be subjectively indistinguishable from genuinely seeing a dagger. Since the two cases are indistinguishable from the inside, he argued, they must be states of the same fundamental kind, involving the same sort of object. Therefore even in veridical perception you are directly aware not of the mind-independent object but of a common mental item. He said this is the only way to give a unified account of the good and the bad cases.

Marta replied that the argument moves from indistinguishability to sameness of kind, and that is the step she rejects. Indistinguishability, she said, is a limit on the subject's capacity to discriminate, not a common object of awareness. A forged banknote can be indistinguishable from a genuine one under casual inspection, but that does not mean there is a single banknote-like item present in both cases. In the good case the object of awareness is the dagger or the stick; in the bad case there is no such object, or the state is of a fundamentally different kind. The two states are merely subjectively indistinguishable.

Daniel answered that this makes indistinguishability mysterious. If the way things seem to me is the same in the good and the bad cases, then the experience itself is the same; and if the experience is the same, then whatever I am directly aware of must be the same in both. Otherwise, he asked, what could subjective indistinguishability consist in?

Marta answered that it consists only in the fact that the subject cannot tell the two states apart from the inside. The slide from cannot tell apart to same experience is precisely the principle disjunctivism denies.

Daniel then offered a causal version of the argument. A perfect hallucination could in principle be produced by stimulating the same proximal brain processes as a veridical perception. If the proximal neural cause is the same, the mental state it produces is the same. So the common factor is secured causally, whatever the disjunctivist says about objects.

Marta replied that this presupposes that the perceptual state is fixed entirely by the internal neural state. Disjunctivism rejects that. A veridical perceptual state is partly constituted by a relation to a mind-independent object, whereas a hallucination is not. She added that philosophers already accept such externalism elsewhere: two subjects can be internally identical while one knows and the other merely guesses, because knowledge depends on facts outside the head. Perception, she said, could be similarly world-involving.

Daniel said the comparison fails. Knowledge can differ with external facts because knowledge is partly a factive or normative notion, but experience is a felt state, and felt states supervene on the subject's internal condition. Rejecting that supervenience, he said, is too high a price: it would make phenomenology float free of the brain.

Marta replied that the price is only high if one insists that phenomenology must be an internal property. On a relational view, the character of a veridical experience is the character of the world being presented to you, while a hallucination merely mimics that character. The same neural process can underlie both without making them the same kind of mental state, just as the same inscription can express a truth in one context and a falsehood in another.

Daniel then returned to the illusion case. Even if hallucination is special, he said, the stick in water remains: you are aware of something bent, and since the stick is not bent, there must be a bent sense-datum. This point, he said, does not depend on indistinguishability at all; it is about what is present in the experience.

Marta answered that this is the weakest step in the argument. From the fact that the stick looks bent it does not follow that there is something bent that you are aware of. The stick's looking bent can be a way the straight stick appears under certain conditions, not the presentation of a bent object. The word looks does not by itself license the inference to a bent item; the sense-datum theorist reifies a manner of appearing into an object of awareness.

Daniel replied that this merely relocates the problem. If the stick is straight and you are aware only of the stick, why does it look bent? The sense-datum account at least explains the phenomenology, whereas disjunctivism leaves it unexplained.

Marta answered that the sense-datum account explains the phenomenology only by positing entities that must then be reconciled with physics and with our ordinary conviction that we perceive the world. Direct realism is no worse off: appearances are modifications of the perceiver, not objects of awareness, and the task of explaining why the stick looks bent belongs to perceptual psychology, not to ontology.

At that point the conversation broke up. Please adjudicate the disagreement point by point: which specific moves are sound, which are not, and who is right about what. In particular, address whether Daniel's original inference from indistinguishability to a common factor can be defended independently of the causal argument, and whether Marta's reply to the illusion case genuinely avoids positing a bent object of awareness. Then say which of the two is more right overall, and identify the most important consideration that neither of them pressed as far as it should have been pressed.
