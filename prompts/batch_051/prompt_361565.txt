I keep going back and forth on what the word "cause" is actually doing when we say "the heat wave caused the blackout" or "his smoking caused the cancer," and I want a working definition of singular causation that I can test against some deliberately annoying cases. My hunch is that the ordinary word does two seperate jobs—one about what made the difference, one about the mechanism or the story—and I want to see whether a single definition can do both or whether I have to give up on one of them.

The cases:

(1) A rooster crows every morning just before dawn; dawn then comes. (Clearly not a cause, I'd say.)
(2) Suzy holds a lit match to a curtain; the curtain catches fire, and without the match there would have been no fire. (Clearly a cause.)
(3) A firing squad of twelve fires; every bullet hits the heart, and each bullet alone would have killed instantly. Did each soldier's shot cause the death?
(4) Suzy throws a rock and breaks a bottle. Billy was waiting with his own rock and would have thrown and broken the bottle a moment later if Suzy hadn't thrown. Suzy's throw clearly caused the break; but the but-for test says it didn't. What gives?
(5) Two arsonists each start a separate fire; the fires merge and burn down a house; either fire alone would have done it. Are both causes, one, neither?
(6) A patient with a headache takes a sugar pill labelled "medicine" and improves, entirely through expectation. Did the pill cause the improvement?
(7) A lobbyist bribes an official who had already decided to vote the same way. Did the bribe cause the vote?

Give me a definition, run it through all seven cases, and say explicitly where it gives a verdict you don't actually believe and whether you'd revise the definition or bite the bullet. Keep Pearl's ladder in view while you do it: I want to know why a rung-1 definition (regularity/probability-raising) and a rung-2 definition (what happens under an intervention, a do() surgery) each fail on some of these, and what a rung-3/structural-equation definition has to assume—modularity, a correct graph, no unmeasured confounders, mechanisms that stay stable under intervention—before its verdicts on (3), (4), and (5) are trustworthy. If the honest answer is that the word "cause" splits into an actual-difference notion and a mechanism/attribution notion, say that; but I want the definition to do as much work as possible before you give up.
