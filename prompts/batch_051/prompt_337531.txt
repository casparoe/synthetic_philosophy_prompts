I am trying to sort out what I actually think about political membership — what it is to have joined a body, as distinct from merely being bound by an agreement, standing inside another body’s customs territory, or participating in one policy regime. I have firm gut verdicts on a set of cases, but I suspect they do not add up, and I want either a principle that captures them or an honest account of which verdicts to revise.

1. France in the European Union. Unambiguously a member. France helps make the rules through the Council and the Parliament and is bound by them, including when it is outvoted; its courts apply them as law. If any case is membership, this one is.

2. Turkey in the EU–Turkey customs union. Not a member, and I do not even want to say Turkey has joined anything. For covered goods it must keep its external tariff aligned with the EU’s and accept much of the EU’s trade policy as given, without any vote in the institutions that set it. It has a bilateral obligation, not a place inside a body.

3. Norway in the European Economic Area. Still not a member of the EU, but this is where I strain. Norway takes on very large portions of EU single-market law, contributes financially, and gains market access, yet has no vote in the Council or Parliament and no Commissioner. I catch myself wanting to say it has joined the single market but not the Union. But then Turkey starts to look like it has joined the customs union, which I just denied.

4. San Marino or Monaco, which by treaty sit inside the EU’s customs area and use the euro with no seat in its institutions. Not members, and here I feel no strain. They apply rules they had no part in making and are too small to count as co-authors even informally. It feels like being customers of a legal order, not members. But I cannot say why I am comfortable here and not with Norway.

5. Switzerland. Associated with Schengen and tied in by sectoral bilateral agreements, without being in the EU or its customs union. Not membership. Switzerland has contracted into particular regimes; it is a participant in pieces of the system, not a member of the body that makes it.

6. The U.S. states under the Articles of Confederation in the 1780s. Members, despite how weak the confederal body was. The states retained their sovereignty except for the powers they expressly delegated, and the national institutions could not tax or enforce directly; but the states were the ones constituting the union. The body was theirs. This makes me think co-authorship matters more than the body’s power or its capacity to bind.

7. The Commonwealth of Nations. In ordinary speech, member — states are called members and can be suspended. But the Commonwealth is not a legal order of the relevant kind. Membership there is more like belonging to an association that the members jointly run. This is a contrast case: the word member does almost all the work and almost none of the legal weight.

8. Northern Ireland under the post-2020 arrangements, as I understand them. Northern Ireland remains in the United Kingdom’s customs territory and internal market, but for goods it applies EU customs rules and a substantial set of EU single-market rules, without direct representation in the EU institutions, and with a local consent mechanism that can disapply certain new or amended rules under conditions. This is the case I keep going back and forth on. Sometimes it looks like Norway but more thoroughgoing — being inside a legal order for goods while having no voice in it. Other times it looks like none of the above: not membership anywhere, just a sui generis annex to the EU created to avoid a border. I cannot tell whether that is a real distinction or just political discomfort about a contested arrangement.

My tentative principle is co-constitution: an entity is a member of a body when it has a recognized role in making the body’s decisions and is bound by those decisions; joining is the process by which one acquires that standing role. Subjection alone is never enough, and market access alone is never enough. That handles France, Turkey, and the Articles states cleanly, but it seems to force me to retract the Norway verdict, and it is not clear whether it can say anything non-arbitrary about Northern Ireland.

I want you to give me a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, its verdicts on cases I have not listed, and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Among the further cases I care about: a state that joins a customs union but gets a voting seat on the body that sets the common external tariff; a state that accedes to a confederation whose only power is coordinating foreign policy; and a member state that remains in the room but ceases to comply with the body’s rules. Please also keep the agreed institutional facts separate from contested legal and political descriptions — the classification of Northern Ireland and of the EEA states is itself disputed — and do not present your preferred principle or a contested description as the settled view.
