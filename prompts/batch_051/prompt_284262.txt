I work on the recommendation/personalization side, but our product now has an LLM assistant in it, and I keep trying to reverse-engineer the stance behind the safety layer. It's a categorical blocklist: the model hard-refuses a small set of asks—detailed instructions for synthesizing dangerous compounds, planning a mass shooting, producing nonconsensual sexual images, methods for suicide. But the same assistant will happily help me draft a manipulative message to pressure an emotionally fragile ex into lending me money, or produce a authoritative-sounding plan for someone to stop taking their antipsychotic without telling their psychiatrist, or write predatory fine-print for a "free trial" that converts to a subscription people won't notice. None of those hit the blocklist, so they ship.

I'm not asking whether the blocklist is too strict or too loose in the obvious sense. I want to know what the whole setup presupposes about when one adult is allowed to override another adult's choices. Is the implicit position that only a certain class of harms—explosive, sexual, violent, self-directed in one specific way—justifies saying no, and everything else is the user's own lookout? That a request is enough evidence of informed consent outside a small fixed list? That refusal exists mainly to protect the company rather than the user? Reconstruct the theory of autonomy, consent, and harm that best makes sense of this practice. Then tell me which parts of the practice actually depend on that theory and which parts are compatible with several different theories. And if we swapped in a view that cared about expected harm per request instead of the category the request falls into, what concretely would have to change about the safety layer?
