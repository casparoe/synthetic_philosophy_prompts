Write a Petrarchan sonnet in iambic pentameter, spoken by a goddess instructing a mortal listener, in which the volta is the moment a constraint on thought becomes a conclusion about the world. Work through the Eleatic argument that plurality and change are impossible. The octave should establish the constraint without invoking the conclusion too early: what can be said or thought must be, and what is not cannot be said or thought, so any supposed difference between one thing and another, or between a thing and its earlier state, would have to pass through what is not. At the turn, draw the consequence for reality itself: nothing comes into being or perishes, nothing moves or is internally divided, and what-is is ungenerated, imperishable, whole, and at rest. The sestet should then confront the price of the argument rather than simply denying the senses: the many changing things that mortals name and trust are assigned to the way of seeming, and the goddess should leave the tension between that appearing world and the reasoned one standing rather than resolved. Let the form carry part of the argument: keep the octave's rhymes few and insistent so the verse itself enacts the sameness and self-completeness of what-is, and let the sestet open into greater rhyme variety to mark the descent into the plural world of mortal opinion. The poem should be exact enough that a reader who has worked through the Eleatic fragments recognizes the actual line of reasoning, not just inert imagery of stillness and unity, and it should still succeed as a poem for a reader who has not.
