I'm working on a piece about the idea of running a prediction market over mathematics, where the outcomes are already fixed by logic but not yet known. Suppose a foundation builds such a market on a list of open arithmetic sentences, including Goldbach's Conjecture and the Riemann Hypothesis. Traders buy and sell contracts that pay $1 if a given sentence is formally proved within a rolling ten-year window and $0 otherwise. The market's automated maker is built to a logical-induction standard: its prices must eventually move to $1 for sentences that get proved and to $0 for sentences that get refuted, while no trader with a fixed computational budget may exploit the stream of prices for unbounded profit. The foundation then adds an allocation rule: at the end of each year, any sentence whose average price over the year fell between $0.40 and $0.60 is declared a prime open problem, and the researchers who nominated that sentence receive a large unrestricted grant. Analyze how this mechanism would actually behave. What incentives does it create for working mathematicians, as traders, as provers, and as nominators? Where does it break first—for instance, around strategically timing a proof, leaking partial results, nudging prices into the grant band, or the maker's ragged awareness of logical consequences? Which of those failure modes could be fixed, and which follow from trying to put a price on logical uncertainty?
