After years of reading rationalist and transhumanist discussion of wireheading, negative affect, and the end of suffering, I keep noticing a missing piece: everyone argues about whether the Hedonistic Imperative is right, but almost no one states the criteria by which an ethical theory earns the right to have a verdict. I want that missing piece. For present purposes, take the Hedonistic Imperative to be the proposal that the long-run end of biotechnology should be not the treatment of pain but the abolition of the capacity for suffering itself — replacing the present hedonic range with information-bearing gradients of well-being whose floor sits at hedonic neutrality or above.

Write the desiderata that a theory of value and practical reason must meet if it is to adjudicate this proposal competently. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable for any adequate theory or as a preference of a particular camp (for example, negative utilitarians, preference utilitarians, bioconservatives, or autonomy absolutists). At a minimum, work through these pressure points: the distinction between pain as a sensory and functional signal and suffering as aversive felt valence; the asymmetry some draw between eliminating suffering and creating positive welfare; the experience-machine and wireheading objections, which threaten any theory that treats felt valence as all that ultimately matters (roughly: if it is, an electrode or a simulated good life should count as success); what negative affect is causally or informationally good for, if anything, and whether a non-aversive warning signal could do the same work; whether a person can meaningfully consent to editing their own capacity to suffer, and what is owed to future people who will never have known the alternative; and whether valuable achievements, virtues, or forms of understanding require the standing possibility of suffering.

The part I most want is the tension map. If some subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — for example, if a theory cannot both treat suffering as the central evil and refuse to endorse wireheading, or cannot both respect autonomy and license the early-childhood interventions the project seems to require — say so explicitly, and say whether that forces a genuine choice or reveals that some alleged requirement was quietly begging the question. Identify which requirement, if any, does the most work to exclude otherwise attractive theories. Skip the running scorecard of named historical views; the criteria and their tensions are the deliverable.
