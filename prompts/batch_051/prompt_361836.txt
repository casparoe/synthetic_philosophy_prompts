By training I am a research psychiatrist who studies hallucinations and anomalous experiences, and my working vocabulary is signal detection theory and Bayesian diagnostic reasoning. I am fluent in sensitivity and response bias, hit rates and false-alarm rates, base rates and prevalence, likelihood ratios, calibration, and independent versus correlated lines of evidence. I have no formal philosophy background.

I want you to translate the central positions in the philosophical debate over whether religious experience is evidence for God's existence into that vocabulary, as precisely as possible, and then report what does not survive the translation. By a religious experience I mean a vivid, subjectively compelling experience that the person takes to be an awareness of God or of a divine presence — not an inference from design in nature, and not testimony handed down in a text.

The positions I have in mind are roughly these. First, the default-trust position: the view that a person's sincere experience as of some reality is prima facie evidence that the reality exists, and that the burden of proof lies on anyone who wants to dismiss the experience; absent specific defeaters, the experience should be accepted roughly as perceptual experience normally is. Second, the reductive-critique position: the view that such experiences are adequately explained by known psychological, neurological, and cultural causes — expectation, suggestion, social shaping of interpretation, unusual brain states, acute stress — so the experience is evidence about the subject's mind or brain, not about a divine referent. Third, the framework-dependence position: the view that an individual experience cannot be assessed as a bare perceptual event because it is already saturated by the experiencer's concepts and tradition, so one can evaluate only a whole interpretive framework, never an isolated experience; include the associated diversity challenge, which holds that mutually incompatible experiences across traditions cannot all be veridical and that this fact itself acts as a defeater.

Keep two stylized cases in view throughout: a person in a communal worship setting who reports a vivid sense of God's presence during a period of personal crisis, and a long-practicing solitary contemplative who reports an unsought, content-rich experience of divine love. Show where the signal-detection translation treats the two cases differently and why.

For each position, give me three things: the clean translation, where the position maps onto sensitivity, criterion, base rates, likelihoods, correlated error, or calibration without distortion; the forced translation, where it can be made to fit only by stretching or fudging; and the untranslatable, where it has no expression in the framework at all. Be explicit about where the translation has to treat God as a recurring signal source, the experience as a detector channel, and the base rate of veridical religious experience as a meaningful quantity — and whether those moves already presuppose something the philosophers are disputing.

Then give the residue report. Take everything that failed to translate or translated poorly, and say whether it points to something my signal-detection framework is blind to, to something unclear in the original philosophical claims, or to a mixture of both. In particular, tell me whether a framework built for repeatable events with knowable base rates can even state the claim that a one-off, non-repeating divine disclosure could be self-authenticating, and whether the framework's inability to say that is a discovery about religious experience or an artifact of the tool.
