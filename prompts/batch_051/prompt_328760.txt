I am a novelist, currently drafting a book set in a fictional constitutional monarchy. My working analytical vocabulary comes from narrative and theatre rather than from law or political science: I think naturally in terms of front stage and backstage, audience, costume, script, improvisation, suspension of disbelief, and character function. I have been reading Walter Bagehot’s *The English Constitution*, and the following passage seems to be making a claim I ought to be able to translate into my own terms, but I suspect the translation will not be clean:

> That authority could only be exercised by a monarch with a legislative veto. He should be able to reject bills, if not as the House of Commons rejects them, at least as the House of Peers rejects them. But the Queen has no such veto; She must sign her own death-warrant if the two Houses unanimously send it up to her. It is a fiction of the past to ascribe to her legislative power. She has long ceased to have any. Secondly, the ancient theory holds that the Queen is the executive. The American Constitution was made upon a most careful argument, and most of that argument assumes the king to be the administrator of the English Constitution, and an unhereditary substitute for him—viz., a president—to be peremptorily necessary. Living across the Atlantic, and misled by accepted doctrines, the acute framers of the Federal Constitution, even after the keenest attention, did not perceive the Prime Minister to be the principal executive of the British Constitution, and the sovereign a cog in the mechanism.

Please restate the position Bagehot is articulating here using my dramaturgical and narrative vocabulary as precisely as you can, specifying which elements of that vocabulary you are using for which elements of his argument. Then give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with strain or distortion, and which cannot be expressed in the target vocabulary at all. Where something fails to translate, tell me whether the failure exposes a limitation in my framework or a genuine ambiguity in Bagehot’s account of constitutional monarchy. I am especially interested in whether my instinct to treat the Crown as a visible but largely inert performer, while the real plot is driven by the Prime Minister and Cabinet, is faithful to Bagehot or whether that theatrical metaphor collapses once the constitutional details are taken seriously.
