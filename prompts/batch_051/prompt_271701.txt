I teach a philosophy of science course for third-year undergraduates, and next week I have a single 80-minute session with about 20 students to make them feel the force of the underdetermination of theory by evidence. I want the point to emerge from what they do, not from a framing lecture; the term "underdetermination" should not be spoken until the debrief, and the students should not be given any reading in advance.

Design a runnable in-class game built around curve fitting, for students with high-school algebra but no statistics. Small teams act as rival labs given the same modest data set; each team fits a lawlike function, then commits to a prediction with a wager for an unobserved value. Then run several adversarial rounds: one lab proposes a single new measurement that it says will break another lab's theory, while the challenged lab may revise parameters or add an auxiliary assumption to absorb the result. The payoff scheme must be non-monetary and should reward both fitting the data and making accurate, non-vacuous predictions—tuned so that competitive pressure pushes teams to patch and complicate their theories rather than concede, since that pressure is what makes the central failure show up in their own behavior.

Deliver the full package: materials, the actual data set and hidden generator, lab instructions, exact rules for proposing measurements and revising theories, the scoring scheme with worked examples, and a timed agenda for 80 minutes. Write a debrief that maps moments in the game onto two distinct theses—first, that a finite body of evidence available at a time is compatible with mutually incompatible theories; second, that even as evidence accumulates, rival theories can often be adjusted to absorb what looked like refutations—and mark precisely where the activity crosses from the first thesis to the second. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (for example, a team that insists the simplest curve is obviously correct before the game has done its work, a team that fits a perfect interpolating polynomial and destroys the competition, or teams that wager so timidly that no one ever risks a sharp prediction), and what I should do in each case. Finally, be explicit about the one thing the activity cannot reproduce—the fully general claim that any finite body of evidence underdetermines theory—and explain how I should supply that inferential step at the end in two or three sentences without turning the session back into a lecture.
