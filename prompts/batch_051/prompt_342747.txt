I lead a second-year undergraduate seminar in political theory; the students have done a short European sequence (roughly Hobbes through Mill) but have no background in Confucian or comparative political thought. In three weeks I have a single two-hour session on the claim that order or harmony, rather than liberty or equality, can be the first political value, set against the liberal-pluralist view that durable moral disagreement is the starting point for politics. The three positions are classical Confucian harmony (he), a conservative emphasis on inherited order, and liberal pluralism in something like the Berlinian and Rawlsian idioms. I am not asking you to explain these ideas to me; I am asking you to plan how I should teach them. I need from you: the order in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what; the one concrete case or scenario that should carry the contrast and what it must contain and must not; the misconceptions students are likely to form — I would expect "harmony" to be heard as uniformity, "conservative order" as nostalgia for hierarchy, and "pluralism" as relativism or license — with the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; a closing question or short exercise that would show me the distinction has actually landed; and what I should openly refuse to explain at this level, with a reason for each omission. End by distinguishing what a competent scholar in this area would treat as a settled characterization from what remains genuinely contested — for example, the compatibility of Confucian harmony with liberal institutions — and make sure not to present a contested reading as the consensus.
