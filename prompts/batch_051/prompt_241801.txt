I'm on the discipline committee of a small Calvinist-leaning church, and we've hit a case our constitution doesn't seem to have anticipated. Clause 7.3, 'Of God's Decree and Human Responsibility', reads: 'God ordains from eternity all that comes about. Yet no one is compelled to sin; each person acts from his or her own will and remains fully answerable for what he or she does. No member may plead the decree of God as an excuse or a lessening of fault, and no church body may accept such a plea.'

The case is this. Our former treasurer, a woman in her sixties, took just under £4,000 from the building fund over about a year and gave it to her brother, who was about to be evicted from his flat. She admits the facts and says what she did was wrong. But at the hearing she argued that she isn't answerable in the way the clause assumes, because on the clause's own first premise God ordained from eternity that she would take the money, so there is no possible world in which she did otherwise. Her line is that the drafters simply asserted that 'ordained' and 'compelled' are compatible, and that compatibility is the very thing in dispute; so clause 7.3 can't settle a case that presses exactly that seam. We need to decide whether she is 'unrepentant' under our discipline rules (which would mean removal from membership), and whether her argument counts as the kind of plea 7.3 says no church body may accept.

How should we rule? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Work through what the plain words, the drafters likely purpose, what a reasonable member would understand, and what the drafters would have said if asked each give, and say where they diverge. Then give me what I actually need for the hearing: where Augustine, Calvin, and Arminius would each come down on whether her 'no alternatives' argument is coherent ground for refusing to repent, and whether the compelled/ordained distinction in 7.3 actually does the philosophical work the drafters thought. British English, please.
