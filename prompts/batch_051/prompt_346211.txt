I'm in a graduate seminar on philosophical methodology, and over the past few weeks I've worked myself into an argument I find suspicious because it seems to prove too much. I want to know which step to reject, and what rejecting that step costs.

(1) Take a finite sequence of possible lives, L₀ through Lₙ. L₀ is a life of continuous flourishing; each successive life differs from its predecessor only by a tiny loss—say, one minute less of a mild pleasure and one minute more of mild discomfort—so that Lₙ is a life of unremitting pain.
(2) For any adjacent pair, comparing only those two lives, the difference is too slight to make one worse than the other: our intuitions about these particular cases say Lᵢ is at least as good as Lᵢ₊₁.
(3) The relation "is at least as good as" is transitive.
(4) By iterated application, L₀ is at least as good as Lₙ.
(5) But L₀ is obviously better than Lₙ.
(6) The same structure seems to apply to theoretical simplicity. Construct a sequence of theories T₀ through Tₙ, where T₀ is an elegant theory and each Tᵢ₊₁ differs from Tᵢ by one small ad hoc qualification—a single extra parameter, one gerrymandered exception. For each adjacent pair, Tᵢ is at least as simple as Tᵢ₊₁, since the addition is too minor to register. If "at least as simple as" is transitive, then T₀ is at least as simple as Tₙ; but T₀ is plainly simpler than Tₙ.
(7) So if the value argument gives us reason to deny transitivity for "at least as good as," the simplicity argument equally gives us reason to deny transitivity for "at least as simple as."
(8) If "at least as simple as" is intransitive, then simplicity imposes no genuine ordering over theories, and it cannot serve as a measure of comparative evidence in philosophy or science.
(9) But simplicity does serve as a measure of comparative evidence; abandoning that is worse than accepting the sorites about value. So the original argument must have shown too much.

I would most like to keep transitivity, both for "better than" in practical reasoning and for "simpler than" in theory choice, because without an ordering I no longer know what it is for one theory to be better supported than another. That seems to force me to reject the adjacent-case judgments in (2) and (6), but those judgments are exactly the kind of case-driven evidence the seminar has been treating as authoritative, so I don't know what I've actually given up. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which premise would you reject, and what does rejecting it commit you to?
