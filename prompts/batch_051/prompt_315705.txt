Novelist here, not a theorist, so forgive me if I'm using the wrong words for things. I've spent twenty years being asked in interviews what a scene "meant," and I usually know, and I'd like to say that what I meant is what the book means, full stop. But an editor once told me the book is out in the world now and I'm just another reader, and a writer friend is always insisting that if the text supports a reading, it doesn't matter whether I ever thought of it. Then on the other side, I've sat on festival panels where a critic treated a dead novelist's private diaries as the key to the "true" meaning of the novel, as if the text needed a password. These can't all be right.

So here's what I actually want: a working definition of "the meaning of a literary text" — or "what a work means" — that I can actually use, the way I'd use a definition of "consent" or "satire." It should count the moral of a fable when the fable says the moral outright, and it should count the political target of an obvious allegory. It should not count a reader's free associations that have no anchor in the words. Then I need definite verdicts on these borderline cases: (1) my own later statement that a passage I wrote twenty years ago was about my divorce, even though the text is vague; (2) a dead author's diary entry saying the lighthouse is grief; (3) a typo in the first printing that creates an unintended, arguably better line that readers now quote; (4) a novel written by a team in a shared world; (5) a word that has changed meaning since the book came out, so old and new readers literally can't be reading the same sentence; (6) a passage that reads as racist now, though I'm confident the writer didn't intend that; (7) a revised edition where the author changed the ending — is the meaning different in the two editions? Bonus if your definition explains why some of these feel like they come in degrees rather then on off.

Format: five sections, in this order. "Working definition" (one paragraph). "The clear cases" (short, just confirming they come out right). "The hard cases, one by one" (a paragraph per case, with your definition's verdict and whether the verdict bothers you). "Revisions or bullets I'm biting" (one paragraph on where you changed the definition, or decided to defend a surprising verdict). "Bottom line" (one sentence I can steal for the next time an interviewer asks what my book means).
