I've been dealing with a drawn-out medical situation whose outcome is mostly out of my hands, and I keep meeting the same Stoic-adjacent one-liner from a friend, a therapist, and more than one popular book on resilience: "You can't control what happens to you, only how you respond." It is always said as though it settles something, but I cannot tell what it actually commits anyone to. It seems to slide among a descriptive claim about the limits of voluntary control, a practical rule about where to direct effort, a normative claim about which emotions it is reasonable to feel, and a claim about responsibility or blame. I would like you to treat the sentence as a collection of possible theses: lay out the distinct precise claims it could express, say which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false or overstated. In doing so, please separate having no direct control over an outcome from having no influence over it, and an automatic emotional reaction from a considered response, since I suspect the slogan slides over both. Then tell me which reading, if any, a Stoic relying on the dichotomy of control would need the sentence to carry in order for it to do the therapeutic work people are using it for, and which popular readings of the sentence the Stoic view would not in fact license.
