Act as a professional philosopher specializing in risk ethics and cost-benefit policy. Work carefully, and begin by brainstorming candidate principles before settling on an assessment.

I have recently joined my city's public health advisory board, and I keep noticing a pair of attitudes that are difficult to hold together. Nearly everyone I know drives, and no one treats each trip as a wrong against the pedestrians and cyclists who are thereby exposed, without being asked, to a small risk of death. At the same board meeting, however, many of the same people insisted that a proposed industrial process may not impose on nearby residents a lifetime risk of death that is, on any reasonable estimate, no larger per exposed person than the ordinary traffic risk, and quite possibly smaller. The difference, they said, is consent: the facility would impose risk on people who never agreed, whereas drivers and walkers are all participants in a shared system.

I want to know whether that contrast can be justified or whether it is mostly a bias in favor of familiar risks. Specifically: identify the strongest candidate principle for treating the two cases differently, whether it is reciprocity of risk and benefit, diffuse versus concentrated harm, novel versus long-accepted technology, the difference between personal everyday agency and corporate risk production, the availability of compensation or political control, or some other principle. Then test it hard against variants. Does it survive the fact that many pedestrians are children, disabled people, or others who never drive and therefore do not receive the reciprocal benefit of the system? Does it survive the case where the industrial risk is ten times smaller per person but spread over a much larger population? What if the company offers a small annual payment to every exposed household but no meaningful opt-out: does that amount to consent, and if not, why is driving different? Where the candidate principle fails, say so plainly.

End with an honest verdict: keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or abandon it, and state what a consistent policy would look like once the verdict is applied to both the highway and the facility.
