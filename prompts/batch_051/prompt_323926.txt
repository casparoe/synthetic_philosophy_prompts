In Freedman's old NEJM piece Equipoise and the Ethics of Clinical Research, he more or less says that a randomized trial is ethical when the community of expert clinicians is genuinely split on which arm is better, not when any individual doctor is at perfect 50/50 (I'm paraphrasing, but that's the shape of it). That looks like a partly empirical claim about the social structure of medicine: there is a community, it has a state of disagreement, and we might be able to measure or at least detect it. If someone said clinical equipoise can be operationalized and that this operationalized thing is what actually does the ethical work—so that trials lacking it would be unethical even when an individual doctor has her own reasons—what is the best concrete test of that claim? What exactly would you measure: the spread of recommendations in guidelines, actual prescribing or referral patterns, willingness to enroll one's own patients, something else? What result would make you accept that equipoise is doing real ethical work rather than just serving as a label for whatever an ethics committee already approves? And be specific about what even a positive result would leave unsettled—for instance whether the norm should be community disagreement at all, as opposed to something about patient-level uncertainty or the quality of individual consent. I want a definite design and a definite verdict on what it could show, not a menu of possibilities.
