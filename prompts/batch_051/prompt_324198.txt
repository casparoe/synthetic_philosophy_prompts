I have noticed a pair of related phenomena in ordinary moral life, and I want to know whether they have established names, and how precisely to carve the boundaries of whatever they are. I am not interested in a bare label; I want the analysis.

First phenomenon. A friend does something that wounds me, and I later learn that the action was caused by a tumor pressing on regions of the brain involved in impulse control. I accept completely that, given the tumor, anyone with that brain state would have acted the same way. Yet the resentment does not go away; it proceeds as if it did not care about my causal beliefs. The mirror image happens with good things: I can feel gratitude or admiration toward someone for being courageous or loyal even while I believe her character is the product of genetic luck and an upbringing she never chose. These feelings seem to treat the person as a proper target of appraisal even when my explicit beliefs say otherwise.

Second phenomenon. With a person in the middle of a severe psychotic episode, or with a very small child, the first kind of response shuts off. I neither resent them nor thank them in the usual way; I do not expect an apology or an accounting. Instead, I find myself treating them as something to be managed, guarded, helped, or studied — as a problem, not as a partner in a back-and-forth of claim and response. The odd thing is that I can flip between these two registers with respect to one and the same person over time, and the shift often feels like something that happens to me rather than something I decide.

Is there an established name for the first phenomenon (the feelings and the implicit demands they carry) and for the second (the switch to managing rather than engaging)? More importantly: (a) are the two registers exhaustive, or is there a third way of relating that is neither? (b) What is the boundary condition for the switch — is it the actual capacities of the person, my beliefs about those capacities, the availability of treatment or training, or something else? (c) How should the account handle marginal cases, such as a person with a mild personality disorder, a teenager, or a non-human agent like a corporation, an automated trading system, or a companion AI? (d) Is the persistence of the first phenomenon under a complete causal explanation a reason to think that moral responsibility is compatible with determinism, or is it compatible only with our unavoidable ignoring of determinism? Please state whether that persistence should be trusted as a clue to how things are, or treated as a mere psychological fact about us.
