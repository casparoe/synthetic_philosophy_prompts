I’m putting together a reading group on Kant’s *Toward Perpetual Peace*, and we’re less interested in exegesis than in stress-testing Kant’s causal claims. Suppose that, starting on January 1, 2026, every member state of the European Union, plus the United Kingdom and the United States, verifiably abolishes its standing professional military, including reserve forces and conventional overseas expeditionary capability. Each state may retain only a citizen militia with the following constraints: membership is open only to those who have completed some form of civilian national service; training is capped at one month per year; units cannot be deployed outside their own territory; and all militia costs must be covered by a direct, separately itemized tax on voting-age citizens imposed in the same fiscal year, not by borrowing or general revenue. In addition, any international security agreement containing secret protocols is legally void, and every defense pact must be ratified by national legislatures after a public review of its terms. A new inspection agency has credible authority to verify compliance, including financial records, training sites, and the composition of forces. Existing nuclear deterrence forces are exempted from abolition but are placed under civilian-managed command, are barred from any conventional combat role, and their costs are itemized separately. Nothing else is changed: alliance memberships, defense industries, local conflicts, and the underlying distribution of power remain as they are.

Over the next twenty years, what should we expect to happen to the frequency and character of armed conflict involving these states, the behavior of alliances, the structure of defense industries, the relationship between voters and war decisions, and the global arms trade? Please reason in terms of mechanisms, incentives, and constraints rather than offering a narrative. For each major prediction, indicate whether it is robust under a range of plausible assumptions or depends on specific and uncertain conditions, and state what would have to be true for the prediction to fail. Where Kantians and their critics differ—for example, about whether the absence of standing armies removes a cause of war or merely shifts violence into proxy and nonstate forms—please make clear which claims are settled and which are genuinely contested among scholars, and avoid presenting one side as the consensus. Answer in American English.
