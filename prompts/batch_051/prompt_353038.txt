I teach a third-year undergraduate module on contemporary critical theory, and the unit on Jürgen Habermas's discourse ethics is one on which scripts tend to be marked inconsistently, because a confidently written answer can read well while quietly committing itself to a mistaken view. The following question appeared on this year's examination paper, and one candidate's answer is reproduced below in full. I would like you to mark it as an external examiner would, on a scale of 0 to 100, where a first is 70 or above and a 2:1 is 60 to 69. Please give a numerical mark and a point-by-point commentary that justifies it, taking each component of the question in turn and identifying, where appropriate, any conflation, misattribution, or step that does not follow. Be as explicit about what the answer gets right as about what it gets wrong.

Question: Explain Habermas's conception of the ideal speech situation and the part it plays in his account of communicative rationality. Your explanation should distinguish communicative from strategic action, identify the validity claims raised by utterances, and say why Habermas regards unconstrained discussion as subject only to the force of the better argument. Does a consensus reached under conditions approximating the ideal speech situation guarantee that the agreement is correct?

Candidate's answer:

Habermas distinguishes communicative action, in which agents coordinate their plans through mutual understanding, from strategic action, in which agents treat one another as means or obstacles in the pursuit of their own success. Communicative action aims at rationally motivated agreement; strategic action aims at producing a desired outcome, through incentives, threats, or manipulation. The two therefore stand in different relations to reasons: in communicative action validity claims are exposed to criticism and may be redeemed with arguments, whereas in strategic action reasons function as instruments whose effectiveness need not depend on their acceptability.

The ideal speech situation specifies the conditions under which validity claims could be genuinely tested. Every competent speaker enjoys an equal right to participate, to advance assertions, to challenge any claim, and to speak free of coercion from within or outside the discussion; the only pressure permitted is the force of the better argument. Habermas holds that anyone who enters seriously into argumentation counterfactually presupposes something like these conditions, since otherwise one would be engaged in mere persuasion rather than deliberation.

In this respect the ideal speech situation plays the same role as Rawls's original position. Just as Rawls asks what principles representatives would choose behind a veil of ignorance, Habermas asks what participants would converge upon once their particular social positions, interests, and rhetorical advantages have been set aside. Both are devices of representation for modelling impartial judgement; they differ only in where the abstraction is located.

Every utterance raises three validity claims, namely truth for claims about the objective world, rightness for norms, and sincerity for the speaker's self-presentation. Truth and rightness can be redeemed discursively through reasons; sincerity is confirmed not through argument but through coherence between word and deed over time. What distinguishes Habermas is the strength he assigns to the resulting consensus. If the ideal speech situation could be fully realised, the agreement reached within it could not be mistaken, because any further standard of correctness would itself have to be introduced through discourse; consensus under ideal conditions would thus constitute correctness rather than track it. It follows that actual agreements reached under approximately ideal conditions are correct in proportion to the degree of approximation, and that Habermas's fallibilism applies only to our estimate of how close a given discussion came to the ideal, not to the idealised consensus itself. A well-run public inquiry or a genuinely open seminar can for practical purposes be treated as having settled the question.
