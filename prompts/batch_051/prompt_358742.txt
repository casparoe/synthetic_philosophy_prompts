I teach a decision theory module inside an undergrad philosophy of science course — about 16 students, one 80-minute slot. Next week I'm meant to introduce the idea that a rational agent's state of mind might be better represented by a set of probabilities (or an interval) than by a single number, and I keep hitting the same wall: as soon as I lay the view out, someone says "okay, but can't you just average all your hunches into one number and be done?" and the discussion dies right there. So I'd like to run an activity where they hit the pressure before I name the position, ideally without hearing the phrase "imprecise probabilities" until the debrief.

My rough hunch is something with bets on two kinds of jar — one jar with the composition written on it (say 50 red, 50 white) and one jar where their only told it contains red and white beads in unknown proportions — and the catch is they have to give both a price at which they'd buy a bet and a price at which they'd sell the same bet. I want the buy and sell prices to come apart in the second jar in a way that a single precise credence can't capture. But I'm open to better mechanics — what I care about is that the interval between buy and sell does the teaching, not me saying it afterwards. I'd like rules, a payoff scheme that works without real money (points or a knock-out tournament is fine), a timeline for the 80 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the imprecise-probability idea. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the view the activity can't reproduce — I suspect a betting frame already assumes your beliefs should issue in determinate actions, which may be exactly what an imprecise probabilist wants to resist.
