I am a philosophy professor preparing a unit for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar on a question that tends to fall through the cracks: whether a finite mind's possible experiences can, in principle, be exhausted. By a finite mind I mean one whose basic experiential resources are fixed — a bounded sensory apparatus, a finite stock of elementary phenomenal qualities, and finite memory and discriminative capacities. By exhaustibility I mean the claim that there is some surveyable, at least in principle, space of experience-types such that a sufficiently extended and suitably organized finite mind could have one token experience of every type in that space, leaving no type unexperienced.

I would like you to write a decision tree for my students that sorts positions on this question by what they answer about cases, not by the labels they already like. Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of positions: start from the key questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate named or unnamed positions as leaves. I would like four distinctions to do the branching, each phrased as a question a student can answer without knowing where it leads: whether experience-types are discrete atoms or can vary continuously along some dimension such as pitch or color; whether identity of an experience-type is fixed by a descriptive scheme or is scheme-independent; whether a novel recombination of elementary qualities is a new type of experience or merely a new token of an old type; and whether exhausting the space requires that the mind be able to recognize its own completion or merely that it in fact traverse every type. The leaves should include at least a strong exhaustivist who takes the space of types to be finite and in principle completable; a combinatorial finitist who takes the types to be finite while tokens are endlessly recombinable, so that novelty is a matter of arrangement and forgetting rather than of new elementary material; a continuist for whom qualitative continua make the space indefinitely deep and inexhaustible; a compositional infinitist for whom finite atoms plus recursive composition generate infinitely many genuinely distinct types, as with language or music; and a scheme-relativist for whom the question has no answer independently of how one carves experience into types. For any combination of answers that no position occupies, briefly indicate whether the cell is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored; and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot keep separate, or that would need a further diagnostic question.

Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. In particular, the discreteness of phenomenal qualities and the scheme-independence of experiential identity should not be presented as received positions; where specialists disagree, mark the disagreement rather than resolving it silently.
