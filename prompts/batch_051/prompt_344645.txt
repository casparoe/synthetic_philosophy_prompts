I'm on the sexual-misconduct hearing board at my university, and we've got a case that's split the panel exactly down the middle. Lena, a PhD student, had been sleeping casually for a couple of months with Sam, a postdoc in the same building. Lena has a clotting disorder and can't use hormonal birth control, and she told Sam early on that an unplanned pregnancy could be genuinely dangerous for her. Sam assured her again and again that he'd had a vasectomy, so they stopped using condoms. It turnes out he never had one; Lena found out from his ex-girlfriend. Sam now admits he lied, but he says it was a private relationship lie, not a conduct matter: Lena consented to the sex itself, she wasn't coerced, and the lie didn't change what happened between them in the room. Half the panel basically agrees with him; the other half says the consent was obtained by deception about something that was, for Lena, the whole point, so there was no consent at all.

I argued about this with a friend last night, and his whole thing was: if that lie voids consent, then lies about your age, your job, your star sign, or whether you're actually single also void consent, and suddenly everyone who ever told a date a flattering lie has committed sexual misconduct. I'm sure that's a false equivalence, but I need to put the distinction into words. Draft the board's decision memo: a clear bottom line (responsible or not, and what sanction if any), the reasoning behind it, and an honest answer to the strongest argument on the losing side — especially the line-drawing worry. The policy requires consent to be knowing and voluntary, but it doesn't spell out which deceptions matter.
