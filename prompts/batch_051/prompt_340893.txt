An AI lab is weighing whether to replace its pre-deployment safety review with a procedure it calls audited adversarial review. Under the proposal, every proposed high-stakes deployment would begin with a recorded, time-limited hearing between two instances of the system under review. The proponent instance is instructed to build the strongest case that the deployment satisfies the lab's written safety standard; the objector instance is instructed to build the strongest case that it does not. Both instances have identical access to evaluation logs, fine-tuning data, prior incident reports, and tools, and neither may be modified once questioning begins. The decision is made by a panel of five people selected for domain expertise in the deployment setting — a hospital administrator for a clinical scheduling tool, say, rather than a machine-learning specialist. Panelists may question both debaters, but every question is sent to both instances at once, and the two written answers appear side by side under identical length limits. A deployment proceeds only if at least four of the five panelists vote to approve.

Two further rules are meant to keep the hearing honest. First, each panelist must write down, before live questioning and after reading the written submissions, a private probability that they will end up approving; at the close of the hearing they write down a second probability. Twelve months later, auditors compare these numbers with what actually happened and score them with a proper scoring rule, in which a well-calibrated panelist scores higher than an overconfident one. Panelists with a below-median score over the year are removed from the pool. Second, the winning debater's system prompt and generation settings become the starting configuration for the next hearing in that deployment class, while the losing side's configuration is shelved. This inheritance is governed only by the panel's vote; the twelve-month audit does not determine which prompts survive.

Walk through how such a mechanism would actually behave: what it does to the truthfulness and quality of the arguments, where effective decision-making power settles, what the debaters are selected for across repeated hearings, and which kinds of proposal tend to clear the bar. Identify where it gets gamed first — by the debaters, by the teams proposing deployments, and by the panel — and say whether these failure modes can be repaired without abandoning the core idea.
