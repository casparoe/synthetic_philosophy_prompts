I'm a PhD student in decision theory, and before I read any more proposed resolutions of the St. Petersburg paradox, I want the specification that a resolution should be graded against. My purpose is this: a resolution is adequate if it lets a rational agent decide what to pay — or whether to pay anything — to enter the standard idealized game, and if it also says the right things about the usual variants. Do not write the scorecard of which resolution wins; put almost all the weight on the requirements themselves, the case behind each, and the internal tensions in the list.

Here is the phenomenon the requirements have to cover. In the standard game, a fair coin is tossed until heads appears; if heads first appears on toss n, the payoff is 2^n units. The expected monetary value is the sum over n of (1/2^n)(2^n), which diverges, so the game has infinite expected value in money. Yet reasonable people will not pay much to play it, and a theory that said "a rational agent should pay any finite price" would seem to be giving absurd advice. The variants I care about: the truncated game, in which the game stops after at most T tosses and the expected value is exactly T; the utility-denominated version, in which the payoffs are 2^n units of utility rather than money, so that concave utility of money cannot be doing the work; and the repeated-play version, where one pays a fixed price per round for many rounds.

Write me the desiderata list, with the consideration behind each requirement. Mark each as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, and tell me why it lands where it does. Be sure to address at least these:

- The requirement that a resolution get the mathematics right: the game's expectation is genuinely infinite in the idealized version, while every actual play with probability one yields a finite payoff. A resolution must not conflate "infinite expectation" with "an infinite payoff is certain," and must state clearly whether the rational agent is forbidden, permitted, or required to pay any given finite fee — not merely report that actual people turn the game down, unless it is explicit that it is doing descriptive rather than normative work.
- The requirement that it handle the truncation series. As T grows, the money expectation of the truncated game grows without bound (it equals T), while the price a reasonable agent will pay stays small and seems to converge. A resolution must explain why growing expected value and a bounded entry price are not in conflict, without simply reasserting risk aversion.
- The requirement that it survive the utility-denominated restatement. If a resolution's entire move is that money has diminishing marginal utility, the paradox reappears when the payoffs are in utility itself. So any resolution that keeps expected-utility maximization must either bound the utility function or explain how the utility version is illegitimate; diminishing marginal utility of money alone cannot be the full answer.
- The requirement that it not dodge the idealization. Saying the game cannot be offered because no one has infinite wealth fails to dissolve the puzzle: the payoff schedule can be stated relative to any finite fortune, and the same structure reappears one level up. A resolution should say what a fully rational agent should do in the idealized case, or explain why the idealized case is genuinely ill-posed in a way that is not just a finite-world objection.
- The requirement that it take a position on the axioms. A resolution should say whether it keeps expected-utility maximization, and if it does not, which of the standard axioms (ordering, independence, continuity or an Archimedean condition) it gives up or weakens, and what the behavioral cost of that weakening is. If it keeps expected utility but bounds the utility function, it should say why the bound is a feature rather than an ad hoc patch.

Then flag the joint unsatisfiability you see. I suspect, but want it checked, that there is a real tension here: if a theory (a) allows unbounded utility functions, (b) keeps expected-utility maximization with real-valued utilities, and (c) requires every gamble to have a finite price or certainty equivalent, then the utility-denominated Petersburg game has infinite expected utility and no finite price exists. So at least one of the three must go, and a resolution should be explicit about which one it is sacrificing. If you think this tension is only apparent, say so and show me why; if you think there are other jointly unsatisfiable subsets, list them.

Finally, tell me which single requirement you would bet does the most work in excluding the most popular resolutions, and one requirement that you think is a prejudice of a particular camp rather than a genuine demand on a theory.

Length: keep the whole thing under 1,000 words; a table for the requirements, and one for the joint-unsatisfiability claim, are fine. And please mark clearly what is settled among people working on this and what is genuinely contested — do not present a contested view as the consensus.
