I spent three decades working in experimental population biology before retiring, and I have only recently started reading philosophy of language seriously. I am currently working through J. L. Austin's paper "Other Minds." There is a passage in it that I keep returning to, and I want to know whether the idea it contains can be given an empirical test rather than left as a piece of philosophical analysis. As I read Austin, he is making roughly this contrast: when someone says, "I know that p," the utterance is not merely a report about the speaker's mental condition; it gives others the speaker's word, staking something in the way a promise does, whereas saying, "I believe that p," is closer to a description of one's epistemic position. I am paraphrasing from memory, so I may have the emphasis wrong, but I take the central claim to be that first-person knowledge attributions carry a special kind of commitment that first-person belief attributions do not, even when the speaker's confidence and evidence are held fixed.

I would like you to treat that as a claim about ordinary language and social interaction, and tell me how one could test it. Design the strongest empirical test you can: what kind of speech would you sample, conversational or written, and what comparisons or interventions would you set up? What result would count as evidence for the claim, and what would count against it? Be as concrete as possible about the dependent measure—for example, how speakers retract, hedge, repair, or respond when challenged. Then say what a clean result would still not settle. In particular, I want you to separate the claim that "I know" carries more speaker commitment from the deeper normative claim that it gives others one's word in something like the way a promise does. Can any behavioral evidence establish that latter point, or does it remain a philosophical interpretation that no observation can fully confirm?
