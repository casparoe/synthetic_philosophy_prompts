Okay, I keep going back and forth on the end of the British slave trade — from the first big petition campaigns in the late 1780s to the 1807 abolition act — and I want someone to make the three candidate explanations fight it out on the actual episode. The three are roughly: (1) arguments won: the moral case against the trade had been around for ages, but the abolitionist movement finally gave it political force (Wilberforce in Parliament, Clarkson digging up evidence, mass petitions, sugar boycotts), and Parliament eventually just couldn't keep voting the other way. (2) interests won: the trade got abolished when it stopped being so commercially essential — the West Indian sugar lobby lost clout, industrial capital wanted different things, and the moral crusade was useful cover for a shift that was already profitable to someone else. (3) honor won (the Appiah-style story): the arguments and the interest shifts had sat there for decades without ending anything; what changed was that the trade became dishonorable, joining a petition or refusing slave-grown sugar became a cheap way to be seen as the right kind of person, and abolition turned into a status contest. I know I'm flattening all three.

What I want is a careful two-way case analysis. For each story: who are the actors, what exactly has to change for it to explain 1807, which features of the episode it handles cleanly, and which ones it has to swallow awkardly. Then flip it around: what does the episode show about each story — where it's confirmed, where it's strained, and where it just goes silent. Don't let 'honor' become a magic word that retro-explains whatever happened; say what evidence would tell honor-actually-doing-the-work apart from honor-talk-sitting-on-top-of-interest. And don't force a three-way tie if the case favors one — I'd rather see where the other two actually break. No citations or reading lists, just the analysis.
