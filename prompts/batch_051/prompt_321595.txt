I spent much of last night arguing with a friend about when, if ever, people may bring their religious convictions into democratic politics. He defended a strict line: because law coerces everyone, the reasons offered for it must be ones any reasonable citizen could accept, so religious arguments have no place in public life at all. I resisted that, but every time I tried to state a general principle I found myself retreating to my reactions to particular cases, and I am no longer sure those reactions are consistent.

My reactions, case by case. A senator votes against a bill permitting assisted dying and later tells a reporter that her vote was guided by her belief that life is a gift from God: acceptable. The same senator, during the floor debate, tells colleagues that a vote for the bill is a sin and that they will answer to God for it: not acceptable. A senator votes for a ban on commercial surrogacy purely out of religious conviction but, in the floor debate, offers only secular arguments about exploitation: acceptable. A citizen at a zoning hearing argues against a casino next to an elementary school on the ground that it would erode the spiritual character of the neighborhood: acceptable. A school-board member proposes replacing a biology unit with a creationist curriculum, explicitly because the Bible is the revealed word of God: not acceptable. A legislator supports a large increase in child-nutrition spending, telling a committee that his church teaches that how we treat the poorest is how we treat God: acceptable. A judge, sentencing a repeat offender, says she is adding two years because the defendant's behavior shows a hardened, unrepentant soul: not acceptable.

My friend seized on the pair I find hardest myself. He argued that I cannot coherently treat a senator's private religious justification as permissible while treating her public religious appeal as impermissible; if a reason is fit to move a vote, it is fit to be said aloud, and if one is ashamed to say it in the chamber, one should not vote on it. He also pressed the symmetry problem: I accept a theologically grounded argument for feeding children, but I would reject the structurally identical argument that God's design for the family bars unmarried couples from adopting. I want to know whether there is a principle that captures all of these verdicts, or close to it; which of my verdicts it forces me to revise; and whether the line between citizens and officeholders, or between public forums and private justifications, is doing real work or is just a face-saving device.

Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) state the principle you think best captures my verdicts; (2) state the strongest rival principle and the one of my verdicts it handles better than your preferred principle; (3) say which of my verdicts your preferred principle forces me to revise, and exactly how; (4) test your principle on one further case you choose that you think is especially revealing; (5) name the point where your principle remains most strained.
