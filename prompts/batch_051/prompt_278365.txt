I've been puzzling over a decision problem I invented, and I'd like a precise diagnosis of where the reasoning goes wrong, if it does. Here is the case. A driver will pass two exits and must take the second one. If she takes the first exit, her payoff is 0; if she takes the second, her payoff is 4; if she passes the second without taking it, her payoff is 1. The catch is that whenever she is at an exit, she cannot remember whether she has already passed an exit: there is no clock, odometer, or landmark, and the two exits look identical. Before starting, she can choose a rule to follow at every exit: take the exit with probability p, where the same p applies at every exit because she has no way to distinguish the exits or count them. The argument then runs as follows.

(1) Her ex ante expected payoff from rule p is p·0 + (1−p)p·4 + (1−p)²·1, which simplifies to 1 + 2p − 3p².
(2) That expression is maximized at p = 1/3, giving an expected payoff of 4/3. So the best plan is to take each exit with probability one-third.
(3) Now consider the driver at an exit under the plan p = 1/3. The probability that she is at the second exit, conditional on being at some exit, is (2/3)/(1 + 2/3) = 2/5.
(4) If she exits now, her expected payoff is 4·(2/5) = 8/5.
(5) If she continues now, she can instead plan to take the next exit for sure: if she is currently at the first exit, that yields 4, and if she is currently at the second exit, continuing through it yields 1. Her expected payoff is therefore 4·(3/5) + 1·(2/5) = 14/5.
(6) Since 14/5 is greater than 8/5, a driver who has already settled on p = 1/3 would, at the exit, prefer to deviate and continue now.
(7) But the same reasoning repeats at any exit, so the supposedly optimal plan is self-defeating, and the same style of reasoning would undermine any stationary rule that randomizes. Expected-utility reasoning therefore seems to give contradictory advice to an agent with imperfect recall.

I don't want to conclude that rational choice theory collapses for forgetful agents; I'd rather think one of the steps quietly imports an ability the driver was denied. Where does the argument go wrong? If several steps are suspicious, identify the first step that fails, explain the reading under which it looked valid, and then price the alternatives: what would rejecting each of the other live options cost? I'd also like to know whether the driver can coherently commit to a rule that her later self would want to break, and if so, what "rational" should mean for that later self.
