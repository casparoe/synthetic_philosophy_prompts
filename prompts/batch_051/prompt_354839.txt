I'm a PhD student working on early modern moral rationalism, and a chapter I'm reading leans hard on the Leibnizian analogy that moral truths are like truths of arithmetic or geometry: they're necessary, knowable a priori, and not made true by anyone's willing them — God no more makes murder wrong by decree than he makes 2+2=4 by decree. The author uses this to do anti-voluntarist work (against divine-command-ish readings of moral obligation) and to defend the idea that ethics is a demonstrative science. I want an audit of how far the analogy holds. What maps onto what between the mathematical case and the moral case? What features of mathematical truth does the analogy quietly import into ethics — about what the truths are about, whether their objects exist, what "necessary" amounts to — that get shakier in the moral case? Which disanalogies actually threaten the anti-voluntarist use, and which are harmless? And after the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion survive, or does it only go through if you've already accepted a lot of Leibnizian metaphysics? Please also separate what's standard Leibniz interpretation from what's genuinely contested among specialists; i don't want one camp's reading presented as the consensus.
