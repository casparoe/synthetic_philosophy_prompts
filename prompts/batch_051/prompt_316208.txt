As a journalist who covers health and inequality research, I've become reluctantly sympathetic to luck egalitarianism. Its attraction is that it yields one principled answer across wildly different cases: an inequality is unjust when, and only when, it arises through no genuine choice of the person who bears it. But the same rigor repels me in a case I can't stop thinking about. A competent adult fully understands the risk, can afford modest flood insurance, declines it, and loses her home. This is option luck, not brute luck: the loss is the consequence of a gamble she chose. The theory seems forced to say that the resulting inequality is not a demand of justice. My intuition is that a society that lets her go under is failing a basic obligation. Give me the strongest version of the case that this intuition is a sentimental trap that should be debunked, and the strongest version of the case that the theory must be amended or abandoned because of it. Is there a negotiated position that keeps luck egalitarianism's core insight without abandoning the demand that she be helped?
