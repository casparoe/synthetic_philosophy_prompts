I've been going down the problem of other minds rabbit hole and trying to settle what I actually think about when a thing can feel pain, and I keep going back and forth. Here are my gut calls on cases, and I want to know whether there's a principle behind them.

A person stubs their toe and yells: obviously in pain.
A dog gets hit by a car and yelps: pain.
A fish on a hook thrashing: I lean yes, but weakly.
An octopus solving a puzzle and flinching when poked: yes.
A bee flying into a window repeatedly: no.
A plant releasing volatile chemicals when a caterpillar chews it: no.
A robot that says "ow" and recoils when you hit it: no.
An LLM that writes "that hurts" when you insult it: no.
A person under general anesthesia while the surgeon cuts: no.
A person with locked-in syndrome who can't move but is awake: yes.
A person in a dreamless sleep: no.
A patient who is brain-dead but whose leg jerks when tapped: no.
A human infant crying: yes, same as an adult.

The pattern I can almost articulate is: I need the right kind of inner machinery, not just the behavior, and the machinery has to be something like a nervous system of the sort evolution built for felt experience. But then I don't know what to do with the octopus: it's nervous system is about as different from mine as a design could be while still being a nervous system, yet I'd unhesitatingly count it as feeling pain. And I can't say why the octopus gets in but the fish barely gets in and the bee stays out. Is there one principle that delivers all of these, or close? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and why? Also, what does the principle you land on say about a case I keep avoiding: a perfect neuron-by-neuron computer simulation of a human brain, running the same computations but made of silicon?
