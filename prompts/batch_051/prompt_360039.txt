In my reading I keep returning to an epistemological disagreement I'd like to force into numbers. In the Nyāya theory of knowledge the standard list of pramāṇas (valid means of cognition) has four members: pratyakṣa, perception; anumāna, inference; upamāna, analogy or comparison; and śabda, verbal testimony from a reliable speaker. Nyāya holds that śabda is not reducible to the others: hearing the utterance is perception and assessing the speaker's reliability is inference, but on this view the warrant testimony gives you is a distinct fourth channel rather than a byproduct of the other two. I'm at about 0.65 that śabda is irreducible in this sense; a friend who knows this literature better than I do is at about 0.2. Give me your own single committed credence that verbal testimony is an irreducible, basic source of epistemic warrant, decomposed into at least these terms: (1) the probability that testimonial warrant is in general fully reducible to perception, memory, and inference (including inference to the speaker's reliability); (2) the probability that even if ordinary testimony reduces, proper deference to experts and unfamiliar informants requires a non-inferential trust relation that inference alone cannot supply; and (3) the probability that the taxonomy of epistemic sources should track fundamental differences in warrant rather than merely psychological routes into belief. Assign a number and a sentence or two of justification to each term, show how they combine into your overall credence, then say which term you are least confident in, where the 0.65 and 0.2 most likely diverge, and what evidence or argument would move your number most. Write the answer in American English.
