I would like you to answer a standing question of mine in a particular way: with a number and the reasoning that produces it, not with a verdict that it depends. The subject is universal basic income, and the proposition is as follows.

In a high-income country with a conventional conditional safety net, suppose the main means-tested cash and near-cash programs for working-age adults were replaced by a universal, unconditional cash grant, at roughly the same total public cost as the programs it replaces, with the grant set so that the bottom tenth of the income distribution ends up with net monetary resources at least equal to what it would have had under the old system had it claimed every benefit for which it was eligible. The proposition is that, a generation later, the country would be all-things-considered better off than it would have been under the status quo, assessed across the outcomes the UBI debate most consistently invokes: the material security of the poor, subjective well-being, the quality and quantity of work, health, civic trust, and the dignity attached to receiving support. The claim need not be true on every one of those dimensions; it is a claim about the weighted whole.

Give me your credence in that proposition as a single number, then the scaffolding behind it. Decompose the number into the three to five sub-claims that do the real work, assign each a rough probability, and show how they combine into the final figure. I want to see, in particular, how much of your uncertainty sits in each of these places: the incidence of the grant (the degree to which adjustments in wages, prices, and especially rents claw it back from the people it is meant to reach); the labor-supply response, and whether you score any withdrawal from paid work as a cost or as a benefit; the social psychology of removing conditionality, stigma, and administrative monitoring; the political economy of durability — whether a universal program is more resistant to retrenchment because everyone has a stake in it, or more exposed because it transfers to people who plainly do not need it; and finally the weighting among the outcome dimensions themselves, since two people can accept the same facts and still diverge on whether leaving a bad job is liberation or loss. If some term not on this list carries more of your uncertainty than any of these, include it and say why.

Then tell me which term you are least confident in, and name the single piece of evidence — a reform, an experiment, a natural experiment, a historical episode, or a well-designed survey — that would move that term, and therefore your overall number, the most. Finally, take two thoughtful people who hold credences of 0.4 and 0.7 in the same proposition. On the strength of your decomposition, where does a disagreement of that shape most plausibly reside?
