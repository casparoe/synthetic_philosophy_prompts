A friend and I recently spent a long train ride arguing about whether generational categories (Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, Gen Z, and their local analogues) carve society at its joints or are closer to astrology with institutional funding. He maintained that cohorts are genuine historical kinds: shared formative shocks — wars, recessions, the arrival of smartphones — imprint a cohort's members in ways that persist long after the shock, so treating a generation as a unit is licensed. I replied that the boundaries are arbitrary, within-cohort variance dwarfs between-cohort variance, and the labels themselves do much of the causal work by telling people who they are supposed to be. As these arguments tend to, it ended with each of us confident the empirical record would eventually vindicate his own view; we parted having designed, in outline, a natural experiment that cannot be run.

Suppose that on 1 January 2000 a wealthy, demographically unremarkable country of about twenty-five million people imposed a strict, purely terminological rule on its statistical office, all government bodies, and the state broadcaster: generational labels were to be omitted from every official publication and public statement, with age data reported instead in rolling five-year birth cohorts. The underlying data was not hidden, aggregated, or altered in any way. By 2020, most large employers, marketing firms, and major private news organizations in that country had adopted the same convention in their public-facing work, while private conversation remained untouched. A comparable neighboring country made no such change and continued using generational labels as before.

Predict, with reasoning, how the first country is different in 2050. In particular: what social scientists there report about age and attitude differences; which findings travel between the two countries and which do not; how political campaigns and advertisers segment the public; and whether ordinary people come to describe themselves by birth cohort, formative event, or something else.
