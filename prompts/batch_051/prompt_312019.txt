I've been collecting my own verdicts about when something counts as a cause, and I want to know if there's a single principle behind them or whether I'm just applying the word by vibes. The bigger question I'm chasing is whether 'cause' names one real relation in the world, or is just a context-dependent way of organizing explanation and blame — maybe something we'd be better off eliminating.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. Two kids throw bricks at a window at the same instant, and each brick on its own would have shattered it. I say both throws caused the breaking.
2. One kid's brick smashes the window first, while a second brick was already in the air and would have shattered it a fraction of a second later if the first somehow hadn't. I say the first throw caused the breaking and the second didn't.
3. A lifeguard on duty doesn't jump in, and a swimmer drowns. I say the lifeguard's failure to act was a cause of the drowning — at least in the sense I reach for when I explain why he got fired. I'm not sure I'd defend that as physics.
4. In the same drowning, I accept that the water being in the pool was necessary for the drowning, but I won't call 'the water being in the pool' a cause of it. That feels like background.
5. A major and a sergeant simultaneously bark the same order at a soldier, and he advances because he's following the major's, since the major outranks the sergeant. I say the major's order caused the advance and the sergeant's didn't, even though the sergeant's order would have gotten the same result if the major had stayed silent.

Is there one account of actual causation that delivers all five verdicts, and if not, which of my verdicts should I give up? I'd rather have a principle than a list. Use whatever principle you land on to predict a few cases I haven't listed, especially involving omissions and preemption, since that's where my intuitions get shakey. And please flag which parts of your answer are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on causation — don't make a contested view sound like the consensus.
