I have been trying to write a short essay about whether an ant colony could be said to have a mind, and I would like detailed comments on the notes below. I am not an academic, so I have probably used some terms imprecisely; part of what I want to know is whether the argument can be made precise without losing its force.

My working title is 'The Mind of the Hive Is Not in the Hive'. The essay would start from the observation that an ant colony does things that look intelligent: it finds food, defends itself, moves nest sites, adjusts to seasons, and so on, even though no individual ant understands these tasks. This is usually explained by saying the colony is a kind of self-organising system: simple local rules produce complex collective behaviour. I want to push back against the way this point is often extended. People sometimes say that because the colony behaves in clever ways, it has a mind in the same sense that a human being has one. I think that conclusion is too fast.

My central idea is to distinguish two senses of the word 'mind'. In the first sense, a mind is whatever produces flexible, goal-directed behaviour by integrating information from many parts of a system. Call this the functional sense. In the second sense, a mind is the thing it is like to be a particular unity; it is a perspective, a single point of view on the world. Call this the experiential sense. My argument would be that an ant colony may qualify in the functional sense, but we have no reason to think it qualifies in the experiential sense. The colony is not a point of view; it is a pattern. There is no place in the colony where the whole is present to itself.

I then want to make a stronger claim. The reason many people slide from the functional sense to the experiential sense, I suspect, is a hidden picture of control. When we see purposeful behaviour, we imagine a little headquarters somewhere, such as a queen's chamber, a brain, or a central office, and we think that is where the mind sits. In an ant colony the headquarters does not exist, and in a brain, I gather, there is no single headquarters either. But in the human case the parts are so tightly coupled that the system can act as one, whereas in the ant colony the coupling is looser and more local. My question is whether tight coupling is enough to ground an experiential point of view. I think it may be necessary, but I am not convinced it is sufficient.

Finally, I want to suggest that the question 'can an ant colony have a mind?' is not primarily a factual question about ants. It is a question about how far our concept of a mind can stretch before it stops doing useful work. If 'mind' just means 'a system that solves problems', then we should say yes about the colony, but we have not said anything deep; we have just relabelled the problem-solving. If 'mind' means 'a single experiencing subject', then we should probably say no, but we owe an account of why a human brain counts and a colony does not.

I would be grateful for comments on the following, in British English. First, is the distinction between the functional and experiential senses drawn clearly enough, and does it beg any questions? Second, is my tight coupling suggestion worth developing, or does it collapse as soon as it is examined? Third, are there obvious counterexamples, such as other collectives, computer simulations, or split-brain cases, that I should address before the argument can be taken seriously? Fourth, is the final turn, where I make the question partly conceptual, a strong move or a cop-out? I am not asking for references or further reading; I would rather have your own assessment of the reasoning.
