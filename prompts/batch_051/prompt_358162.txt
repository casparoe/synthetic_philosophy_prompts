I'm teaching a unit on fakes, forgeries, and the value of originals, and I want to make Borges's story "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote" the centerpiece. The story's premise is that Pierre Menard, an obscure French symbolist, sets out to arrive independently at several chapters of Don Quixote — not to transcribe or copy Cervantes but to write the same words as his own, out of his own historical situation — and the narrator then argues that Menard's verbally identical fragments are subtler, denser, and different in meaning from Cervantes's, because they were produced in another century by another mind. The narrator makes the point vivid by taking a passage present in both texts and reading it differently in each context. The story works both as a parody of literary criticism and as a thought experiment about how much of a work's identity and value depends on its history of production rather than on anything perceptible in the object itself.

I want to read it against the philosophical debate about perfect forgeries. The standard case: if a fake is indistinguishable from a Vermeer to an informed viewer, is it aesthetically just as good, or does its having been forged leave it missing something the original has? One influential way of drawing the distinction says painting is an autographic art, where the history of production is internal to the identity of the work, while literature and music are allographic, where any correctly produced copy of the text or score is a genuine instance of the work. Menard's Quixote seems to be an allographic perfect-forgery case with the deception removed and the valuation inverted: the later, duplicated text is treated not as a debased copy but as the greater achievement. I'd like you to press on exactly where that analogy holds and where it breaks. Does the story genuinely support the view that semantic and aesthetic properties outrun everything perceptible, or does its satire cut against that view even as the narrator asserts it? What would a defender of the perceptibility-only view have to say about Menard's fragments, and what would a defender of the history-of-production view have to say about the fake Vermeer? I want a reading faithful in both directions — one that does not reduce the story to an illustration of a theory and does not distort the philosophy to fit the story — and I'd like it to end by saying what the story adds to the debate that argument alone does not.

Throughout, please separate what is fairly settled in the aesthetics literature from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus.
