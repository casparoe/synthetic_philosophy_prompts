I've just come from a long argument with a friend about whether unelected judges should be able to strike down legislation, and afterward I wrote down the position I found myself defending. I'd like to know whether it actually hangs together.

1. I take the counter-majoritarian difficulty seriously: when a court nullifies a statute, it overrides the judgment of elected legislators, and the burden of justification should always rest on the court.
2. The only kind of justification I find fully persuasive is representation-reinforcement: courts act legitimately when they repair defects in the democratic process itself — protecting the franchise, preventing politicians from entrenching themselves, and shielding groups that are systematically outnumbered or stigmatized in ways that keep them from fair political bargaining.
3. That is why I think Lochner was wrong, and with it any modern attempt to have courts strike down economic regulation on the basis of a contested theory of economic liberty: deciding that kind of trade-off is the legislature's job.
4. And yet I think Brown, Loving, and Obergefell were rightly decided, and I would defend all three without embarrassment.
5. I am also suspicious of living-constitutionalist talk about evolving standards and moral progress. My instinct is that courts should stay anchored in text, structure, and legal tradition rather than in the judge's own view of what justice now requires.

I came away from the argument genuinely unsure which of these would give way first, so I'd like an audit rather than a lecture. Go through these five and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. For each genuine tension, say how severe it is, what the cheapest repair would be, and what it would cost. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set coherent.

Don't let me hide behind generalities: for each tension, say exactly which commitment someone in my position would have to qualify or abandon, and why saying I am simply balancing the commitments is not enough. Finally, tell me where your audit is most likely to go wrong, and what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would most plausibly lead you to a different verdict about whether the package as a whole hangs together.
