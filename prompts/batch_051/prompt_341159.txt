I spent my working life in the natural sciences, where the logic of a dated prediction was simple: a theory that says the event will occur by time t is at least provisionally disconfirmed when t arrives and the event has not. Since retiring, I have been reading the philosophy of science, and I keep noticing how much more forgivingly long-run predictions are treated in the historical and political debates I now sit in on.

The one-liner I keep hearing is "right but early." It is most often said of Malthus and of the world-modeling studies of the early 1970s that updated him. The usual form runs something like this: Malthus identified the operative mechanisms—population can grow faster than the means of subsistence, so something must eventually give—but both he and his descendants underestimated how much technological and institutional change could postpone the reckoning; they were right about the forces and merely wrong about the schedule. The phrase was used against me recently in a discussion of whether present-day food systems are nearing their limits. When I pointed out that earlier versions of the same warning had repeatedly outlasted their own deadlines, I was told that those predictions had been right but early, and that "early" is not the same as "wrong."

I would like to be fair to this move rather than dismiss it out of hand. Please lay out the distinct precise claims that "right but early" could be making. I can already see several possibilities: a claim about the soundness of an underlying mechanism, a claim that the timing was an auxiliary assumption rather than part of the core theory, a claim that vindication is still expected at some unspecified later date, and a claim that no genuine disconfirmation has occurred at all. For each candidate reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something false or methodologically unsound, and state what evidence would be required to earn it. Then tell me which of these readings would actually do the work my interlocutor needed it to do in setting aside the missed deadlines, and whether a scientist's instinct—that a theory with a date loses when the date passes—can be answered by "right but early" without turning the theory into something that cannot fail.
