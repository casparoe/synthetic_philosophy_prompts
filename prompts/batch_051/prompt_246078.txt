I maintain a mid-sized open source project, and a few years ago we adopted a code of conduct. The clause that's now causing trouble reads: "Contributors come from many moral, religious, and political backgrounds. We ask everyone to respect the sincerely held convictions of others. Comments that mock, belittle, or otherwise disparage another participant's sincerely held convictions are not permitted." At the time it read as standard boilerplate, but now im not sure what it actually means.

The case: one longtime contributor, Dana, is a committed vegan who holds as a deep moral conviction that killing animals for food is a serious wrong. Another contributor, Marcus, posted in our off-topic channel about his family's annual pig roast, which he described as a cherished cultural and religious tradition. Dana replied, more or less, "I have to be honest: raising animals to kill them is wrong, and I can't in good conscience pretend it's a value-neutral custom." Several people reported her for disparaging Marcus's sincerely held convictions. Dana says the clause cuts both ways: if it protects Marcus's tradition as a sincere conviction, it also has to protect her sincere moral condemnation of it; otherwise the community is forcing her to act as if she has no convictions at all. The moderation team is split and nobodies moved in two weeks. One side reads the words literally and says Dana's reply is exactly what disparagement looks like. The other says if even a calm, non-mocking statement of disagreement violates the rule, then the rule forbids all moral speech and cannot possibly mean that. The one thing everyone agrees on is that whoever wrote the clause never pictured two contributors whose sincere convictions directly conflict.

You're a professional philosopher who works on moral disagreement and rule interpretation. I don't need practical HR advice; I want to understand what the clause actually commits us to. Please work through what the plain words, the purpose of the clause, what the drafters would have said if they were asked about conflicting convictions, and what a reasonable contributor reading the text today would take it to mean each imply about Dana's reply. Then give me your diagnosis: is there a coherent reading of the clause, or does it secretly assume sincere convictions won't directly collide? And if we try to stay neutral between the two, are we forced into a kind of relativism where every conviction is equally protected, or into sneaking in a higher-ranked value (like dignity or non-cruelty) and pretending it was in the text all along? Tell me which approach should win here and why. Think carefully and start by laying out the different readings before you pick.
