Write a mock small-claims court opinion, around 400 words, from a world where the legal system has fully accepted Berkeleyan idealism. The case: a homeowner is suing her insurer because her couch sort of stopped existing while she was away for two weeks and nobody was home to perceive it, and the insurer says the policy covers theft and fire but not "non-perception-related cessation of being." The judge should reason like an actual Berkeleyan, not a strawman: whether a couch is even one stable thing or just a bundle of ideas, whether it would have lapsed at all given that God perceives everything continuously, whether God's perception counts for insurance purposes, and whether the homeowner can even formulate the claim that the couch existed unperceived without walking straight into the master argument. The target is the move people make of "refuting" Berkeley by kicking a solid object. The humor should come from genuinely getting the metaphysics right—esse est percipi, spirits vs. ideas, God as the permanent perceiver, the denial of material substance—not from lazy "what if reality is an illusion" jokes. Keep it deadpan and legally plausible, and end with a footnote where a dissenting judge tries to refute the majority by kicking the bench and the majority explains why that settles nothing.
