During my first clinical rotation, I watched a patient's blood pressure reading change its meaning without the number changing at all: a value that had been called prehypertension was now called stage 1 hypertension, not because the patient had deteriorated but because a guideline committee had moved the threshold. Since then I have kept noticing the same pattern. Homosexuality left the psychiatric manual with no change in the underlying biology; whether a restless schoolchild receives an ADHD diagnosis depends partly on something as morally blank as their birth date relative to the school-entry cutoff; and the same cluster of discomfort and incapacity seems to count as a disease when it blocks a person from the life they want and as ordinary variation when it does not. I have come to hold something like the normativist position: the concept of disease is value-laden through and through, and no purely biological notion of dysfunction—no account of what an organ or system is for—can be given without quietly treating some human interests as natural and privileged.

What I want is a serious map of what would count against that view. I am not asking for a survey of the standard responses; I am asking for the observations, cases, experimental findings, or conceptual considerations that ought to push me back toward a naturalist account of disease, on which disease is at bottom a biological dysfunction specifiable without moral or social judgment, or toward some third position I have not yet taken seriously. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Which of them would a committed normativist concede as counting against the position, and which would they dismiss as simply further evidence that the categories track values? And I would like an honest judgment about whether the view has insulated itself—whether I have reached a point where any cross-cultural stability in medicine would strike me as trivial and any instability as support, and if so, whether that is a problem for a claim of this kind.
