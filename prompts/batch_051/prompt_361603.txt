I spent forty years doing kinetics on enzymes and transport proteins, so the vocabulary I actually live in is molecular species, elementary steps, transition states, rate laws, coupled fluxes, steady states, free-energy profiles, that sort of thing. Since I retired I've been reading philosophy of science, and I keep coming back to the mechanistic explanation literature of Machamer, Darden, and Craver and the people after them. As I've picked it up, the view goes like this: a mechanism is composed of entities and activities. Entities are the things (proteins, ions, vesicles) and activities are what they do (bind, hydrolyze, translocate, occlude). Activities need entities, and entities without activities explain nothing. Mechanisms are organized in space and time, and in virtue of that organization the activities produce the phenomenon; the productive continuity bit is that each step actually hands something off to the next, not merely correlates with it. Explanation on this view is supposed to describe the actual mechanism in the world, not just a model that tracks the data, and it bottoms out at whatever level the relevant field treats as unproblematic. Take the sodium-potassium pump: entities are the pump, ATP, sodium and potassium; activities are binding, phosphorylating, conformational change, release; organization is the ordering of those steps and their coupling to ATP hydrolysis.

What I'd like is a systematic translation of that whole picture into the chemical-kinetics and thermodynamic vocabulary I just described. For each piece—entities, activities, organization, bottoming-out, productive continuity, the ontic-versus-model claim—tell me (a) what translates cleanly, (b) what only translates with distortion, and (c) what has no expression at all in my vocabulary. Then do the part I'm actually after: look at the residue. Is the stuff that won't translate a defect of my kinetic vocabulary, or a sign that the philosophical claims are less sharp than they sound? I honestly go back and forth on wether activities are just elementary reaction steps in fancier clothes, or whether something real gets lost.
