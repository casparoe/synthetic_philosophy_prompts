I've been using a distinction in how I read papers and I want someone to tell me if its actually doing work or just feels like it does. I call it inference leakage. The idea: a p-value is supposed to be the probability of data at least this extreme under an analysis you fixed in advance, but in practice the analysis is a function of the same data. You see a histogram and decide to log-transform; you drop the "bad" participants after looking at their responses; you switch from a t-test to a nonparametric test because the residuals looked off; you peek at the effect size halfway through and then decide whether to keep collecting. Each step leaks a little information from the data into the choice of analysis, and by the time you report p = .04, the analysis itself has the data built into it. So I've been saying the replication crisis is mostly a cascade of these leaks: replication failure should track the number of leaky decisions that went into the result more than it tracks the nominal p-value. My working claim is that the best predictor of whether a study replicates is roughly how many forks the authors could list if you bought them a beer, not how small the p is.

I know this sounds like researcher degrees of freedom, and it definitely overlaps with the garden of forking paths idea (the point that many analyses could have been run and you only see the one that worked). But I think the leak idea adds something specific on the Bayesian side: the prior and even the model space are usually chosen after the data has been seen too, so saying you condition only on what was observed doesn't buy you anything, because what you treat as the model is itself informed by the data. Leakage is my name for that general thing, the data contaminating the analysis plan whether the analysis is frequentist or Bayesian.

So, honest assessment please. Is this a real concept or just a metaphor with a valve? Does it already exist under a better name? Give me your three strongest objections and say which one is fatal rather than just uncomfortable. And here's a specific place I worry it breaks: a fully preregistered analysis with a principled, pre-specified prior should have zero leakage by my definition, but it feels more like the leak just got pushed into the preregistration. Someone had to choose the prior and the model class before seeing this dataset, maybe using earlier datasets, and that still seems like data getting into the analysis somehow. Does my concept force me to say anything preregistered is leak-free, or can it handle that?
