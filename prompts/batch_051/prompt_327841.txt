I'm a doctoral student, and next week I have to run a fifty-minute section for first-year undergraduates introducing Nozick's tracking account of knowledge, focused on its consequence that knowledge is not closed under known entailment. The students have just covered justified true belief and why Gettier cases unsettle it, but they have no formal possible-worlds machinery. I don't need the theory explained to me; I need a plan for explaining it to them.

Specifically, I need five things. First, the order in which the ideas must be introduced and what depends on what. My working sequence is to motivate why anyone would want a counterfactual condition on knowledge in the first place, then state the sensitivity condition roughly as the requirement that a knower would not have believed p by the same method had p been false, then contrast it with safety, and only then let the closure denial emerge from a concrete case. I want to know whether that ordering is right, and which step will carry the real pedagogical weight. Second, the carrying example: I plan to build the session around the zebra-at-the-zoo case, where a visitor would still have taken the animal for a zebra if it had been a cleverly painted mule. Is that the right carrier for the closure-failure point, or would a lottery case serve better? What exactly must the example have, and what must it avoid suggesting? Third, map the misconceptions and when to head each off. I expect at least four: students will collapse sensitivity into safety; they will think rejecting closure under entailment is the same as rejecting modus ponens or making deduction illegitimate; they will drop the same-method clause; and they will think a sensitive belief is one that survives every far-fetched skeptical scenario. For each, tell me at precisely which point in the sequence to preempt it rather than correct it afterward. Fourth, what to leave out at this level: whether to present all of Nozick's conditions or only sensitivity, and how little possible-worlds formalism I can get away with while keeping the talk honest. Fifth, give me one question to ask at the very end that would show someone has grasped the closure-failure position rather than merely repeating the slogan that knowledge is not closed.
