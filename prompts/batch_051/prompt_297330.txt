An independent advisory body proposes that a metropolitan government create an Equity Monitoring Office governed by a single scoring rule. Each year the Office would compute a standardized 'advantage index' for every resident—a composite of income, wealth, health, and available leisure, normalized so that higher always means better off. Under the base proposal, a policy would be assessed exclusively by the change it produces in the variance of this index across residents: it passes if it lowers the variance, fails if it raises it, and is indifferent otherwise; the mean level of the index enters nowhere in the score. A board faction then proposes an amendment replacing that rule with a prioritarian score: the sum over residents of the square root of each person's post-policy index (with the index floored at a small positive value), so that gains to lower-index residents count for more, but gains to anyone still count for something.

Walk through how the base rule would actually behave once agencies, advocates, and the statisticians who maintain the index understand it: which policies it would certify or reject, what it would reward, and where it would be gamed first—index redefinition, boundary drawing, encouraging residents to move, or deliberately worsening the best-off while leaving everyone else unchanged. Then do the same for the prioritarian amendment, and assess whether it genuinely escapes the leveling-down problem or only relocates the gaming. For each rule, say which of the failure modes could be fixed without gutting the scheme. Finally, state what would change your assessment: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict about either rule, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
