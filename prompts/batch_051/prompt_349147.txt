I'm a lexicographer at a small dictionary publisher and we're redoing our headword list. Every few days I have to decide whether two forms should be filed under one entry or two. Is "dogs" the same word as "dog"? (I'd say obviously yes.) Is "dogged" in the sense of persistent the same word as "dog"? (I'd say no, but I can't articulate the rule I'm using.) And is "the dog's" one word or two? The decisions affect how many entries we print, how the app handles lookups, and what gets counted as a "word" in our stats, and the senior editor will sign off on whatever procedure I write but expects it to be followed the same way every time. I'm not a trained morphologist, and I have maybe twenty minutes per contested case.

Write me a decision procedure I can actually run, grounded in what linguistics actually knows about inflection vs. derivation and the boundaries of a word: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure says "same lexeme, one entry," where it says "seperate entries," and where it says "not a word at all / a clitic." Make sure it handles the hard distinctions: productive regular inflection (walks), irregular and suppletive inflection (went, better), derivational affixes that are very productive (-ness, -ly), compounds vs. phrases (blackbird vs. black bird), the English possessive 's and reduced forms like n't, and bracketing ambiguities like unlockable. Flag the steps that are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on: (1) kind / kindness / kindly, (2) go / went, and (3) the dog's. I want at least one case where your procedure forces a filing I will not like, and a short honest note at the end about where the procedure will misfire.

Format: open with the checklist itself, no preamble; then the worked applications; then the "what this can't settle" note. Hard cap: 900 words.
