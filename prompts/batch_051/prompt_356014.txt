Consider the following clause from a historical association's code of practice: "No statement about the past shall be published as historical knowledge unless it can be traced to a documentary source that directly testifies to the fact asserted; the historian's task is to let the sources speak, not to introduce interpretations of his own." A group of economic historians has digitized several thousand seventeenth-century probate inventories and used machine-learning methods to infer regional distributions of wealth and prices across decades. The algorithm yields statistical claims about whole regions, but no individual inventory, and no contemporary observer, states those regional facts. Does the clause permit these claims to be published as historical knowledge? Consider the question first under a Rankean reading of the clause as requiring neutral transcription of the sources, and then under the sceptical challenges of Carr and Hayden White, for whom selection, emplotment, and the framing of facts are already present in what counts as a source and in what the sources are taken to say. What does each approach imply about whether the algorithmic output is more or less objective than a conventional narrative based on the same records, and which of the clause's possible purposes should govern the case?
