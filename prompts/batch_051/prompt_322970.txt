I've been reading Rousseau's Social Contract for a local reading group and this line from Book II, ch. iii keeps bugging me: "IT follows from what has gone before that the general will is always right and tends to the public advantage; but it does not follow that the deliberations of the people are always equally correct." He then goes on to distinguish the general will from the will of all. Somebody in the group proposed a scheme to operationalize that distinction: a citizens' assembly of 200 people selected by lot. Before they deliberate on any policy, each member fills out a private card with two entries. The first is an honest answer to the question "what do I, consulting only my particular interest, actually want here?" The second is "what does the common good require, taking everyone's interests as equal?" The two sets of answers are aggregated separately. Only the second aggregate is binding on the assembly's decision, but both are published anonymously after the vote. Also, if the two aggregates diverge by more than a fixed margin, the policy question gets sent back for a second round with new briefing materials. I want you to analyze this thing as a mechanism, not as a moral proposal. What does it incentivize? How would different types of members — ideological people, sectional interest reps, contrarians, people who just want the process to end — game it? Does making the self-interest aggregate public change what people write on the first card, and if so how? Would the divergence threshold become a coordination target? And does the scheme actually separate the general will from the will of all, or does it just launder the latter through the language of the former? Where does it fail first, and can any of those failures be fixed without turning the whole thing into ordinary deliberation?
