Hey, I help run a small grantmaker in the animal welfare space and we're redesigning how we score applications. The proposal on the table is impressivly simple. For each project, the affected animals get a consciousness factor c between 0 and 1: zero means we're convinced they have no morally relevant experience at all, one means full capacity for experience, and intermediate values cover both genuine uncertainty about whether they're conscious and genuine partial capacity—the proposal pointedly declines to say which. Seperately, estimate w: the amount of suffering averted or positive experience created if the animals counted at c = 1, on whatever welfare scale we already use (pain-hours, life-years improved, you pick). Final score is c times w. We rank projects by score and fund down the list until the budget runs out.

The appeal is that it makes awkward tradeoffs feel mechanical: chickens at c = 0.9 beat shrimp at c = 0.3 even when the shrimp project helps orders of magnitude more individuals, which matches a lot of gut instincts on our board. But I want to understand what this formula actually does to the people applying to us and to our own staff.

Walk me through it as a mechanism, not a claim about the right moral theory. What gets funded and what gets squeezed out? How will grantees game c and w—cherry-picking species categories, choosing units for w, commissioning sentience research, bundling projects? What happens at the edges: c near zero, c itself uncertain rather than a single number, w astronomical while c is tiny? And does multiplying c by w quietly commit us to a linear picture of how consciousness and hedonic value combine—one that we might disown once it's made explicit? If we patch the worst bits, say by treating everything below some c as effectively zero or by switching to an additive score like a·c + b·w, do the failure modes go away or just move?
