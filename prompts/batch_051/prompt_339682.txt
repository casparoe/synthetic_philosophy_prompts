Exam question:

Compare a counterfactual theory of causation in the style of David Lewis with an interventionist theory in the style of James Woodward. Your answer should explain (i) how each view analyzes a sentence of the form "C caused E"; (ii) the role of counterfactual dependence in each; (iii) how each view excludes, or fails to exclude, spurious correlations such as the falling barometer preceding a storm; and (iv) how each view handles symmetric overdetermination, such as two rocks striking a window simultaneously. Be explicit about where the views agree and where they diverge.

Student's answer:

Both Lewis and Woodward hold that causal claims are closely tied to counterfactual claims, but they evaluate the relevant counterfactuals differently. For Lewis, "C caused E" is true just in case E counterfactually depends on C—that is, in the closest possible worlds where C does not occur, E does not occur. Closeness is fixed by a similarity weighting that respects the actual laws and matters of particular fact, and the analysis is intended to be reductive. Woodward also places counterfactuals at the center of his account, but he gives them an interventionist reading. On his view, a variable X is a cause of a variable Y when Y would change under an intervention on X across some range of background circumstances. An intervention is a manipulation of X by an experimenter that changes X independently of the other causes of Y. Because "intervention" is defined partly in causal terms, Woodward's account is non-reductive.

The two accounts come apart over spurious correlations. Consider the falling barometer and the approaching storm. Woodward's intervention condition excludes the case, because intervening on the barometer would not change the weather. Lewis's possible-worlds treatment, however, has no resources here: the counterfactual "had the barometer not fallen, the storm would not have occurred" is evaluated while holding the actual prior atmospheric conditions fixed, so it comes out true, and Lewis must classify the barometer as a cause of the storm. This is why Woodward imposes the intervention condition.

The accounts also compare over symmetric overdetermination. If two rocks strike a window at once, each sufficient for the break, then for each rock the counterfactual "had it not been thrown, the window would not have broken" is false, so Lewis's analysis says neither rock caused the break. Woodward's interventionist test yields the same result, because holding the other rock fixed, an intervention on the first makes no difference to the window. Hence, despite their differences over manipulation, the two theories agree, problematically, about overdetermination.
