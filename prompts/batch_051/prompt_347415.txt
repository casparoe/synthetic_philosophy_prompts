Walking the dog yesterday I got into an argument with my flatmate. She said she would report someone she saw kicking a dog, but she couldn't say who the report was owed to — not the dog, since the dog isn't a person, and maybe not the owner if it was a stray. We both felt cruelty to an animal is wrong, and yet neither of us could quite say that it wrongs the animal. The same asymmetry seems baked into a lot of our practice: we think cruelty to a dog is a matter for the law, but when we try to say why, we reach for the owner's feelings or the public's sensibilities rather than something the dog itself is owed. Kant appears to have had the same structure in mind: on his account there are no direct duties to animals because they are not rational, only indirect duties regarding them; cruelty matters because it coarsens us and makes us worse at our duties to humans.

My question is whether this asymmetry is actually defensible. Give me the strongest principle you can that says an action can be wrong, even seriously wrong, without being wrong to the creature in front of you. Then test it against the case I can't get out of my head: someone who feels genuine sympathy for animals and is gentle with them in daily life, but tortures them in private as a way of venting cruelty he would otherwise direct at people. On an indirect-duty view, is that better, worse, or the same as torturing them callously in public? Whatever you answer, does that show the asymmetry should be kept, given up, or reversed?
