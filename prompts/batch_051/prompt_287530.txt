The question I set was: "Descartes held that nonhuman animals are machines without minds, not simply creatures with weaker minds than ours. Lay out the main reasons he gives for this view, and then present either what you take to be its most important consequence or the strongest objection to it."

Sam's answer was: "Descartes gives two main reasons for thinking animals are thoughtless machines. The first is the language argument. He says that no animal has ever been observed to use signs to express thoughts the way humans do: a parrot can repeat words, but it only plays them back mechanically, whereas even a very dull human can put words together in new ways to answer whatever is said to them. That shows animals lack a thinking mind. The second is the skill argument. Some animals are better than us at one particular task, but the same animal is bad at other tasks that would be easy if it had real intelligence. Descartes takes this to show that their behavior comes from the arrangement of their organs, not from reason, like a clock that keeps time better than a person does. Since Descartes is a dualist, it follows that animals have no mind at all. An important consequence is that animals can't feel pain; when a dog yelps after being kicked, that's just a mechanical response, like a clock chiming. Descartes was also explicit that this means we're allowed to treat animals however we like, since there is no suffering to worry about. The strongest objection, I think, is that a creature could have feelings or simple thoughts without being able to express them in language, so the language argument proves too much."
