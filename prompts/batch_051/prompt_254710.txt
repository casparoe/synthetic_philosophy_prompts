I'm a retired chemist who's gotten into legal philosophy lately, mostly because I've spent an embarrassing ammount of my retirement clicking through terms-of-service screens for lab software and medical devices I still fiddle with. The question that won't leave me alone is whether clicking "I agree" is agreement at all, or whether the whole thing is a theatre of consent. I'd like to put a piece in my old research group's newsletter that lands this with some actual bite.

Would you write me a deadpan parody of a clickwrap terms-of-service agreement, about 600 words, formatted as numbered clauses, for a fictional but plausible product that won't turn on until you click—say a pacemaker firmware update or an insulin pump app with an unskippable EULA. The target should be the legal fictions around assent itself, not any particular company: the duty-to-read rule, the idea that the act of clicking is what matters and your private lack of understanding is irrelevant, the meeting-of-the-minds fiction, unilateral amendment of terms with notice nobody reads, and the arbitration clause that also decides whether a contract was ever formed. The humor has to come from exaggerating real features of these fictions rather than inventing outlandish ones, so a contracts person would nod along and a layperson would still feel the point. At least one clause should claim the user was already bound before clicking, and one should make continued use of the device count as acceptance of amendments. Let the clauses do the arguing—don't editorialize or wink at the reader.
