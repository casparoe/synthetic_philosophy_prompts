At work we are in the middle of an argument about whether to cut a new major release, and I have started to suspect that I think about human generations the way I think about versioning a codebase. In a repository the code evolves continuously, and the points at which we decide that something is 2.0 rather than 2.1 are partly arbitrary conventions. But once a tag exists it becomes causally real: users pin their dependencies to it, we promise not to break its public interfaces, and a major-version bump signals breaking changes, with the result that people coordinate around the boundary, delay upgrades, or refuse to cross it even when the underlying code is barely different from the previous commit. Nobody believes the code has a natural seam at 3.0; the seam acquires its force from being treated as one.

Generational labels look structurally similar to me. Birth years form a continuous stream, and the boundaries drawn around Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z are disputed and to some extent arbitrary, yet once the labels exist they are used by marketing departments, policy analysts, and journalists, and they are taken up by people as identities or accusations. The alleged shared traits of a cohort start to look like compatibility promises made about an unstable codebase: some description is roughly accurate, some becomes self-fulfilling because people adjust to the label, and some is documentation nobody genuinely relies on. I have no formal training in philosophy, so I cannot tell whether this is a real structural parallel or loose pattern-matching.

What I would like is a verdict on the analogy itself. Is the parallel between semantic versioning and generational categories deep or superficial? If it is deep, what exactly does the software case predict about generations that turns out to be true, and what does it predict that turns out to be false? In particular, does the distinction between breaking and non-breaking changes have a genuine social analogue, and would a generation be more like a major version, a minor version, a long-term support release, or a marketing codename? If the parallel is superficial, where do the two structures come apart, and does treating generations as versions bias the more basic question of whether they are real kinds at all? Please answer in British English.
