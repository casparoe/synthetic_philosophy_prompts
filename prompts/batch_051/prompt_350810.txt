Over the years I've kept a file on how a colleague of mine, who says she runs the golden rule, actually rules in particular cases, and I've never managed to state the principle behind her verdicts. The bit I'm confident about is that she defers to the other party's preferences instead of projecting her own. Some entries: she says a host should honour a dinner guest's dietary restrictions even when the guest is too polite to mention them and the host would happily eat anything put in front of him. She says not to throw a surprise party for a friend who has repeatedly said she hates surprises, even though you'd love one yourself. She says a surgeon must not override a lucid patient's refusal of a treatment the surgeon would want for herself. And she says you should keep sending birthday cards to a neighbour who drops hints about them, even though you'd rather no one sent cards to you. So it isn't the do-as-you-would-be-done-by rule, and I don't think it's the negative version either unless the negative version has been fixed up beyond recognition.

The case I actually need a prediction for is this. Her nephew is at the airport with her and has worked himself into a state about flying. At the gate he announces he's cancelling and will drive the eleven hours home. His partner, on the phone, says this is exactly what happened last time: he panics everytime at the gate, and afterwards he always says he's glad someone made him board. The nephew is an adult, knows this history, and while panicking says quite clearly that he wants to be left alone to drive. I don't care which way she goes; I want the rule. The candidates I can formulate are: (a) treat people as they actually want to be treated, full stop; (b) treat people as they would want to be treated when calm, informed, and thinking clearly, which is why the earlier cases won't separate (a) from (b). Maybe there's also (c), the rule read only as a floor against doing what the other person wouldn't want, so the real question becomes whether making him board crosses that floor. I'd like you to lay out which rules fit the record, what each candidate predicts in the airport case, your best guess about her verdict and the reasons for it, and one further case that would most cleanly distinguish the candidates you take seriously. Please write the entire answer in British English. Finish with a line beginning "Verdict:" followed by your best guess in a short phrase, such as "Verdict: make him board" or "Verdict: let him drive".
