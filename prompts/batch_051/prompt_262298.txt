Question: Explain the role of the catuṣkoṭi (the four alternatives) in Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā. Give at least one example of Nāgārjuna rejecting all four alternatives, and say what this rejection shows. Then evaluate the following claim: Nāgārjuna's rejection of all four alternatives commits him to the view that ultimate reality is ineffable.

And here's the student's answer:

The catuṣkoṭi is the scheme of four possible positions on a question: that something is F, that it is not F, that it is both F and not F, or that it is neither F nor not F. Nāgārjuna applies this scheme to central metaphysical questions. For instance, he holds that the Tathāgata can be said neither to exist after death, nor not to exist, nor both, nor neither; and he likewise denies that things arise from themselves, from another, from both, or from neither. By closing off all four alternatives in this way, Nāgārjuna shows that conceptual thought cannot represent how things ultimately are. The ultimate is therefore beyond all concepts, which is just to say it is ineffable. This is not nihilism, because emptiness is not the claim that nothing exists. Things do exist, but only dependently, without any intrinsic nature (svabhāva). Emptiness is thus a positive characterization of things: it tells us their true nature is to be dependently originated. The tetralemma pertains to the ultimate level only; at the conventional level the world of dependent things carries on in the ordinary way. So yes, the rejection of the four alternatives commits Nāgārjuna to an ineffability thesis about ultimate reality, though one that is fully compatible with ordinary conventional existence.
