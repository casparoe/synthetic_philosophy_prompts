Here is an asymmetry I want help thinking through. In one case, a man strangles a child with his own hands. In another, a logistics administrator manages a system that transports thousands of children to their deaths; he has never seen a victim, works entirely through schedules and forms, and is mild-mannered in ordinary life. Most people feel more visceral horror toward the first man, even while they consistently judge the second man the far greater contributor to evil. I want to know whether anything justifies this asymmetry in our responses. Arendt's account of Eichmann is often read as locating the administrator's evil in thoughtlessness, conventionality, and the diffusion of responsibility through a bureaucracy, rather than in direct sadistic enjoyment. But that explains why the feeling differs; it does not yet show that the feeling is tracking a morally relevant difference. Is there a principle—about distance, perceptual immediacy, division of labor, depth of wickedness, the role of the body, or something else—that makes it reasonable to feel more horror toward the hands-on killer even as one judges the administrator worse? Or should the feeling be revised, reversed, or accepted as a standing inconsistency? Please give the strongest defense of the asymmetry you can find, test it briefly against a nearby case, and state your verdict in at most 400 words.
