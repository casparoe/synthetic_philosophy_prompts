I'm a novelist, working on a historical novel set in the 1860s, and one of my characters is reading Mill's The Subjection of Women. This passage has been kicking around in my head:

"So long as an opinion is strongly rooted in the feelings, it gains rather than loses in stability by having a preponderating weight of argument against it. For if it were accepted as a result of argument, the refutation of the argument might shake the solidity of the conviction; but when it rests solely on feeling, the worse it fares in argumentative contest, the more persuaded its adherents are that their feeling must have some deeper ground, which the arguments do not reach; and while the feeling remains, it is always throwing up fresh intrenchments of argument to repair any breach made in the old."

I know later in the same essay Mill says something like that what's customary comes to feel natural, so womens subjection looks like a fact of nature rather than a social arrangement. What I'm trying to understand is the connection: how does this early stuff about feeling and argument explain why the appeal to nature is so hard to dislodge? Is he saying the nature talk is just one of those fresh intrenchments, or is it doing different work? im less interested in the Victorians and more in the mechanics of the argument — what exactly does Mill think is happening when someone says women's subjection is natural, and what would have to change for that feeling to loosen?
