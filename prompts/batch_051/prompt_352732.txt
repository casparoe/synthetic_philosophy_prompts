I have been trying to think through a practical-moral question via Hume’s account of justice and convention. I keep coming back to a passage in A Treatise of Human Nature, Book III, Part II, Section II, ‘Of the origin of justice and property’, and I would like help understanding exactly how far it takes me.

> ’Tis by society alone he is able to supply his defects, and raise himself up to an equality with his fellow-creatures, and even acquire a superiority above them. By society all his infirmities are compensated; and tho’ in that situation his wants multiply every moment upon him, yet his abilities are still more augmented, and leave him in every respect more satisfied and happy than ’tis possible for him, in his savage and solitary condition, ever to become.

The immediate question is this: many everyday conventions — which side to walk on, whether to queue, whether to tip a certain percentage in restaurants, whether one may jaywalk when the road is empty — feel optional in the abstract but obligatory once one is in a community that has settled into them. I am trying to determine whether a visitor who knows the local convention but flouts it is guilty of a specifically moral fault, or merely of imprudence, rudeness, or a failure to coordinate. Hume’s passage seems to start from the advantages of society and the inadequacy of solitary effort, but I cannot see precisely how he gets from there to the idea that particular conventions acquire normative force for individuals — especially for a visitor who has not been shaped by the same history and may not benefit in the same way. Is the visitor’s obligation grounded in the same thing as the native’s, or does Hume’s story leave the visitor outside the convention’s moral reach?

Could you walk me through the passage sentence by sentence, and then show how someone using Hume’s approach would answer the visitor question? I would also like to know whether there are distinct defensible readings of the passage on that point. In particular, please separate what is settled in the text from what is genuinely contested among readers of Hume, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
