Here are the criteria I was taught for judging that a depiction of war glorifies war: it presents combat as heroic or admirable, it minimizes or aestheticizes suffering, it frames the conflict as one in which one side is simply right, and it turns death into entertainment. Now consider a hypothetical strategy game set during a real twentieth-century war. The game models supply lines, disease, shell shock, friendly fire, and reprisals against civilians; its text and art stress that most casualties are non-combatants, and players often describe it as grim rather than heroic. At the same time, its core loop rewards rapid advances, marks successful airstrikes with satisfying audiovisual feedback, and the fun of the game consists almost entirely in planning and executing violence. Does the game glorify the war it depicts? Work through each criterion, note where the criteria leave the verdict underdetermined, and then say whether the criteria themselves should be revised—in particular, whether a definition of glorification must separate what a game represents from what its mechanics make pleasurable.
