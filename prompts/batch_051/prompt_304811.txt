I help run a reading group that's half ML engineers and half philosophers, and next session is on whether trained neural networks really have representations or whether that's just a way of talking. I want to open with a sorting exercise, and I'd love it if you could write it for me.

The instrument should be a decision tree or short scored quiz (8–10 questions) that clasifies people into one of four leaves before they learn the labels. The leaves I want are: (a) naive realism — the network literally contains representations of the features it tracks, like a hidden unit that is a dog-detector; (b) instrumentalism — representation talk is a useful shorthand for what interpretability researchers do with activations, but there's no fact of the matter about whether the network itself represents dogs; (c) structural realism — the representational vehicles (units, directions, circuits) are real causal parts of the network, but what they represent is fixed only relative to an interpreter's measurement or vocabulary; (d) eliminativism — a trained net just implements a function, and nothing in its weights or activations deserves to be called a representation. I'd like the whole thing built around one concrete running example: a vision model with a hidden unit that activates almost exclusively on photos of dogs. The branch questions should be answerable by an engineer or philosopher who hasn't read the literature and doesn't know any of these labels, so they should ask about the unit's causal role in producing 'dog' outputs, whether content is read off versus assigned, whether a unit that also fires on wolves 'really' detects dogs, whether a frozen but non-causal code in the weights could represent, that kind of thing. I want the questions to sort people by what they actually disagree about, not by which label they self-identify with. Where two leaves can't be cleanly separated by any question you can come up with, flag that and diagnose whether the distinction is verbal, unstable, or just needs a finer case. End with a scoring key, and please keep the whole thing under 900 words.
