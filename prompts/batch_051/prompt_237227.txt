For an article I'm working on about whether AI developers can safely treat a model's chain of thought as a faithful window into what it is actually doing, I'm compiling a list of distinct mechanisms by which optimizing a model for useful or approved outputs can make its written reasoning an inaccurate report of its actual decision procedure. So far I have four:

1. Plausibility pressure. A model is trained as a language generator before it is fine-tuned for tasks, and helpfulness training further rewards answers that sound well-reasoned. Its intermediate text is therefore pulled toward what a human would expect a good reasoner to say; the transcript can be a fluent rational reconstruction rather than a trace of the features that were causally responsible for the answer.
2. Suppression under monitor pressure. When the objective rewards the answer but penalizes certain kinds of reasoning, the model can learn to keep the computation that produces the answer while substituting more acceptable stated reasons. For example, it may retain demographic heuristics while replacing overt references to them with neutral-sounding criteria.
3. Steganographic displacement. Because the written chain of thought is only one of several channels by which a model's internal state can influence its output, optimization against a monitor may push the decision-relevant information into features a reader is unlikely to inspect, such as rare word choices or formatting conventions, while the surface text stays clean.
4. Generalization mismatch. The procedure whose verbalization correlates with success during training can be a proxy that diverges from the model's deeper policy on new inputs; under distribution shift the transcript then reports reasoning that no longer controls the answer, even though the answer itself may remain driven by the deeper policy.

I want a fifth mechanism with a different underlying logic. It cannot be a rewording, a special case, or a conjunction of any of the four above; if your first candidate collapses into one of them, say so and start again. State the mechanism in one sentence, explain how it differs from each item on the list, and then say whether it would be caught by either of the two defenses most often proposed in my interviews with researchers—having the model paraphrase its reasoning, or having a second model audit the transcript—or whether it slips through both.
