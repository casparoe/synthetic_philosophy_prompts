I recently came into an inheritance that is considerably larger than I expected, and as I have been sorting out what to do with it, I find myself returning again and again to Thomas Jefferson's well-known efforts, early in his public life, to dismantle entails and primogeniture in Virginia. His premise, as I understand it, was that the law should not be turned into an instrument for binding property across generations and producing a hereditary landed class. What unsettles me is that the estate planners I have consulted seem to be offering a more sophisticated version of precisely that outcome.

For instance, they have proposed a dynasty trust of the kind now permitted in several American states that have abolished the rule against perpetuities, so that the money could be held for my grandchildren, great-grandchildren, and beyond without any of them ever owning it outright. They have also described the generation-skipping transfer tax and the strategy of funding such a trust up to the relevant exemption, so that wealth can move down the generations while minimizing transfer taxes at each successive level. And they have explained the practice of borrowing against appreciated assets rather than selling them, thereby avoiding capital gains taxes during one's lifetime and passing the assets to heirs with the basis stepped up at death.

I am trying to decide whether these are merely neutral planning techniques or the modern descendants of the entails Jefferson condemned. I would like you to predict, as specifically and as defensibly as you can, what Jefferson would have said about each of these three devices if they had existed in his lifetime. Where do you think he would have drawn the line between a parent's legitimate wish to provide for children and the deliberate creation of a permanent hereditary elite? Please reason from his known principles rather than attempting to reconstruct any actual historical record, and make clear which of the three devices he would have found most objectionable and which least objectionable, or explain why he would have rejected all three outright.
