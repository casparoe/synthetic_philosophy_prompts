I'm teaching a small undergrad seminar on early modern rationalism, and next week is our last session on Leibniz's moral thought. We'll have spent the hour on two ideas and the connection between them: his theodicy (God actualizes the best of all possible worlds, apparent evils get folded into the best overall harmony, and metaphysical evil is just limitation in creatures) and his ethics of wise charity (justice as charity informed by wisdom, love as taking pleasure in another's happiness). The class is realy split: half think this is monstrously complacent, half think it's the only answer to evil we've seen that doesn't give up on either God's goodness or God's power.

I'd like to close with a poem. A villanelle, 19 lines, no strict meter. Here's the structural thing I want, and I care about it more than about prosody: let the two repeating refrains carry the philosophical tension. One refrain should speak in the voice of the theodicy itself, the principle that no particular horror escapes the chosen whole and that what looks like a broken thread is load-bearing in the total design. The other refrain should return as the particular that wont be absorbed, a specific concrete grief (a child's fever, a suicide, a famine, whatever you pick), the kind of thing Leibniz's critics said can't be waved away by pointing at the larger composition. The villanelle's formal trick, two lines alternating and returning no matter how far the poem pushes them away, should enact the way Leibniz's optimism keeps reasserting itself even as the objection keeps reasserting itself too. Don't resolve it into the slogan that all is for the best, and don't turn it into a Voltaire-style joke. I want a Leibniz specialist to recognize the actual positions (the infinite possibles in the divine understanding, pre-established harmony, evil as privation or limitation, justice as wise charity), and I want the final quatrain to leave the tension open rather than land on a verdict. American English, please.
