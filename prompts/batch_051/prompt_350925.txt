I've been rereading Ralph Burns's poem "Barbed Wire" from his book *Mozart's Starling* (Ohio Review Books, 1990), because a friend and I have been arguing about whether martial virtues can mean anything in peacetime. The poem includes long catalogues of named wire types, and near the end Burns writes:

“Unloved, unloving; that to name these
Does no political good, but as precision
Is polemical, against vague statement
And circular evasion, as the sharp angle of sun
And crossed wires together body forth a spark,
It is some kind—cold, unmusical, utterly itself,
Keeping cattle in, or the enemies of sheep
Out.”

I have an epistemic habit I want you to evaluate. I tend to distrust people who talk about courage or valor in civilian life unless they can be almost tediously concrete about the practices, implements, or conditions they have in mind—what kind of fencing weapon, what kind of civilian self-defense situation, what specific failure or risk they are actually imagining. My friend says this is exactly the mistake of treating a poetic stance as epistemology, and that someone can be brave while being inarticulate about the tools. But Burns seems to say that precision itself is “polemical, against vague statement and circular evasion.” Is my habit defensible? If so, what exactly is the justifying principle? If not, is there a narrower version that keeps the pressure toward concreteness without discounting inarticulate courage?
