i'm writing something on whether Hume's monetary essays still have bite now that most money is bank credit and fiat, and i keep ending up in a standoff between two ways of reading this passage from David Hume's "Of Money" (Political Discourses, 1752):

> That provisions and labour should become dear by the encrease of trade and money, is, in many respects, an inconvenience; but an inconvenience that is unavoidable, and the effect of that public wealth and prosperity which are the end of all our wishes. It is compensated by the advantages, which we reap from the possession of these precious metals, and the weight, which they give the nation in all foreign wars and negociations. But there appears no reason for encreasing that inconvenience by a counterfeit money, which foreigners will not accept of in any payment, and which any great disorder in the state will reduce to nothing.

So write me a dialogue in which two philosophers of money argue over it. One of them is more or less on Hume's side: more money in a kingdom mostly just re-prices its labour and goods, the one real edge of a big specie stock is geopolitical — paying armies abroad, hiring mercenaries from poorer neighbours — and paper credit is best treated as a counterfeit version of the real thing. The other is a credit theorist who thinks "counterfeit money" begs the question and that Hume's commodity-money starting point leaves him unable to see what bank credit actually does to production and trade. Keep the dialogue close to this passage: what it assumes, whether the last sentence still holds in a fiat-money world, and whether the real/counterfeit distinction earns its keep. Both of them should be genuinely strong, and each should answer the other's actual best point. dont let it become two monologues taking turns.
