I manage a five-person political-risk analysis unit at a specialty insurer that underwrites coverage against expropriation, forced contract renegotiation, and currency inconvertibility. Each week my analysts independently assign a probability to a specified triggering event within a policy period—for example, that a new government will impose capital controls within eighteen months—and those numbers feed directly into pricing and capacity decisions, where a ten-point error on a large account can cost seven figures. The analysts receive the same briefing file and use the same scoring template, but they cover different country desks, cultivate their own sources, and have only roughly comparable five-year calibration records. When two analysts land more than a few points apart, we currently have no rule at all: sometimes the more senior analyst's number wins, sometimes we take the median, and sometimes everyone keeps their own estimate and the decision falls to me. I want one reusable procedure the team can run in under an hour whenever two or more analysts disagree, grounded in whatever is actually established in philosophical work on disagreement among epistemic peers—when encountering a rival judgment rationally requires revising your own estimate, and when it does not. The procedure must be usable by people with no philosophy background and only basic statistics, and it must be explicit about the points where the theory stops and a manager has to decide.

Please write the procedure as numbered steps or a flowchart in words. It should state what makes two analysts epistemic peers for our purposes and what evidence bears on that; how far a genuine peer disagreement should move each analyst's estimate and how the estimates should then be combined; what conditions have to be met before someone may remain unmoved; and where the procedure stops without requiring any revision or hands the matter to a person because the question is one of values, or because peerhood cannot be settled on the available record. Mark every step that is a judgment call dressed up as a mechanical rule.

Then apply the procedure to these three situations. First, two analysts with nearly identical calibration scores disagree, 35 percent versus 50 percent, on whether a government will impose currency controls within the policy period, after reading the same file. Second, our most senior analyst has just returned from meeting the finance ministry and insists the risk is far lower than an equally calibrated colleague's model-driven estimate, though he says the decisive evidence is a feel for the room that cannot be written into the file. Third, a junior analyst with only fourteen months at the firm disagrees with a senior analyst whose judgment she did not consult before assigning her own number. I particularly want to see at least one case where the procedure tells me to accept a number I am inclined to reject, and I want you to say plainly what this procedure cannot accomplish.
