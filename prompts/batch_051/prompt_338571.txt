Given that Galen Strawson's Basic Argument shows that nobody can be ultimately morally responsible for anything they do, why doesn't it also settle the ordinary question of whether people ever deserve blame? I'm a student in an upper-level course on free will and moral responsibility, and this is the point I can't get past. The argument, as we covered it, is that you can be morally responsible for what you do only if you are responsible for the way you are, since your actions flow from the way you are; but to be responsible for the way you are you would have to have created yourself, and any act of self-creation would itself flow from how you already were, and so on. Nothing can be causa sui. So far, so clear. What I don't see is why this is treated as a point only about some special kind of responsibility. Ordinary actions issue from a person's character and motives every bit as much as ultimate ones do, and the person did not choose those either. So if the regress is sound, then when someone lies or breaks a promise, the action ultimately traces back to facts about them that were not up to them — and blaming them for it looks no more deserved than blaming someone for their blood type. Why, then, do philosophers still talk as if ordinary blame can be deserved? Is there really a distinction between what the Basic Argument rules out and what everyday desert requires, or is the field just unwilling to follow the argument where it leads? I would also like to know what would change your mind: if you think everyday blame survives the argument, which consideration could show that it does not after all; if you think it does not survive, which consideration could show that it does. Please also say where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Please write your response in American English.
