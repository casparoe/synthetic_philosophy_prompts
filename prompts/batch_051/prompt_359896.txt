One of the short-answer questions on the assignment asks students to explain a distinction from Rawls's discussion of the moral psychology of justice and then assess a claim about it. The class materials cover Rawls's account of the moral sentiments, including his treatment of envy, resentment, and excusable envy. The expected answer should stay close to that material; it is not meant to require outside reading.

The question was:

Rawls distinguishes envy from resentment and introduces the idea of excusable envy. Explain that distinction and then assess the claim that excusable envy is best understood as a signal that the social order is unjust.

A student submitted the following answer:

Rawls distinguishes envy and resentment by reference to the kind of judgement each involves. Resentment is a moral feeling: it arises when we take ourselves to have been treated in a way that violates the principles of justice, for instance when another person fails to do their part in a cooperative scheme from which they benefit. Envy, by contrast, is not a moral feeling; it is pain at another person's being better off, even when that advantage was legitimately obtained and does not reduce what the envious person has. The central difference, then, is that resentment appeals to principles of right and justice, while envy does not.

Rawls then considers whether envy should be built into the theory of justice. He rejects that idea, but he allows that some envy is excusable. Excusable envy arises when the social inequalities are large, when the better-off are not thought to deserve their advantages, and when the comparison undermines the self-respect of the less advantaged. In such circumstances Rawls says the envy is not a moral failing in the way ordinary envy is; it functions as a signal that the basic structure is failing to secure the social bases of self-respect, and in that respect it operates like resentment as evidence of injustice. That is why he treats excusable envy as a morally legitimate response, one that points towards the difference principle. A society that satisfies the difference principle would be one in which the conditions that make envy excusable are less likely to arise, so the presence of excusable envy gives us reason to judge the basic structure unjust.
