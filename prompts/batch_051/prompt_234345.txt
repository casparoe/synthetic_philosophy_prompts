I run a small forecasting unit at a policy nonprofit, and we put numbers on conditional claims all day — "if the bill passes, the agency misses its deadline," "if we escalate, the currency drops more than 10%." Our default habit is to treat the probability of those sentence as the conditional probability P(consequent | antecedent), drop the result into the same event space as ordinary events, and then combine it with other forecasts using and/or/negation. I keep telling the team this is a shortcut, and now I've been reading Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals and I'm worried the shortcut isn't just sloppy but actually inconsistent.

The way I understand the result (tell me where I'm wrong as part of the answer): if you require P(if A then C) = P(C|A) to hold for every A and C in your event space, and you also treat "if A then C" as an ordinary proposition in that same space, then your probability function collapses unless it's trivial — roughly, it can put positive weight on at most two mutually exclusive possibilities, so most of the distinctions the forecast is trying to track get crushed.

So I want a reusable checklist my analysts can run on a conditional claim before they publish a number. Not a lecture. The checklist should say, for each claim, whether they may safely write P(if A then C) = P(C|A) as a plain number, or whether the conditional has to be handled as a rule for updating (a two-stage model, imaging, whatever the right mechanism is — I don't care about jargon as long as the procedure says what to do), or whether the case can't be settled and has to go to a senior reviewer.

The procedure has to be runnable in about twenty minutes by an analyst who doesn't know the formal epistemology. It should ask questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and stop with a "no" when it must. It should mark the steps that are genuinely judgment calls, and say which steps need a senior reviewer's sign-off rather than an analyst clearing it on their own. I especially need it to catch these failure modes: an analyst who wants the shortcut for a one-off estimate and no more; an analyst who does it "just this once" and then quietly extends it; an analyst who conditions on the antecedent later after new evidence arrives; and an analyst dealing with an antecedent he currently gives probability zero.

Then run the checklist on these three:
1. A one-off: the team is 0.7 confident that if the trade deal is signed by December, the port clears it's backlog by March, and P(backlog clears | signed) = 0.7. Nobody later combines this with anything else.
2. A routine model that scores every bill in a session with "if the bill reaches the floor, the whip count moves by ten or more," and the analysts then combine those conditional scores across bills with ordinary and/or.
3. An analyst who gives P(antecedent) = 0 today, but knows a relevant report might arrive next month that would make the antecedent live, and wants to leave the old conditional probability on the books unrevised.

And be honest about the limits: show me a case where the checklist forces the analyst to throw away the number he's most confident in, and a case where the checklist lets something through that still blows up downstream. Say plainly what the checklist can't do, and where the theorem itself should make us stop trying to use ordinary probabilities for these sentences at all rather than patching the shortcut.
