On the legal-affairs beat I keep tripping over an asymmetry in the way courts and parliaments handle retrospective law, and I can't work out what principle is supposed to be underneath it. If a government tries to make a new criminal offence apply to conduct that happened before it was enacted, everyone treats that as an attack on the rule of law itself, not as an ordinary policy difference. But the same systems pass retrospective civil legislation pretty routinely, and tax is the most vivid case: a budget announced in the autumn will often apply from the start of that same tax year, hitting income you were paid months before the announcement, and the complaints are framed as unfairness or bad policy rather than a threat to legality. So what picture of the rule of law is that pattern presupposing? Does it commit us to thinking the rule of law is fundamentally about protecting people from state coercion aimed at their liberty, so that settled economic plans just don't get the same protection? Or can a single coherent ideal explain why retroactive criminal law is beyond the pale while retroactive tax law is only ever a grumble? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the asymmetry is actually defensible under it or is mostly political convenience, and spell out what would have to change in tax practice if we took seriously the line that a law which is not prospective is not law in the full sense.
