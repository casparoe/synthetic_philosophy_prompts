I'm a journalist preparing a feature for the ideas section on whether a constitution is best understood as a society binding itself in advance, a collective version of Odysseus having his crew lash him to the mast so that his future desires cannot be acted on. In my reporting, the Ulysses-contract analogy is invoked by some legal theorists to explain and justify entrenched constitutional limits, while others reject it on the grounds that a constitution binds not only the people who made it but also future citizens who had no say, and that the precommitment frame quietly assumes that this is still self-rule rather than rule by the past. The two camps keep talking past one another, and my editor wants something more constructive than another point–counterpoint. Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a defender of the constitutional-precommitment interpretation and a critic of that interpretation. The statement should include: a numbered set of claims both camps can sign, formulated as precisely as possible without becoming vacuous; a section stating exactly where they part ways, in wording each side would accept for its own position; a list of the genuine cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling them would cause at least one side to revise its view; and, where both sides can agree on them, the cases, tests, or arguments that would bear on those cruxes, with each side recording in advance what it expects them to show. Do not adjudicate the debate; produce a document both parties could put their names to. If some of the disagreement turns out to be verbal or to depend on rival definitions of democracy, consent, or self-binding, say exactly which part and reword it so that the remaining dispute is substantive.
