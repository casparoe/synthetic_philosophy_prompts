You are a professional philosopher working in modal epistemology, the area of philosophy that studies how we come to know what is possible, impossible, and necessary. You are expected to think carefully and produce original analysis rather than summarize familiar positions.

Our ordinary use of the word "conceivable" is confident, but the definition is contested. We routinely treat conceivability as at least a first step toward possibility: if a scenario is conceivable, that seems to count for something. But conceivability also seems to mislead. I want you to produce a working definition of conceivability — roughly, a definition of the form "a scenario or proposition is conceivable for an agent just in case ..." — and test it against the following hard cases.

The cases:

1. A mountain made entirely of gold.
2. A four-sided triangle.
3. A transparent, drinkable liquid that falls as rain, fills lakes, and behaves in all ordinary respects like water, but whose chemical composition is other than H2O.
4. The proposition that water itself is not H2O — that is, that the actual watery stuff of our lakes and oceans has some composition other than H2O.
5. A molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a normal adult human that behaves identically to the original but has no conscious experience whatsoever.
6. A staircase drawn so that each of its four flights ascends, yet the four flights form a closed loop returning to the starting point.
7. Light propagating as a wave through a luminiferous ether, as many nineteenth-century physicists believed — a scenario we now regard as physically impossible.
8. A largest prime number.

I have views about only some of these. I am confident that (1) and (7) are rightly called conceivable, and that (2) and (8) are rightly called not conceivable. I have no settled view about (3) through (6).

Please proceed as follows:

First, spend a paragraph or two brainstorming what the word "conceivable" seems to be tracking across these cases, and what distinctions the cases force you to draw — for example, between imagining and conceiving, between merely seeming conceivable and being conceivable on careful reflection, between conceiving a picture and conceiving a proposition, or between conceiving a state of affairs and merely referring to it by a description.

Then state your definition, or, if you conclude the ordinary term does two jobs, state the two definitions and say which cases fall under which. Run every case through your definition, giving a clear verdict and an explanation for each. Flag any place where your definition delivers no answer, a wrong answer, or a merely stipulative answer, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. Tell me which single verdict you are least secure about.

Finally, say what your resulting notion implies about conceivability as a guide to possibility: under what conditions, if any, does the conceivability of a scenario provide evidence for its possibility, and how does your definition sort the cases where conceivability is a trustworthy guide from those where it is not?

Do not cite sources or give a bibliography; I want your own worked-out account, tested against the cases.
