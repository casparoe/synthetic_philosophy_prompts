After four decades as a research chemist, I have spent the last few years reading philosophy of language, and I keep coming back to a puzzle about the difference between laws in the natural sciences and rules in grammar. In chemistry, a law that meets a single well-attested counterexample is in trouble; in English, meanwhile, the past-tense rule (add -ed) coexists comfortably with went, the comparative pattern (-er/more) with good-better-best, the plural rule (-s) with children and mice, and the residue of once-regular sound changes has left pairs like foot/feet. When I raised this with a linguist friend, he shrugged and said these cases are exactly where the exception proves the rule, apparently meaning that suppletive and irregular forms somehow confirm the regular pattern rather than counting against it. Since then I have encountered the proverb in several other settings, and I have begun to suspect it is made to do quite different work on each occasion.

I would like you to do for this one sentence what analytic philosophy does best with slogans: lay out the distinct, precise claims the exception proves the rule could be used to assert, from the trivial through the substantive to the false. I am especially interested in which of these readings, if any, would support my friend's claim that suppletive past tenses do not weaken the generalization that English verbs form their past tense by suffixing -ed, and which readings, however useful in legal or epistemic contexts, would not transfer to grammatical rules at all.

Please reply as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. In each point, state one candidate reading and then give a short verdict: whether that reading is trivial, defensible, or false, and whether it actually gets my friend his conclusion about grammatical irregularity.
