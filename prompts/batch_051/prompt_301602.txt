In his essay on the so-called cannibals, Montaigne describes the customs of a Brazilian people and seems to do two things at once: criticize European ethnocentrism and praise their way of life. What exactly is he doing here? Does the essay commit him to the view that moral standards are entirely relative to each culture, or is that too strong?

And here's the student's answer, unedited:

Montaigne's essay is an early statement of cultural relativism. He argues that we have no right to call the Tupinambá barbarous just because their practices differ from ours, since every culture finds its own customs natural and calls others' customs barbarous. By the end he's saying there is no neutral standard by which to compare the two ways of life, so the only honest position is to suspend all moral judgment. His praise of the Tupinambá as simpler and closer to nature is part of the same move: he's basically treating them as noble savages whose lack of European institutions makes them more virtuous than civilized Europeans, which is why he contrasts their cannibalism favorably with European torture and religious cruelty.
