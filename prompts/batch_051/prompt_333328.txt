I've been reading rationalist-adjacent blogs for years, and I keep seeing Popper's old argument against historicism used as a kind of conversation-ender: the idea that there can't be laws of history, that history is a one-off sequence, and that social predictions either fail or change the thing being predicted. As I understand him (call me out if I've garbled this), he said large-scale historical regularities are at most trends, not laws, because they depend on particular background conditions, and because a prediction about a society can alter what that society does. Recently, though, I've seen people claim that modern computational social science just buries this: you can train models on centuries of economic and political data, find regularities that hold across quite different societies, and make out-of-sample predictions about things like state collapse or democratisation. My question is: does Popper's argument actually survive that development? In particular, which specific premise does the machine-learning view actually bear on, and which parts of the argument would be untouched by even a very good predictive model? Is there a coherent position that accepts the predictive regularities are real while denying they are laws of history in Popper's sense, or does accepting that force you to say his argument was just wrong? I'm interesed in what happens to the argument, not in whether we should build such models or use them for policy. Also, please write your answer in British English.
