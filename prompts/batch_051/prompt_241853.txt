Yesterday evening a friend and I argued for hours about whether "democratic backsliding" picks out a real kind of thing or is just a partisan label. His view was that every government shifts powers among branches, tolerates disorderly politics, or ignores whatever norms it finds inconvenient, and that whether someone calls it backsliding tracks almost perfectly their view of the government in question: people say it about Hungary, Poland, and Turkey, and about presidents they oppose in the US, but rarely about allied governments doing analogous things. I resisted that, insisting there are discernible mechanisms — incumbents changing electoral rules to entrench themselves, hollowing out courts, degrading the information environment, harassing independent media — that mark a difference in kind rather than degree. By the end, though, I had to admit that my examples were doing the work that an explicit criterion should have been doing, and that I could not actually state the standard I was applying.

I want to fix that. Acting as a political theorist who works on comparative democratization, I would like you to think carefully and begin by brainstorming possible requirements before committing to the ones you actually defend. Then produce a list of desiderata that any adequate account of democratic backsliding must meet, where "adequate" means: usable for cross-national comparison, capable of being applied by observers with opposed partisan sympathies with at least broadly similar results, and able to distinguish real democratic decline from ordinary political contestation that one side happens to find antidemocratic. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp. The list should address at least these pressure points: how a criterion distinguishes backsliding from ordinary partisan policy change; whether the concept tracks a decline from an earlier baseline or merely failure to clear a threshold; whether it should be cashed out in terms of discrete institutions, in terms of the broader competitive playing field, or both; how it handles the fact that the relevant norms are non-legal and contested; and how it avoids becoming a covert way of ratifying a set of preferred policy outcomes. Treat the familiar cases — Hungary, Poland, Turkey, the US — as shared reference points rather than as facts to verify in detail. Then flag any pairs of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and tell me which single requirement, taken seriously, does the most to exclude the too-thin or too-partisan accounts on offer. Keep the focus on the criteria themselves; I will do the country-by-country scoring later.
