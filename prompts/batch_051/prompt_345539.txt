I edit a general-interest magazine that publishes argument-driven essays, and a good chunk of them lean on thought experiments—trolley cases, Mary's room, Chinese rooms, that kind of thing. I want a reusable screening procedure my editors can run on a submission to decide whether a thought experiment is actually doing the argumentative work the author claims, or just pumping an intuition: vivid one-sided framing, a gut reaction smuggled in as a premise, details that don't matter to the conclusion, and so on. It needs to be something a non-philosopher editor can run in about thirty minutes, it should say what to check first, what counts as passing, where the procedure stops with revise-or-reject, and where it has to hand the call to a person because the judgment can't be mechanized. Ground the checklist in whatever we actually know about how thought experiments persuade and mislead—robustness to fiddling with irrelevant details, whether the stipulation is merely logically possible or relevantly possible, whether the intuition is functioning as evidence rather than just illustration, and anything else you think earns its place. Then actually run the procedure on three cases from recent submissions: a trolley-style variant used to argue that a certain moral distinction is superficial, a Mary's-room-style case used to argue that experience has non-physical features, and a Chinese-room-style case used to argue that syntax can't produce understanding. Make sure at least one lands somewhere my editors won't like—either clearing a thought experiment they find fishy or killing one they find extremely persuasive—and say plainly what the procedure can't do.
