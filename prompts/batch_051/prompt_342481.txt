So for a seminar on comparative federalism we've been messing around with designs for subsidiarity with teeth—the idea being that in a big federation the national government always has an incentive to hoard powers because nobody can price the benefit of running things locally. One classmate floated this mechanism and we cant decide whether it's brilliant or a slow motion disaster.

Imagine a federation with twenty states that vary a lot in wealth and policy preferences. Every time the federal legislature passes a law in a shared-competence area (environment, labor, health, education, the usual stuff), the law automatically contains an escape hatch: after three years, if 8% of the registered voters in any single state sign a petition, that state may take the policy area back into its own hands for a four-year period. During those four years, the state's residents keep paying whatever federal tax or fee is earmarked for that program, at the federal rate, but the revenue goes into a solidarity fund that is distributed per capita to the states that did not opt out. The opting-out state can set its own standards, but not weaker than the federal baseline, and it must accept federal inspectors checking compliance. After four years it automatically rejoins unless it repeats the petition; and a state that opts out twice in the same area may then opt out permanently, but loses its vote in the federal legislature on anything touching that policy area.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a position we already hold. Walk us through who opts out first and about what, how the federal legislature would write laws knowing the hatch exists, how the solidarity fund reshapes the politics, what happens to interstate spillovers, and where the whole thing gets gamed or quietly breaks down. What kind of federation do we actually end up with ten years in?
