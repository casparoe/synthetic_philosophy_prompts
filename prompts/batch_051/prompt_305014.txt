For a graduate seminar in philosophy of medicine, I want the standards by which I should judge any account of clinical equipoise — the condition under which it is ethical to enroll a patient in a randomized controlled trial. Fix the purpose this way: the account is to tell an investigator who is also the treating clinician when enrolling a particular patient in a randomized comparison is compatible with the duty of care owed to that patient, and it is to yield a rule an institutional review board could apply to a protocol both before it opens and as its data accrue. I am not asking for the theories yet; I am asking for the desiderata they must satisfy.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp (the individual-equipoise camp, the community-equipoise camp, the uncertainty camp, the consent-suffices camp). Among the pressures your list must address, at least include: the double role of the doctor who both chooses treatment for the patient in front of them and enrolls that patient in a trial that overrides the choice; the placebo-arm problem, when an effective treatment already exists; the moving target, as accumulating trial data make the arms stop looking symmetric; the level question, whether the relevant uncertainty belongs to the individual investigator, to the expert community, or to something else; the consent question, what informed consent can repair and what it cannot if the randomizing condition is not met; and the distinction between not knowing which arm is better, having no reason to think either is better, and merely having a hunch.

The centerpiece should be your analysis of which subsets of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied. In particular, test the following suspect pairs and say which amount to a real impossibility for any account and which are only apparent. First, requiring that the enrolling clinician hold no comparative preference while also requiring that enrollment serve the individual patient's best interest, where best interest is read comparatively. Second, requiring that the standard be stable enough to license a trial of fixed size while also requiring that the trial stop the moment accumulating evidence destroys the uncertainty. Third, requiring both that the relevant indeterminacy be genuinely epistemic and that it be ascertainable in practice by investigators who are themselves parties to the research. Where a pair is unsatisfiable, say which requirement should give way, and what an account that gives it up is forced to say about the ethics of trials.

Then, in no more than a third of the response, run the main contenders through the list: the individual-equipoise condition, the community or clinical-equipoise condition, the uncertainty-principle condition on which randomization is permitted only when both enrolling clinician and patient are substantially uncertain, and the no-equipoise view on which trial participation is governed by a research ethic distinct from the duty of clinical care. Close by naming which desideratum does the most work in excluding candidates.
