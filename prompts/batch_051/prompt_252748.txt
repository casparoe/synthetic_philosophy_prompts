Two colleagues of mine — a nutritionist and a field coordinator for a rural development program — spent several evenings locked in an argument while we were evaluating a smallholder-farming region where chronic child malnutrition is widespread and most households keep a few goats and chickens. I listened without taking a side, and I still cannot sort out who has the better of it.

The nutritionist began: "The familiar ethical arguments against using animal products are written from a position of dietary abundance. They move from the wrongness of causing animal suffering to the conclusion that we should avoid animal products, but they quietly assume that adequate plant-based alternatives are available and affordable. In this region, for many families, a fully plant-based diet would create real nutritional risk for growing children. An obligation that people cannot meet in their actual circumstances cannot simply be pronounced against them."

The field coordinator pushed back: "The animals' suffering does not shrink just because avoiding it is costly for the eater. If the underlying act is wrong, then hardship changes what we should do about the obligation — how gradually we phase in changes, what we do to build alternatives — not whether the obligation exists. Having no attractive options does not erase the moral weight of the harms that the chosen option causes."

The nutritionist replied: "That treats morality as though it could issue verdicts for one world and then hand them to people living in another. For a household whose children depend on goat's milk and eggs for adequate protein and micronutrients, a demand to abstain is not something they can perform; it is a judgment passed by people whose circumstances make obedience easy. Ethics should begin from the capacities and circumstances of actual agents, not from an ideal those circumstances make unattainable."

The field coordinator came back: "But the claim that plant-based diets are impossible here is partly empirical and partly about current infrastructure, not natural necessity. The region could support legume varieties and fortified staples suited to its soils; that it does not is a policy failure. If we treat today's scarcity as given, we let an unjust agricultural system set the ceiling on individual duty, which conveniently flatters those who benefit from that system."

The nutritionist answered: "Even granting that, the fixes you mention are institutional changes no single family can bring about. For an individual now, the question is what to prepare this week. A morality that replies, 'First change the food system, then eat differently,' is not offering guidance; it is postponing it. In the meantime, the real comparison is between a child's concrete nutritional needs and ongoing animal suffering, and no general principle can settle that comparison from outside."

The field coordinator replied: "But notice what you have granted. You are no longer saying that animal suffering matters less here; you are saying it must be weighed against serious human costs. That means the core of the original argument — that animal suffering counts, and counts from the animal's perspective — survives intact. What varies is not whether the duty exists but how it should be balanced against other urgent considerations. That is a far more modest conclusion than the one you opened with."

Neither would make the final concession.

I would like a careful adjudication: who is right about which specific points, whether either mischaracterized the other's position, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
