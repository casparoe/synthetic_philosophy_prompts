I've been thinking about the familiar moral-luck case of the two careless drivers. Both run a red light at the same speed; a child steps into the path of the first and is killed, while the second drives through an empty crosswalk. The usual reaction is that the first driver is more blameworthy, or at least deserves harsher legal treatment, even though the only difference between them is luck.

Now vary the case in two ways. First, suppose both drivers know the pedestrian probabilities for their crosswalks at that hour: the first driver's crosswalk has a one-in-a-hundred chance of containing a pedestrian at that moment, and the second driver's has a one-in-ten chance. Second, suppose neither chose his route on the basis of the risk; a dispatcher assigned each route, and both know the risk attached to the route they were given. The first driver hits and kills a child; the second passes through an empty crosswalk. So the driver who was, a minute earlier, ten times less likely to hit someone is the one who actually does, while the driver who knew his route was riskier gets lucky.

Does the usual asymmetry survive this variation, reverse, or disappear? I see three natural verdicts: blame the killer more, blame the larger risk-taker more, or treat the two alike. Which one does the changed case best support, and why? In particular, if someone still says the first driver deserves more blame, what must that person say about the fact that a reasonable observer would have predicted the harm was more likely to come from the second driver?

3. The strongest statement of each of the three verdicts: blame the killer more, blame the larger risk-taker more, treat them alike (one paragraph each).
4. Which verdict the varied case cuts toward, and why (two to three paragraphs).
5. What someone who keeps blaming the killer more must now say about the harm being less likely to come from him, and whether that position can be held without internal strain (one to two paragraphs).
6. My bottom line: whether the variation strengthens, weakens, or redirects the original puzzle (one paragraph).
