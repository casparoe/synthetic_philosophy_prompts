I am a doctoral student working on self-knowledge, and I keep meeting a discrepancy in how first-person reports are weighted both in empirical practice and in ordinary life. If a participant reports that she currently has a mild headache, that she is now picturing a blue square, or that she felt an urge to move just before moving, the report is entered as data; nobody asks her how she knows, and demanding independent evidence would seem unserious. Yet when the same participant reports that she pressed the key because the stimulus felt familiar, that she is angry because the experimenter was patronizing, or that she is not anxious even though her hands are trembling, the report is treated as a fallible explanation, to be checked against her behaviour, history, and physiology. Both kinds of statement issue from introspection in some broad sense, and both are made with equal subjective confidence. My question is whether any epistemic principle actually separates the two classes, or whether the difference is only a practical and social habit. Please give me the strongest candidate principle you can construct, the reading of introspection it presupposes, the hardest borderline cases against it, and a verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or given up. The cases I would like it to handle include a report of a current emotion such as envy, a report of the reason one has just acted on, and a report of an ongoing bodily state such as nausea.
