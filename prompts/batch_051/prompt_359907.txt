My mother's family is from Millers Fork, and I grew up on the town's founding story: in the spring of 1878 the river rose so fast that the only reason half the town got out alive was a woman named Ada Litchfield, who woke from a dream, ran the streets banging a kettle, and drowned before morning. There's a statue, a school pageant, and every March a lantern walk down to the river. I was never much of a joiner, but last year I got into family history, and I found the death register first: Ada Litchfield died of typhus on February 11, 1878. The flood was in late March. Then I read the contemporary papers: the flood really happened and a lot of people did get out, but the evacuation was already underway by sundown because of telegraphs from upriver, and the aldermen had riders out before midnight. Ada is real, she's buried in the churchyard, but she couldn't have banged the kettle, and the kettle story didn't appear in print until the 1890s.

Now, because I made the mistake of mentioning the death register to my cousin, someone at the historical society heard, and I've been asked to give the short speech at this year's lantern walk. I can't get out of it. The audience is a few hundred people from town and around, including Ada's descendants, a lot of kids, and the state historian who's article first noted that the kettle story only shows up in print in the 1890s. I will not lie from the podium, and I won't pretend I never saw the record, but I also don't want to be the person who stands up on the roughly 150th anniversary and tells everyone their story is false, especially in front of Ada's descendants and small children.

Please write the speech, about four minutes. It should be an actual speech, not an essay. It needs to hold both things at once: the town's memory of Ada as something that has held the community together, and the historical record that says the kettle story is not true. Take the conflict between what a community remembers and what the record shows seriously, rather than dissolving it into the idea that facts don't matter or that the story is false and should simply be retired. Whatever you decide the speech should do—bless the memory, redirect it, confess—commit to it and make it land for both the historian and the descendants. And be honest with me afterward about what the speech you wrote can and cannot accomplish.
