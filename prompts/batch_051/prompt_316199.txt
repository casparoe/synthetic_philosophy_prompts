I'm TAing a philosophy of law course, and the midterm had a short-answer question on Warren and Brandeis's "The Right to Privacy" (1890). I've got one answer I keep going back and forth on, and since I have to leave comments defending whatever mark I give, I'd like a careful second opinion. The question was:

"Below is a passage from Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis, 'The Right to Privacy' (1890). Explain the role the appeal to 'mental pain and distress' plays in their argument for legal protection of privacy. In particular: (a) is the claim comparing this pain to 'mere bodily injury' a normative claim, a conceptual claim, or something else, and (b) does the passage support the view that the value of privacy reduces to the avoidance of this pain? You may quote briefly."

The passage given to the students was:

"The intensity and complexity of life, attendant upon advancing civilization, have rendered necessary some retreat from the world, and man, under the refining influence of culture, has become more sensitive to publicity, so that solitude and privacy have become more essential to the individual; but modern enterprise and invention have, through invasions upon his privacy, subjected him to mental pain and distress, far greater than could be inflicted by mere bodily injury. Nor is the harm wrought by such invasions confined to the suffering of those who may be the subjects of journalistic or other enterprise. In this, as in other branches of commerce, the supply creates the demand. Each crop of unseemly gossip, thus harvested, becomes the seed of more, and, in direct proportion to its circulation, results in the lowering of social standards and of morality."

Here is the student's answer:

"Warren and Brandeis begin from the thought that modern life has made people more sensitive to being watched and talked about, so privacy—understood as a retreat from the world—has become practically necessary. For part (a), I'd say the comparison between mental pain and bodily injury is a normative claim: they're telling us that the law should treat psychological suffering as at least as serious as physical injury, since it can be worse. Their deeper argument is that invasions of privacy produce this distinctive mental pain, and that is the real reason legal protection is needed. The bit about gossip lowering social standards is just a downstream consequence of that same individual harm, not an independent reason to value privacy. So for part (b), the passage does support a reduction: if someone genuinely didn't mind having their private life spread across the papers, there would be nothing left for the law to protect in their case. Privacy's value here is purely instrumental—it's worth protecting only because it spares people this psychological distress, not for any broader social or political reason."

I'm especialy interested in whether each step of the student's reasoning is actually licensed by the passage, rather than just whether the overall conclusion sounds plausible.
