I'm drafting a philosophical profile for a seminar presentation on Kuhn, and I'd like a professional philosopher of science to audit it for internal consistency before I present. Here is the position I want to defend:

(1) Normal science is puzzle-solving within a paradigm: a mature field shares exemplars, techniques, and standards of acceptable solutions, and most practitioners never question these.

(2) Across a revolution, rival paradigms are incommensurable: there is no neutral, shared set of rules or observations that could settle the dispute between, say, Newtonian and Einsteinian mechanics.

(3) For that reason, theory choice in a revolution is not forced by logic plus evidence; coming to see the new paradigm as correct is closer to a gestalt switch or conversion than to a proof.

(4) Nevertheless, I think scientific change across revolutions is progress: later paradigms are better at solving the problems their fields take seriously, and this is not merely change or fashion.

(5) I also hold a modest scientific realism: mature theories typically succeed in referring to real entities, and our current physics is, overall, closer to the truth than Newton's was.

(6) I think observation is always theory-laden; there is no neutral observational court of appeal available to both paradigms.

I want to know whether these six commitments hang together. Start by making each commitment precise enough to test against the others, then work through the pairs that look as if they could conflict and tell me which are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. Rank any genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to be given up and what the cheapest revision would be. Finally, say which single revision would do the most to make the whole set stable. Take your time and reason through this carefully; I would rather have a rigorous audit than a quick list. Throughout, please separate what is settled among philosophers of science from what is genuinely contested, and do not present one contested reading of Kuhn as the consensus.
