I have a rule about dog whistles, and I want to know whether it is defensible. When someone claims that a statement was a racist dog whistle, I refuse to accept the claim unless I am shown independent evidence that the speaker meant to signal to a like-minded audience — an established coded history for the phrase, say, or a record of the speaker using it knowingly before such an audience. If the only evidence is that some group heard the statement as coded, or that it caused harm, I treat the attribution as speculation and I do not repeat or act on it. I apply this rule to political speech in public debate and on social media, where I hold no official role and all that is at stake is what I come to believe and say. My argument for the rule is that otherwise nearly any ambiguous statement can be labeled a dog whistle on the strength of audience reaction alone, which leaves the speaker no way to answer the charge and makes the concept easy to weaponize as a debater's move. My worry is the opposite: the rule may permit the most effective dog whistles, which work precisely by leaving no independent evidence of intent. Is my rule a defensible epistemic policy, a shield for uncomfortable conclusions, or something in between? What would a defensible revision look like, and under what conditions would following it go seriously wrong?
