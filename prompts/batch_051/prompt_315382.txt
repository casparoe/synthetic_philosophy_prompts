You are an expert in axiomatic bargaining theory and in translating formal allocation rules into workable decision procedures. Think slowly, and begin by brainstorming the ways a bargaining solution can be misused when an actual institution tries to run it, before you commit to a final procedure.

I chair the shared-resource office of a research consortium with two groups. Twice a year I must propose an allocation of a single block of flexible funding and a fixed number of hours on a specialized instrument. The two group directors must both sign off on my proposal; if they do not, a funder default rule applies under which the money is returned and no instrument time is awarded, so each side's disagreement payoff is zero. I have one afternoon with the two directors, no authority to impose a settlement, and a board that will demand a written justification. I want a reusable, defensible procedure based on the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution.

For context, the KS solution works like this. Take the disagreement point d and the utopia point b, where b_i is the highest utility that party i could reach in some individually rational feasible agreement. Rescale each party's utility so that d_i becomes 0 and b_i becomes 1. The KS outcome is the feasible point of the form (t, t) with the largest t, converted back to the original utilities; equivalently, it is the Pareto-efficient point at which each party's gain over the disagreement point is the same fraction of that party's maximum possible gain.

Design the procedure as a numbered sequence of steps I can run in that single afternoon. For each step give: the exact question to ask or computation to perform; what counts as an adequate answer; what to do when an answer is missing or a director refuses to give one. Mark any step that is really a judgment call rather than a technical computation, and say whose judgment it should be. Say where the procedure must stop with no result, and where it hands the decision to me. Also say how to construct a convex feasible set when the parties can only describe finitely many discrete options, for example by time-sharing or lotteries, and how to handle a director who is risk-averse about lotteries.

Then apply the procedure to three cases:

(a) The only resource is $400,000, both groups have identical linear utility in dollars, but Group A has four times the staff and twice the recent publication output of Group B. Show what the procedure awards, and tell me what to say to Group A's director when they call the result absurd.

(b) The resources are the same $400,000 plus 200 instrument-hours. Group A cares mostly about money and little about instrument time; Group B is near a deadline and cares mostly about instrument time. Invent concrete utility functions for the two groups, justify them briefly, and show the arithmetic through the normalization and utopia-point steps.

(c) Group B's director refuses to assign utilities or trade-offs, arguing that no numerical score represents scientific value. Show exactly which step fails, what the status of the resulting allocation is if I supply the numbers myself, and whether the procedure can continue under its own rules.

Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do: where it will present a 50/50 split as a theorem even when that ignores need, track record, or size; where it will silently import my own guesses about the parties' utilities; which axioms of the solution I should be prepared to defend, including Pareto efficiency, symmetry, invariance under positive affine transformations of each side's utility, and the monotonicity condition KS uses in place of the Nash solution's independence of irrelevant alternatives; and the conditions under which I should prefer the Nash bargaining solution or a non-game-theoretic method instead.
