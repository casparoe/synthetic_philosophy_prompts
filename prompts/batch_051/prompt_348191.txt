The background: he's been doing small fire-pit nights in his backyard most Saturdays, burning scrap lumber and dead branches, and his neighbor's kid has asthma. At the party she told him he was imposing a health risk on her family without their consent. It went roughly like this.

Her: "That smoke doesn't stay in your yard. You're deciding your Saturday ambience is worth some chance of making my kid's lungs worse, and you never once asked. That's not yours to decide for us."

Him: "If I needed permission for every small risk I impose, I couldn't drive to work. Every time you drive you impose a risk on pedestrians and cyclists that they never consent to. Society only works because there are minor risk impositions we don't run past each other."

Her: "Driving is reciprocal. Roughly the same people who impose the risk also take it, and most of us depend on the system itself. There's no system here. You get the nice fire, and the smoke and whatever's in it lands on my family. Calling it 'minor' is just you deciding my kid's risk tolerance for me."

Him: "If reciprocity had to be that exact—like each act has to balance out for each neighbor—we'd all have vetoes over each other's leaf blowers, grills, and pizza deliveries. Living around other people just means accepting a shared background of small nuisances and small risks without explicit consent each time. A once-a-week fire is inside that background, or close enough. You'd draw the line at whatever you happened to hate."

Her: "But a background only counts as shared if there's some real 'sharing': people can leave, or there's a rule everybody signed onto. You don't get to unilaterally write a social contract and then tell me I'm bound by it. And it isn't one small event. It's fifty fires a year, for years. A risk that tiny, repeated forever, doesn't stay tiny—and I never get a re-vote."

Someone asked about the brisket around then, so it ended there.
