The prototype–exemplar debate in the psychology of concepts has run for nearly half a century without a clean resolution. Prototype theories say a concept is a summary representation of the central tendency of its instances: you classify something by its similarity to a stored prototype. Exemplar theories say a concept is the remembered collection of individual instances: you classify something by summed similarity to the stored exemplars. A third position, the theory theory, says both camps are asking the wrong question, because conceptual content comes from folk theories about a domain rather than from any isolated similarity computation. The prototype–exemplar dispute drove much of the experimental literature on category learning — artificial dot-pattern categories (random distortions of a prototype, learned with feedback), linearly separable versus nonlinear category structures, and the observation that items equally similar to the prototype can be classified differently when they sit in dense versus sparse regions of the training examples — but the field did not converge; in practice it mostly moved on to hybrid models rather than declaring a winner.

Diagnose this impasse. What kind of disagreement was being conducted? Consider as hypotheses: (a) it was a factual dispute about what is stored in long-term memory, which proved untestable with the available tools because the two model classes are flexible enough to approximate one another under many conditions; (b) it was a dispute about standards of model evaluation — quantitative fit versus parsimony and psychological plausibility — with no shared rule for deciding when extra parameters buy more than they cost; (c) it was a conflation of levels of analysis, so the two sides used the term concept at different levels (a computational question about what classification does, an algorithmic question about representations and processes, and a knowledge-level question about how concepts depend on theories); or (d) it was a largely verbal disagreement, with the two sides using storage and representation in incompatible senses. State which diagnosis, or which combination, best captures the structure of the dispute, and defend it by reference to what each side actually claimed and to the empirical results that were supposed to decide between them.

Then say what would count as progress under that diagnosis: a decisive experimental manipulation that breaks the mutual mimicry, a formal equivalence or non-equivalence result, a reformulation of the question, or an argument that no experiment could settle it. If you conclude the original question — what is stored in a concept? — was ill-posed from the start, say why and say what question should have been asked instead.
