I'm organizing a graduate reading group on agency and decision theory, and for our next meeting I circulated a short popular handout that one of the students recommended on the idea that "deliberation crowds out prediction." I'd like to use the handout as an exercise in reading charitably but critically, but I have a nagging sense that it compresses the view in ways a careful philosopher would resist — I just can't quite locate where the missteps are. The handout's core summary runs as follows:

"When you are genuinely deciding whether to do A or B, you cannot coherently assign a probability to the proposition that you will do A. To use such a probability in expected-value reasoning, you would have to treat your own future action as a state of the world, but your choice is exactly what is up to you, not an exogenous event to be predicted. Worse, if you assign, say, probability 0.7 to your choosing A, the assignment tends to make itself true — you have already half-decided — or else it is contentless, because a genuine chooser cannot have evidence about the output of a process she is currently running. So the moment you form a probability about your own act, you leave the deliberative stance and become a spectator of yourself, predicting a system rather than directing it. Decision theory is therefore strictly a third-person instrument: it can predict and explain other agents' choices, but it cannot be used by an agent in making her own choice."

My hunch is that the last two sentences in particular overreach — that the observation about what deliberation feels like or requires may not support the strong conclusion that decision theory is unavailable from the inside — but I am not sure whether the slide is real or merely apparent. I'd like you to audit this summary the way a philosopher of action or a decision theorist would. Please go through it claim by claim and distinguish what a defender of the "deliberation crowds out prediction" view would accept as accurate, what is a genuine point overstated until it becomes misleading, and what is simply confused or unsupported. I am especially interested in whether the argument runs together the absence of probabilities over one's own acts during deliberation with the impossibility of such probabilities in principle, and whether the closing claim about decision theory's scope actually follows from the accurate parts. I am not looking for citations or further reading; I want the substantive assessment itself, so I can bring it back to the group.
