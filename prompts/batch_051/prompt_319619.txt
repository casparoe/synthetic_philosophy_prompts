In a seminar on Abelard's ethics we have been working through his claim that sin does not reside in the outward deed, nor even in the bare impulse or desire, but solely in the act of consent by which the soul binds itself to what it knows it ought not to do. One of my friends has been pressing this harder than the rest of us, and he has assembled an argument to show that, taken seriously, the position has consequences few of its defenders would actually accept. I cannot find the way out of it, so I should like to know precisely where it goes wrong, if it does.

(1) If the gravity of a sin is fixed entirely by what the agent consents to, then two agents whose consents are identical in content are equally guilty, whatever happens afterwards in the world. (2) The person who fires and kills and the person who fires and misses only because the gun jams consent to exactly the same thing, namely the victim's death. (3) So attempted murder and completed murder are equal in guilt. (4) The same principle does not stop at attempts: someone who earnestly consents to a dozen murders, each one thwarted by accident, is guilty of a dozen murders. (5) A person who has never harmed, injured, or even endangered anyone can therefore be exactly as guilty as the most accomplished killer, purely in virtue of an interior act. (6) But an account of ethics according to which the distinction between the persistently thwarted would-be murderer and the successful killer, and between the attempt and the corpse, makes no difference to guilt has lost touch with the very distinctions that moral and legal judgement exist to draw. (7) Therefore Abelard's consent-only account of sin must be rejected.

I am not convinced my friend has shown what he thinks he has shown, but I cannot identify the step at which the argument overreaches. My own inclination is to hold on to (3), since Abelard's distinction between consent and deed seems deliberately to commit him to it. Which step should be refused, and why did that step look so secure in the first place? What does one have to pay, morally or legally, if one refuses it rather than accepting the conclusion? And is the move from deeds to attempts the same as the move from attempts to the dozen-thwarted murders, or does the argument do something new at that point?
