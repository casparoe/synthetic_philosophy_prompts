One of the engineers on my team works on emotion recognition in audio, and he has been pressing a view I would like pressure-tested. He holds that a passage of music is sad in roughly the way a sad person's voice is sad: the sadness is a set of acoustic and temporal features — slower tempo, lower pitch, descending contours, quieter dynamics, a darker or rougher timbre — and music borrows those features from the sound of human sadness. He sometimes describes the position as sad prosody with the speaker deleted. His argument is that, in the speech case, a detector can classify a voice as sad from the features alone without knowing anything about the speaker's inner state; by parity, he says, the features are all that is real about the sadness of music, and any appeal to an emotion in the music itself, or to a felt meaning behind the features, is sentimentality doing no explanatory work. His practical claim is that an emotion-recognition model trained purely on acoustic features could in principle capture everything there is to capture about sad music, with no additional semantic layer for emotion.

I want an audit of how far this analogy actually carries, not a plain yes or no. Please give me: (1) the correspondence table — feature by feature, what maps from a sad human voice, and, where relevant, from sad expressive behavior more generally, to the features of sad music; (2) the places where the mapping breaks; (3) a separation of the disanalogies that are fatal to his practical claim from the ones that are merely cosmetic; (4) a judgment on whether the debunking conclusion survives once those disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was quietly carrying it; and (5) if the voice analogy is the wrong tool for the job he is using it to do, a description of a better analogy, including what that replacement would and would not license.
