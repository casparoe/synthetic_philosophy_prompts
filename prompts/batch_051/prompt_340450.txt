You are an expert in ancient Greek epistemology and in designing facilitated activities that make a philosophical distinction felt before it is named. I am a novelist running a one-day craft seminar for twelve fiction writers, mostly mid-career, during a three-hour morning block. I want them to grasp, in their bones rather than by lecture, the difference Plato has Socrates draw near the end of the Meno between a true opinion and knowledge. The image I want the session built around is Socrates' comparison of true opinions to the statues of Daedalus: they are fine things while they remain, but they slip away unless they are fastened down. A right guess can guide a person as well as knowledge while it stays, but it is unstable, and it is not really the person's own until it is tied down by reasoning about why it is true.

For novelists, this is not merely academic. A character can arrive at the right decision for a shallow or accidental reason, and the scene feels unearned or brittle in revision. Another character can arrive at the same decision from a place of earned understanding, and the scene bears weight. I want the participants to experience both of those positions as makers, not just as readers. So the activity should induce the group to hold right answers that are not grounded, let those answers function for a while, and then expose what happens to them under pressure. Only after that should we attach the philosophical distinction.

Please design a runnable structured activity for this group and time. Do not open with lecture or with the term true opinion. Include rules, materials, roles if needed, a timeline, and a non-monetary scoring scheme. The key goal is that the idea show up in what happens: in the incentives, the failures, and the choices participants make. Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity to the distinction between right opinion and tied-down understanding, connecting each moment back to the Daedalus image and the Meno's question of why knowledge is more valuable than true opinion. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle that. And be frank about which feature of the Meno problem the activity cannot reproduce, such as the metaphysical claim that the fastening is explanatory or causal in a way no social game fully captures, and what I as facilitator will need to supply afterward. Start by brainstorming two or three possible mechanics, then choose one and defend the choice.
