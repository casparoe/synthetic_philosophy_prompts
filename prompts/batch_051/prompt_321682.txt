I am taking a course on chance and computation, and I am trying to get clear on what is being asserted when we call a finite binary sequence random. I would like a working definition of "random" as applied to finite sequences — or, if you think no such definition is possible, an explanation of what property we are really tracking when we use the word.

The definition should handle these cases:

1. 0000000000. I take this to be not random.
2. 0110101101, which I just produced by flipping a fair coin. I take this to be random.
3. The first million binary digits of π. These are generated by a deterministic rule, yet long stretches of them are commonly treated as random-looking in statistical practice.
4. A ten-bit string output by a pseudorandom number generator whose seed I do not know. The generating process is entirely deterministic.
5. Two tokens that both read 1001011011, one produced by a fair coin and the other chosen deliberately by a person. Do we get one random outcome, two, or none?
6. The one-bit string 1. Is asking whether it is random even well-posed?

Propose a definition, test it against each case, and then say whether the cases force you to revise the definition, abandon the search for a single definition, or accept a surprising verdict — and which case is doing the forcing.
