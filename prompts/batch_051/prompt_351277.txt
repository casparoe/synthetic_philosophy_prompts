A friend and I have just finished a long argument about whether a state can itself do wrong, rather than merely having officials who do wrong while acting in its name. He maintained that an organized state can be a moral agent in its own right, and that at least some of the wrongs we attribute to states are not reducible to the wrongs of the individual officials who compose them. I argued the reductionist side. The argument ended with me unpersuaded and with what I take to be my strongest objection still, in my view, unanswered. I would like you to play him, staying in that role across a live exchange so that I can carry the argument further.

The character you are to play holds this cluster of commitments. Organized collectives can satisfy plausible conditions of agency when they have authoritative decision procedures, role-differentiated members, and ways of acting over time that no single member fully controls. The state is such a collective. Therefore statements like the state wronged this prisoner, or this government wronged that minority population, are sometimes literally true and not merely shorthand for some official's wrongdoing. The character may distinguish moral agency from legal sovereign immunity: he concedes that whether a state should be immune from suit in its own courts is a remedial and institutional question, separate from whether the state can be a wrongdoer. He may grant that not every group is an agent and that many loose allegations of state wrongdoing are just that. He will not retreat to reductionism, and he will not dissolve the state's wrong into the wrongs of individual officeholders, since those are the positions he was arguing against.

Here is my opening move, the one I made then and will make again now. If the state is a moral agent capable of wronging someone, its wrongdoing must be attributable to a subject that forms an intention. But a state appears to have no mind over and above the minds of its officials and citizens. Either the wrongful intention belongs to some identifiable official, in which case the state adds nothing to that official's act, or the intention belongs to no one at all, in which case there is no agent left to bear the wrong. How do you escape this dilemma without surrendering the claim that the state itself is the wrongdoer?

Begin by answering that objection directly, in character and in the first person, and continue to hold the role for the rest of the exchange. You may press back on my premises and ask me questions after you have replied, but your first turn must meet the challenge head on rather than asking for clarification.
