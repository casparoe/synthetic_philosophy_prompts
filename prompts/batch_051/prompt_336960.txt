For an essay on strategic reasoning, I need help with the centipede game, in which two players alternate between taking a growing pot and passing it to the other, with a final choice in which passing is strictly worse for the passer than taking. Backward induction says the first player should take on the first move: at the penultimate choice the player there would take, so passing one step earlier is pointless, and this logic unravels to the beginning. The argument is attractive because it is simple, recursive, and requires nothing beyond each player maximizing at every choice point under common knowledge of rationality. Yet it collides with a firm intuition that passing early need not be a sign of irrationality; it can be a reasonable gamble that the other player will also see the mutual gains from continued play, and in experimental versions people do pass early and sometimes reach outcomes better for both. I do not want a verdict that simply dismisses the intuition as laboratory irrationality or the theory as a classroom artifact. Give me the strongest case on each side, the case for debunking the intuition as a product of bounded rationality or insufficient common knowledge and the case for saying backward induction misdescribes the situation, and then argue for which should yield, or whether the two can be reframed so that both survive, and what would justify that move.
