Question: Courts and theorists often resist the thought that bare statistical evidence can satisfy a legal burden of proof, even when the relevant probability seems high. Explain the standard hypotheticals used to press this idea, and assess whether this resistance reflects a rational epistemic principle or a legal convention. In particular, consider whether the phenomenon is the same under the civil preponderance standard and the criminal beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard.

Student answer: The two core cases concern the civil standard. In the blue bus case, a pedestrian is struck by a bus in a town where 80% of the buses are operated by the Blue Bus Company. With no other identifying evidence, the probability that a Blue Bus vehicle was responsible is 80%, which exceeds the civil preponderance standard; yet the plaintiff is generally thought to lose, because the evidence describes a class rather than this particular accident. The gatecrasher case illustrates the same point differently. If 499 of 1,000 attendees at a rodeo paid for admission and one attendee is selected at random, that attendee is 50.1% likely not to have paid. That technically exceeds the civil standard, but the suit is generally thought to fail because the evidence is statistical and the margin is extremely thin. Together these cases show that legal proof requires more than a bare statistical probability: it requires individualized evidence linking the defendant to the particular event. This is a rational epistemic principle, not merely a legal convention. A fact-finder who knows only base rates about a class does not know whether the defendant actually did the act in question, so a verdict would outrun the evidence. The same principle applies in the criminal context under the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard; only the probability threshold is higher.
