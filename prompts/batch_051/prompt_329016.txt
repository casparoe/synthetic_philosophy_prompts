I work as a biostatistician, mostly on observational studies where selection bias and censoring are the usual suspects, and I have no formal background in philosophy of science. A colleague recently pressed Hempel's raven paradox on me as though it showed something deep about how evidence works. I follow the surface: the generalization that all ravens are black is logically equivalent to the generalization that all non-black things are non-ravens, so any non-black non-raven—a green apple, say—ought to count as confirming evidence for the second generalization and therefore for the first, which sounds like a reductio. I want you to translate the paradox, and the standard moves in the debate around it, into the vocabulary I actually work in: sample spaces, sampling designs and selection mechanisms, likelihoods and likelihood ratios, alternative hypotheses, and conditioning on how an observation got into the dataset. Take the claim that a positive instance confirms a universal generalization and restate it as a precise claim about what an observation does to belief under a specified data-generating process. Where does the green-apple inference break down—is it a biased draw, an observation from the wrong reference class, an implicit shift in the alternative hypothesis, or a likelihood that changes direction once the design is fixed?

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the paradox translate cleanly into this statistical vocabulary, which parts translate only with distortion, and which parts have no expression in it at all? And is what fails to translate a real problem about confirmation that my vocabulary is too coarse to see, or is it an artifact of not having conditioned on the sampling scheme in the first place? My working suspicion is that the puzzle mostly dissolves once you specify how the observations were collected, but I have the feeling that conclusion is too quick.
