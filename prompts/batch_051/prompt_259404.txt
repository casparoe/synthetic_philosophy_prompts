I have no formal training in economics or demography, but for the past few years I have been reading on my own about population, resources, and the long argument over Malthus, and what keeps bothering me is not the question of whether he was right or wrong but the fact that the argument never seems to settle. The same two positions get reoccupied in almost the same shape generation after generation. On one side: Malthus was simply mistaken, because for two centuries population has grown enormously and food output per person has risen along with it, so his prediction of mass immiseration failed; he badly underestimated technology, trade, institutions, and the demographic transition. On the other side: Malthus was right but early, because he identified a real pressure between population and finite resources, the long run is still not over, and today's environmental and resource constraints are just his checks returning under new names. The phrase right but early is used by some people as a vindication and attacked by others as a way of making the theory unfalsifiable, as though it can never actually lose.

I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, rather than a verdict about Malthus. Is it a factual disagreement about which evidence should carry the most weight? A disagreement about what the theory actually claimed — a law, a tendency, a conditional warning — since his essay changed across editions and readers have emphasized different parts of it? A disagreement about standards of evidence, in particular about whether a long-run prediction can be tested before the long run is finished, and how bad a record of failed predictions would have to be before a defender would stop saying early? Or are the two sides answering different questions under one name, with one asking whether Malthus's stated mechanisms predicted the course history actually took and the other asking whether there is some real tension between finite resources and population? I am also unsure whether calling a theory right but early is best understood as a claim about its truth, its usefulness, or something else entirely. My own working suspicion is that much of the impasse comes from treating the theory sometimes as a prediction about a specific historical trajectory and sometimes as a standing constraint on any possible society, but I do not know how to make that distinction precise or whether making it precise would actually move the debate.

Please base your diagnosis on what each side actually says, and then say what would count as progress, given that diagnosis — a distinction, a test, a change of question — or whether no progress should be expected and why. And please be explicit about what would change your mind about the diagnosis, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
