In a course on early Christian exegesis, we spent most of a seminar on the twenty-first chapter of the Gospel of John, where the risen Jesus directs the disciples to cast the net and the narrator records the exact size of the catch. The King James Version renders John 21:11 this way:

"Simon Peter went up, and drew the net to land full of great fishes, an hundred and fifty and three: and for all there were so many, yet was not the net broken."

Our tutor offered a standard symbolic reading: 153 is the triangular number of 17, since it is the sum of the integers from one to seventeen; 17 can then be split into 10 and 7, the number of the commandments and a number associated in the tradition with completeness or with the seven gifts of the Spirit. On this reading the catch gathers the fullness of law and Spirit into one unbroken net, the Church. The reading had real force in the room, and nobody wanted to surrender it. But on the way home I built the following argument, and I cannot decide which premise to abandon.

(1) If that reading of the 153 fish is justified, it rests on a warrant of roughly this form: a number's having a striking arithmetic property is evidence that the text uses the number symbolically. (2) That warrant is far too permissive, because every natural number has some striking arithmetic property, or can quickly be given one: even or odd, prime or composite, triangular or square, a sum of two squares, a product of numbers already marked as significant nearby, and so on. (3) So the warrant implies that every number in the Gospel is evidence of symbolic intent, and an interpreter who applies the warrant consistently must treat every count and measurement as fair game. (4) Yet no serious interpreter does treat every number in these narratives as symbolic; some numbers are plainly ordinary counts or distances. (5) Therefore either the warrant fails, in which case the reading of the 153 fish loses whatever supported it, or interpreters are being arbitrary when they stop at 153 and not at the next number, and the idea of a symbolic number becomes empty.

I would far rather keep the symbolic reading of the 153 fish and reject the conclusion that every number is fair game, but I want to know what I am paying for that choice. Where does the argument go wrong? If it is valid, which premise is the least costly to give up? Whatever step I reject, it seems to do real work elsewhere in the exegetical habits I am being trained into.
