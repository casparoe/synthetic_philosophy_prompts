I have a standing epistemic rule: once I take myself to know something on the basis of solid evidence or proof, I do not lower my confidence when apparent counterevidence turns up unless the new evidence is itself so strong and specific that it would be irrational not to reopen the question. In ordinary cases — a conflicting anecdote, a small exploratory study, an objection I can already see a reply to — I classify the counterevidence as misleading and move on. I adopted the rule because otherwise I would be endlessly reopening settled questions and would never retain knowledge.

A conclusion Saul Kripke draws in his paper Two Paradoxes of Knowledge seems to support the rule. He points out, roughly, that if one knows a proposition, then one should also take any evidence against that proposition to be misleading; otherwise one would not count as knowing it after all. On that way of thinking, refusing to be moved by counterevidence is not necessarily a bias but part of what knowing requires. Yet the same paper exposes my worry. The policy looks exactly like what a dogmatist would do: a person with a false belief and the same subjective confidence could adopt the same rule, dismiss the decisive objection as misleading, and never correct himself. So either my rule is rationally indefensible, or there must be a way to state it that privileges genuine knowledge over mere conviction — and I cannot see what that would amount to from the inside.

- The puzzle, restated: identify exactly where the tension lies between retaining knowledge and avoiding dogmatism, as I have set it up. (~150 words)
- The case for the rule: the strongest arguments that something like my rule is not merely permissible but required for rational knowledge retention, including the Kripkean line that knowing a proposition commits one to regarding counterevidence against it as misleading. (~300 words)
- The case against the rule: the strongest arguments that the rule as stated collapses into dogmatism, licenses motivated reasoning, and makes error correction practically impossible. (~300 words)
- The salvage attempt: propose the best available version of the rule — however qualified or restructured — that keeps the benefit of not reopening every settled question while denying the dogmatist a license. Be specific about what conditions, if any, would distinguish the knower from the merely convinced person in practice. (~350 words)
- Verdict: whether my rule, as I actually stated it, is defensible, and whether the salvaged version is something I can follow without already knowing whether I know, or only a description of how knowledge would look from the outside. (~150 words)
