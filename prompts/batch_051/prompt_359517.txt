I've spent enough years on rationalist and EA forums to have seen Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" used over and over as a compressed trade-off problem: one child's misery as the price of a utopia, and the question of whether you may take the bargain. I'd like to set that framing aside and ask for a close reading of the story through the older logic it seems to me to be in dialogue with — ritual substitution and the transfer of guilt: the scapegoat and sacrifice tradition in which a community's sin or impurity is placed on a victim who is then killed or driven out, and the community is cleansed by the transaction.

Please read the story through that lens, and hold both the story and the sacrificial logic to account, being explicit about where they diverge. In particular:

1. In the Leviticus scapegoat rite and in Girard-style accounts of the scapegoat mechanism, the victim is cast as guilty or impurity-bearing, and the violence against it restores communal order partly because the community can believe in that guilt. Omelas appears to keep the mechanism while dropping the mystification: the citizens know the child is innocent, know they are complicit, and accept it anyway. Is the child still functioning as a scapegoat under those conditions, and what happens to the concept once the community no longer believes the victim deserves it?

2. Is anything actually transferred to the child — guilt, impurity, the community's bad conscience — or is the child better described as a standing condition of a bargain, a utility source, or the victim of a substitution with no expiatory content? The story presents the child's abjection as the condition of the city's flourishing: is that condition causal, contractual, or magical, and how much turns on the distinction for whether this counts as sacrifice at all?

3. Where does this lens illuminate details the utilitarian reading misses, and where does the story resist the lens? I'm especially interested in the ones who walk away: are they the refusal of the sacrifice, a self-exclusion from its benefits, or themselves a kind of reverse scapegoating — and does the story's ending, in which they head toward something undescribed that may not exist, undercut or complete the sacrificial reading?

I'm writing an essay for a rationalist-adjacent audience, so I want less a survey of positions in the theory of sacrifice than a defended, close reading: what the story adds to the theory of scapegoating that the theory alone does not give us, and what the theory reveals in the story that the standard trade-off reading misses.
