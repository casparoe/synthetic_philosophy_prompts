It's common in historical-Jesus work to use something called the criterion of embarrassment. The rough idea: if the early Christian movement preserved a story that would have been awkward for it — Jesus being baptized by John (which seems to rank John above him, or at least implies Jesus had something to repent of), Peter denying Jesus, the disciples never understanding the parables, the crucifixion itself — then the story probably wasn't invented, because a movement trying to win converts wouldn't make up material that made its founder and its leaders look bad. So embarrassment gets treated as evidence of authenticity.

What I want is an essay that treats this criterion as a practice with a hidden theory inside it, and digs that theory out. What does the criterion presuppose about how the early tradition actually worked? For instance, does it presuppose that the tradition was controlled enough that embarrassing material could only survive because it was too widely known to suppress — and is that a different picture of the tradition than the one assumed by the criterion of multiple attestation (independent streams) or the criterion of dissimilarity (the movement was willing to invent material that fit its theology)? Spell out the implicit commitments about memory, motive, and transmission that using the criterion of embarrassment commits a scholar to, and separate the commitments the criterion strictly requires from the ones it merely happens to fit with. Then say where the practice is underdetermined: what rival pictures of the early tradition would all still license using the criterion of embarrassment, and where would they part ways? And one more wrinkle: does the criterion quietly assume that we, from this distance, can reliably tell what would have been embarrassing to a first-century group — and how costly is that assumption if we can't?
