I'm giving a presentation next month to my high school's psychology and robotics club, as part of an independent project in phenomenology, on Maurice Merleau-Ponty's account of the lived body and motor intentionality. The thesis I'm defending is roughly this: skilled action — catching a ball, playing an instrument, weaving through a crowded hallway — is not best explained as the brain computing an internal model of the task and then issuing commands to a passive body. The lived body is already geared into a meaningful world before deliberate planning: the hallway already shows up as traversable, the cup already solicits a particular grip. On this view, the brain modulates an active body that is already in contact with meaningful situations; it does not supply all the intelligence from inside while the body merely executes.

I need to be ready for the discussion period. The audience will be mostly students who have taken AP Psychology, plus several robotics students. They are likely to assume that the brain is doing the real cognitive work, that movement must be planned by internal representations, and that phrases like a body that understands its surroundings are either metaphor or mysticism. They care about experimental evidence and about whether an account can be implemented and tested.
