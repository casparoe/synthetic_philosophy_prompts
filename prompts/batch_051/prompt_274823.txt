Been chewing on a parallel between Arrow's impossibility theorem and the CAP theorem in distributed systems, and I genuinely can't tell whether it's a real structural identity or just the fact that both are "you can't have everything" results.

Arrow says: take any rule that maps a profile of complete, transitive individual rankings over three or more alternatives to a social ranking, and require unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship. No such rule exists — in fact, if you keep the first three, the only survivors are dictatorial rules. CAP says something superficially similar: a replicated data store can't have consistency (reads reflect the latest write), availability (every request gets an answer), and partition tolerance (it keeps working when the network splits) all at once. The slogan is "pick two of three," though I know the precise statement is subtler than the slogan.

The mapping I keep toying with is this. Voters are like nodes. A preference profile is like the set of local states after some sequence of writes. The social welfare function is like the protocol that has to turn those local states into a single global answer. Pareto is like "if every node saw a write, the answer reflects it." IIA is like "the answer for a node's value depends only on writes relevant to that value, not on unrelated traffic" — and that last one is where I'm least confident. Non-dictatorship would be "no single node's state always wins."

My worry is that Arrow is about aggregating conflict: different voters rank the same alternatives in opposite orders, while CAP is about replicating one fact: nodes don't rank candidate values, they just have possibly stale copies. That seems like a deep disanalogy, unless a stale node and an up-to-date node "disagree" in a degenerate two-value way that's enough to carry the analogy.

What would have to be true on the distributed-systems side before an Arrow-style dictator shows up as a theorem rather than as a design choice? And here's a candidate break: Arrow hands you a residual degenerate solution — the dictator satisfies the other three conditions — whereas CAP doesn't hand you a degenerate system that satisfies all three, it just says no system exists. Is that because one theorem is about aggregating opinions and the other about replicating facts, or is it an artifact of the formalizations? If the parallel were deep, something should transfer from one side to the other; name one prediction it makes that comes out false, and say whether that kills the analogy or just narrows it.
