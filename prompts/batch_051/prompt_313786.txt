Walter Bagehot argued in The English Constitution that the British monarchy survives in a democratic age by being separated from the practical machinery of government: one part of the constitution attracts loyalty and reverence, while the other part, less visible and less venerated, actually governs. Modern constitutional practice has inherited that division. Yet it also preserves a set of reserve powers for the monarch—refusing royal assent, dismissing a ministry, dissolving parliament—whose entire rationale is that they would be used in some extreme constitutional emergency. The same commentators who insist that a monarch must never actually block legislation or displace a government will often defend the existence of the reserve powers as an essential safeguard. I have never heard anyone argue that a monarch should accept an invitation to veto legislation, even in a crisis. Is there a principled asymmetry between maintaining an unused power as a safeguard and endorsing its use, or is this an inconsistency that constitutional monarchy has simply decided to live with? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the hardest case: a questionable but not clearly illegal power grab by a prime minister or parliamentary majority that only formal royal action could stop.
