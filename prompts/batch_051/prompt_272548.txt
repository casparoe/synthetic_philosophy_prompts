I got roped into leading a small group on the Trinity a few weeks back and I'm in over my head. One of the members, a math guy, stayed after last night and said the doctrine can't even be stated without a logical error. His argument, cleaned up:

(1) The Father is God.
(2) The Son is God.
(3) The Father is not the Son.
(4) If "is God" in (1) and (2) means identity, then by symmetry and transitivity the Father is the Son.
(5) So, given (3), an orthodox Trinitarian has to say "is God" does not mean identity there.
(6) But if "is God" doesn't mean identity, the only thing left is ordinary predication — the Father and the Son each have the property of being divine.
(7) And that just says three persons share a property, which is compatible with there being three gods and gives up the claim that each person just is God.
(8) So either the Trinity contradicts itself on the identity reading, or it stops being the claim that the Father and Son are each God on the predication reading.

I've got a nagging feeling that (6) is smuggling something in, but I cant say what without sounding like I'm special pleading.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails, and under what formalization? On the predication reading, is there genuinely no way to say each person is God without it collapsing into "each person is divine"? When you give your verdict, tell me which consideration would make you flip it and which part of your own analysis you're least sure about. Keep it under 500 words.
