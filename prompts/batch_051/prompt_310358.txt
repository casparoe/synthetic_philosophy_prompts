In Book II, Chapter IX, Section 8 of An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, John Locke writes:

“When we set before our eyes a round globe of any uniform colour, v.g. gold, alabaster, or jet, it is certain that the idea thereby imprinted on our mind is of a flat circle, variously shadowed, with several degrees of light and brightness coming to our eyes.”

Locke then argues that judgment, by habitual custom, alters this flat, shadowed appearance into the perception of a convex body, and in the same section he introduces the question Molyneux put to him: whether a man born blind and made to see, who had learned by touch to distinguish a cube from a sphere, could tell which was which by sight alone before touching them. I want to know how one could actually put a version of Molyneux’s question to empirical test, rather than treating it as a piece of armchair psychology. What would a sound experimental design have to control for in order to distinguish genuine cross-modal transfer from rapid learning or from the use of cues such as contours, shading, and motion? What pattern of results would count in favour of the man’s being able to identify the shapes, and what pattern would count against? And even after such a test, what part of the original dispute between Locke and his opponents would remain undecided by any bare behavioral outcome—for instance, whether the issue is about the unity of spatial perception, the nature of judgment, or the difference between innate structure and acquired calibration?
