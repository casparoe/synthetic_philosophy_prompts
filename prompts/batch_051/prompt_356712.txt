Write a dialogue between two moral philosophers who disagree about what gives an action moral worth. One holds a broadly Kantian view: an action has moral worth only when it is done from the motive of duty, that is, because the agent judges the action to be right or required. The other holds that an action can have full moral worth when the agent is moved directly by the features that make the action right—for example, that it relieves someone’s pain—without needing to represent the action to herself as right or required.

Have them focus on a concrete case: a person who was raised to believe that helping members of a particular group is forbidden, but who, when face to face with one of them in urgent need, helps anyway out of humane sympathy, while continuing to believe that she has done something wrong. They should also discuss the person who helps his spouse out of love rather than duty, and the charge that the motive-of-duty view inserts a superfluous thought between a good person and the person she helps.

The two should press each other on questions such as these: whether the wrong-believing helper is creditworthy for responding to the features that genuinely make her act right even though she does not see her act as right; whether moral worth depends on an agent’s own conception of what duty requires or on the objective reasons to which she responds; whether taking rightness itself as one’s motive can be empty or self-congratulatory if the agent cannot identify the particular things that make the act right; and whether the motive of duty is needed precisely for cases in which sentiment and love fail.

Neither character should be a strawman. Each should restate the other’s view in its strongest form and respond to the actual points raised rather than talking past them. They may reach partial agreement, but the core disagreement should remain substantive and unresolved.
