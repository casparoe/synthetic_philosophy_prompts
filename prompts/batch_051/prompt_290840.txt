For a seminar I am putting together on the epistemology of mysticism, I want the standard to come before the evaluation. What would a theory of ineffability actually have to get right? In the literature people tend to argue over whether a given apophatic text is coherent — whether, say, the claim that the One is beyond being is literally assertible — by pulling on a single example, and I want the full list of requirements first.

Write the desiderata that a philosophical theory of ineffability must meet, where a theory here means a secular, philosophically precise account of what apophatic practitioners are doing and claiming when they say that the object of their practice — God, the One, emptiness, the Dao — cannot be said. The theory need not be an apologetic and need not preserve the truth of the practice's claims, but it must explain the practice as a going concern.

For each requirement, give the consideration or motivating case behind it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or a demand of a particular camp, naming the camp. The list should at least engage with the following, though you should add anything else that matters:

- the paradox of utterance: an assertion that X is unsayable seems to say something about X, so a theory has to avoid making the assertion self-refuting without draining it of content;
- the difference between ineffability and merely lacking words at present, or never having found them, with a criterion that keeps the two apart;
- the difference between a limitation in our epistemic or linguistic equipment and a claim about the object itself, including the negative-theological move from our inability to describe to the conclusion that the object exceeds all finite categories;
- the fact that the unsayable gets talked about at enormous length — negations, metaphors, corrections, instructions to pass beyond words — so a theory must say what all that speech is doing if it is not describing;
- whether ineffable knowledge is a coherent notion given a standard propositional account of knowledge, and if it is coherent, whether it should be reconstructed as acquaintance, practical knowledge, or something else;
- whether ineffability attaches to the experience, to the object, or to the content, and why the difference matters;
- whether the negation used in negative theology is ordinary propositional negation or something weaker, such as a corrective or an approximation;
- whether the contrast between saying and showing, and the claim that what cannot be said can nevertheless be shown, can be made precise;
- whether ineffability claims are descriptions of a state of affairs or normative restrictions on what may be asserted, and which kinds of negative theology require which;
- how far the requirements differ across the apophatic traditions people actually cite, so that the theory neither homogenizes them nor overgeneralizes from one tradition.

Then say which single requirement does the most to exclude the popular candidate accounts now on the table, and — this is the part I most want — whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. If, for example, the paradox of utterance, strict unsayability, and the knowledge requirement cannot all be met, tell me whether that is a reductio of the concept of ineffability or a sign that my requirement list is misdrawn. Do not score each existing theory in detail; the criteria are the deliverable, not the ranking of contenders.

I am not asking for a reading list or citations; engage the issues directly.
