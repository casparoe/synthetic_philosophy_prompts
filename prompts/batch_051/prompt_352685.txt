I've been in an epistemology reading group on peer disagreement, and last week someone ended the whole dispute with a one-liner that's been bugging me since. We were trying to figure out whether you should move your credence when you find out that an epistemic peer—someone just as smart, just as well-informed, looking at the same evidence—has come to the opposite conclusion. One camp said you'd have to lower your confidence, maybe all the way to suspension; the other said you can just stick to your guns. Then somebody shrugged and said "reasonable people can disagree," as if that answered the question. I can't shake the feeling that this sentence is doing way too much work. It could be making a boring point about how being reasonable doesn't guarantee being right, or an interesting and controverisal point about how the same evidence can justify more than one doxastic attitude, or just a social suggestion to stop arguing. And only some of those readings would actually block conciliationism. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that "reasonable people can disagree" might be expressing, say which are platitudes and which are substantive—and whether any are outright false—and then tell me which readings would actually support a steadfast response to peer disagreement and which would leave conciliationism untouched? I'd also like to know whether using the slogan to end that argument was just question-begging against the conciliationist.
