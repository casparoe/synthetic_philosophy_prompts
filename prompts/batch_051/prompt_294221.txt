I run a philosophy club at my high school, with about fifteen members, and I can have seventy-five minutes for a single meeting. I would like an activity on performative speech: the fact that some utterances do not describe how things stand but constitute actions—promising, betting, resigning, naming, giving permission—and that whether such an utterance performs its act depends on conditions that lie outside the words themselves, such as who speaks, in what setting, under what procedure, to whom, and whether the other party takes the utterance up.

I want the members to encounter this phenomenon before anyone names it, through an actual game or role-play rather than a lecture. Please design the activity itself: the fictional situation participants are acting within, the roles or cards assigned to each person, the exact formulas or utterances they are allowed or required to use, the rounds, and a point or token system that gives real incentives without involving money. The rules should be set up so that the same sentence predictably succeeds in some hands or settings and fails in others, and so that some attempts fail outright while others succeed but strike everyone as defective or hollow. The activity should also let the group feel a three-way split: the literal words used, the act done in using them, and the effect the act has on the other participants.

Afterward, I want a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the distinctions at stake—for example, a moment where an utterance changed the shared situation, a moment where the same words did nothing because the speaker lacked the right standing, a moment where they failed because the wrong procedure or formula was used, and a moment where the act came off but the audience suspected insincerity. I would like the standard vocabulary, such as performative utterance, felicity conditions, and the word/act/effect distinction, introduced only after those patterns have been felt, and I would like the discussion to end by connecting the game to ordinary cases: apologizing, resigning, being given a grade, someone saying a meeting is adjourned.

Finally, please tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it, and be explicit about which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator will have to supply—for instance, whatever depends on private states of mind rather than on what is publicly shown in the game.
