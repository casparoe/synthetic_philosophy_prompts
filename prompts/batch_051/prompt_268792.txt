After a long career as a field entomologist, I have recently turned to philosophy of mind, and I keep coming back to C. Lloyd Morgan’s An Introduction to Comparative Psychology (1894). I have agreed to give short opening remarks at our natural history society’s Animal Behaviour evening, where a couple of academic philosophers will be present among mostly retired naturalists and serious amateurs. The speech is to be about five minutes, and I would like it to have its centre of gravity in a passage from the third chapter, Other Minds than Ours. As I read it, Morgan there states his well-known caution that an action must not be interpreted as the outcome of a higher psychical faculty if it can be interpreted as the outcome of one lower in the psychological scale, and he then turns to the problem of how we have any basis for such an attribution at all, proposing what he calls ejective inference. Please write the speech so that it works out this passage rather than merely quoting it. In particular, the speech should give a non-crude reading of higher and lower, so that the canon is not presented as a blanket refusal to attribute mind to animals; it should briefly test the canon’s own reliance on ejective inference, allowing the audience to feel that our knowledge of other minds is conjectural from the start; and it should close by framing the evening’s reports from members as occasions for disciplined inference, not for debunking. The register should suit a natural history society: warm, exact, and not a lecture.
