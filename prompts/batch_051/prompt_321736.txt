I cover science and ideas for a general readership, and I have been trying to understand a dispute that has looked frozen for a long time. In its standard form: choose between a world with a moderately large population in which every life flourishes and a vastly larger world in which each life is only barely worth living but the sum total of well-being is larger. One side says the second world is better because it contains more well-being; the other says the first is better because each life in it goes better. Those who favor the second world are accused of endorsing a result many people find monstrous. Those who favor the first world are accused of the mirror implication that adding a happy person, or a person whose life is good but slightly below the current average, can by itself make a world worse. Formal refinements have accumulated, but the underlying intuitions have barely moved.

What puzzles me is that the same tension appears in ordinary moral thinking in ways the academic exchange rarely examines. Most people judge that four happy children are not worse than two merely because parental attention is spread thinner. Many of the same people judge that a school should not add a tenth struggling pupil to a class of nine, even if each pupil stays above a decent threshold and total learning in the room would rise. City dwellers complain that growth makes their city worse even while favoring a larger national population. Conservation-minded people often praise a small, flourishing population and abundance alike, depending on whether they are thinking of a habitat, a species, or the biosphere.

My hypothesis is that this is several disagreements held together by one word, better: whether value attaches to persons or to worlds, whether merely possible people count, whether there is a quality floor below which extra lives should not be added, and whether ordinary family and city judgments are tracking value at all rather than social roles, scarcity, or habituation. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Why has the debate stalled, and what kind of disagreement is it really: verbal or substantive? Do the sides differ over facts, standards of evidence, which cases count as data, or starting intuitions neither can argue for? Which hidden question is doing the most work? And given your diagnosis: what would count as progress, such as a distinction, an empirical finding, a formal result, or a change of question, or is progress not to be expected, and why?
