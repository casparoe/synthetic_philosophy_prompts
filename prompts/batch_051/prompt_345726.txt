Something I keep noticing: dictionaries, grammar checkers, and language textbooks all talk as if each word, in each sentence, has exactly one correct part of speech. "Before" is a preposition in "before the game" but an adverb in "I'd seen it before," and dictionaries list these as distinct entries; part-of-speech taggers inside search engines and autocorrect assign one tag to every occurrence word by word; fifth graders get graded on labelling the parts of speech in a sentence as if there's a right answer. But then there are the words that won't stay put. "Worth" is a preposition in some dictionaries and an adjective in others. "Near" is an adjective in "the near side" and a preposition in "near the door." "Fun" was long treated as a noun but people now happily say "a very fun party." "-ing" forms get called gerunds in one framework and participles in another. Cross-linguistically it gets worse: Japanese adjectives behave more like verbs, Chinese words translated as "prepositions" often behave like verbs, and Latin bundles into case endings what English does with prepositions or word order.

What I'd like to know is what picture of grammatical categories this whole sprawling practice presupposes. My guess is that it presupposes parts of speech are real, discrete, maybe universal joints in the structure of any language — natural kinds. But is that actually presupposed, and where? Or is the practice compatible with thinking of parts of speech as just a useful labeling scheme? And are the different parts of the practice — dictionaries, taggers, school grammar — consistent with each other in what they assume, or do they quietly assume different things? Then the part I'd actually use: if someone took seriously the view that parts of speech are language-specific constructions, or family-resemblance clusters held together by prototypes, what would have to change about how dictionaries, taggers, and grammar lessons get made? Don't just tell me the practice fits better with one theory; tell me what it strictly commits someone to. Please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among linguists and philosophers here, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
