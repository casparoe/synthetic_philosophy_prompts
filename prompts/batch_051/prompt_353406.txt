I have recently been re-reading Gaunilo's In Behalf of the Fool in preparation for a reading group on Anselm's Proslogion. Gaunilo makes a general objection that has come to feel more pressing to me than the famous island parody itself. He writes: "Could it not with equal justice be said that I have in my understanding all manner of unreal objects, having absolutely no existence in themselves, because I understand these things if one speaks of them, whatever they may be?" He then applies this to the lost island, which is more excellent than every other land but which no one thinks exists merely because of that. Here is what puzzles me. In the way this material is taught and argued, Anselm's proof about that-than-which-nothing-greater-can-be-conceived is treated as a serious argument whose flaw must be precisely located, while Gaunilo's island is treated as an obviously decisive parody. But if Gaunilo's objection is sound, it seems to undercut both equally—or if it fails, it fails for a reason that should also tell us something about the island. Is there any principled justification for this asymmetry in how the two moves are received, or is it merely a difference in what we antecedently care about? Give the strongest candidate distinction, test it against nearby cases such as a perfect cup of coffee or a maximal house, and honestly state whether the asymmetry should survive.
