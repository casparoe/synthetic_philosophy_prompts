I have been puzzling for a while over something I have seen called the paradox of deontology, and a recent conversation with a friend who builds risk-modeling software for a public agency made it feel much less like an abstract game. As I understand it, the paradox starts from a simple observation. If a certain kind of act is genuinely wrong—say, deliberately killing an innocent person—then in one important respect it would be better for there to be fewer acts of that kind. Yet deontologists typically insist that I may not commit one such act myself even in order to prevent several other people from committing the same kind of act. That seems to put the prohibition in tension with itself: the very act that would minimize the total number of wrongs is the one I am forbidden to perform, and being moral may require leaving the world with more wrongs in it than it would otherwise contain.

What my friend pointed out is that this kind of choice is increasingly made upstream, before any particular emergency exists. Institutions now design policies—often implemented in software—that determine, across a whole population of future and not-yet-identified cases, how many violations of a given constraint will occur, with probabilities attached. The designer is not looking at a named victim or a concrete moment; she is choosing a rule that will predictably produce a certain number of wrongful acts over time. Suppose such a designer faces the same structural choice the paradox describes: adopt a policy that will generate one instance of the wrong kind of act, in order to prevent several other instances of that same kind. The numbers are known in advance, but no individual who will be harmed or spared has yet been identified.

What I want to understand is whether the original paradox survives this change. Does it get sharper, because the policy designer is now doing deliberately and at scale what the individual is forbidden to do once? Does it dissolve, because the designer's relation to the eventual victims is too attenuated, or because what she is choosing is a rule rather than an act? Or does the paradox remain intact but reveal that its real load-bearing premise is something other than the one usually emphasized—for example, the agent's relation to a particular person at a particular time, rather than the mere number of violations? I am less interested in what the institution should actually do than in what happens to the paradox once the choice is moved from the moment of action to the design of a policy.
