I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been trying to pin down a pattern I see repeatedly in operational work — reading dashboards, error logs, and incident postmortems — but I don't have a precise name for it, and I'd rather have the concept carved at the joints than just labeled.

The pattern runs like this. A team notices a striking coincidence: two services that are supposed to be independent fail within the same minute; a rare data-quality defect appears on exactly the day a customer reports it; a metric that normally sits in a narrow band spikes at the same moment as an unrelated deploy. Someone then estimates how unlikely that particular pairing is under the assumption of independence, finds the probability tiny, and concludes that the coincidence is too improbable to be chance, so there must be a shared cause. Often there is no shared cause, and the team spends days investigating a phantom.

I know the birthday paradox: with 23 people in a room, the probability that some pair shares a birthday is already better than even, although any specific pair has only about a 1-in-365 chance of matching. The mistake I'm seeing seems to have the same shape. I suspect the relevant question is not "how unlikely was this exact coincidence?" but "how likely was it that some coincidence at least this striking would turn up somewhere in the system, given how many events, pairs, and time windows we observe?" And there seems to be a second layer: the reason we are asking about this event at all is that it was surprising enough to catch our attention, so our attention is being sampled on the condition that something surprising happened.

I can see that this is adjacent to the multiple-comparisons problem and to data dredging, but it doesn't seem identical. In multiple comparisons there is a formal procedure testing many hypotheses, and the correction is to adjust thresholds. Here there is usually no formal test at all — just a person looking at a dashboard and having their eye caught. It also resembles the move of drawing a target around wherever an arrow happened to land, but the person is not deliberately choosing a flattering test; they are genuinely struck by the coincidence.

So my question is threefold. First, is there an established concept for this specific configuration: inferring a common cause from the surprisingness of a particular coincidence, without conditioning on the number of opportunities for some coincidence to arise and on the fact that our noticing it depends on its being surprising? Second, how exactly should that concept be distinguished from the multiple-comparisons problem, from data dredging, and from the target-drawing move — which is the best fit, and where are the boundaries? Third, if there is no settled name, or if the settled name lumps together things that ought to be kept apart, I would like you to coin a term, define it, and give boundary cases. Above all, I want a principled answer to the practical question: when should a striking coincidence in operational data still count as evidence of a real common cause, and when should it be discounted as the near-inevitable product of looking at a large enough haystack?
