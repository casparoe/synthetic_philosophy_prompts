I sit on a university ethics committee that reviews proposals involving digital representations of people who cannot consent. Our past decisions are these. We approved the digitisation and public display of a Victorian diarist's private letters, reasoning that no living person could be harmed and that the historical value was considerable. We rejected a film studio's proposal to use a deceased actor's likeness in a new film, over the objection of the actor's spouse; we judged the foreseeable distress to the spouse to outweigh the film's value and noted that the actor had never endorsed such a use. We approved, subject to conditions, a museum chatbot that lets visitors converse with a long-dead historical figure using only that figure's published writings, on condition that the bot refuse to answer beyond the written record. We rejected a start-up's grief-voicemail app, which generated messages in a recently deceased woman's voice from recordings supplied by her daughter, after the woman's husband objected; we held that the husband's distress and the absence of any instruction from the deceased were decisive, though the panel divided over whether the deceased herself could be wronged. We must now rule on a proposal for a griefbot trained on a deceased man's private messages and diaries, to be used by his grieving partner. The bot would occasionally extrapolate messages the man never wrote, flagged as such, and he expressed no wishes about posthumous simulation. No living person objects. If we want to be consistent with our own record, what should we decide, which precedent is the closest analogue, and, if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the decision, what principle would we be adopting with each possible choice?
