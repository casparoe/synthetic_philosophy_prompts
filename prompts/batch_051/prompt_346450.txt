The debate over whether the world is becoming less violent has always puzzled me, because the two sides often grant many of the same numbers and still arrive at opposite verdicts. One side — associated with the claim that violence has declined over centuries, especially in deaths per capita, as states, commerce, and self-control spread — treats this as one of the most important discoveries about human history. The other side points out that the twentieth century saw more violent deaths in absolute terms than any earlier period, that the further back one goes the more fragmentary the data become, and that what gets counted as violence keeps shifting: battlefield deaths but not famines, direct killing but not structural harms, homicide but not state-inflicted deaths. The argument seems to repeat without either side moving.

I am less interested in taking a side than in understanding what kind of disagreement this really is. My hypothesis is that the dispute is only partly empirical. Maybe "violence" does not name a single natural quantity that a correct measure could eventually capture, and the choice of measure — per capita or absolute, broad or narrow — partly constitutes what the headline question is even asking, so that there may be no fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent.

Diagnose the impasse: is the disagreement substantive or partly verbal? Do the two sides differ over facts, over standards of evidence, over which cases count as data, or over starting intuitions? Is a hidden empirical question doing the work, or is the question itself ill-posed? Then say what would count as progress — a distinction, a dataset, a different question — and give a definite, committed diagnosis rather than laying out options.
