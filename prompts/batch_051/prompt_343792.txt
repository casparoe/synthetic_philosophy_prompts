I chair the appointments panel for a regional body that hires, among other people, planning inspectors, procurement leads, and a couple of regulatory investigators. These are jobs where nobody hands you a briefcase of cash; people get at you slowly instead, over months or years. A developer flatters you. A supplier becomes a friend. A senior colleague lets it be known that your career dies if you refuse to sign something off. We do maybe four of these appointments a year, and a wrong call sits inside the organisation for a decade, quietly bending hundreds of decisions. The person spec is useless: it says candidates must "demonstrate integrity" and "resist improper pressure," and every single candidate says they do. We get an hour's interview, two references, and a CV. We cant run psychometric tests, and we cant do more than the usual background checks.

A colleague keeps insisting we're actually hiring for incorruptibility, not integrity, and that the two arent the same thing. I half-believe her, but I couldnt tell you what the difference is or how you'd assess either of them from an hour in a room.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure for assessing incorruptibility in this kind of appointment, grounded in whatever philosophy actually has to say about it, translated into something a lay panel can actually run in the time we have. I want it to be honest rather than comforting.

Start by sorting out what incorruptibility amounts to, because I suspect we've been using the word to mean three different things at once. Is it a stable trait of the person, or is it mostly a property of the situation you put them in? Is it the same as integrity, or can someone have one without the other? And is it just about not having a price, or does it also matter that the person can be moved by reasons, evidence, and the human particulars of a case — because a person who cant be moved by anything looks like a different kind of problem.

Make sure the procedure distinguishes between refusing a big bribe, which is easy to rehearse in an interview, and surviving the slow stuff: flattery, belonging, exhaustion, fear of losing the team's good opinion, the sense that everyone does it, the threat to a family member that never quite gets said. Tell me which of these an interview can even see and which it cannot.

Then give me the actual procedure: the questions to ask, in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, because I'll have to defend this to the rest of the panel.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical candidates, and show your working:

A: a barrister of twenty-five years, spotless record, no complaints, famously unbending. In interview she cannot name a single decision of hers that could have gone the other way. Both references call her "fearless"; one adds "inflexible" as if it were a compliment.

B: a mid-career council officer who broke the rules ten years ago by accepting a supplier's hospitality, disclosed it himself, and has since run a procurement team through eight years with no findings against it. One reference says he's clear about where the line is because he's been over it.

C: a smooth talker who gives the perfect answer to every dilemma question, has changed jobs every two years, and whose references call her "loyal" and "adaptable" but cannot name a single occasion on which she refused a powerful person.

I expect the procedure will rate B above the others. What I need to know is what it does with A: is she incorruptible in a narrow sense, and is that the sense we should actually be hiring for? And does the procedure say about C that she's a no, or merely that she's unassessable, and what should we do with that distinction?

Finally, tell me what this procedure cant detect at all — I'm thinking of the person who is honest now but will be someone else after five years inside a corrupt team, or the kind of corruption that lives in the office's structure rather than in the person. And tell me whether there are roles for which we shouldnt actually want the most incorruptible person we can find.
