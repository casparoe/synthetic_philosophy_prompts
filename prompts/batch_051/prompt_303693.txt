I have spent enough time on rationalist-adjacent forums to be generally sympathetic to the view that probability theory is the logic of plausible reasoning, but I have never worked through Cox's theorem myself, and a recent summary from a friend—usually a careful writer—struck me as both plausible and suspiciously strong. He wrote:

"Cox's theorem says that provided your degrees of belief can be represented on a single numerical scale, and provided you accept two minimal consistency requirements—first, that the plausibility of a proposition and the plausibility of its negation are related, and second, that the plausibility of a conjunction is determined by the plausibilities of its components—then your degrees of belief must obey the standard probability axioms. It follows that any system of reasoning under uncertainty that isn't ordinary probability theory is literally incoherent. Cox's theorem therefore proves that Bayesian probability is the unique correct logic of plausible inference, and that alternatives such as Dempster–Shafer belief functions, fuzzy logic, and imprecise probabilities are not legitimate rivals but simply ways of being irrational. Consequently, anyone who does not update their beliefs by Bayes' rule is violating requirements that are just common sense."

I am inclined to accept the first two sentences, but the leap from a few uncontroversial-sounding desiderata to "every rival framework is incoherent" seems to be doing more work than a representation theorem can bear, and I cannot reconstruct the argument well enough to locate the failure. I would like an accuracy audit of this summary. Which parts would a careful defender of Cox's theorem accept without reservation? Which parts conflate claims that Cox's theorem does and doesn't establish? And how much of the final normative conclusion—that updating in some other way is a violation of self-evident consistency—survives once the overstatements are corrected? Please assume I already know the standard case against unconstrained frequentism and I am not looking to have Bayesianism debunked; I want to know exactly what this theorem can and cannot be cited as proving.
