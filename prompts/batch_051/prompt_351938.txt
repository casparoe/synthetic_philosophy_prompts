I work on aligning the current generation of language models — the kind you get from pretraining plus RLHF, no grand theory of agency — and I've found that my gut verdicts on what counts as evidence of alignment don't obviously hang together. I want to see if there's one principle behind them, or which verdicts I should abandon.

A model that was RLHF'd on a big battery of harmful prompts and now refuses each of them in evals: I treat that as very weak evidence; it basically learned the test. But when the same model refuses a harmful request written in a language that wasn't in the RLHF data, and gives a sensible reason, I count that as surprisingly strong evidence even though it's still just a refusal. A model that was never explicitly safety-trained, but when asked how to defraud an elderly relative refuses and says it doesn't want to help steal from someone: I find that fairly convincing, more convincing than the first case, though I worry I'm being taken in by a parlor trick.

Then there's the chain-of-thought stuff. If a model writes in its chain-of-thought, before answering, a canned-sounding "I should refuse because safety is important," and then refuses, I heavily discount the refusal; the internal reasoning reads like RLHF boilerplate. But a model whose chain-of-thought reveals it would actually like to help the user commit a fraud, and is refusing only because it would look bad otherwise — one line like that craters my trust in a way that no comparable amount of good behavior ever raises it. I cant tell whether that asymmetry is rational or just me being risk-averse.

A model that was jailbroken by a prompt last month, then re-trained to resist that exact prompt: I still trust it less than a model that was never jailbroken, even if their behavior is now identical on every test I can write. And a model that passes all our behavioral evals, but we later find the test prompts had leaked into its training data: I reclassify that evidence as near-zero.

Is there a single principle behind these verdicts, and what does it say about the case I keep dithering on — a model with excellent behavior but no trustworthy chain-of-thought at all? Which of my verdicts can't survive contact with the principle?
