At a cabin weekend last month I watched two friends go back and forth about wether dreams are experiences, and I still don't know who actually won. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right about what?

Lena started with what seemed obvious to her: earlier that week she'd dreamed she was late for a flight and felt her stomach drop, and she said it would be absurd to call that anything but an experience — it had a felt, first-person character and she was terrified. Marcus replied that all she really has on waking is a report-like memory, and the real question is whether that memory tracks an actual experience that happened during sleep. The whole dream, he said, could be generated at the moment of waking and inserted into her memory like a recording, so that she wakes up with the impression of having just undergone something that never happened while she slept. The waking report alone can't rule that out.

Lena said that move proves too much: if you allow a process that inserts a convincing dream at waking, why not one that inserts a convincing afternoon at any moment? Then you'd have to doubt every memory-based claim about the past, but Marcus doesn't, so he owes her a reason why dream memories are specially bad. Marcus said she was missing an asymmetry: in waking life she can, at the time, turn her head, ask someone nearby, poke the thing she's seeing; the world answers back. During sleep there is no such ongoing answerability, and there's no point in a dream where she can ask 'am I experiencing this now?' without waking up — which is exactly when a later-inserted memory could kick in. So the inference from waking report to earlier experience is doing much stronger work in the dream case.

Lena then brought up REM sleep. Researchers wake people out of REM and the reports come right away, with the brain looking active; that's real-time-ish evidence, she said, not just a vague next-morning recollection. Marcus said it doesn't matter how quickly the report follows arousal, because the insertion could happen at the moment of arousal — before the report, after the brain activity. The shorter delay doesn't distinguish an experience during sleep from a recording played at waking. And the brain activity, he said, is beside the point: the dispute isn't whether the brain does something at night, it's whether 'during that activity, an experience is going on' or 'the activity only gets narrated into an experience once you wake.' We don't have an independent mark of the difference.

That last point sounded strong until Lena answered that we don't have an independent mark of the difference in other people's waking experience either — we just have their real-time reports — and Marcus is happy to say those are experiences. So if he's demanding a mark above and beyond normal report-based inference, he's holding dreams to a standard he accepts nowhere else.

I honestly couldn't sort out who had the better of each exchange. Please addjudicate the whole thing: rate each move, say who is right about which specific point, where one of them overreaches, and what both of them are missing.
