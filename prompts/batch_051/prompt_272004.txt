I'm teaching a one-off session on social choice to philosophy undergrads — call it 14 people, 75 minutes — and I want a game where they hit the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem themselves before I ever state it. The thing I want them to feel is: with three or more candidates, if every candidate is cabable of winning under some sincere profile and the rule isn't a dictatorship, somebody can game it. So set it up as designers vs. manipulators. Write me the full activity end-to-end: the rules as I'd read them aloud, the materials, the scorekeeping, the timeline, and the debrief.

Round one, each of three groups writes down a single-winner voting rule for elections with exactly 3 candidates and 5 voters. Every voter reports a strict ranking of all three candidates, and the rule has to name exactly one winner for every possible collection of five ballots. The rule also has to be non-dictatorial (no single voter's top choice is always the winner) and onto (all three candidates can win under some profile of sincere rankings). Round two, rotate and attack: each group builds a five-ballot sincere profile and shows that some voter gets a winner she strictly prefers by submitting a ranking other than her true one. Designers score 3 points for each attack that fails, attackers score 2 for each successful manipulation. Give the designers 10 minutes to patch or replace their rule, run one more attack round, and then debrief. No real prizes — points are for bragging rights only.

In the debrief, have them state the theorem's conditions in their own words and point to the moment each showed up in play: unrestricted domain of strict rankings (the moment a rule broke on a weird profile), every-candidate-can-win (the moment someone tried to rule a candidate out of bounds), non-dictatorship (the moment the 'obvious' rule was voter 1's top choice), single-valuedness (the moment a tie went unresolved), strategy-proofness (the moment a misreport produced a better winner). Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it — under-specified tie rules, hidden dictators nobody notices, weak rankings sneaking in when the inputs are strict, groups dodging a profile by saying no rational voter would have those preferences. And be honest about what the game can't reproduce: the theorem says every non-dictatorial onto rule with 3+ candidates is manipulable on some profile, but the game only visits a handful of rules and profiles, so a group whose rule got broken just once can walk away thinking it was 'almost' strategy-proof. The all-quantifier is the part I'll have to put in words.
