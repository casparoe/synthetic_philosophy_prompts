I had a long argument with a friend last night about constitutional monarchy and I came away feeling like I’d lost even though I still think I’m right. My friend kept pushing on the fact that the British monarch—and others like it—retains these real legal powers: withholding royal assent, dissolving parliament, dismissing ministers, and so on. But by convention she never uses them except on ministerial advice, and everyone treats the fact that she doesn’t use them as part of what makes the system work. He called this a constitutional fiction, almost like a bomb you promise will never go off. I found myself defending the setup but couldn’t say exactly why the same arrangement would horrify me if the person holding the powers were elected.

So here’s the asymmetry that’s bugging me. If a president had those same latent powers and promised to never exercise them except on advice from the legislature, we’d demand the powers be stripped or handed to an accountable body. A “trust me, I won’t use them” president is not a stable constitutional solution. Yet a hereditary monarch with much less accountability, no election, and no term limit holding those powers seems to many democrats like it’s not just tolerable but genuinely stabilising. Bagehot’s old point that the sovereign’s influence runs through advice, encouragement, and warning rather than actual decisions seems to depend entirely on the fact that the adviser has no mandate and no practical way to turn counsel into command later. I want to know whether this asymmetry can be justified, or whether my friend is right that it’s just sentimental british exceptionalism.

Act as an expert political philosopher, not a constitutional lawyer. Please think carefully before answering—maybe start by brainstorming the strongest justifications someone could give, then stress-test them against nearby cases. Is there a real difference between an unaccountable hereditary actor who never uses power and an accountable elected actor who promises not to use power? If the first is acceptable, what exactly does the justification commit us to? Does it survive a monarch with a more assertive personality, a parliament that starts losing public confidence, or a prime minister who tries to use the palace as a political weapon? Should the asymmetry be kept, formalised, or given up?
