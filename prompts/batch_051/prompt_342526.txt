I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been trying to work out whether Stephen Wolfram's A New Kind of Science is doing something deep or something my field already knows under a different name. The analogy I keep returning to is this. Wolfram's method is to enumerate the elementary cellular automata, all 256 one-dimensional, two-state, nearest-neighbor rules, run them from simple initial conditions, and sort the resulting behavior into his four classes: dying out to a uniform state, simple repetition, apparent randomness, and localized structures that persist and interact. He treats the appearance of complex behavior in rules like Rule 30 as the central experimental result, and he takes it to support the principle of computational equivalence, which says, roughly, that almost any process that is not obviously simple is as computationally sophisticated as any other. To me this looks like a mirror image of the distinction I live with at work between proving properties of a system through formal reasoning and exploring what it actually does by running it across a large or exhaustive set of cases. Science as I learned it resembles formal verification: you have a hypothesis that acts as a specification, derive predictions, and check them. Wolfram's approach resembles sitting down with an exhaustive test harness over a finite space of rules, running each one, and browsing the traces for anything interesting rather than checking an expected result. The structures match on the surface, finite enumeration, empirical inspection, no closed-form analysis, but I suspect the analogy fails exactly where testing gets its authority. A test result is evidence only relative to an assertion that a particular output is correct, and Wolfram has no such assertion; that Rule 30 looks complex is not a property his harness checks, it is his reaction to the output. If a software engineer ran an exhaustive suite with no assertions and reported that some outputs were remarkable, we would call that exploration, not verification, and we would not treat it as evidence about programs in general. Yet maybe that is the radical part of his position: in a computational world, the observation of complexity is itself the specification, and my profession has been missing the point. I cannot tell whether I have found a real structural difference between two kinds of evidence or am merely projecting my job onto a book. Please say how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the point you are least sure of. If the analogy is real, I would also like to know whether anything from software engineering transfers back to judging whether Wolfram's project counts as science.
