I've come to think that satisficing is not merely a concession to our bounded computational capacities but the correct standard for practical rationality. My reason is that real choice situations rarely come with a closed list of options; "the optimal choice" is defined only relative to a specification of the menu, and that specification is largely the agent's own doing. Maximization then looks less like a stricter ideal that satisficing approximates and more like a rival description of the same activity, one that gets its determinacy by quietly assuming the menu is fixed. What would count against this view? Set aside the descriptive point that people sometimes maximize; I want the considerations that would show satisficing is second-best, incoherent, or unstable as a normative standard. For each, tell me whether it would overturn the view outright, merely push it to qualifiers, or be something a committed satisficer can absorb without embarrassment. Also locate the point in the argument where I am most likely to be wrong, and name the line of attack that should most reasonably move me away from the view.
