In a morphology module I took, the lecturer gave us four rough tests for seperating inflection from derivation, and I've been turning over a case that seems to split them neatly down the middle. The tests were: (i) inflection is required by the syntax and happens automatically, derivation is something you opt into; (ii) inflection is fully productive across a word class, derivation is gappy and full of lexicalised exceptions; (iii) inflection leaves word class and core meaning alone, derivation tends to change one or both and creates a new lexeme; (iv) inflection is semantically transparent, derivation is idiosyncratic.

The case is English -ish on adjectives: greenish, tallish, cheapish, newish, good-ish, old-ish. I don't mean the clearly derivational noun-attaching use (childish, bookish), which has its own idiosyncratic meanings; I mean the productive adjective-attaching use that means roughly 'somewhat X, hedged a bit'. That use is madly productive in informal British English — I can stick it on nearly any gradable adjective and get a vague, hedged version. It's transparent, and on adjectives it doesn't change the word class. But it's never grammatically required, and it feels like it gives you a new dictionary item rather than a form of 'green' — people treat greenish as its own word, not green in a particular form.

So: is adjective -ish inflectional or derivational? Go through the four tests one by one, say which way each points and how strongly, then give an overall verdict. More usefully, tell me which test is doing the least work here, and whether this case shows that productivity and transparency aren't really the heart of the inflection/derivation distinction. And would your answer shift if the decisive question were 'is greenish the same lexeme as green?' rather than 'does it look regular?' What would actually settle the boundary?
