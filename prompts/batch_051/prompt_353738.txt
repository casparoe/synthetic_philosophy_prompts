I am a doctoral student in philosophy of language working on the semantics of normative discourse, and I have been asked to lead a session on the Frege–Geach problem. I drafted the numbered reconstruction below, but I am uneasy that some step may be doing more work than it appears to, and I would rather not circulate a handout I cannot defend. Please evaluate the argument step by step. Is it valid as written? If it is not valid, identify the first numbered step that fails, explain whether the failure is a false premise, an equivocation, or an invalid inference, and say how a sophisticated expressivist should respond to that step. If the argument is valid, say which premise an expressivist is best advised to reject and why. If the argument is valid only against an unattractive version of expressivism, say so and state precisely what would have to be added to make it press on the contemporary view.

Consider the inference:
(A) If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong.
(B) Lying is wrong.
(C) Therefore, getting your little brother to lie is wrong.

The argument:

1. The inference from (A) and (B) to (C) is an instance of modus ponens and is valid.
2. For that inference to be valid, "lying is wrong" must make the same semantic contribution in (A), where it appears embedded in a conditional, and in (B), where it appears as a free-standing premise; otherwise the inference equivocates.
3. Expressivism, on the standard reading, holds that the semantic contribution of "lying is wrong" is exhausted by the non-cognitive attitude it is used to express—paradigmatically, disapproval of lying.
4. A speaker who sincerely asserts (B) thereby expresses disapproval of lying.
5. A speaker who sincerely asserts (A) need not express disapproval of lying at all: she may be indifferent toward lying, or even approve of it, while accepting the conditional because she accepts that if the wrongness of lying is conceded, the wrongness of getting one's little brother to lie follows.
6. Thus, in (A), "lying is wrong" occurs without the speaker expressing the attitude that step 3 identifies as its semantic contribution, while in (B) that attitude is expressed.
7. Therefore, by step 3, "lying is wrong" makes different semantic contributions in the two occurrences.
8. By step 2, the inference then equivocates and is not valid.
9. That contradicts step 1, and since no metaethical theory may give up the validity of modus ponens with moral sentences, expressivism is refuted.

Please tell me where this argument goes wrong, if it does.
