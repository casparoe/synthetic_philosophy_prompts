I am a student currently taking a seminar on utilitarianism and its critics, and I have worked out a distinction I want pressure-tested before I build my final paper around it. I call the distinction the difference between "first-person aggregation" and "third-person aggregation."

By first-person aggregation I mean the ordinary trade-off inside a single life: I accept a present loss because I expect a later gain large enough to make the loss worth bearing, and the loss and the gain occur in the same subject, so the compensation is paid to the same person who incurred the cost. By third-person aggregation I mean what happens when losses and gains are summed across different lives and the positive balance is treated as if it were a benefit received by someone.

Rawls's separateness-of-persons objection is usually presented as the claim that only the first sort of aggregation is legitimate, because in the second sort there is no single subject whose balance the sum could be. I accept that, but I think it is a symptom of something more specific, which I have been calling "the unsponsored aggregate." My claim is that Mill's proof passage does not actually perform third-person aggregation at the step where the objection is usually raised; it does something subtler. Here is the passage as it appears in John Stuart Mill's *Utilitarianism*, Chapter IV:

"No reason can be given why the general happiness is desirable, except that each person, so far as he believes it to be attainable, desires his own happiness. This, however, being a fact, we have not only all the proof which the case admits of, but all which it is possible to require, that happiness is a good: that each person's happiness is a good to that person, and the general happiness, therefore, a good to the aggregate of all persons."

On my reading, the first half of the argument licenses "a good to that person" by pointing to a real subject: each person stands to their own happiness as the account to which it is credited. But the conclusion then calls the general happiness "a good to the aggregate of all persons." I call that phrase an unsponsored aggregate because it is grammatically designed to look like a beneficiary, yet no one stands to the total happiness the way each person stands to their own. The inference only looks parallel because the syntax stays the same across the two halves: "X's happiness is a good to X" first picks out a subject, then the same form is reused with a merely notional plural subject.

So my homemade theory is this: the separateness objection is not only the metaphysical point that no super-subject feels the summed utility. It is also an objection to a semantic slide — the slide from a good-to-someone to a good-that-no-one-owns but that is nevertheless treated as available for aggregation. The aggregate is unsponsored because no actual standpoint is cited as the beneficiary of the total.

I want an honest assessment of this construct. Is it just the standard objection restated in my own vocabulary, or does the idea of the unsponsored aggregate pick out a separate move? Is there a reading of "a good to the aggregate of all persons" under which the aggregate can sponsor the good — for example, a distributive reading on which the phrase means only that the general happiness is a good because each of its parts is a good to someone? And what is the strongest objection to my claim that Mill's proof depends on this slide? Could a utilitarian reply that Mill is not here deriving the moral requirement to maximize from facts about individual goods at all, but only showing that the general happiness is a proper object of desire, so my objection is aimed at the wrong step?
