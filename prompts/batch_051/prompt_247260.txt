I am a network scientist: I am comfortable with nodes, edges, directed and signed ties, homophily, community detection, structural holes, weak ties, information cascades, and trust propagation on graphs, but I have essentially no background in social epistemology. I keep running into a distinction philosophers draw between epistemic bubbles and echo chambers, and I want to know whether it survives translation into my vocabulary.

Here is my rough understanding of the target distinction. An epistemic bubble is a state in which relevant outside information or voices are simply absent, typically through filtering, curation, homophily, or self-segregation. The defining mechanism is omission: the counter-evidence never reaches the person. Because the person's basic trust in the channels of information remains intact, the bubble can be popped by exposure to the missing information. An echo chamber, by contrast, is a state in which outside sources are not merely absent but actively discredited; members are brought to distrust outsiders, so that when contrary information does arrive, they process it as evidence of the outsiders' untrustworthiness rather than as a reason to update. The usual slogan is that bubbles concern what one does not hear, while echo chambers concern whom one trusts.

Please do two things. First, restate this distinction systematically in network terms. For each construct, tell me what the corresponding exposure network looks like: which nodes and edges exist, where trust and distrust enter, what changes when a contrary message is injected, and what network-level intervention would dissolve the structure. In particular, tell me whether an echo chamber is representable simply as a graph with positive in-group and negative out-group ties, or whether something essential to it falls outside even a signed graph.

Second, give me the residue report. Which parts of the distinction translate cleanly? Which parts can be represented only with distortion or ad hoc additions? Which parts have no counterpart at all in network vocabulary — for example, the idea of trust as an attitude one adopts toward another person's standing as a knower, or the fact that echo chambers are often built and maintained by interested parties? I want an honest translation: do not quietly import philosophical notions like testimony or epistemic responsibility into the network model under relabeled names.

Finally, give me your verdict on the residue. Does it show that my vocabulary is missing distinctions it would need, or that the philosophers' two-category contrast is coarser than its slogan suggests? Throughout, flag which parts of the underlying philosophical picture are settled among those who work on it and which are genuinely contested, and do not present one contested reading as the consensus.
