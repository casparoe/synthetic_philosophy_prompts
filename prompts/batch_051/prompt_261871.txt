I am giving a talk at a monthly discussion group that reads political and economic classics only in bits and pieces, but the members are not academics; they are retired professionals, a few small-business owners, and one local elected official. The title I have given the talk is something like, Adam Smith thought the British Empire was a bad bargain for Britain, not just a moral crime against the colonists. I have no academic background; I have just been reading the Wealth of Nations on my own, especially the long chapter on colonies, and I think I have found an argument in Smith that is less sentimental than the usual anti-imperialist one. My claim is this: Smith's main case against the American empire is an accounting argument. The colonies are a net cost to the mother country, because the monopoly of colonial trade forces British capital into less productive channels, the defense of the colonies is an open-ended expense, and the political class in Britain has been corrupted by the easy money. Smith, I argue, therefore thought the two sane options were either a real Atlantic union, with Americans sending representatives to Parliament and paying taxes, or a clean separation into friendly independent states; what he could not defend was the actual half-empire, where Britain claimed sovereignty but could not govern the colonies properly. I need to be ready for the discussion period. The audience is mixed. A few think of Smith as the apostle of free trade and will want him to be against all empire on principle; a couple think the British Empire spread law, property, and commerce and will press me on whether Smith was unrealistic; one person keeps insisting that economic explanations always miss the moral core of politics. I want you to prepare me for the objections I will actually get from this exact room, not the ones a professor would raise. Please list them in something close to the order they will come, phrased in the way each type of audience member would put them, with which ones have real force against my reading, what I should concede up front, the one reply that has to land for my talk to survive, and the question I am hoping nobody asks. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write an email or a letter.
