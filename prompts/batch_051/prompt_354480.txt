Two friends of mine got into it last night over whether the sentence "mosquitoes carry malaria" is actually true, and I've been turning it over all day. Background: they'd recently been through the usual philosophy-of-language puzzle about generics — "ducks lay eggs" is true even though no male duck and no duckling has ever laid an egg, and "mosquitoes carry malaria" gets used as true even though fewer than one percent of mosquitoes actually carry the parasite. So the real question underneath is what makes a generic statement true at all.

Here's the back-and-forth as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) Noah said the puzzle is overblown. A generic is just a loose statistical claim with a floating threshold. "Ducks lay eggs" comes out true because, once you restrict to the ducks the predicate itself selects — mature females — the proportion is effectively 100 percent. And he bit the bullet on the malaria case: he said "mosquitoes carry malaria" is just false as a generic, and when we say it we're being sloppy, meaning something like some mosquitoes of the relevant species are the vector. Not a true generic; just shorthand with a scary edge.

(2) Priya pushed back that this gets the data backwards. "Mosquitoes carry malaria" is a paradigm true generic — nobody treats it as loose talk, they treat it as the thing that's true of the kind — and it's true because carrying the parasite is a property of the kind, not a property spread across most of its members. Generics track what's characteristic, causal, or striking about the kind; that's why "sharks attack bathers" is fine even though almost no shark does. Headcounts are the wrong currency.

(3) Noah replied that "striking" is doing all the work, and it's psychological, not semantic. What counts as striking depends on what you're afraid of or care about, so her account can't tell true generics apart from, say, "pit bulls maul children" or "immigrants commit crimes" — any low-frequency property a speaker happens to find salient gets called striking and plugged in. At least a statistical account has a constraint: the proportion of Fs among the Ks has to be non-trivial relative to some comparison class, and his view explain why the prejudiced ones are false.

(4) Priya answered that the statistical account is only "constrained" because it silently helps itself to kind-level facts it pretends not to need. To make "ducks lay eggs" true it restricts the domain to mature females; but with exactly the same move it would also make "ducks are female" true, since within that restricted class the proportion is also 100 percent — and every English speaker knows "ducks are female" is not an acceptable generic. The restriction is only allowed for what's constitutive of the duck life cycle, which is precisely the kind-level notion Noah said he didn't need. Besides, "non-trivial proportion" has no principled cutoff — four percent? forty? — so the statistical view dosen't actually make any determinate prediction.
