I'm giving a paper at a philosophy of religion workshop in a few weeks and I'm already dreading the Q&A. My thesis is a defense of what I'm calling "radically extrinsic divine simplicity." The background problem: classical theists want God to be utterly simple — no composition of any kind — but they also want to say God is genuinely wise, powerful, just, and so on, which looks like a plurality. The standard fix is to say the attributes are distinct only in our thinking but with some real "foundation" in God (a "virtual distinction"); I think that's cheating, since any foundation in God is a distinction in God. So I defend the stronger claim that the difference among the attributes lives entirely on the side of how created intellects denominate God: the attributes are true of God purely extrinsically, as ways creatures name the one simple reality in relation to created effects — and I try to argue this doesn't reduce God-talk to fiction, because the denominations track real relations creatures bear to God even though nothing in God corresponds to the differences among them.

The audience is the problem. Half the room are Thomists who treat simplicity as nonnegotiable but think the "foundation in God" move is necessary to preserve the truth and multiplicity of the perfections; the other half are analytic philosophers of religion, mostly perfect-being theorists, who already suspect strong simplicity empties God of a rich inner life and makes the doctrine either incoherent or religiously useless. I expect the Thomists will say I've collapsed real and merely rational distinctions in the worst possible way and sold out divine truth; I expect the perfect-being people will say I've handed them the argument that God has no intrinsic character at all. Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the seven or so objections in the order they'll actually land, phrased the way each camp would phrase them, which ones have real force, what I should concede up front and what I should hold, with a two-minute reply for each and a note on where it's soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and tell me what would change my mind — which single concession would make the radical-extrinsic reading not worth defending and send me back to the standard virtual-distinction view.
