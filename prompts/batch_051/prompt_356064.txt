Last night I spent several hours arguing with a friend about physicalism, and specifically about whether the view has any determinate content. He kept pressing both horns of Hempel's dilemma, and I found myself giving formulations that each seemed right when I said them but did not clearly fit together once he wrote them down. He accused me of moving between them as I needed them. Before I talk with him again, I want an audit of the position I actually hold.

Here are the commitments I came away with.

1. Physicalism is true, and it is a substantive, contingent claim about the world — not a tautology, not a definitional truth, and not merely an attitude of deference to science.

2. Physicalism is an exhaustiveness thesis: every fact, or at least every mental, biological, and social fact, either is a physical fact or obtains solely in virtue of physical facts. I have not settled whether the relevant relation is identity, constitution, realization, grounding, or supervenience, only that the physical facts are in some sense basic and the rest hold in virtue of them.

3. By "physical" I mean the kinds of entities, properties, and processes that physics is in the business of describing: the fundamental constituents and forces of the world, along with the properties of systems composed of them. I take this to be a constraint on the term, not a stipulation I can attach to whatever I like.

4. I will not define "physical" by enumerating what current physics accepts — the Standard Model particles, the known forces, spacetime, and the like. Current physics is incomplete and probably partly wrong, so a definition tied to its current ontology would make physicalism hostage to a particular, temporary stage of science and would probably be falsified by ordinary scientific progress.

5. I also will not define "physical" as whatever a completed, ideal physics would posit. I cannot now say what a completed physics would posit; if the definition simply defers to the final theory regardless of its content, the thesis becomes empty. In particular, if the final theory had to quantify over irreducible mental forces, I would not want my definition to count those as physical and thereby make physicalism true by stipulation.

6. The physical is to be characterized negatively: as what is not fundamentally mental, not irreducibly intentional, and not occult or supernatural.

7. The causal closure of the physical is one of my central reasons for rejecting interactionist dualism. By causal closure I mean that every physical event has a complete physical cause; a nonphysical mind would therefore either be epiphenomenal or would require physical events without sufficient physical causes.

8. The vagueness of "physical" is not a serious problem. There are borderline cases — quantum vacuum states, mathematical structures, social institutions — but many scientifically respectable terms have fuzzy boundaries without being empty, and I can fix enough central cases to make progress: quarks and action potentials are physical; Cartesian souls and entelechies are not.

I do not need Hempel's dilemma explained; I need to know whether these eight commitments can live together. Please audit the set. Go through the commitments and say which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be in tension once the relevant terms are distinguished. For each genuine tension, order them by severity, state the cheapest repair, and say what that repair costs. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set coherent, and whether the resulting position is still recognizable as physicalism.

Give me a committed judgment rather than a menu of options: say which commitments I should revise or abandon and why. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
