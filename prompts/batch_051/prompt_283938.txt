I'm on the undergrad commitee of my uni's math department, and we're deadlocked on a proposal to change how our intro-to-proofs course is assessed. Right now the two timed exams mostly ask students to reproduce proofs from the course, or small variants of them. The proposal would replace one exam with a portfolio: each student picks three theorems we covered and, for each one, writes a page or so explaining (a) the main idea of the proof, (b) what breaks if a specific hypothesis is dropped, and (c) why a certain step has to be there given what the proof is trying to do — without necessarily writing the proof line by line. The proposers say this actually tests understanding, and that the current exams reward memorizing the standard proofs. The opponents say it lets students who can gesture at a proof but can't actually run the machinery pass — and that in mathematics, being able to produce the steps is a big part of what understanding a proof is, not just evidence for it. The chair has asked me, as the subcommittee of one, to draft a one-page memo for the department with a definite recommendation: adopt, reject, or adopt a modified version, with criteria for evaluating the portfolios, and an honest treatment of the strongest worry on the side I don't choose. Don't hedge — commit — and keep the memo under 500 words.
