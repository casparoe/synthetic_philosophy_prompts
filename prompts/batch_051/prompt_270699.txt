I help run a private foundation that funds biographies, and we've been loosing faith in the genre: most new biographies are either hagiography (the subject's allies on every page) or debunking (the subject's worst week on every page), and neither does what Plutarch was trying to do, which is use a whole life as material for thinking about how to live. Here's a scheme we're considering. For each figure we fund — someone recently dead, of public significance — we commission two biographers of roughly equal seniority. One is instructed to write the strongest honest case for the subject: the most charitable reading the record will support. The other is instructed to write the strongest honest case against: the harshest reading the record will support. Same advance, same length, same guaranteed access to the materials we negotiate, and the two books are published on the same day. Then a third writer, with no ties to either camp and no stake in the subject's reputation, writes a short comparison in the manner of the comparisons Plutarch attached to his paired Lives, pointing out where the two accounts diverge, what each omits, and what a reader should actually believe. We'd fund maybe five pairs a year.

Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral position, and tell me how it would actually play out. What does it incentivize for the biographers, for the estates and admirers who control interviews and papers, for the publisher, and for readers? How do people game it — the two biographers quietly coordinating, the hostile brief being sandbagged into a strawman, estates granting real access to one side only, the subject's allies leaking the friendly volume early? Does the comparison author effectively become the real biographer while the two books turn into raw material? Does the scheme actually recover the Plutarchian aim of learning from lives, or does it just industrialize the hagiography/debunking split with better branding? Which failure modes are fixable without gutting the scheme, and which are baked in?
