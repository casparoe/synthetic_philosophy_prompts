Take the role of a historical linguist who regularly explains this material to non-specialists. I'm leading a ninety-minute session for an adult-education reading group on a single question: When does Latin become Italian? The group is curious and literate but has no training in linguistics, and several members arrive assuming that languages have birthdays and death dates, that Latin died, and that Italian is therefore a corrupted or simplified descendant. Do not explain the answer to me; plan how I should run the session. Work out the order in which the underlying ideas need to be introduced so that the question gets rephrased by the end—spoken continuity versus written fixity, the difference between a language and a standardized norm, and standardization as a social and political choice rather than a linguistic event. Choose one concrete grammatical feature to carry the whole explanation; I suggest the erosion of Latin's noun case endings and the shift to prepositions plus fixed word order, but if you use a different carrier, say why and specify what the example must have and must lack. Then map the misconceptions the audience will bring or develop (for example, Latin died, Italian is simplified Latin, there must have been an official moment of birth) and state where in the sequence each one should be headed off. Conclude with a check for understanding that would show they have grasped that the boundary is drawn rather than discovered, and identify what you would deliberately leave out at this level. Before drafting the final plan, first list the conceptual dependencies the session rests on.
