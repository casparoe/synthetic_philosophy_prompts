I keep coming back to a way of taxing inheritance that I would like you to pick apart. The usual approach taxes the estate—a big pile left behind by one dead person—before anything is distributed. But what I actually care about is what living people receive and what it does to their position relative to everyone else. The same $200,000 means one thing for a thirty-year-old with nothing and another for someone already sitting on five million. Here is the scheme I keep wanting to defend.

Abolish the estate tax and put the tax entirely on the receiving side. Every person gets a lifetime tax-free receipt allowance of $500,000. All inheritances, and all gifts above a small annual per-giver exemption, count toward that allowance, so a family cannot hand the money over early in little pieces. Nothing is owed until a person's cumulative receipts pass the allowance. After that, every additional dollar is taxed on a schedule whose rate is set not by the size of the estate it came from but by the recipient's own net worth in the year they receive it—home equity, retirement accounts, trusts, business interests, all counted the way a serious wealth tax would have to count them. Roughly: a recipient with little wealth pays close to nothing, the rate rises through the middle, and for someone already wealthy it approaches or exceeds the top income-tax rate. The same parent could leave the same amount to two children and it would be nearly free for one and heavily taxed for the other. From an equal-opportunity point of view that is the entire point; from other points of view it is likely the first objection. The intended effect is that an inheritance is lightly taxed when it is most likely to close a gap in lifetime prospects, and heavily taxed when it mostly thickens existing dynastic wealth.

I am not asking whether this is a good idea in the abstract. I am asking how it would actually play out. Treat it as a mechanism and walk me through it: who is incentivized to do what, and with what timing; what the obvious workarounds and gaming strategies are and how far they would get; what it does to heirs of modest means, to people inheriting illiquid assets like a family farm or a small business, to marriages and family disputes, and to gifts that skip a generation; and where the scheme breaks first. And I want you to be explicit about what would change your mind about whether it does the equal-opportunity work I am hoping it does, or where your verdict is least stable.
