I work on ontologies for a medical device company, building a knowledge graph of product components, so I spend all day deciding whether a particular collection of parts is one object or several. Someone recommended I read some mereology to see how philosophers think about this, and I've gotten a little obsessed — partly because my manager keeps asking why the ontology can't just "match how engineers actually talk," and I now can't defend it to myself. The question van Inwagen called the Special Composition Question seems like exactly my problem: when does a bunch of things compose a further thing, rather than just being a bunch? But the philosophical debate feels strangely stuck, and it's started tripping up my day job too. The nihilists say composition basically never happens — no tables, just particles arranged table-wise — and they cheerfully bite the bullet that my inventory should contain no assemblies at all. The universalists say any two things, no matter how scattered or gerrymandered, compose something — so my ontology would have to include completely useless objects like the thing made of this screw and that hospital's doorknob. The moderate views try to draw a line (things in contact, things that make a life, things that are fastened together) but they keep getting hit with counterexamples where the line looks arbitrary. What's maddening is that nobody denies the appearances — tables seem like single objects, a heap of sand seems like a loose plurality, a screw-plus-doorknob pair doesn't seem to form anything — and everyone agrees a principle would be nice. They just weigh the costs differently: nihilists can't stomach arbitrariness, universalists can't stomach unexplained specialness, moderates can't stomach the revisionism at either extreme, and each camp accuses the others of merely relocating the problem rather than solving it.

So my question is: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a verbal dispute where "object" and "exists" just work differently inside each theory, so there's no shared question left to argue about? Is it a dispute about which theoretical virtue — simplicity, conservatism about ordinary belief, non-arbitrariness — should win? Is the real issue methodological, about whether ordinary intuition counts as evidence at all, or whether the question is ill-posed because it asks for a line where the world doesn't draw one? Or — here's my hypothesis, which I'm not sure how much to believe — the debate is stalled because everyone is trying to answer the SCQ in the abstract, rather than asking what a composition principle is for: regimenting an artifact ontology, serving science, or capturing how ordinary people re-identify things over time. I'd like a diagnosis of the impasse, grounded in what the three camps actually say, and a sense of what would count as progress given that diagnosis — or an argument that the debate is permanently stalled and why.
