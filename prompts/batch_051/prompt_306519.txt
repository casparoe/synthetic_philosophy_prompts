I've been defending a Jaspers-ish line on collective guilt, and I want to know what would actually count against it, because I suspect I've built a position that's unfalsifiable by definition and I can't tell if that's a bug or a feature. The position, roughly: criminal guilt attaches to violating a positive law and is for courts; moral guilt attaches to what an individual does or fails to do; political guilt attaches to membership in a polity whose deeds you remain answerable for even if you opposed them; metaphysical guilt I mostly set aside. A nation can be guilty in the political sense, but that is really liability — being on the hook for what a state did and continues to do — not guilt in the criminal or moral sense. So when someone says Germany is guilty for the Holocaust, I translate it as: the German political community today owes repair for what was done in its name. That is still a heavy claim, but it is not literally the same predicate as saying she is guilty of murder.

What would count against this? I want the strongest arguments, cases, or intuitions that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be. Maybe group agents can intend, remember, and be ashamed through institutions, in which case the nation is more like a person than I admit. Maybe the notion of guilt doesn't actually require could-have-done-otherwise the way I'm leaning on. Maybe inherited guilt is supposed to be a standing feature of a collective identity — like a polity that keeps affirming the wrong as its own — rather than a relation to a past act. Distinguish what would be decisive from what would merely be awkward, and say where a defender of my view would themselves concede trouble, not just what critics would hurl at it. Finally, tell me honestly whether I've insulated the view by defining guilt so narrowly that the claim that a nation can never be guilty is true by stipulation, and whether that should make me worry. The thing I'd personally find most persuasive would be a real, non-metaphorical sense in which today's members participate in the deed itself, not just its aftermath.
