So I help run an after-school program for high school kids, and a couple years ago we went all-in on grit: we do weekly "resilience check-ins," give out small prizes for sticking with hard projects, and our whole mentor training is built around the idea that teaching kids to persevere matters more than trying to raise their raw ability. The original pitch, from Duckworth's work, was that grit—understood as passionate persistence toward long-term goals—predicts success better than talent or IQ, so schools should stop just selecting for it and start deliberately building it.

Lately though I've been reading the newer research, and the finding that keeps coming up is that once you control for the Big Five trait of conscientiousness, grit's incremental predictive power mostly dissapears. Basically, grit may be conscientiousness in a new hat, which would mean our program is teaching something real but not the thing we said we were teaching. My question is whether Duckworth's argument survives that finding. Does her case for deliberately cultivating grit as a distinct trait fall apart, or can it be repaired in a weaker form—like, "we are just teaching conscientiousness under a better brand, and that's still worth doing"? I'm also wondering if the deeper problem is ideological rather than statistical: maybe the whole grit/resilience framing flatters kids for enduring bad conditions that adults should be fixing, and the empirical deflation just makes that problem harder to ignore. If you had to say which specific claim in the original argument takes the hit, and whether what remains is still enough to keep a program like ours going, what would you say?
