I teach an intermediate undergraduate course in nineteenth- and twentieth-century European philosophy. In two weeks I have a single eighty-minute session on Sartre’s *Existentialism Is a Humanism*, with about twenty-five students — some majors, most second-years taking the course as an elective. The obvious route is to march through the familiar themes in Sartre’s order and then raise objections at the end. I want to reverse that. I want to center the session on the charge of incoherence: the objection that once you make subjectivity as radical as Sartre does, you can no longer assert the very things the lecture asserts — that there are truths, that one action can be better or worse than another, that the doctrine itself is true. My hinge for the session is this passage, near the beginning of the piece, where Sartre states what the doctrine affirms:

“Many may be surprised at the mention of humanism in this connection, but we shall try to see in what sense we understand it. In any case, we can begin by saying that existentialism, in our sense of the word, is a doctrine that does render human life possible; a doctrine, also, which affirms that every truth and every action imply both an environment and a human subjectivity.” — Sartre, *Existentialism Is a Humanism* (1946), trans. Philip Mairet.

The passage is my hinge because it commits Sartre to two things at once — the side of situations and constraints, and the side of human freedom and subjectivity — and the charge of incoherence is precisely that the second side swallows the first, leaving him no stable standpoint from which to state either. I would like a teaching plan for this passage and this objective, not the explanation itself. What has to be in place before students can even feel the force of the charge — in particular, which conception of essence and which assumption about truth or universality need to be on the table? Then give me the order of ideas: what depends on what, and where in the sequence the passage should be introduced, stressed, and returned to. Provide the one carrying example or case that will do the most work across the eighty minutes, and say what it must have and what it must lack. Then the misconception map: the things second-years predictably bring into the room — existentialism as subjectivist license, as relativism, as a mood, as the view that you can do anything — and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off, or better, used to drive the argument forward. End with how I will know by the end whether they have understood the charge as an objection that applies to Sartre’s own statements, rather than either accepting or dismissing existentialism as a lifestyle, and what should be frankly marked as left out at this level. No readings.
