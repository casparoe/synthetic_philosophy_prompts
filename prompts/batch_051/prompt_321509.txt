I'm a philosophy PhD student TAing an upper-level seminar on epistemic dependence on AI and the erosion of understanding, and I've been put in charge of writing one 90-minute final essay question. I need it to come with a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme. I really want this to seperate the students who get the issue from the ones who just write "AI is a black box and automation bias is real."

The crux: a reliable but opaque AI seems, on its face, no different from a thermometer or an expert's testimony—we rely on all of them without understanding their inner workings, and mostly what we want is reliability. So the students should have to say exactly what epistemic good might be eroded even when the AI is more accurate than the people it replaces. I want them to have to work through knowing-that vs understanding-why, and individual diagnostic skill vs the clinic's shared capacity to explain, check, and catch errors, and then say whether the loss is genuinely epistemic rather than merely pragmatic (de-skilling, liability, losing a craft, etc.). It would help if the question were built around a concrete scenario, like a clinic that replaces its weekly tumor-board slide review with a dermatology AI that beats experienced dermatologists in trials, so the answers can't just stay at slogan level.

Please draft: (1) the exam question, (2) a model answer, and (3) a marking scheme with five must-hit points worth 20 marks total, where each point specifies its marks and has a one-line note on how candidates typically miss it. Don't make it a question about whether the technology is good or bad; the interesting part is whether "just a reliable instrument" is actually a good analogy.
