I spent my career in the experimental sciences, where one learns to treat an instrument as a transparent extension of one's own judgment rather than as a source of independent initiative, and only recently have I begun reading the philosophical and technical literature on artificial intelligence. I have come to hold a view that I would like subjected to serious stress-testing rather than merely restated or politely criticized. The view is roughly this: we should deliberately keep artificial systems on the tool side of the tool-agent distinction. By that I mean we should aim to build systems that function as legible extensions of human decision-making—systems that predict, propose, transform, or execute within clearly bounded tasks—rather than systems that are given open-ended goals and allowed to select their own actions in a dynamic environment in pursuit of those goals. My reason is not that agentic systems must be malicious. It is that once a system carries its own goal and its own action-selection over time, the burden of predicting its behavior, of keeping its goal aligned with human interests, and of preserving a short and auditable chain of responsibility becomes very heavy, and safety-critical. A tool keeps the locus of decision with a human operator and, at least in principle, keeps failure modes comparatively local and inspectable.

I know this framing is contested, and I am not asking for the familiar objections rehearsed once more. What I want is a structured map of the position's exposure. Specifically: what would have to be true—conceptually, technically, economically, or in practice—for the tool-first position to be false, or at least seriously damaged? Which observations or developments would count decisively against it, and which would count as merely awkward or as showing only that the distinction needs to be redrawn? Where exactly is the tool-agent distinction most likely to collapse, and what would an intermediate case have to look like for that collapse to matter rather than being a terminological curiosity? I also want you to distinguish the defeaters that a committed defender of the tool-first position would themselves concede as genuine damage from the ones they would legitimately dismiss as changing the subject. I am prepared to learn that the distinction cannot bear the weight I have placed on it. My own residual doubt, as someone trained to notice that instruments can amplify the operator's errors as well as the operator's competence, is this: a tool can be an extremely powerful optimizer within a constrained task, and such an optimizer placed in the hands of careless, biased, or malevolent humans may simply relocate the safety problem rather than eliminate it. Tell me whether that is the deepest point against the view, or only the most obvious one.
