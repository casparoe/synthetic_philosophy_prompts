Okay, I keep hitting the same wall in my philosophy of religion seminar, and I'd like a fresh angle. The problem is petitionary prayer: if God is omniscient and immutable, what's the point of asking Him for things? He already knows your needs and won't change His mind. I've got the standard moves:

1. The character move — prayer changes the pray-er, not God: it disciplines desire, cultivates humility, etc.
2. The obedience move — God commands prayer, so we pray out of obedience, not to inform or persuade Him.
3. The decree move — God eternally wills to grant certain goods only on condition they're asked for, so prayer is part of the plan from eternity; no change in God required.
4. The relationship move — petition isn't really about outcomes; it's about intimacy, like telling a friend your worries even though she already knows them.

I also know the usual nearby variants: prayer as trust, prayer as a way of recognizing God's answer as a gift, prayer as consent/cooperation with what God is already doing, and the open-theist or process moves that just deny the premises.

Now the task. Take the role of an expert philosopher of religion who likes drawing fine distinctions. Brainstorm at least five candidate answers first, and set aside the ones that are just restatements or combinations of 1–4 or those nearby variants. Then give me a proposal for how petitionary prayer to an omniscient, immutable God could still be effective — not on my list, not a variant or special case or combination of it, and not a premise-denial. Use your own words; no citations needed. Finally, tell me honestly whether the proposal is any good, how it relates to the standard moves, and what it's main weakness is.
