An essay I read on the psychology of authority summarizes the legacy of Milgram and Arendt this way:

"Milgram's obedience experiments demonstrated that ordinary people will, without any real coercion, deliver what they take to be severe and dangerous electric shocks to an innocent stranger, so long as an authority figure in a laboratory instructs them to continue. Arendt's portrait of Eichmann as a mediocre, thoughtless bureaucrat supplies the explanation: such people are not sadists, they simply hand their conscience over to whoever structures the situation. The lesson is that deference to authority is not a virtue but a standing temptation to moral abdication; the plea that one was merely following orders is an alibi, not a mitigating condition. Military ethics, with its demand that subordinates obey lawful orders, merely gives that abdication an institutional form."

I doubt this summary is as tidy as the author makes it sound, but I do not know enough to say where the problems are. Please go through it claim by claim and give me an accuracy audit: which parts a careful social psychologist would accept about Milgram's findings, which parts overstate or misdescribe them, whether Arendt's account actually explains Milgram's results in the way the essay implies, how the superior-orders defense and the military distinction between lawful and unlawful orders complicate the essay's sweeping conclusion, and whether the final claim—that deference to authority cannot be a virtue—follows from anything true in the summary. I want the precise line between what the research and arguments establish and what the essay adds by rhetoric, not just a verdict of fair or unfair.
