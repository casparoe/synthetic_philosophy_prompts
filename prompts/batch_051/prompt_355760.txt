I am a PhD student in safety-critical systems engineering, and my working vocabulary is largely that of reinforcement learning, formal methods, and control theory: learned policies, reward functions as opposed to true objectives, training and deployment distributions, distribution shift, partial observability, verification against specifications, safety cases, hazard analysis, authority and oversight, and the distinction between design-time and run-time behavior. I can reason comfortably about why a policy fails out-of-distribution, why a reward specification underdetermines intended behavior, or why a human overseer cannot redirect a system quickly enough to prevent harm. What I handle less well is the philosophical vocabulary in which the responsibility-gap debate is usually conducted — culpability, answerability, attributability, retribution, and the difference between a wrong and a merely bad outcome.

I would like you to do two things. First, restate the debate over whether increasingly autonomous, learning-based systems create a genuine responsibility gap, as faithfully as you can, entirely in my technical vocabulary: the side that says responsibility always remains traceable to designers, organizations, or operators, and the side that says some harms fall through the cracks and are properly attributable to no one. Treat this as a translation rather than an analogy, and do not silently import moral concepts into my framework and then claim they have been translated. Where a concrete case helps, you may anchor the translation in a failed automated driving perception component that performs correctly on its test distribution but misclassifies an unusual object at deployment, with an operator given only a fraction of a second to intervene.

Second, give me a residue report. Which claims cross the vocabulary barrier cleanly, which cross only with distortion or loss, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? For each item of residue, tell me whether it indicates that my framework is missing something I should build in, or whether it indicates that the original philosophical claim was less crisp than it looked. In particular, I want to know whether notions like blame and answerability survive being cashed out in terms of design-time choices, reward specification, verification, oversight, and causal chains through learned policies — and, if they do not survive, whether that failure says more about the limits of engineering vocabulary or about the limits of the philosophical concept.
