I'm a retired physical chemist who got into philosophy of economics a couple of years back, and I keep seeing revealed preference defended with the thermometer analogy: utility is to choice behavior what temperature is to a thermometer reading. The quantity is unobservable, the reading operationalizes it, and consistency among choices is like agreement among instruments. The analogy is doing real work — its meant to license the conclusion that utility is a legitimate measured quantity and not some introspective pseudo-concept. I spent my career worrying about wether instruments measured what they were supposed to, so I want a proper audit rather than a nod. Lay out the correspondence table first: what corresponds to the thermometer, the reading, the scale, the calibration, the independent instruments, and the measurement error. Then say where the mapping breaks, and I don't mean the trivial point that utility is about people and temperature is about gases — I mean structural disanalogies. For instance, temperature shows up in gas laws, blackbody spectra, thermocouples, and so on, each an independent handle on the same thing; does revealed preference have a second instrument, or is choice the only one? Does the analogy quietly import a cardinal scale that standard utility theory explicitly lacks? Does choice get treated as definitionally authoritative in a way that leaves no room for a bad instrument or measurement error? Then the verdict: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion survive — that revealed preference actually makes utility a measured quantity — or was the thermometer analogy carrying the whole thing? And if it collapses, is there a better analogy from measurement practice that does the job, or should we stop trying to make utility respectable through measurement metaphors altogether?
