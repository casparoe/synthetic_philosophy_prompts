I organize a small reading group for people without any formal training in philosophy, and we are about to discuss George Orwell's Animal Farm. In preparation I have been reading Simone Weil, and I was struck by her insistence that the trouble with politics is not merely this or that bad party but the party form itself. As I understand her, she thinks a party is a machine for organizing collective passions around the pursuit of power; that it needs rivals or enemies in order to define itself; that it rewards loyalty to a line over independent judgment; and that it steadily absorbs individual conscience into group discipline, so that even a party with decent stated aims tends toward a kind of miniature totalitarianism. Reading the novel afterward, I saw this everywhere: the pigs' capture of the revolution, the way Snowball is rebuilt as an enemy, the hollowing-out of the animals' votes, the recited slogans, the rewritten commandments, and the final card game in which pigs and humans become indistinguishable. But I also kept feeling that the book might not fully bear out Weil's stronger claim, since Orwell seems to be attacking one-party dictatorship and the betrayal of a revolution, not the party form as such, and he plainly retained some allegiance to democratic socialism.

Could you give me a careful interpretation of Animal Farm through Weil's critique of political parties? I want the reading to run in both directions: where does the novel dramatize, sharpen, or complicate her ideas, and where does it resist them? Please do not reduce the book to an illustration of a theory, and do not stretch the theory to fit the book. I would especially value attention to scenes our group can actually turn to in our copies — the early Sunday-morning debates and the expulsion, the slogan, the confession-and-execution scene, and the ending card game — and an honest statement of what the novel shows that Weil's argument, taken as abstract argument, leaves out.
