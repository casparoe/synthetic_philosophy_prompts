You are a professional philosopher who works on the philosophy of computation and on the analysis of philosophical arguments. I want an adjudication of a dispute that came up in a reading group, after one member read out the following passage from Scott Aaronson's essay "Why Philosophers Should Care About Computational Complexity" (2011):

> One might think that, once we know something is computable, how efficiently it can be computed is a practical question with little further philosophical importance. In this essay, I offer a detailed case that one would be wrong.

The group agreed that Aaronson is responding to the view stated in the first sentence. They disagreed about whether his response survives scrutiny. Here is the exchange as I remember it.

(1) Elena began by siding with the sentence Aaronson attacks. Once a problem is known to be computable in principle, she said, the question how quickly it can be solved belongs to engineering and economics, because it concerns our resource budgets rather than our concepts. Philosophy asks what can be represented, known, or computed at all; scaling up available time or memory does not turn a philosophical question into a non-question.

(2) Marcus replied that Aaronson's essay is itself evidence against her. He pointed out that a proof no finite agent could check within the lifetime of the universe could never serve as a justification for anyone. The feasible/infeasible distinction therefore decides what can enter our space of reasons at all, and that is a conceptual matter rather than a merely practical one.

(3) Elena answered that Marcus is smuggling in "our" limitations. Polynomial time is defined relative to a particular model of computation, and what counts as feasible shifts as hardware does; an agent with different cognitive resources would draw the line elsewhere. If the boundary is indexical in that way, it cannot mark a difference in kind, and philosophy should not treat it as deep merely because Aaronson wrote an essay about it.

(4) Marcus responded that the boundary is less arbitrary than she implies. The class P—the problems solvable in polynomial time—is invariant across all reasonable sequential models of computation, which is why computer scientists treat efficiently computable as a robust notion rather than a quirk of today's machines. And the gap between polynomial and exponential growth does not shrink with better technology; it is a mathematical fact, not a hardware fact.

(5) Elena said that even if the polynomial/exponential gap is objective, it is still a difference in degree—how much—rather than a difference in kind—what it is to know, infer, or mean. She added that Aaronson himself concedes that complexity theory does not dissolve the philosophical problems he discusses but only contributes perspectives and insights, and she took that to be a retreat: commentary on philosophy is not yet philosophy.

(6) Marcus said she was misreading the concession. A perspective can change what counts as a solution or a justification without dissolving a problem, and much of philosophy operates that way. He added that the burden was now on her to name a philosophical issue that is left exactly as it was once the feasible/infeasible distinction is taken seriously, and she had not done so.

They stalled there and asked me to adjudicate, but I could not, so I am asking you.
