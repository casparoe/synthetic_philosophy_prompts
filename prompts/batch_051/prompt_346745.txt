Here is a passage whose precise role in the Epicurean argument about death I keep returning to. It is from Lucretius, De Rerum Natura, Book III, in William Ellery Leonard's translation:

And, even if time collected after death
The matter of our frames and set it all
Again in place as now, and if again
To us the light of life were given, O yet
That process too would not concern us aught,
When once the self-succession of our sense
Has been asunder broken.

I read Lucretius here as doing more than running the familiar "no subject, no harm" point. He is, I think, proposing a continuity criterion: a future state can concern me—can count as my survival, or as my being better or worse off—only if there is an unbroken self-succession of sense connecting me to that future. An exact recomposition of my matter would not satisfy the criterion, however faithfully it restored my shape, memories and dispositions, because the thread of sensation has been cut. I find this criterion compelling. It is simple; it explains why I do not regard the time before I existed as bad for me; it explains why I would not regard a perfect duplicate wheeled into existence two centuries after my death as me; and it underwrites the stronger Epicurean conclusion that death is not bad for the one who dies.

Yet I also have an intuition I cannot rid myself of, and it seems to contradict exactly this criterion. Suppose a nurse spends forty years writing a memoir, completes it, and dies in her sleep before it can be published. Her executor then destroys the only manuscript. I have the firm intuition that the destruction is bad for her—that it makes her actual life worse, not merely unfortunate for the survivors or contrary to what she would have wanted, and that she is wronged by an event occurring after her death. If Lucretius's criterion is right, nothing after the break in the self-succession of sense can be bad for her, so my intuition must be explained away as displaced grief, or as a moral illusion, or as a merely symbolic matter. I do not find any of those explanations convincing.
