I spent my career in a physiology lab and only got into philosophy after I retired, so I keep trying to turn philosophical claims into experimental questions. Here's the one I'm stuck on. Philosophers often say pain is intrisically bad: the badness is built into the feeling itself, not something your reaction adds later. Is that an empirical claim, a conceptual one, or some mixture of both?

The case that keeps bugging me is pain asymbolia. Some patients can tell you a stimulus hurts — they can point to roughly where it is and describe the quality as sharp or burning — but they don't show the usual distress or avoidance, and they say it doesn't actually bother them. The standard gloss is that the sensory part of the pain has come apart from the affective/motivational part.

So here's the assignment. Design the best test you can — a real experiment, a natural experiment, or a hypothetical intervention — that would distinguish the view that pain's badness is part of the sensation itself from the view that it's a normally automatic response to the sensation. Tell me exactly which data would count for each side. Then be honest about what a positive result would and wouldn't settle: if you can show the two come apart, does that force us to say the badness of ordinary pain is only a response, or does that last step require a bit of conceptual legislation that no data can supply?

Keep the whole thing under 400 words.
