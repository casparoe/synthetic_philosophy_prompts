I am pulled in two directions about the traditional doctrine of hell and want a careful verdict on which side should give way — or on how they can both be kept. The theory I find compelling is retributive and proportionalist: a penalty should answer to the gravity of the offense; an offense against a being of infinite dignity can differ in kind from any offense against a finite being; and if the gravity outruns every finite penalty, only a penalty without end is proportionate. That yields a clean, non-arbitrary explanation of why sin against God warrants something no human crime could. Yet I also hold a firm intuition I cannot shake: no finite creature, acting from finite understanding within a finite life, can make unending conscious torment a deserved response. Annihilation I can take seriously, and so can I take eternal loss or even universal reconciliation as a limit of mercy; what I cannot accept is endless active suffering as something the offense merits. Should the intuition be debunked — as a scale error, or as a refusal to take the infinite seriously — or should the theory be amended or abandoned? What exactly would justify each of those moves, and is there a reading on which the proportionality insight and the intuition both survive, for instance by letting the wrong justify exclusion from God while the endlessness arises from the creature's continuing will rather than from a multiplier applied to a finite act? State how confident you are in your conclusion and single out the one point you are least sure of.
