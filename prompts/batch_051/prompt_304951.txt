As someone who spent much of my working life in molecular biology before retiring and turning to early modern philosophy, I have been reading Descartes's Meditations with a mixture of admiration and unease. In the Second Meditation, shortly after the malicious demon has cast everything into doubt, Descartes reaches the point at which he holds that the proposition that he exists is necessarily true each time it is expressed by him or conceived in his mind. He then immediately cautions himself not to confuse the self whose existence has been secured with the ordinary idea of a human being, and he provisionally identifies it as a thing that thinks. That passage has always struck me as doing more work than it is usually given credit for, because it runs together an occurrent episode of thinking, the first-person indexical, and the thing that is said to do the thinking. My old habits make me want to perform a kind of conceptual knockout experiment and remove one of those components at a time.

I would like to see how much of the argument survives when they are pulled apart. Consider the following variation. Suppose the meditator, instead of asserting the first-person proposition from which the original argument moves, is allowed, at each moment of consciousness, only a bare demonstrative thought: that very thinking is occurring. Suppose further that these episodes are not connected by any memory of previous episodes; each is a momentary flash of thinking with no retrospective or prospective awareness. Now run Descartes's reasoning at a single moment. Does it still entitle the meditator to the existence of a thing that thinks, or only to the existence of an occurrent thinking? Then run it across the sequence of flashes. Can the argument, without appealing to anything beyond what is available in the modified scenario, establish a single persisting thinker, or does the risk arise that each flash secures only its own momentary subject? Finally, if something is lost in this variation, specify precisely which premise or move in the original argument under the standard Cartesian analysis is doing the work, and whether the loss is better understood as a defect of the variation or as a sign that the original argument was less transparent than it first appeared.
