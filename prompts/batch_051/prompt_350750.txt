You have been asked to give a single fifty-minute lecture to advanced doctoral students in statistics who are fluent in probability theory but have had no exposure to formal epistemology. The topic is the Principal Principle, understood roughly as the norm that a rational agent whose total evidence includes the fact that the objective chance of A is x, and includes no inadmissible information, should have credence x in A. Design the lecture. Specify the order in which the component ideas must be introduced and why each later idea depends on the earlier one; choose a single carrying example that runs through the whole session and say exactly what it must capture and what it might misleadingly suggest; list the misconceptions this audience is likely to bring or develop, and the point in the sequence at which each should be preempted; propose a final check that would distinguish students who have grasped the principle from those who can merely restate it; and state what you will deliberately refuse to resolve, such as whether objective chance should be taken as primitive or analyzed in terms of frequencies or best systems, and what to do about evidence that itself bears on what the chances are. The lecture must leave students able to say why the principle is a substantive norm on rational credence rather than either a definition of chance or a trivial consequence of conditionalization, and why admissible information cannot simply be equated with information about the past. Begin with a one-sentence bottom line stating the central idea around which the lecture is organized, and then give the full plan with your reasoning; do not preface the answer or restate the question.
