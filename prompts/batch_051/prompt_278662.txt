My colleague keeps pressing an argument he says is just Quinn's self-torturer, and I can't find the exit. A person can move along a long sequence of settings, from no pain at all to a final setting of severe, unrelenting pain. Advancing one step makes the pain only imperceptibly worse but pays a fixed sum that is clearly worth taking in isolation. The argument runs:

(1) For any two adjacent settings, the later one is all-things-considered rationally preferable to the earlier one: the added pain is imperceptible, and the money is a genuine gain.
(2) Rational preference is transitive: if setting A is rationally preferable to B, and B to C, then A is rationally preferable to C.
(3) Therefore the final setting is rationally preferable to the initial one.
(4) But the final setting is not rationally preferable to the initial one: by hypothesis, the person would refuse a direct choice between no pain/no money and the final setting's severe pain plus accumulated money.
(5) So either rational preference is intransitive, or some adjacent step is not rationally preferable after all — which seems to plant a sharp cutoff in a sequence where every adjacent pair feels the same.

My colleague concludes that rational preference must be allowed to be intransitive. I want to keep transitivity, and I also don't want to deny that the money settles matters at any single step. Which premise do I reject, and what does rejecting it really cost? In particular, does rejecting (1) force me to say there is a first step where an imperceptible difference suddenly matters, or is there a third option?
