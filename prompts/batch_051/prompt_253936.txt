Last night I spent two hours arguing with a friend who runs randomized clinical trials, and I need to turn that argument into something I can circulate before my committee meets on Thursday. I serve on the research ethics committee of a teaching hospital. We are reviewing a proposed randomized trial in relapsed aggressive B-cell lymphoma. Patients who fail first-line therapy are typically offered one of two salvage regimens, X and Y. X is older, more toxic, and longer-studied. Y is better tolerated, and over the past few years a growing share of oncologists have adopted it as their default on the strength of uncontrolled series and registry comparisons; no randomized comparison has been done. The trial would randomize eligible patients 1:1 between X and Y, with overall survival as the primary endpoint. Crossover after progression is permitted, so in practice the trial compares two treatment policies, X-then-Y against Y-then-X, rather than two isolated drugs.

The committee is split. Several co-investigators now prescribe Y almost exclusively and have stopped offering X outside the trial; they would be enrolling patients into an arm they no longer use in ordinary practice. One faction says this is straightforwardly unethical: these investigators lack individual equipoise, since each has a settled preference. The other faction replies that individual equipoise is the wrong standard and unattainable here; the community as a whole is genuinely divided and uncertain, and blocking the trial will simply let the field finish an unplanned, low-quality experiment without consent or public accounting. My friend is sure the second faction is right and that blocking the trial would be moral timidity dressed up as concern for patients.

Draft the committee's decision memo. Recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions, including any time frame for reassessment; be specific enough that the investigators and the dissenting committee members could both respond to the reasoning; and take seriously the strongest consideration on the losing side. In the memo, address two further questions explicitly: first, whether the committee should distinguish the ethics of approving the trial from the ethics of an individual investigator with a settled preference enrolling their own patients; and second, whether the fact that the real comparison is X-then-Y versus Y-then-X, not X versus Y alone, changes the analysis. End with a short paragraph stating how confident you are in the recommendation and singling out the specific point you are least sure of.
