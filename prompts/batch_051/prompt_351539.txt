I am a high school student, and over the past year I have settled into a fixed method for deciding where I stand on contested questions — whether a school policy is fair, whether a moral argument in class is sound, whether a historical claim is well supported. I start by writing down the specific cases about which I have the strongest immediate judgments, then look for general principles that fit those cases, and whenever a principle and a case conflict I revise whichever I am less confident in, repeating until everything coheres. Only then do I regard my view as justified. A friend says I have reinvented reflective equilibrium, and that because both the case judgments and the principles come from me, the whole procedure simply launders whatever I already believed. Is my policy defensible as an everyday epistemic habit, or is my friend right? Under what conditions does moving back and forth between cases and principles actually correct bias rather than entrench it, what signals from the inside would let me tell which is happening, and is there a revised version of the procedure that keeps its benefits while protecting against the laundering failure?
