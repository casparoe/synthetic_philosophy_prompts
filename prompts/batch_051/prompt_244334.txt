I'm a teaching assistant preparing a single seventy-five-minute class on Keynes's distinction between probability and the weight of evidence in A Treatise on Probability, for third-year philosophy students who have already done Bayesian confirmation theory but have never read Keynes. Don't explain the distinction to me; plan how I should teach it. In Chapter VI of the Treatise, Keynes describes cases in which a proposition has a determinate probability on the available evidence, yet the body of evidence is small or impoverished; he calls this second dimension the "weight" of the argument and suggests that two equally probable conclusions may differ in how rational it is to rely on them, because in one case the probability rests on a substantial body of evidence and in the other on very little. I want the students to leave with a precise sense of what this second dimension is and why the Bayesian framework they have learned does not automatically accommodate it.

For the session plan, I need: the exact order of components and what each depends on; the one carrying example that can do the most work, and what it must avoid so that it doesn't simply reduce weight to sample size or to confidence intervals; the misconceptions students will predictably form — I suspect at least one is that weight is just a second-order probability about one's own confidence, and another is that if weight matters, probability must be supplemented with an extra number in every rational choice — and the exact moment in the sequence to head each of these off; what to say about whether Keynes's distinction is consistent with their Bayesian commitments or marks a genuine gap; and one final question that would tell me whether they have understood the weight of evidence as a logical feature of the relation between evidence and conclusion, rather than as a psychological feeling of hesitation.
