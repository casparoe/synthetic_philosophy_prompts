I am a novelist, and there is a device I keep reaching for in my prose without knowing whether it has a recognized name. It begins with an assumption so deeply built into ordinary language that it passes unnoticed: one side of a human contrast is treated as the plain, unadorned case, while the other side can be referred to only with an added word, prefix, or qualifier. The device is to invert that arrangement inside a story, usually without comment, so that the side once treated as the familiar default becomes the one that must be flagged every time it appears, and the side once treated as the exception is picked out by the bare, unmodified term.

Two instances may make it clearer. In one story, I imagined a world in which the ordinary word for a human being has come to denote only one sex; the other sex is always designated by an extra syllable, and I wrote a long scene in which precisely the reverse holds, so that the reader's own home language suddenly comes to seem parochial rather than neutral. In another, I did the same with a prestige dialect: the language of the court is simply the speech of one class, while the other class's speech is always treated as a dialect requiring a special label, and then the story flips which class gets the unadorned term.

I know this overlaps with satire, and with the broader idea of making the familiar strange, but I am not asking about either of those in general. I am asking about the specific move of swapping which side of a contrast needs the linguistic scaffolding. Does that move have a name? Whether it does or not, I am less interested in a bare label than in the carving: where the boundary falls between this device and ordinary parody or a mere thought experiment, when it fails into heavy-handedness or a cheap joke, and what a precise, defensible name for it would be if none already exists.
