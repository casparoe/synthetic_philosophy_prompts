Take your time and think it through step by step. I'd like you to start by breaking down the possible interpretations of the clause below before you settle on one, because I care more about what each reading commits us to than about the final vote.

I'm a trustee at Pemberton College, a small liberal-arts campus. In 1893 the college accepted a bronze statue of Josiah Pemberton, the mill owner whose money effectively underwrote the institution, subject to a deed of gift that runs like this:

"The College shall at all times keep and maintain the bronze statue of the said Josiah Pemberton upon the South Lawn, and shall not remove, alter, re-site, or suffer the same to be removed, altered, or re-sited; provided always that if the said statue shall have fallen into irreparable decay, or if the said South Lawn shall be required for the erection of a building for the instruction of the students of the College, the College may remove the said statue and re-site it in some other suitable and prominent place within the College grounds."

Everyone assumed the decay clause meant bronze disease and the building clause meant new lecture halls. What nobody foresaw: a historian in our archives has found the mill's first ledgers, and the fortune was built on enslaved labour in the Atlantic cotton trade, with Pemberton himself financing the parliamentary lobbying that delayed abolition by years. So now there's a campaign to move the statue into a small museum with contextualising panels. The campaigners have got clever about the building clause: they've raised funds for a "Centre for the Study of the Atlantic Slave Trade and Its Legacies" to be built exactly on the South Lawn, so that the statute is removed under the very clause meant to keep it prominent. The old guard on the board says the clause plainly permits removal only for a new lecture building, not for a building whose point is to repudiate the man the clause exists to honour; the activists reply that the words don't specify what kind of instruction must happen inside the building, and that if the drafters didn't see this coming, they're entitled to read the clause by what it actually says.

How should I think about whether the clause reaches this? I'm especially interested in whether this is even an "unforeseen case" at all, or whether it's the exact future the drafters were trying to bind when they wrote "at all times" — and what it would mean to treat a legal instruction to honour someone as binding once honour has turned to shame.
