Discuss the roles of kamma and intention (cetanā) in early Buddhism, and note any important internal diversity in the tradition."

Student answer:

"Buddhist ethics is at bottom a consequentialist enterprise. The First Noble Truth diagnoses existence as pervaded by dukkha, and the whole point of the path is to bring about the cessation of suffering—not just for oneself but for all sentient beings. The Mahāyāna bodhisattva ideal makes this explicit: the bodhisattva defers nirvāṇa to keep working for the welfare of others, which is just welfare-maximization across all beings. Even the precepts should be read this way: they are not absolute rules but general guides that hold because following them tends to produce the best consequences. That is why some Mahāyāna texts permit a bodhisattva to violate a precept, even to kill, when it would prevent greater suffering—a straightforward act-consequentialist calculation.

The doctrine of kamma confirms this reading. Kamma is a natural causal law by which wholesome actions ripen in pleasant results and unwholesome ones ripen in painful results, across lifetimes. So the universe literally rewards actions in proportion to the good consequences they produce and punishes them in proportion to the bad ones. An action's moral status is therefore fixed by its consequences, exactly as consequentialism claims. Some interpreters have even classified early Buddhism as a kind of act-utilitarianism on these grounds.

The role of intention does not undermine this picture. In the early texts, intention determines whether an action is kammically wholesome or unwholesome, but this is best understood as an epistemic shortcut: since agents cannot calculate every long-term consequence, the Buddha used intention as a rough proxy for an action's likely outcome. The ultimate test, however, remains the outcome. So the internal diversity between Theravāda's training rules and Mahāyāna's compassion-based exceptions is really just two applications of a single underlying consequentialist principle."
