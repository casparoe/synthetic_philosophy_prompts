You are a professional philosopher who works on formal epistemology and the theory of self-locating belief. Think carefully, and before you settle on a diagnosis, brainstorm several candidate ways of understanding the impasse I describe, then work through which one best fits the actual arguments.

Here is the case. In the Sleeping Beauty problem, Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday. A fair coin is tossed. If it lands heads, she is woken once, on Monday, interviewed, then put back to sleep until Wednesday. If it lands tails, she is woken twice, on Monday and Tuesday, and interviewed each time; after Monday's interview she is given a drug that erases her memory of that waking, so Tuesday's waking feels exactly like Monday's. On Wednesday she wakes and the experiment ends. She knows all these rules perfectly in advance. When she finds herself awake during an interview, what credence should she assign to the proposition that the coin landed heads?

The disagreement I want you to diagnose is the long-running one between thirders and halfers. Thirders say the answer is 1/3, because on waking Beauty should regard herself as uncertainly situated among three subjectively indistinguishable awakenings, only one of which accompanies heads. Halfers say the answer is 1/2, because the coin is fair and waking gives Beauty no new information that discriminates heads from tails—she would be woken at least once either way. Both sides seem to accept the same stated facts of the case, and both sides accept the ordinary probability calculus; yet the debate has barely moved in decades.

My reading group has reached the same impasse. We agree on the setup, and each side can state the other side's argument perfectly well, but neither side's confidence has shifted at all. My own hunch is that the dispute is not really about the coin, but about what a credence is even keyed to: whether waking evidence should be modeled as a set of possible worlds or as a set of centered worlds, and whether a principle of indifference may be applied across subjectively indistinguishable awakenings. But I am not sure that is right.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a substantive disagreement about rational self-locating belief, a verbal dispute about the word "credence," two camps answering different questions under one heading, or a sign that the original question is ill-posed? Base your diagnosis on what halfers and thirders actually say—the arguments I sketched and the more developed forms of them you know.
