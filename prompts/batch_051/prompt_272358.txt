You're a political philosopher who works on distributive justice. I've been going around in circles on this argument against luck egalitarianism, and I need someone to tell me exactly where it fails, if it fails.

Here's the argument as I keep hearing it sharpened:

(1) Luck egalitarianism says an inequality is unjust when it's traceable to brute luck rather then to the choices of the person who ends up worse off.
(2) A person who freely buys a lottery ticket and loses ends up poorer because of the draw of the lottery.
(3) The draw of the lottery, considered from the gambler's point of view, isn't under their control; it's brute luck that the ticket lost.
(4) So the gambler's ending up worse off is traceable to brute luck.
(5) Therefore luck egalitarianism is committed to compensating the losing gambler just as much as the victim of a sudden, uninsurable natural disaster.
(6) But that's absurd: if you choose the gamble, you own the loss. So luck egalitarianism is false.

I half-suspect the issue is in the move from (1) and (3) to (4), but I can't say why without smuggling in some ex ante reading of responsiblity. Is the argument valid? If not, which exact step fails, and what's the corrected version of the claim? If the argument is valid on the best reading, then say instead what a luck egalitarian should give up.

Give me your answer in these sections, in this order:
- "The diagnosis" (about 150 words): where exactly the argument does or doesn't bite, and which step is load-bearing.
- "The luck egalitarian's best reply" (about 250 words): the strongest version of the view that survives, and which premise it rejects or reinterprets.
- "Your verdict" (about 100 words): whether the objection succeeds against the best version of luck egalitarianism.
