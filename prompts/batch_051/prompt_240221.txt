I am a novelist, and I have noticed that I apply a consistent rule whenever I read history or listen to people explain why the world took the shape it has. I discount any explanation whose main causal weight falls on named persons or on discrete events. A friend ascribes the French Revolution to Robespierre's vanity, or the spread of a religion to a single ruler's conversion, and I privately file the account as anecdote: not exactly false, but surface. Before I take an explanation seriously, I require that it be restated in terms of slow, impersonal structures — coastlines and rivers, climate and soil, demographic pressure, the carrying capacity of land, the drift of institutions over decades or centuries. I push the same reflex into smaller cases: I will sooner credit an account of a war's outcome that runs through logistics, terrain, and manpower than one that turns on a general's choice on a single afternoon. I absorbed this stance from Braudel's way of looking at history, and it feels like a discipline against storytelling. But I can also see that a novelist who builds plots from exactly these slow forces has a private interest in finding the great-man plot naive. Is this rule a defensible epistemic habit, or an aesthetic preference dressed up as a method? Is there a version of the rule that keeps the discipline against anecdote without the overcorrection — one that tells me, precisely, when an event or an individual may be allowed back into the explanation?
