I've been trying to get my views on betting and long-run rationality straightened out, and I'd like someone to check the whole set for tension before I commit to it. Here's where I've landed.

(a) In any single decision, I'm a plain expected-utility maximiser — I choose the option with the highest expected utility, and I have no special attachment to log utility in particular.

(b) But when I'm offered the same favourable gamble repeatedly and can reinvest, I think the fraction of my bankroll to stake is the one that maximises expected log wealth. That is, I should bet full Kelly each round.

(c) What convinces me about (b) is the long-run argument: a full-Kelly bettor ends up with more wealth than someone who always stakes any different fixed fraction, with probability approaching one as the number of rounds grows.

(d) I also think a one-off favourable gamble should get whatever fraction the repeated version would get. No bet is truly one-off — it's always one round in a longer, although messier, sequence of financial decisions.

(e) Even so, in real life I'd bet half Kelly, not full Kelly. Full Kelly's drawdowns would make me too nervous, and I suspect I'd abandon the strategy at the worst possible time.

(f) More broadly, I'm convinced that for wealth that compounds, the arithmetic mean is the wrong quantity to track; it gets inflated by a few enormous outcomes. The geometric mean — equivalently expected log wealth — tracks what a typical path actually does.
