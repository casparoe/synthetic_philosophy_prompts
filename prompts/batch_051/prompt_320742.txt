so i'm a backend engineer at a small saas company and i got asked to give the five-minute toast at our twenty-fifth anniversary party next month. around two hundred people: early employees, the two cofounders and their families, plus a big block of newer hires who don't know the lore. the thing is, everyone has the founding story memorized. something like "the three of us rewrote the core engine from scratch in one 96-hour weekend and that demo saved the company." it's on our about page, there's a framed photo of the supposed napkin sketch in the lobby.

the problem is last year i lead the svn-to-git migration and had to clean up the repo history. the actual first working commit of the core engine wasn't from that weekend. it was six weeks later, on a wednesday afternoon, by an intern who quit two months after and is now a real estate agent. the famous weekend gave us a branch called scratch that sat there half dead until someone deleted it in 2011. the napkin photo's timestamps are wrong too.

so now i have to stand there and toast the company in front of the people who built the thing and their families, and i can't just recite the about page, but its also not a code review and the founders' kids will be there. underneath it i keep going back and forth on a question i don't have words for: when what the community remembers and what the record shows come apart, what's supposed to give way? can the story do one kind of work and the record another, or does the record eventually have to win? i want that question to be the spine of the toast, not a final caveat. i want the whole thing to be warm and actually funny, not a whistleblower sting, but i also don't want to stand up and say things i now know aren't true. write it so it lands for both halves of the room, and maybe so the people who already know the real history can tell that i know too, without me blowing up the party.
