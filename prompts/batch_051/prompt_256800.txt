I'm taking a course on the ethics of consumption and we've spent the past week on Thoreau's Walden, mostly the "Economy" chapter. I have to write a short paper defining "voluntary simplicity," and I keep tripping over this sentance:

"Most men, even in this comparatively free country, through mere ignorance and mistake, are so occupied with the factitious cares and superfluously coarse labors of life that its finer fruits cannot be plucked by them."

What's throwing me is that Thoreau seems to be doing more than saying "own less stuff." He's drawing a line between factitious or superfluous things and the "finer fruits" of life, and claiming that the first kind of busyness can actually keep you from the second. So I'm trying to build a working definition of voluntary simplicity that respects that distinction, and I want to test it against a list of cases my friends and I argued about: a Trappist monk who takes a vow of poverty; a single mother working two jobs who owns almost nothing because she can't afford more; a software engineer who lives on $30k a year and saves the rest so he can retire at 40; someone with depression who stops buying things and lets their apartment go bare because they feel they don't deserve anything nice; a minimalist who owns very few but very expensive objects; a hoarder; and a person who grows most of their own food and barters but still earns a normal middle-class income. Can you give me a definition that sorts these correctly, or at least tells me which ones are genuinely borderline? And where exactly does Thoreau's sentence force the line to be drawn — does the motive for having less matter, or just the having less itself? Which of my verdicts should I be least confident in? Please answer in American English.
