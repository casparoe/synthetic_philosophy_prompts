In a moral psychology seminar I have been returning to Bernard Williams's example of the man who must choose between saving his wife and a stranger, both drowning, and who saves his wife. Williams's worry is not with the act but with the deliberation that might accompany it: if the man's motivating thought were something like, she is my wife, and in situations of this kind it is morally permissible to favour one's own, the second, justificatory thought would be one thought too many. The outward action is unchanged, but the presence of that thought is supposed to show — or partly constitute — an estrangement from the relationship, a failure of unmediated engagement, and a threat to the agent's integrity. The claim is at once conceptual and psychological: a feature of the agent's deliberative field is said to degrade the value of an action or relationship even when it makes no behavioral difference.

The seminar has split between those who treat this as an important truth about the ethics of intimacy and those who regard it as an empirically empty piece of armchair moral psychology. I want to know how much of the dispute could be brought under experimental pressure. Design the strongest concrete test you can of the claim that the presence of a justificatory thought of the relevant kind — as opposed to the act, its outcome, or the simple absence of that thought — changes or reveals something defective in the action or relationship. Say exactly what you would manipulate or measure, what control or comparison would separate the claim from nearby deflationary hypotheses (for example, that we merely disapprove of people who over-deliberate, or that slower deliberation primes perceived coldness), and what pattern of results would count for and against the claim. Then be explicit about the residue: what a favourable result could not establish about whether the thought is cause or symptom, whether relationships in which it never occurs are genuinely better, and whether third-party judgments or behavioral proxies can reach the first-person significance the claim asserts.
