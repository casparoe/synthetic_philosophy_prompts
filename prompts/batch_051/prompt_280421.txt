I spent my working life in the natural sciences, chiefly retinal physiology and visual psychophysics, and have only recently begun to read philosophy of mind seriously. One topic I keep circling back to is the idea of forbidden colours. The textbook account, going back to Hering's opponent-process theory, is that certain hues are impossible: nothing can look at once reddish and greenish, or at once bluish and yellowish, because early colour vision consists of opponent channels—red versus green, and blue versus yellow—and a single channel cannot be in both of its opponent states simultaneously. Yet a celebrated experiment from the early 1980s reported that observers viewing stabilized red and green fields, with an apparatus that prevented small eye movements from refreshing the boundary, sometimes described a hue that was simultaneously reddish and greenish. The claim has become part of the standard literature, but its replication record seems to me shakier than the textbook treatment suggests, and it remains unclear whether the impossibility belongs to the earliest opponent channels or to the larger geometry of colour experience. The question has also outgrown ordinary psychophysics: with current neural-interface methods it becomes natural to ask whether an unusual pattern of retinal or cortical stimulation could produce not merely an unfamiliar colour within the existing colour solid, but a genuinely novel quality—a new dimension of colour experience rather than a new point in the old one.

Suppose I were able to direct a three-year project on this cluster of questions, staffed by one capable postdoc and equipped with a modest psychophysics and computational budget. I can see at least four lines of work. First, conceptual groundwork: what evidence or criterion would justify saying that some experience is a genuinely novel quale rather than an unfamiliar blend, a simultaneous presentation, or a relabelling of familiar qualities? Second, replication and systematization: with modern displays and eye trackers, can the stabilized-image reports of reddish green be reproduced, and under exactly which conditions? Third, theoretical modelling: what kind of opponent architecture actually forbids a reddish-green experience, and do the constraints operate at the peripheral channel level or in later cortical colour computations? Fourth, neural-interface exploration: what would a stimulation experiment in retina or cortex have to show before one could responsibly claim to have opened a new sensory dimension? I am not asking for a survey of the literature. I want a dependency-aware prioritization: which of these lines of work, or which combination of them, should come first for that three-year project, and why? Please defend the ordering by stating the criteria you are using—importance, tractability, neglectedness, or how many other questions depend on the answer—and say what would change the ranking.
