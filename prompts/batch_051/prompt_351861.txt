I keep coming back to the thought that The Grapes of Wrath is actually doing Braudelian history in novel form, and I want to test that against a careful reading rather than just assert it. For an essay I'm writing: read Steinbeck's novel through Braudel's distinction between the longue durée (the slow, almost unchanging structures — climate, soil, geography, population, the routes people travel), the medium-run cycles of prices and institutions, and the event, the brief flash of politics and individual action. Braudel thought historians overrate the event and underrate those slow structures, and the novel famously alternates between the Joads' particular story and intercallary chapters about dust, tractors, banks, and the road itself. So my question is whether the novel really belongs to the longue durée. What does it get right about the way slow material conditions shape a family's choices, and where does it resist Braudel — for instance in the way it treats Ma Joad's refusal to let the family disintegrate, or Jim Casy's death, or Rose of Sharon's final gesture? I want a reading that is honest in both directions: it shouldn't reduce Braudel to a fancy label for the idea that the Joads are just victims of big forces, and it shouldn't pretend the novel is all about individual agency when so much of it is about weather, soil, and foreclosure. If you think the novel ends up showing that Braudel's three levels can't be pulled apart in practice, say that, and point to moments (broadly, no need for exact quotations) where the event and the longue durée interpenetrate. Mostly I want to know what the novel adds to the philosophy of history that Braudel's categories on their own don't capture.
