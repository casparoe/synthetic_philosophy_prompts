I've been reading a lot of narrative history lately — the outbreak of the First World War, the fall of the Roman republic, that kind of thing — and I keep tripping over a pattern I don't have a word for. A historian will list five or six causes of some enormous event and talk as if each one, on its own, would have been enough: the war was coming because of the alliance system, and also because of militarism, and also because of economic rivalry, and then, seperately, the assassination at Sarajevo was the spark that lit it. But if you press them on any single cause, they back off and say the event would probably have happened anyway through the other channels; so no one cause was really indispensable. Then they turn around and write a whole chapter about why the trigger mattered enormously.

What puzzles me is this. If several causes are each sufficient, then none of them is necessary, and the ordinary notion of a cause goes wobbly: why call the assassination a cause at all if the war was coming anyway? Yet the historians don't want to give up either side — the event was almost bound to happen, and the particular spark still mattered. And I can't keep this straight from a second configuration, where each of several causes is needed and only all of them together are enough, so that taking any one away prevents the event. That second one sounds like the but-for test I half remember from tort law, and it seems nearly the opposite of the first, but historians slide between the two without flagging the difference.

Is there an established name for the first configuration — several independent causes, each sufficient, none necessary — and does it actually fit what historians are doing, or do their explanations only look that way because the counterfactuals are left vague? I'm not after just a label. I want the carving: how this differs from the but-for configuration, whether it has distinct sub-types (for example, cases where the independent causes all operate at once versus cases where one is merely waiting in reserve), and how you'd tell in an actual historical case whether you're looking at redundant causes or just an under-specified counterfactual. If there's no settled name, or the nearest phrase only half fits, say so and either way define it precisely enough that I can place border cases.

Answer in continuous prose like a letter, please; no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
