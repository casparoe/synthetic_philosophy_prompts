In a language-and-cognition reading group I help run, the same argument has been circling for months. We have been discussing how an English speaker actually forms the past tense of a verb. One side holds that there is a literal stored symbolic rule — something like add -d or -ed by default — and that irregular forms such as went, brought, and sang are memorized exceptions that block the rule. This side points to children who pass through a phase of saying goed and bringed after earlier using went and brought, and to novel verbs like wug, which almost everyone turns into wugged. The other side holds that there is no stored rule at all: both regular and irregular forms come from a single associative system, whether a neural network or an analogy-to-exemplars memory, and regularity is just what a frequent, phonologically broad pattern looks like from the outside. On this account, wug becomes wugged because it resembles many stored regular items, and children say goed because that pattern is currently stronger than the stored irregular form.

The infuriating thing is that neither position can be pinned down. Overregularization, U-shaped development, the treatment of novel words, frequency effects, and the regularization felt when a verb is used as a technical or denominal term — the outfielder flied out, not flew out; the cursor moused over the file — are each produced by one side as a decisive result and then absorbed by the other with a plausible auxiliary assumption. Suppletion pushes the issue further: went is historically the past tense of a different verb, wend, so no rule operating on go could produce it; it simply has to be listed. Yet the associative side counters that a high-frequency suppletive pair is only the endpoint of the memorization continuum. Sound change adds a further twist: pairs like keep/kept and think/thought look irregular today but descend from an earlier regular pattern made opaque by later sound changes, which one side takes to show that irregularity is the wrong category, while the other insists history does not tell us what a present-day speaker computes.

I suspect the standoff is really two disputes under one heading. One is largely verbal — about what rule means: a symbolically explicit combinatorial operation, or a statistical and analogical pattern. The other is a difference about what the question is — about the abstract grammar the speaker knows, or about the real-time processing that produces forms. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself rather than a verdict for one side. Which parts are verbal, which parts are about standards of explanation or about which data count, and is there a hidden empirical or formal question underneath? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress: a decisive experiment, a formal result, a new distinction, a change of question — or should we expect no progress because the original question is ill-posed rather than merely hard?
