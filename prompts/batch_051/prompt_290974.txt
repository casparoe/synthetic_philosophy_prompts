I work as a journalist covering science and ideas, and I also run a monthly project review for my outlet's long-form work: reported features, podcast series, and occasional data investigations. The decisions I make in that review are high-stakes and recurring: each month I look at five to eight in-progress projects and decide which to continue, which to re-scope, and which to kill, with the managing editor signing off on every kill. Continuing a project consumes scarce budget and writer time that could fund several smaller stories; killing one writes off completed reporting and can damage relationships with writers and sources. The review runs under real constraints: roughly thirty minutes per project, no shared economics training among the people in the room, and a reasoning trail the managing editor can audit later.

The intellectual difficulty is the sunk cost effect. By the time a project appears before me, we have usually spent months of reporting and a material share of the freelance budget, and the strongest argument offered for continuing is often that we have already put too much into it to stop. I accept the standard principle in one form: costs that cannot be recovered should not count simply because they were paid. But I also accept its more demanding corollary, which is harder to run in practice: past spending may still legitimately influence a decision when it changes the future through some forward-looking channel — as information about how hard the project will actually be, as a commitment that affects what collaborators and sources will do, as a reputation effect, or as option value — but never as a bare reason of the form that stopping would waste what was already spent.

Please turn that principle, together with its corollary, into a reusable decision procedure I can run at each monthly review: a checklist, a set of ordered tests, or a flowchart in words. It should be usable by a colleague who has never read a word of decision theory, and it should force the distinction between bare sunk-cost reasoning and legitimate forward-looking reasoning instead of merely substituting one slogan for another. In particular, it should make me separate three questions we habitually collapse: whether the project is still urgent; whether its expected value from here is positive; and whether continuing is the best use of the next dollar rather than merely better than nothing. It should also catch the failure mode I see most often in my own thinking: past overruns get reinterpreted as evidence that the project matters, when they are usually evidence that the project is harder than we estimated. And it should test whether a new editor who had not launched the project would make the same call, so that my need to justify my own earlier decision is separated from the project's present prospects.

I would also like the procedure to be honest about its own limits. Mark each step that is genuinely a judgment call rather than an estimation of fact, and say where the procedure should hand the decision to a person — for example, where the question is how much a reputational risk is worth running, or how to value work that is unlikely to break even but may serve readers in ways we cannot price. State up front what the procedure cannot do: it cannot make the decision for me, and it should not pretend that a decision already made is an analysis.

1. The Procedure (around 35%): a numbered sequence of steps or tests to be run in order. For each step, give the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to pass it, where the procedure terminates with kill or continue, and which steps are flagged as judgment calls rather than factual checks.
2. Why Each Step Is There (around 15%): for each step, a brief justification connecting it to the principle and its corollary — how it blocks bare sunk-cost reasoning while letting in information, commitment, reputation, and option value.
3. Worked Applications (around 35%): apply the procedure to three fictional but realistic projects. Include one over-budget investigation where the procedure says to continue for a forward-looking reason; one widely promised series where the procedure says to continue mainly because killing it would damage the outlet's credibility with sources or readers; and one project I am personally attached to where the procedure says to kill it — in that last case, say what I would likely resist about the reasoning and why the procedure stands by its answer.
4. Where the Procedure Fails (around 15%): the cases it will classify wrongly or leave ambiguous, the information it assumes that I will often lack, and the respects in which it falls short of actual decision-making.
