I have begun keeping a list of my cousin's verdicts about what counts as art. There are enough of them now that they must fit some principle, but I cannot extract it from her, and several different principles look as if they would fit. Here are the verdicts, all stated firmly:

- A faithful oil painting of a landscape, made by an art student as a copying exercise: art.
- A plain galvanized bucket bought at a hardware store, unaltered, placed on a white pedestal in a gallery with a title by an exhibitor she has never heard of: art.
- A piece of driftwood that happens to resemble a kneeling human figure, left where the tide dropped it: not art.
- That same piece of driftwood, still untouched, placed on a plinth with an identifying card by a local art society at its annual show: art.
- An elaborately decorated, technically astonishing wedding cake made by a master pastry chef: not art.
- A poem generated by a computer program, where the person credited as author produced a thousand drafts, selected one without changing a word, and published it in a small poetry journal: art.
- A mathematical proof that professional mathematicians call beautiful: not art.
- A visually striking and technically innovative highway billboard made to advertise a car: not art.

The case I want to use to settle it is this. Our neighbour, who has no art training, no gallery connections, and has never made a work of art, takes an ordinary metal folding chair from his kitchen, does not alter it, and sets it by the curb in front of his house with a hand-lettered sign asking passersby to look at it the way they would look at a public sculpture. She laughed and said she would have to think about it.

Predict her verdict, and say which of the eight earlier cases is the closest analog, including whether that analog weighs for or against calling the chair art. State at least two candidate rules that fit all eight earlier verdicts equally well, and spell out what each rule predicts for the folding chair. Then give your best guess about which rule she is actually running, and why. Finally, name one further case, different from the folding chair, that would force the same distinction, and say what each remaining rule predicts for it.
