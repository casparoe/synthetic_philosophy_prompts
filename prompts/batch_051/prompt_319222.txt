I’ve been reading Augustine’s City of God, Book 18, Chapter 43, and I am trying to figure out how far his argument about the Septuagint is supposed to reach. He writes:

“For the same Spirit who was in the prophets when they spoke these things was also in the seventy men when they translated them, so that assuredly they could also say something else, just as if the prophet himself had said both, because it would be the same Spirit who said both; and could say the same thing differently, so that, although the words were not the same, yet the same meaning should shine forth to those of good understanding; and could omit or add something, so that even by this it might be shown that there was in that work not human bondage, which the translator owed to the words, but rather divine power, which filled and ruled the mind of the translator.”

Now consider a case Augustine could not have pictured. A translation team is preparing the first complete Old Testament in a signed language. The language has no written form, and its grammar is spatial and visual. To render a Hebrew sentence, the translators must commit themselves to information the Hebrew leaves unspecified—for example, the relative position of participants, whether an action is repeated, or how a speaker’s gaze is directed. The team is not a body of seventy scholars chosen by a high priest; it is a mixed committee of deaf and hearing linguists, exegetes, and interpreters, working by consensus under an editorial policy that treats the source text as norm and the signed rendering as subordinate. They pray for the Spirit’s guidance but explicitly deny that their product is inspired in the way Augustine says the Seventy were.

My question is not whether the signed translation is useful or accurate in practical terms. I want to know whether Augustine’s principle has anything to say to them. If “the same Spirit” could cause the Seventy to say the same thing differently, or to add and omit, then why couldn’t that same Spirit exercise a similar freedom in this case? Is that line of reasoning limited to the Seventy, or to translation from Hebrew into Greek, or to translations made under particular ecclesial authority? What would Augustine have to say if asked about a translation whose medium forces additions and omissions far more pervasive than anything the Septuagint displays? And if his principle did carry over, would the team’s disavowal of inspiration be an error, or a faithful refusal to claim something Augustine himself might not have extended to them? I’d like you to work through what the words, Augustine’s purpose in the chapter, what he would likely say if asked, and what a reasonable reader would take him to mean would each yield, and where they pull apart.
