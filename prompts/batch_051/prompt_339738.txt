I've ended up defending a view about what I ought to do, and I want you to try to break it rather than patch it. The view: the thing I should do in any concrete situation is whatever a version of me would settle on if that version had all the relevant facts, could vividly feel what each option would actually be like, wasn't rushed, exhausted, scared, or angling for their own advantage, and had mulled it over long enough that the conclusion no longer moved around. So it's an 'what I'd choose under ideal conditions' test. But the idealized version is still supposed to be me, not a perfectly impartial spectator or someone who just applies a rule: the whole point is that the test inherits my cares but straightens out my thinking. I take this as a general picture of how I should decide, including moral and prudential choices.

What I want is a map of the position's exposure: what would have to be true for this to be the wrong test for me to use, or for there to be no fact of the matter about what it recommends. I don't mean only the familiar circularity worry, that the idealization already encodes which facts are 'relevant' and what stability means—I want that too, plus the cases that could actually embarrass the view. For instance, which version of me gets to count as the ideal one, and along which dimensions is the idealization allowed to change me before the answer stops being mine? Are there cases where the test gives no answer, or where the answer it gives is wrong by lights I already accept, or where idealizing far enough just turns me into a different person? Does the view need a further fact—about what I'd want under idealization—and if that fact doesn't exist, is the test inventing the answer by choosing the idealization procedure? And there's the self-reference point: to know how to idealize, do I need the ideal version of me to already have run the same test, and does that lead to a regress or just to a harmless fixed point?

Sort the defeaters from 'probably just calls for a patch' through 'genuinely awkward' to 'fatal,' and say which ones a defender should simply dismiss as misdescribing the view. Then give your honest read on whether the position, as I've stated it, risks anything at all, or whether it can absorb every counterexample by tweaking the idealization procedure—and if it can, whether that's a problem rather than a feature. Also, since I want to know how much to trust the map: say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you're least sure of.
