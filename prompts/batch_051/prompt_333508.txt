Question: Can what cannot be said nevertheless be known? Answer with reference to mystical experience. In doing so, distinguish ineffability from unknowability, and discuss William James's claim that mystical states have a noetic quality.

Student answer:

In The Varieties of Religious Experience, James identifies four marks of mystical states: ineffability, noetic quality, transiency, and passivity. Ineffability means that the person having the experience finds its content impossible to put into words. Noetic quality means that although the state resembles a feeling, it presents itself to the subject as a kind of knowing, as an insight into something. So James gives us the tension in a nutshell: the experience seems to carry knowledge, but its content cannot be said.

It is important to distinguish this from unknowability. There is no contradiction in someone knowing something they cannot express: we recognise faces without being able to describe them, and a pianist can know how to phrase a passage without being able to state the rule. So ineffability as such does not imply that nothing is known.

However, this only shows that ineffable knowledge is not outright impossible. The harder question is whether a mystical experience can actually be a source of knowledge, and here I think the answer is no. A genuine item of knowledge must be able to figure in reasoning: it must be usable as a premise and assessable by others. An ineffable content, by definition, cannot be put into propositional form, so it cannot enter into inference or be evaluated for truth. The mystic may feel certain that she has grasped something, but felt certainty is not knowledge. At most the experience gives a non-propositional acquaintance, and acquaintance falls short of knowledge. So although such states are genuinely ineffable and are felt as noetic, they cannot transmit knowledge, either to the mystic or to anyone else.
