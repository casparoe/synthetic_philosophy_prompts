One of the more striking features of American abortion law after Dobbs has been the re-emergence of the Comstock Act of 1873, a federal statute that bans mailing abortifacient drugs and instruments, among other things. For roughly half a century, under Roe v. Wade, the Act was not enforced against medical abortion; after Dobbs, some legal actors argued that the dormant text could be used as a nationwide de facto prohibition on mailed abortion pills without any new legislation. The Biden administration’s Office of Legal Counsel took the view that the Act reaches only mailings intended for unlawful use, while the Supreme Court in 2024 disposed of a challenge to mifepristone’s approval on standing grounds without resolving the Comstock Act question.

I want you to analyze this episode through the lens of desuetude: the idea that a law that is never enforced loses its claim to bind, and that resurrecting it after long non-enforcement may conflict with rule-of-law values such as fair notice, reliance, and non-arbitrary enforcement. But do not just rehearse the standard desuetude position. Be disciplined about mapping: what exactly would desuetude require in a system, like the federal U.S., that generally lacks a robust civil-law desuetude doctrine for criminal statutes? Was the Comstock Act desuete in the relevant sense, or was it simply unenforced because a constitutional decision made its core application temporarily unavailable? Does a change in background constitutional law change the relevance of desuetude, or does fair notice still run against the individuals who relied on decades of mail-order abortion practice? What would “revival” do to the rule of law even if the statute’s text was never repealed?

Then reverse direction: what does this episode show about desuetude as a theory? Does it expose a flaw in the theory—for example, that non-enforcement due to a constitutional decision is not the same as legislative abandonment, or that desuetude gives too little weight to later democratic majorities that want the law enforced—or does it offer a particularly hard test that desuetude passes or fails? Reach a view about whether the post-Dobbs Comstock Act situation is better seen as a legitimate reassertion of an old statute or as the kind of arbitrary revival desuetude is meant to condemn, and justify it.
