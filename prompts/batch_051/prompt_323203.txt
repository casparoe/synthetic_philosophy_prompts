I'm a retired chemist who got into philosophy late, and I've been reading around in the ethics of migration for about six months. I keep tripping over the word refugee the way I used to trip over words like measurement when a lab argument got going: everyone uses it with confidence, but the minute you try to pin down what it means it starts to slide. Could you act as a philosopher who works on migration and political concepts, think slowly, and give me a working defintion of refugee — not a dictionary entry, but something that does justice to how the term actually works in moral and political argument.

Here are my test cases:

1. A woman flees Eritrea because the state has been persecuting her for her Pentecostal religion, and she crosses into Ethiopia. I'm confident she's a refugee on anyone's account.
2. A family's farm in Guatemala fails after successive droughts, and they cross into Mexico because their children are malnourished. My gut says not refugee, but I can't say why without something doing the work, and I don't like that.
3. A man is displaced from Aleppo to another part of Syria by the civil war and never crosses a border. Lawyers say no; ordinary talk often says yes. I'd like a definition that gives one answer and explains the pull of the other.
4. A teenager in Honduras flees because his local gang will kill him if he doesn't join and the police won't protect him. Some lawyers read membership in a particular social group to cover this; others don't.
5. A young Russian leaves Russia to avoid conscription into a war he thinks is criminal and claims asylum in Norway.
6. A woman is trafficked to the UAE and forced into domestic servitude, then escapes and claims asylum rather than return to Uganda.
7. A Canadian who embezzled from his employer flees to Brazil and claims to be a refugee because his trial back home would be unfair.

I have a half-baked suspicion I'd like you to try out. Maybe refugee is not a natural-kind word but a status concept, like patient or defendant, so a good definition has to say not just what makes the concept true of someone but what responding to that status commits others to — states, host communities, maybe the international system. I might be wrong about that; if so, say so.

Please start by brainstorn two or three candidate definitions (e.g., a fear-based one, a responsibility-based one, a bare threshold-of-harm one), run each against the cases, then either revise the strongest or defend the surprising verdicts. Tell me which cases your definition leaves most uneasy and why. Don't worry about citations; I'll add them myself later.
