A friend sent me this argument and now I can't tell whether it's a good point or a sleight of hand. It goes like this:

(1) Printing money doesn't create more land, machines, workers, or know-how; it only creates more claims on those things.
(2) If you only create more claims on a fixed pile of real stuff, you haven't created wealth — you've just moved it around, because every new, bigger slice comes out of somebody else's.
(3) So printing money never creates wealth; it just redistributes it.
(4) Now consider a goverment that pays people to dig holes and fill them back in, funded entirely with newly printed money.
(5) By (3), that program creates no wealth, even though the workers get paid.
(6) But suppose this happens in a deep recession, with idle workers and closed-down factories. Then the program really seems to create wealth: the workers spend their wages, shops reopen, suppliers hire people, and output gets produced that otherwise wouldn't have existed.
(7) So either (3) is false, or the hole-digging really does create new resources — but the holes get filled back in, so where would those resources come from?

I'd most like to keep (1), since you can't print tractors, but I also don't want to say (6) is just an illusion. The argument seems to prove that expansionary policy never creates anything real, which feels much too strong. Which step do I actually need to give up, and what does it cost?
