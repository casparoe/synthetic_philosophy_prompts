I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I keep meeting the same claim in essays about minds, markets, and model interpretability: some regularities are real patterns, not just convenient summaries. The same essays explain a system from one of three stances — the physical stance (its parts and forces), the design stance (what its components are for), or the intentional stance (beliefs and goals). I run into this concretely whenever I decide whether an alert reflects a real signal, or whether to model a flaky subsystem from its parts, its design, or its apparent goals. I don't want another exposition of the view; I want the spec first. Before I read any more of this material, write the desiderata that an adequate theory of real patterns has to meet: the cases it must classify correctly, the distinctions it cannot collapse (pattern vs. noise, real vs. merely useful, stance-independent vs. stance-relative, approximate vs. exact), and the facts about sampling, measurement, and finite data it cannot contradict — with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and a verdict on whether it is non-negotiable or just a camp preference. Include at least these cases: a glider in Conway's Game of Life, a center of gravity, an upward drift in a noisy metric, a sampling artifact that disappears when the sampling rate changes, a one-off crash that never recurs, and a subsystem whose behavior is only predictable when described as pursuing a goal. Then flag the tensions: say which requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — for example, a compression-based criterion of reality may be at odds with letting unrepeatable events count as real — and whether any subset is jointly satisfiable only if one requirement is weakened. That tension analysis is the part I most want. Keep the scorecard brief: how a purely physical-stance realism, a design-stance realism, and an intentional-stance realism each fare against your list, and which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidates.

Format:
1. Scope (one short paragraph) — what problem a theory of real patterns is meant to solve, stated in terms I would recognize from systems work.
2. Desiderata (about 1,200 words) — numbered requirements, each with its motivating case or consideration and a verdict on whether it is non-negotiable or a camp preference.
3. Tensions (about 400 words) — where the requirements pull against each other or appear jointly unsatisfiable, and why.
4. Scorecard (a short table) — the three stance-readings rated against the list, followed by one sentence naming the requirement that excludes the most plausible candidates.
