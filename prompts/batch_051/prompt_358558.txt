Over the past year I have been in treatment for what my doctors describe as anxious depression, and in trying to understand it I became absorbed in predictive processing and the free energy principle. The more I read, the more I find myself unsettled by a simple thought. If a self-organizing system is, at bottom, in the business of minimizing surprise—of keeping prediction error as low as possible—then a life of radical retreat would seem to be the theory's ideal. A person who rarely leaves home, expects little from others, and treats every new encounter as a source of error would be wrong about the world less often than anyone else. Withdrawal, on this reading, is not a pathology but something close to a free-energy minimum. Presumably no defender of the principle means that, since nobody recommends becoming a recluse. But then I cannot see where the argument goes wrong. Is the mistake in treating the claim that the brain minimizes free energy as a prescription for acting, in thinking that surprise is always to be avoided at the level of a whole life, or somewhere else entirely? Where exactly does the free energy principle stop short of the conclusion that a quietly uneventful life is the ideal?
