I'm in a reading group on political philosophy and we've been picking at Rousseau's Social Contract. In Book II, chapter 1, he says: "In reality, if it is not impossible for a particular will to agree on some point with the general will, it is at least impossible for the agreement to be lasting and constant; for the particular will tends, by its very nature, to partiality, while the general will tends to equality. It is even more impossible to have any guarantee of this agreement; for even if it should always exist, it would be the effect not of art, but of chance." We got stuck on whether this actually justifies a pretty deep asymmetry in how ordinary people judge political decisions. If a democratic assembly passes a general law that applies to everyone, including the people who voted for it, most of us think it's fine even when it costs us something. But if the same assembly passes a law that clearly singles out some identifiable group—a minority, a trade, a neighborhood—many of us think it's suspect, even if the majority wants it. The group seems to think Rousseau's distinction between particular and general wills gives a principled reason for this: the first expresses the general will because it tends to equality, the second is just a particular will with a majority behind it.

But I'm not convinced the distinction holds up. Every general law has particular effects, and you can usually draft a particular-seeming law in general language if you're clever enough. So is the asymmetry defensible? Focus on the hard case: one law is formally general but its burdens fall almost entirely on a small group, another is formally particular but would predictably equalize advantages. Which one, if either, deserves to be called the general will? And if the answer depends on actual effects, does Rousseau's passage still justify the asymmetry, or do I need a different principle? I want your honest verdict on whether to keep, revise, or drop the asymmetry.
