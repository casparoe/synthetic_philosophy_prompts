I spent my working life as a research scientist, and I only began reading philosophy seriously after retiring. The habit I keep coming back to is one I acquired in the laboratory: we routinely reasoned with objects and properties we knew to be strictly false. We treated a gas as a collection of noninteracting point particles, a crystal as a perfect periodic lattice with defects added afterward, and a semiconductor as containing positively charged holes, knowing that these were names for collective excitations or absences in an underlying many-body system rather than fundamental constituents. We did not believe in these objects the way we believed in the apparatus, but neither did we take ourselves to be asserting falsehoods. The falsity was doing work, and we kept it inside carefully marked boundary conditions.

Since retiring I have been reading the philosophy of law, and I keep encountering the same pattern under another name. A corporation is not a person, yet the law treats it as one for standing, liability, contract, and constitutional speech. The reasonable person is not any person. A person who records a deed is treated as having given notice to the whole world. An adopted child is treated as if she had been born into the adoptive line. Lawyers call these legal fictions, and the standard observation is that no one is deceived: the proposition is known to be false if read literally, but it is maintained deliberately because it organizes legal reasoning, allocates burdens, and produces workable results.

Here is the comparison I would like examined. On my reading, both practices share the following structure: a counterfactual posit is affirmed for a purpose while everyone competent in the practice knows it is not literally true; the posit earns its keep by coordinating inferences across a wide range of cases; it has real consequences in the world rather than only in a model; it has a domain of reliability and degenerates outside it; and it is corrected less often by being abandoned than by adding qualifications that patch its edges.

The question is whether this is a deep structural identity or a superficial analogy, and I want the answer to be rigorous rather than suggestive. What would have to be true of legal fictions for results about scientific idealization to transfer to them—for example, the idea that an idealization is acceptable when we can specify its error, recover the exact case as a limit, and mark its domain of validity? Does law have analogues of controlled error and a limiting procedure, or do its fictions do a different kind of work that makes those standards beside the point? And where does the analogy fail? Please name at least one feature that is true of one kind of fiction and false of the other, such that someone who imported an idea from one field into the other would be led astray. Finally, say what the comparison should make us think about the ontology of legal constructs: are legal fictions best classified as idealizations in the scientist's sense, or does the word fiction conceal something important about what law is doing?
