Last week in a reading group on the first Critique we were working through the passage in the Transcendental Analytic where Kant distinguishes intuitions from concepts. On his account there, an intuition is a singular representation that relates immediately to its object, whereas a concept is a general representation that relates to objects mediately through marks that can be shared by many things. One member of the group defended the following argument, which she says shows Kant cannot have the distinction he wants. I have been trying to figure out where it goes wrong.

(1) For a representation to be singular, it must represent exactly one object rather than a plurality.
(2) To represent exactly one object rather than a plurality, it must distinguish that object from all other objects.
(3) Distinguishing one object from all others requires applying a mark, criterion, or rule for identity: something that determines which object is in view and which others are excluded.
(4) Any mark, criterion, or rule is general, since it could in principle apply to more than one object, even if it happens to single out only one here.
(5) Therefore, a singular representation is possible only through the use of a general representation.
(6) For Kant, concepts are general representations.
(7) So every intuition depends on a concept, and Kant’s idea of intuition as an independent source of givenness collapses.

She thinks this is not a refutation of Kant but an invitation to abandon the sharp distinction. I want to resist it because it seems to make animal perception and pre-conceptual experience impossible, and because it quietly turns receptivity into a kind of spontaneity. But I cannot find the precise step to reject. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to? Or is the argument sound, and what would it cost to accept it?
