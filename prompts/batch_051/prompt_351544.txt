I run an economics club at my high school, about fifteen students aged sixteen to seventeen, meeting for one hour. I want to run a session in which the members feel the force of a foundational problem in choice theory before anyone names it. The target problem is this: economists often speak as if a person's utility is revealed by their choices, but if utility is defined only as whatever choices reveal, it becomes unclear whether utility explains the choices or merely restates them, and the same observed choices can be summarized by more than one utility assignment. I would like the participants to discover that problem through an activity, not through a lecture.

Please design a runnable game or simulation for this group, with explicit rules, materials, and a payoff structure using small real stakes such as candy, points, or minor privileges. The terms revealed preference and utility-as-representation should not be introduced until the debrief. The design should put participants in a position of making actual choices, recording them, and then being asked what those choices show about how much each person wanted each option, so that they themselves notice that more than one story fits the same record and that saying someone chose an option because it maximizes their utility can become circular when the utility is read off from the choice.

The deliverable should include a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the philosophical points: the difference between a record of choices and a story about the wanting behind them, the claim that people choose what maximizes utility, the circularity worry when utility is inferred from choices, and the underdetermination of a utility reading by the choices actually observed. The debrief should also note what will likely go wrong when the activity is run—for example, participants not taking the small stakes seriously, or one student treating the game as a puzzle to solve rather than as a record of their own choices—and how to handle each. Finally, please be explicit about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I as facilitator must supply, whether that is the felt difference between wanting something more or less from the inside, or the formal assumptions needed to represent preferences by a real-valued utility function.

Please approach this as an expert in the philosophy of economics and in designing experiential classroom simulations. Before settling on a final design, brainstorm at least two or three candidate mechanics and explain why the one you recommend is best for this group and this insight. Think carefully about the order of the session, since the moment of realization should come from the activity itself rather than from anything the facilitator says afterward. At the end, tell me which part of your design you are least confident about and what would change your mind—for example, an argument that a particular rule smuggles in a substantive conception of utility, or a reason the debrief would lead this particular class toward a conclusion you did not intend.
