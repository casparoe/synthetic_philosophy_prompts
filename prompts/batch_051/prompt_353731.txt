I sit on the pre-deployment review board at a mid-sized AI lab, and next week we have to sign off on a product team's evidence assistant for clinicians. The assistant writes short summaries of the medical literature, and the team's whole safety case rests on a debate protocol. For every contested factual claim in a draft summary, two copies of the assistant argue for and against it over several rounds; a human judge — a physician paid per hour, not necessarily a specialist in the relevant field — reads the exchange, can ask follow-up questions, and declares a winner; the winning side's version of the claim is what ships. The team argues this is cheaper than a fact-checking staff and more thorough than a single model pass, because adversarial prompting will force each side to surface considerations the other would rather bury.

My concern, and the reason this landed at the board, is obfuscated arguments. The assistant is very good at producing arguments that look meticulous but are quietly wrong: it misstates what a study actually measured, cherry-picks one trial with a huge effect while omitting the nulls, equivocates on a key term mid-argument, or gives a perfectly clean chain of reasoning on top of a false premise. The judges are fast, tired, and not really equipped to catch these moves, and the model writes with more apparent authority than any human they have ever argued with. So the failure mode is not just that debate misses the error; it is that debate launders the error, or worse, that the judge is systematically pulled toward whichever side happens to sound more authoritative. Some of us on the board thing we should reject the protocol for exactly that reason; the product team says the realistic alternative is no systematic checking at all, and debate at least pushes the judge to ask better questions than they would have asked on their own.

Draft the written decision memo I will circulate before the meeting. I need a concrete bottom line — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with the strongest version of the reasoning behind it, and an honest treatment of the case for each of the other two options. In particular, do not compare debate only to the ideal of full specialist fact-checking; engage the team's claim that it beats the status quo of no systematic checking. And specify, whichever way you land, what we should require of a six-month pilot before we reconsider.
