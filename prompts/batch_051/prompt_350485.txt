I keep noticing a configuration in the way people handle myths that exist in many versions, and I want to know whether it has an established name and, more importantly, how to carve it off from the things it resembles.

Take Cinderella, but only as one instance. The version most people in the English-speaking world carry around has a fairy godmother, a pumpkin coach, and a glass slipper. The story also exists in many other recorded forms: in some the helper is a fish or a bird, in some the shoe is not glass, and in some the godmother does not appear at all. Some of those forms are recorded earlier than the fairy-godmother version, and many do not look like retellings of it; they look like branches of something older that is no longer attested. Yet in ordinary conversation the fairy-godmother version functions as the story. The others get described as simplified or garbled versions of it, even where they could not have descended from it and are plausibly older than it.

I see the same thing with the flood story. A story of a catastrophic flood that nearly ends the human world turns up all over the place. Within each tradition that has one, people mostly treat their own version as the primary one and other people's versions as echoes, memories, or corruptions of it. Setting aside the question of which flood story, if any, is oldest, the striking thing is that each version operates in its own setting as a standard against which the others are measured and found wanting.

The configuration I am trying to isolate has three features. First, a story exists in many attested forms across times, places, or communities. Second, one form is not merely liked or familiar but treated as the real one: it is the form people use to sort the others into early and late, faithful and divergent, original and added. Third, that office does not seem to come from evidence that this form is actually the oldest, or closest to a common source, or independently more reliable. It comes from some fact about how the form was fixed to begin with: an influential retelling, an editor's choice, a translation that travelled, an institution's decision. The fixed form is then projected backward and used to arrange everything else around it.

I am asking for the concept, not just a label. Does this configuration have an established name, or a family of names, in folklore studies, philology, literary theory, or philosophy? If not, what would a justified coinage be, and what definition would keep it distinct from its neighbours? In particular, I want to know:

- Is the special office of the one version essential, or is any widely reproduced "standard" enough, even if nobody treats it as the original?
- Must there be several attested variants before the promotion, or can a single influential retelling create the effect by drowning out earlier ones?
- Must the promotion be deliberate, or can it emerge from accumulated use and forgetting?
- How does this differ from the genuine cases in which one form really is the common ancestor or the attested source of the others, and from mere popularity, familiarity, or literary influence?
- Once the process has happened, is there any principled sense in which the privileged form may properly be called the real or correct version, or is the idea that one version is "the" myth mainly a product of the process itself?
