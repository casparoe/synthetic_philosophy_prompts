I have been reading through the sign-up materials and contracts of cryonics organizations, and I am struck by how much philosophy the industry quietly bakes into its practical arrangements. Subscribers typically fund vitrification through a life-insurance policy payable after legal death; many providers offer a cheaper neuropreservation option (head only) alongside whole-body preservation, on the assumption that whatever matters about the person is encoded in the brain and can later be recovered, repaired, or emulated; the paperwork tends to speak of legal death as an interruption rather than an end, treating the preserved person as a patient awaiting future care; and the whole arrangement is marketed as a rational wager — an extremely small probability of an enormous payoff, worth the cost even if revival is overwhelmingly unlikely.

I would like to know what this practice actually presupposes, and where it stops presupposing a single coherent philosophy. Specifically: what theory of personal identity would have to be true for neuropreservation to preserve a person as well as whole-body preservation does, and does choosing the neuro option already commit someone to something like an information-theoretic account of the self rather than a body-based account? What view of death is presupposed by treating legal death as a curable episode rather than the permanent end of the person, and is that view available to a consistent materialist? And what does the wager itself commit subscribers to — expected-value reasoning over astronomically small probabilities, an unusual degree of confidence in future institutions and future desires, a near-zero discount rate on one's own far-future welfare? Finally, I want to know which of these commitments are genuine presuppositions of the practice, which are merely flattering post-hoc rationalizations it could survive without, and whether the practice is internally consistent if one starts to doubt any of them.
