So I got into an argument with some friends about Searle's Chinese Room, and somebody brought up the Dennett-style point that Darwin and Turing each did a strange inversion of reasoning: Darwin gave you design without a designer, and Turing gave you competent output without the machine's having anything we would call comprehension. We ended up talking about a variation on the Chinese Room that we couldn't settle, and I want to watch it get worked through carefully.

Take the Chinese Room, but change where the rulebook comes from. The person in the room has no manual written by any linguist, programmer, or translator. Instead, imagine you start with a huge population of candidate rulebooks that map a complete written conversation so far to a next string of Chinese characters. Each candidate is run through a large number of written conversations with native speakers (or with an automated judge trained on native-speaker judgments), and the candidates that produce the more appropriate replies survive and spawn copies with small random mutations and occasional recombination. Keep this running until one fixed rulebook is left that is excellent at sustaining a written Chinese conversation. No one ever wrote a single rule, and no one ever had to inspect or understand how the surviving rulebook maps inputs to outputs; the rulebook is just the survivor of a blind selection process, like an organ or an instinct. The person in the room now follows it mechanically, exactly as in the original, and has no idea where the book came from.

Now: does Searle's original argument survive this change? Work through what Searle would have to say about this version, and whether his conclusion—that neither the man nor the room understands Chinese, and the system has no intentionality—gets weaker or stronger when the competence was produced by a Darwinian process rather than by an intentional designer. Then run the Systems Reply on the case: if the system as a whole was supposed to understand in the original, does the selection history make that more or less plausible? Finally, take the Dennett-style reply seriously: if we already accept that evolution produced competent designers without comprehension, and that a Turing machine can produce competent answers without comprehension, should we conclude the same about the room, and what would the residual disagreement between Searle and Dennett actually be about once that point is granted? I am less interested in who wins than in exactly where the two camps part ways.
