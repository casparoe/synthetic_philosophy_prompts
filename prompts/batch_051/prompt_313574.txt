I'm a journalist working on a piece about why some people update when you hand them a fact and others just dig in, and in the middle of the reporting I rewatched The Truman Show for the first time in years. The piece keeps bumping into the standard distinction people who study misinformation draw between an epistemic bubble and an echo chamber. The way I've come to understand it: someone in an epistemic bubble simply doesn't encounter the relevant voices or facts, because the information gets filtered out before it reaches them; popping the bubble is mostly a matter of exposure. Someone in an echo chamber, by contrast, has been trained to distrust the people and sources who would challenge their view, so exposure alone doesn't do it—the disconfirming evidence arrives but gets discounted as coming from the deluded, the enemy, or the inauthentic. I've got enough of the theory to be dangerous, but I'm less sure the film lines up neatly with either pole, and that's what I'm asking you to help me think through.

I'd like a reading of The Truman Show through that lense, for a reader who knows the film but hasn't read the philosophy. Where is Truman straightforwardly in an epistemic bubble, and where is something more like an echo chamber being built around him? Think about the way Christof's operation handles Sylvia, the way Meryl and Marlon talk Truman out of his doubts, the drowned-father story and his fear of water, and the final boat scene. Does the ending show that Truman was never really in an echo chamber—just a bubble that finally popped when information got through? Or does the film blur the distinction in a way that is actually more realistic than the philosophy? And finish by saying what the film gets right that the distinction misses, if anything. Work from ordinary memory of the movie; exact dialogue doesn't matter.
