I am a historian of historiography, not a philosopher, and next month I am giving a job talk to a mid-sized history department whose senior members include a colonial American social historian, two archivally trained medievalists, a historian of French revolutionary political culture, and a scholar of public memory and commemoration. The department prides itself on archival rigor, contextualism, and what its chair calls source-based history. My talk is a modified Nietzschean intervention. Working from the second of Nietzsche's Untimely Meditations, the essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, I argue that Nietzsche's three relationships to the past, the monumental, the antiquarian, and the critical, are best read not as genres of historical writing but as stances toward temporality, each with a characteristic failure mode once it comes loose from the needs of a present form of life: the monumental hardens into a myth that hides causes, the antiquarian into a curatorial piety that merely administers the archive, and the critical into a tribunal that dissolves a past it cannot replace. My diagnostic claim is that the profession's current norms, the presentism taboo, the equation of rigor with exhaustive contextualization, the suspicion of exemplary or edifying uses of the past, institutionalize the antiquarian stance in exactly this hypertrophied form. My normative claim is that the discipline should deliberately rebalance: it should rehabilitate the monumental in undergraduate teaching and public-facing work and treat the critical as a legitimate tribunal on inherited categories rather than a lapse of scholarly objectivity. I concede the risks, but I hold that a living relationship with the past cannot be made risk-free, and that the discipline's attempt to achieve risklessness is itself a refusal of the question of what its history is for.
