In my economics club we have been arguing for weeks about a number that keeps turning up in climate policy: the discount rate. Everyone agrees future harms should be discounted somewhat, because people in 2100 will likely be richer and because there is some chance civilization won't survive that long. The fight is only about whether to add an extra discount on top, purely because the harm is in the future. One side says no: future lives count equally, so the pure rate of time preference should be near zero. The other says the pure rate should be inferred from how people actually trade money across time, which yields a substantially larger number. Both sides accept the same facts about interest rates and projected growth, and neither moves; the same impasse has run through the public debate for decades. I suspect the real problem is that discount rate names two different things: one side is choosing a moral parameter about how much we ought to care about future people, and the other is estimating a descriptive parameter about how resources actually trade across time. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is the disagreement substantive or partly verbal, and does it dissolve once that descriptive/normative distinction is pointed out? Are there hidden empirical questions, or is the core a bedrock ethical divide about pure time preference that no data could touch? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
