You are a professional epistemologist who has spent years working on the semantics and pragmatics of knowledge ascriptions, and you have been asked to serve as the neutral scribe for an adversarial collaboration between two parties: a contextualist about the word “knows” and a subject-sensitive invariantist who accepts pragmatic encroachment. The contextualist holds that sentences of the form “S knows that p” can express different propositions in different conversational contexts, so that practical stakes can affect the truth-conditions of a knowledge ascription even while the subject’s situation is held fixed. The subject-sensitive invariantist holds that “knows” contributes the same relation across contexts, but that the knowledge relation itself is sensitive to the practical stakes of the subject. The two have spent months arguing past one another, and now want to produce a document they can both sign. You are to draft that document.

Before writing the final version, work through a private brainstorming phase. In that phase, sort the apparent points of agreement, identify formulations each side has used that the other would reject only because of ambiguity or loose wording, and isolate the disagreements that survive precise restatement. Then produce the final text with the following sections:

1. Agreed Claims: a numbered list of non-vacuous claims about knowledge, practical stakes, and the relevant linguistic or experimental data that both parties accept. Each claim should be specific enough that it could in principle turn out to be wrong, and worded so that neither party would object.

2. Disagreements: a precise statement of each remaining disagreement, phrased neutrally so that both parties would accept it as a fair characterization of their opposed positions. Where an apparent disagreement is merely terminological or turns on an equivocation, say so and restate the issue in terms neither side can reject.

3. Cruxes: for each genuine disagreement, identify the sub-question such that a decisive answer to it would lead at least one party to revise their view, and explain briefly why it has that status.

4. Evidence and Predictions: for each crux, describe a concrete case, experimental probe, or argument that both parties agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts. Include at least (a) a cross-context reporting case in which someone in a low-stakes context evaluates an earlier high-stakes knowledge denial that concerns the same subject and proposition, and (b) an error-possibility case in which the conversational stakes are low but a skeptical alternative is made salient. For each, state what pattern of ordinary speakers’ judgments would count against each view.
