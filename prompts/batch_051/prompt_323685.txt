I am currently taking a course on moral philosophy, and in our seminar on the possibility of genuine moral dilemmas we spent most of the time on the usual binary cases — the student torn between two inescapable demands, the impossible choice between two lives, the two equally weighty promises — and these now feel rather over-rehearsed. I would like a constructed case that pushes on a less-discussed question: what happens when the conflict is not binary but three-way. Could you construct a thought experiment to the following specifications?

The scenario should involve an ordinary professional in a realistic institutional setting (for instance, a school, hospital, or local council), and it should place her under three distinct pro tanto obligations, each generated by a different kind of consideration — say, a promise, a role-based responsibility, and a general duty of care or fairness — but of roughly comparable seriousness. The obligations must be pairwise jointly satisfiable: fulfilling any two of the three is feasible. But they must be jointly unsatisfiable: it is impossible, for non-trivial structural reasons such as scheduling, geography, or confidentiality, to fulfil all three. No single obligation may be obviously weightier, and the scenario should be designed so that an opponent of genuine dilemmas cannot dissolve the conflict simply by pointing to an overlooked ordering or by saying that one requirement has expired; the case should force the opponent to take a more interesting line.

After presenting the scenario, please walk through the dialectic. First, explain why a defender of genuine moral dilemmas would classify your case as a genuine dilemma — which requirement is violated, why the violation cannot be dismissed as merely failing to do the all-things-considered best act, and why symmetry or incommensurability prevents ranking the obligations. Second, give the strongest reply available to someone who denies that genuine dilemmas are possible, and be precise about which commitment that reply rejects: it might deny that all three considerations generate binding obligations, appeal to aggregation so that fulfilling two obligations outweighs fulfilling only one, or reject the principle that ought agglomerates over conjunction — but whichever strategy you choose, show it working on the details of your case rather than merely asserting it. Third, identify the single feature of the case that is doing the most work, state how the case would collapse if that feature were removed (for example, if the obligations arose at three different times rather than simultaneously), and explain how a defender of the view that comes out worse would attack the design of the case.

Please write your answer in British English.
