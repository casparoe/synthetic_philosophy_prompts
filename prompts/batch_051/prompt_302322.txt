I'm taking a module on the ethics of historical fiction and we keep circling back to the same clause, because our lecturer used to assess funding applications under a national film body's Code of Practice for Historical Drama. The clause reads: 'No production shall attribute to a real person any words, actions or beliefs for which there is no evidence in the historical record, unless any such invention is plainly flagged to the viewer as invention. Where the person depicted has died within the last fifty years, the producer shall consult surviving family before depicting that person's private suffering, sexuality or mental illness.'

A production company has now announced an interactive biopic of a novelist who died in the 1990s. There's no script; the viewer talks to a system trained on her diaries, letters, and interviews, and it answers in the first person in her voice, generating sentences she never actually wrote. In the preview demo the model was asked about a journal entry and went on to describe a depressive episode in some detail, drawing on private material; her nieces say nobody consulted them. The companies response is that the Code predates generative media and assumes a finished, authored programme, that each exchange is produced by the viewer's prompt rather than 'attributed' by the producers, and that the preview was a technical demonstration rather than a production.

I want to work through whether the Code reaches this at all and what it requires. Go through the interpretive approaches — the plain words of the clause, the purpose behind it, what the drafters would plausibly of said if asked about this in the 1980s, and what a reasonable viewer takes such a system to be — and tell me which approach should win here and why. Also, does the case show that the clause needs rewriting, or just that it needs to be applied honestly? Answer in British English please, and don't worry about citations.
