I spent my career as a research physicist, reasoning classically as a matter of course, and only since retiring have I begun to read philosophy of mathematics in earnest. I find the constructivist account of mathematical language genuinely compelling: a proposition is a claim about what can be constructed or proved, the meaning of each connective is given by what counts as a proof of it, and on that reading the law of excluded middle is not a neutral logical truth but a sweeping assertion — for any proposition whatever, either there is a proof of it or a proof of its negation — for which we have no general justification in advance. But I cannot give up the conviction, formed over a lifetime of classical reasoning, that there simply is a fact of the matter about whether there are infinitely many twin primes, even if no mind ever settles the question, and that the sentence 'either there are infinitely many twin primes or there are not' is true in a way that cannot be reduced to the present or eventual possession of a proof. I want to know which of these should yield. Give me the strongest case for each side: on one side, that my realist, excluded-middle intuition is an artefact of classical training — reliable when applied to finite domains and measurement outcomes but projected without warrant onto the completed infinite; on the other, that the constructivist semantics is a revisionary proposal that gains clarity about proof at the cost of quietly changing the subject away from mathematical truth as I understand it. Then offer a verdict. Should I debunk the intuition and drop it; should I amend or abandon constructivism in favor of a moderate position, such as retaining classical logic for arithmetical truth and going constructive only where an algorithm or witness is actually being claimed; or is there a reading on which the intuition and the semantics are compatible? For each move, tell me what would justify taking it and what it would cost, rather than simply picking a side.
