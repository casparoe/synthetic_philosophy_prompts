Longtime reader of the rat-adjacent blogs here. Chesterton's fence gets deployed around me as a conversation-ender more and more often, and I'm never quite sure what the person using it thinks they've established. The most recent case: a thread about removing a long-standing manuscript triage step at a journal some friends edit, where someone wrote roughly that you shouldn't tear a fence down until you know why it was built, and the reform proposal basically died on the spot. I get the broad point about not reforming things destructively before understanding them, but the one-liner seems to blur a bunch of distinct claims. It could be just a bare epistemic norm: do your homework before you change something. Or a burden-of-proof rule where the would-be reformer has to prove the original reason no longer holds. Or a presumption that the people who put the fence up knew something the reformer doesn't. Or the stronger claim that the fence's continued survival is itself evidence it is doing useful work, which quickly ends up as full Burkean traditionalism: the accumulated wisdom of an institution outweighs any individual's audit of it. In the subculture I read, it also get glossed as a point about tacit knowledge and legibility, roughly that the reason for an institution might be real but not something any one person can reconstruct or state, so an individual reformer's insistence that he sees no use in it is systematically unreliable. I'd like to sort out the readings so I can link people to something the next time the line is trotted out. Could you lay out the main precise theses Chesterton's fence might be expressing, flag which are near-trivial, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false or only weakly true, and then say, for each, whether it actually gets you from "this practice is old" to "therefore don't change it"? And specifically: which reading would the commenter have needed to justify killing the triage reform, and does that reading itself survive cases where institutions persist because changing them is costly, or because no one with power cares, rather than because they encode some wisdom?
