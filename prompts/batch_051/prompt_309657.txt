I host a small podcast about technology and practical ethics, and next week I'm interviewing a researcher who defends what she calls reflective-endorsement paternalism for AI assistants. Her view, roughly, is that an assistant may nudge or outright refuse a user's request only when it can predict, from the user's own history and stated values, that the user would endorse the intervention on reflection—and it has to choose the least agency-overriding intervention available. She has a habit of waving off autonomy objections by saying something like "every assistant is already steering you with defaults, rankings, and wording, so the real question is wether the steering is accountable, not whether there is any."

Draft me eight to ten questions that go beyond just having her restate the view. I want at least one question on each of: (a) the counterfactual problem—how an assistant could know what someone would endorse on reflection when reflection itself can change what a person wants; (b) whether a nudge and a refusal really differ in kind, or whether her "least agency-overriding" requirement just collapses into always nudging; (c) the worry that "what you would reflectively endorse" is just a placeholder for the designers' values; and (d) where the line falls between protecting a user from themself and protecting third parties, and what her view says when those pull apart. For each of those, add a follow-up keyed to the reply she's most likely to give. And sequence it all so her "all steering is already paternalism" move gets progressively harder to use, ending with a question about what evidence would count against her view.
