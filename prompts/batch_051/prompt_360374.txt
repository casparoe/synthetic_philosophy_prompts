I'm retired now — thirty years in a geochemistry lab — and I've only recently started reading philosophy properly. Lately I've been trying to pin down the difference between blasphemy and sacrilege, and whether "the sacred" has to be religious or can be civic too. My lab brain keeps wanting a clean operational distinction, but the cases don't cooperate. Over the last few months I've been pestering my friend M, who is genuinely thoughtfull about this stuff, with examples. Her track record, as best I can set it down:

- A gallery shows a photograph of a crucifix submerged in urine. M: genuine sacrilege.
- A comic records a stand-up special calling God a petty tyrant and Muhammad a fraud. M: offensive, but not sacrilege.
- A novel, deliberately written to outrage, gives Jesus explicit sex scenes. M: offensive, but not sacrilege.
- A consecrated communion wafer is flushed down a public toilet on video. M: sacrilege.
- Someone spray-paints obscenities on the great door of a cathedral. M: sacrilege.
- A protester pours red paint on a tomb of the unknown soldier. M: sacrilege.
- A woman stands outside a church reciting the words of the Mass backwards. M: not sacrilege, though she says she nearly said yes.

Now here is the case I want you to help me think through. Someone steals consecrated wafers from a church, takes them home, and burns them in a clean steel pan until nothing is left but ash. No filth, no obscenity, no mixing with anything impure — just clean, deliberate destruction. He films the whole thing and gives the video the title "Destroying God." Predict what M will say: sacrilege or not? But I'm less interested in the bare prediction than in the reasoning. I want the rule or rules that fit her existing verdicts, which of those rules would say what about the burning case, and which of her earlier verdicts is the strongest analog. And if her record genuinely underdetermines it, tell me which extra case would settle which rule she is actually running.
