Here's one I'd like a careful grade on. I set this short-answer question on an animal ethics unit:

"Evaluate the following claim: for an animal-welfare consequentialist, replacing beef with chicken in a typical Western diet makes things worse, because each calorie of chicken requires many more individual animals to be killed. In your answer, say what work, if any, deaths from crop cultivation and wild-animal suffering should do in the comparison. Focus on the structure of the trade-offs, not precise numbers."

A student wrote:

"Replacing beef with chicken makes things worse, mostly because of the numbers. A steer produces hundreds of pounds of meat while a broiler produces a few pounds, so you need something like a hundred chickens to match one steer; per calorie, the chicken diet kills orders of magnitude more individuals. A cow's life plausibly counts for more than a chicken's, but not by a factor of a hundred, so weighting by welfare capacity does not close the gap. Crop deaths don't overturn this either: cows and chickens both eat crops in feedlot systems, and adding the crop deaths from an animal's feed to the animal's own slaughter just adds more deaths to every choice, so the chicken side still looks worse. In fact, for each extra chicken eaten the relevant harm is the chicken itself plus the crop deaths from its feed, while for each extra steer the crop burden is shared across far more meals; so including crops only strengthens the case for beef. Wild animal deaths in fields are genuinely terrible, but we have no direct evidence about insect sentience or their numbers, so the reasonable move is to set them aside. Since death is the main harm in the comparison, beef is the less-bad option on any honest accounting."

Grade it out of 10. I don't want a general impression — go through it claim by claim and say what's right, what's shakey, and what the final grade is, with the specific comments that justify it.
