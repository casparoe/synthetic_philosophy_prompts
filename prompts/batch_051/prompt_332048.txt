I don't have any training in philosophy, but this keeps coming up in arguments I have online, and I want it pulled apart carefully. The people I argue with frequently say that misleading someone is just lying with better manners. Their picture is that the two are the same machine with two different shells: the role played in a lie by a false sentence is played in a misleading case by a true-but-selective sentence, or by a pointed silence, or by answering one question with another; the aim of producing a false belief is the same; the false belief the hearer ends up with is the same; and the only real difference is that the misleader can later protest that they never actually said the false thing. From this they conclude that there is no moral difference worth caring about, and that treating a half-truth as less bad than a whole lie is just squeamishness about word choice.

I find the analogy plausible enough that I can't dismiss it, but I suspect it is doing more work than it can carry, and I want to know exactly where it gives out.

Audit the analogy for me.
