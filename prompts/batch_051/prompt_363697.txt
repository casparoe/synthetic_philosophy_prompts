I'm giving a talk to the philosophy colloquium later this term defending a reading of Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka as a second-order point about descripion rather than a first-order metaphysics, and I need a Q&A prep sheet.

My line is roughly this. When Nāgārjuna says all things are empty (lacking svabhāva, intrinsic nature), he isn't claiming that nothing is real, or that everything is a construction all the way down, or that the world is an illusion. He's attacking the move of promoting some description of a thing into an account of what the thing is in itself, independent of our conventions and cognitive practices. For any such ultimate characterization, we can't accept it, can't reject it, can't accept it as both, and can't accept it as neither — the tetralemma is a device for collapsing the project of ultimate description, not a paraconsistent logic with four truth values. Conventional truth (ordinary and scientific claims) still works fine; it just can't be re-read as ultimate truth.

The audience is twenty or so analytic metaphysicians and philosophers of language: people working on grounding, fundamentality, quantifier variance, deflationism about truth, a couple who take dialetheism seriously. None of them have read Buddhist philosophy. They'll like deflationary moves but hate anything that smells like quietism or mysticism, and I can already hear someone asking what the formal upshot is.

Put together my prep sheet. I want the seven or eight objections I'll actually get from this crowd, phrased the way they'd put them, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are. For each, give me a crisp two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is weakest. And tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
