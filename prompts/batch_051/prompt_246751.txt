I do historical linguistics, mostly reconstructing a small family of languages with no long written record, so I lean heavily on regular sound correspondences and a few morphological patterns. Over time I've settled into a habit: I never let a lone exception weaken a proposed rule. Say I have a sound change that should turn an earlier *t into /s/ in a certain environment, and in a couple of hundred words it does, but in one word it surfaces as /ʃ/. My first move is always to reach for borrowing, dialect mixture, an unnoticed conditioning factor, or analogy dragging the word back into line with some other pattern. I don't lower my confidence in the rule until several exceptions show up, and ideally until they pattern together in a way that looks like a second process. I'd treat something like go/went as suppletion — one verb supplying a past tense that no rule ever generated — not as an exception that breaks the English past-tense rule. A collegue keeps telling me this is unfalsifiable: if every counterexample can be explained away after the fact, then my rules can't be tested. I think it's the only sane way to work, because languages are full of sporadic mess, and treating every odd form as a refutation would leave you with no generalisations at all. Who is right? Is there a defensible version of my rule, and what should the threshold actually be — in terms of number of exceptions, or in terms of their shape — before I treat them as a problem for the rule rather than noise around it?
