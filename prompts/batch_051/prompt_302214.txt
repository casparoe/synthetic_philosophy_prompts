I've been trying to get a handle on the claim that for Marx the economic base 'determines' the superstructure, and a friend sent me this argument that the functionalist rescue of it just collapses:

(1) To say the base functionally explains the superstructure is to say that legal, political, and ideological arrangements are the way they are because they tend to keep the economic base stable.
(2) If something is explained by its tendency to produce an effect, then either the effect itself or the present disposition to produce it has to be among the causes of the thing's existing or persisting.
(3) A thing cannot be caused by its own future effects; causes have to precede their effects.
(4) So superstructural arrangements are caused either by their future effects, which is backwards causation, or by someone intentionally setting them up with the benefit in view.
(5) Backwards causation is incoherent, and the intentional-design story is not materialism.
(6) Therefore the functionalist reading of 'the base determines the superstructure' is either incoherent or non-materialist.

I feel like the trick is in the move from (2) and (3) to (4), since (2) already mentions a present disposition, but I can't state the third option cleanly. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails? If it is valid, which premise should a functionalist deny, and does denying it leave them with a real explanatory story?
