I run a small philosophy-and-literature reading group and next month we're doing Hesse's Siddhartha. I want to push the conversation past the usual it's-about-self-discovery reading and read it through the lens of asceticism, frugality, and voluntary simplicity as ideals. The book seems to me geniunely conflicted here: it clearly admires the discipline Siddhartha learns with the Samanas (the fasting, the patience, the ability to go without), but it also has him walk away from that life and throw himself into the world of Kamala, Kamaswami, money, and gambling, and by the end he lands somewhere that isn't really rich or ascetic. I'd like you to help me read the novel through that tension. Where does it treat ascetic discipline as really valuable, and where does it suggest asceticism is its own kind of trap or pride? Does the book distinguish voluntary simplicity — chosen freely and meant to clarify attention — from the Samanas' self-mortification, and does that distinction hold up under what the novel actually shows? Is Siddhartha's stretch as a merchant and gambler a failure of the ideal, or does the book think he had to go through it to get anywhere? And how should I understand the ending with Vasudeva and the river: is it a recovery of simplicity, or something closer to acceptance than to renunciation? I'd also like a couple of questions on what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that straight essays on simple living miss — for instance, I worry it's saying asceticism can become its own craving, a kind of spiritual self-display. Give me mabye eight to ten pointed discussion questions, with a short note after the ones you expect to be contentious about how the conversation might go.
