I have a friend who works on early modern chartered companies and the history of sovereignty, and over the past year I have been collecting her binary verdicts — sovereign or not sovereign — on a variety of corporate and quasi-corporate bodies. I have deliberately been treating her as a black box and inferring the rule from its outputs, because I want a prediction that is stable against whichever explicit definition she currently gestures at.

Her verdicts so far are as follows.

- The Dutch East India Company at its mid-seventeenth-century height: sovereign. She emphasizes that it ruled territorial footholds, administered justice, minted coin, fielded armed forces, and concluded treaties in its own name.
- The English East India Company in Bengal after it acquired the diwani (revenue and civil administration): sovereign.
- The Hudson's Bay Company in the early eighteenth century: not sovereign. On her telling, Rupert's Land was an administered trade monopoly — a network of posts governed for the company's own servants — rather than a general civil jurisdiction over a settled population.
- Florida's Reedy Creek Improvement District as it operated until recently: not sovereign. It had taxing, zoning, utility, and bond authority over a district, but no criminal jurisdiction and no foreign relations.
- A large institutional arbitration provider whose awards are final, binding, and routinely enforced by national courts: not sovereign.

The next case I want to be able to predict is a modern concession. A consortium of three corporations is granted, by a treaty between a host state and two neighboring states, exclusive authority over a forty-square-mile enclave for ninety-nine years. The consortium may enact civil and criminal law through its own courts, operates a standing police force, levies taxes, controls immigration, and is not subject to the host state's ordinary legislation except insofar as it chooses to incorporate it. The grant is revocable only for gross and persistent rights violations, and disputes under the grant go to arbitration rather than the host state's courts. But the consortium may not conduct diplomacy, conclude treaties, maintain armed forces beyond internal policing, or claim representation in international bodies.

If she is being consistent with her prior verdicts, what should I predict she will say about the enclave?

Please work through: (1) the most natural rule that fits her verdicts so far; (2) the other rules that fit them equally well; (3) which of those rules diverge on the enclave, and what each predicts; (4) your best guess at her label, with the reason you assign more weight to one rule than the others; and (5) the further case, real or contrived, that would most cleanly settle which rule she is actually running.

End your response with a single line of the form \answer{sovereign} or \answer{not sovereign}, giving your best prediction.
