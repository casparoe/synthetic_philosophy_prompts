I serve on my city's Public Safety Technology Commission — I was appointed for my work as a software engineer, not for any legal or philosophical training. The city council has asked the Commission for a written opinion before Thursday's meeting on a proposed ordinance, and I'm circulating the first draft of the memo.

The ordinance responds to an incident last October, when an outdated shelter-closure notice was repeatedly re-ranked by a commercial content system as "urgent" during a flash-flood evacuation and reached tens of thousands of residents. The proposed language makes it a misdemeanor for a person to "deploy an automated system that materially amplifies false or misleading information about a public emergency, where the amplification creates a substantial risk of public panic or interference with emergency response." Our city attorney has cautioned that, under the void-for-vagueness standard, courts ask whether an ordinary person can tell what the law prohibits and whether the language leaves too much discretion to police and prosecutors. My instinct is to treat a statute like a specification: if I can't turn it into tests with defined failure modes, it delegates too much interpretive power to whoever runs it. But I also know emergency information is noisy, and I don't want to pretend a criminal law can be written like an API contract.

Please draft the short written opinion I would circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action — advance the ordinance as written, reject it, or send it back to the city attorney with specific redrafting instructions, including the particular terms or criteria to change — and it should reason through how precise a criminal law has to be in this setting, use the engineering angle where it genuinely helps, and engage honestly with the strongest consideration on the side I'm recommending against. Write the memo in American English.
