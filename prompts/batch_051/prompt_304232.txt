Below is a close paraphrase, in modern English, of a passage from one of Johannes Kepler's early seventeenth-century works defending a reformed astrology. Kepler compares astronomy to a wise mother and astrology to her foolish daughter, and he adds that though the mother would not starve without the daughter, it is the daughter whom the public pays and who thereby supports many working mathematicians. I want help understanding this image within Kepler's wider attitude toward astrology. Specifically, does the passage offer only a social or economic justification for tolerating astrology, or does it also reflect a theoretical view about which parts of astrology remain legitimate once the physical causes of celestial influences are properly understood? And how should the characterization of the daughter as foolish be read: as contempt for popular predictive astrology, or as something compatible with Kepler's own practice of casting horoscopes and publishing calendars? Explain what conceptual position the passage commits Kepler to, and distinguish it from both straightforward acceptance of judicial astrology and straightforward rejection of astrology.
