I run an upper-level seminar on moral psychology, 14 students, 90 minutes, and we're partway through Adam Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments. The students can restate the impartial spectator idea but I don't think they feel why Smith thought we needed it, and I'd rather they run into the problem themselves than have me explain it. I want a structured activity — not a free-forall discussion — where the class feels the asymmetry Smith is responding to: the same person who is a clear-eyed, sometimes harsh judge of other people's conduct turns into a lawyer for themselves the moment it's their own case. For Smith sympathy isn't mainly feeling sorry for someone; it's imagining your way into their situation until you share their sentiments, and the impartial spectator is the correction: an imagined standpoint from which you judge your own conduct the way a stranger with full information would. The wrinkle I want them to get is that this inner spectator is built from repeated give-and-take with actual onlookers but ends up solider than any particular bystander — so the activity shouldn't leave them thinking it's just a poll of the room.

So: design something for the full 90 minutes with the 14 students. Give me concrete roles or teams, rules (a bit of scoring or tiny stakes would help, so people have to actually commit), a timeline from setup through debrief, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto sympathy, self-partiality, and the impartial spectator. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when real students do it — people being too nice, people refusing to judge real cases, one pair dominating — and how to handle each. And be honest about the one thing the activity can't reproduce: Smith's spectator is an ideal, not a vote of whoever's in the room. End with a one-sentence summary of the core mechanic as the last line.
