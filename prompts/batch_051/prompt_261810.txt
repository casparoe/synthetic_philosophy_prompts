I have been reading two disconnected bodies of material: compiler correctness and formal semantics on one side, and theological arguments about whether a translation of Scripture can be inspired on the other. In the software case, a compiler is called correct when, given a formal semantics for the source and target languages, every observable behavior of the compiled program is one the source program could have had; translation validation proves this relation rather than trusting the compiler itself. In the theological case, some traditions treat a translation not merely as a fallible rendering but as carrying the same authority as the original: the Septuagint had a special status in some ancient Jewish sources, and KJV-onlyism holds that the English text is itself inspired rather than merely derivative from the autographs. My parallel is this: both projects face the problem of preserving a normative property across a language boundary when the end user cannot inspect the source. A KJV-only churchgoer and a user of a certified compiler both trust a derived artifact as if it were the source, on the basis of someone’s claim that the derivation preserved the right properties.

I can propose a first-pass mapping: source text maps to source program; inspired originals map to specification; translation maps to compilation; doctrinal or referential content maps to observable behavior; authoritative tradition maps to the verification regime; and unresolved ambiguity in the original maps to underdetermined specification. But I suspect the analogy may be doing more work than it should. In compiler verification, the semantics is fixed artificially in advance, whereas in translation the source meaning is exactly what is contested, and a community’s recognition of a translation seems partly constitutive of its inspired status in a way compiler certification is not.
