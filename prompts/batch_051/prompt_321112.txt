I am a retired physical scientist, and I have only lately begun reading philosophy with the care it deserves, so I will ask your patience if my formulation is imprecise. I have been puzzling over a specific tension in the history of opposition to capital punishment, and I would like a professional answer. Answer as a moral and political philosopher who has studied Camus closely, and take the question seriously rather than smoothing over its difficulties.

Camus opposed the death penalty categorically. As I read him, his case was not fundamentally about procedural reliability. It was a case about what the state is permitted to do: he thought the state has no right to carry out a coldly premeditated, irreversible killing, whatever the crime, because doing so forecloses any possibility of amendment and claims a finality no human institution is entitled to claim. Accept, for the moment, that I have him roughly right. Here is my difficulty.

Most of Camus's life and death—he died in 1960—belongs to a debate carried on in categorical terms. The center of gravity has since shifted. In the United States, at least, the most influential abolitionist arguments of recent decades have not been arguments of the sort Camus made. They are arguments from wrongful conviction, exposed by post-conviction DNA testing; from botched and visibly agonizing executions; from geographic and racial arbitrariness; from cost and delay. The public face of the abolitionist movement is now very largely the face of the innocence movement and the critique of execution protocols.

This looks to me like a strategic bargain. By moving from principle to procedure, abolitionists have made their cause persuasive to people who are not opposed to state killing in principle but are worried that the machinery is unreliable. The bargain seems to purchase traction at the price of Camus's central claim, because it implicitly concedes that a competent, accurate, properly administered system of execution could be acceptable.

My question is a counterfactual one about Camus. What would he make of this development? Specifically: would he accept the procedural and innocence-based arguments as legitimate second-best reasons that can coexist with a categorical abolitionism, or would he regard them as a trap that changes the subject and leaves the state's right to kill intact? Think through the strongest version of both positions before settling. Begin with a one-sentence statement of the conclusion you will defend, then give your reasoning. Where you are genuinely uncertain how far the historical Camus can be extended to a world he never saw, mark that uncertainty rather than hiding it. Keep your answer to no more than 600 words.
