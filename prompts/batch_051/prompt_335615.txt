I am a sansei—my mother's parents were teenagers when they were removed from their home in Los Angeles and sent to Manzanar in 1942—and I have spent the past few months trying to understand, not just feel, how a settled and law-abiding community came to be treated as an enemy race. Last week I read David Hume's essay "Of National Characters" (1748), and one passage has been bothering me ever since:

"The human mind is of a very imitative nature; nor is it possible for any set of men to converse often together, without acquiring a similitude of manners, and communicating to each other their vices as well as virtues. The propensity to company and society is strong in all rational creatures; and the same disposition, which gives us this propensity, makes us enter deeply into each other's sentiments, and causes like passions and inclinations to run, as it were, by contagion, through the whole club or knot of companions. Where a number of men are united into one political body, the occasions of their intercourse must be so frequent, for defence, commerce, and government, that, together with the same speech or language, they must acquire a resemblance in their manners, and have a common or national character, as well as a personal one, peculiar to each individual."

Earlier in the essay Hume distinguishes what he calls moral causes—government, national circumstances, the habits of conversation—from physical causes such as air and climate, and he argues that national character is mostly built from the moral ones. Here is the episode I want you to work on. In February 1942, about ten weeks after Pearl Harbor, President Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066; by the end of the war, roughly 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, about two-thirds of them American citizens, had been removed from the West Coast and confined in camps. The official justification was the risk of sabotage and espionage, framed in terms of an enemy race whose loyalties were presumed to follow ancestry; no act of sabotage or espionage by a Japanese American on the West Coast was ever established. The detention of citizens was upheld in Korematsu v. United States in 1944, and in 1988 Congress formally apologized and paid reparations.

Act as an expert in Enlightenment moral philosophy and the legal history of the United States. Begin by brainstorming, then write carefully. Treat this as a case analysis that runs in both directions: the theory should illuminate the episode, and the episode should test the theory.

First, reconstruct precisely the mechanism in the quoted passage. For Hume, what actually produces a "common or national character," and what roles do imitation, contagion, shared language, and membership in a single political body play? Second, map the internment onto that mechanism. Who were the supposed set of men sharing a character, and who was doing the ascribing? Which assumptions would Hume's account need if the government's inference from ancestry to disloyalty were to come out as a reasonable inference about moral causes, and do those assumptions hold here? Where does the theory explain something instructive about the episode, and where does it misdescribe it or fall silent—for instance about the difference between a national character slowly acquired through shared institutions and a racial character imposed from outside by the state? Third, run the test in the other direction: does the internment confirm Hume's account, stand as an anomaly against it, or reveal a case it never anticipated? Finally, say what the episode teaches about the larger question of whether national character is a coherent notion and when, if ever, it is acceptable to generalize about a people. Work only from the passage and the facts supplied; do not give me a reading list or citations.
