You are a professional philosopher who works on the foundations of optimisation and inductive inference. Please think carefully, and give your final answer in British English throughout.

I am a software engineer, with no formal philosophy background, and I build systems that optimise things (delivery routing, scheduling, recommendations). Lately I have noticed that I hold a set of specific judgements about when I am entitled to say what a system is 'really' optimising, and over what search space, on the strength of its behaviour alone. I want a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that makes sense of these judgements, tells me which of them I should keep, and rules on cases I have not yet made up my mind about. Here are my cases and verdicts.

Case 1. I observe two delivery-routing systems that produce exactly the same route choices on every one of ten thousand historical orders. I am told afterwards that the first was trained to minimise mean delivery time, and the second to optimise a weighted combination of on-time percentage and fuel savings. From the choices alone I refuse to attribute either objective, and even after hearing the intended objectives I do not think the behaviour itself picked one out. Verdict: the objective is underdetermined by the behaviour.

Case 2. A scheduling service emits the same weekly timetable no matter what demand, staffing, or outage data it receives. I do not take this as evidence that it has a rich 'preference' for that timetable. Almost any objective function that ranks that timetable at the top, combined with a very narrow search, would produce the same output. Verdict: with no variation in the chosen output, I will not infer an objective at all.

Case 3. A chess engine beats me from every opening I try. I have no hesitation saying that it is optimising for winning (wins preferred to draws preferred to losses), and that its search space is at least roughly the set of legal moves from any position. Verdict: behaviour across a wide and adversarial-looking range of inputs can reveal both the objective and the search space.

Case 4. I later discover that the same chess engine, under time pressure, prunes nearly all candidate moves except captures and checks. My inference about its search space was simply wrong, but my inference about its objective survives. Verdict: objective-inference and search-space-inference are asymmetrical — the objective can be right even when the search space is misread.

Case 5. Two planners always output the same list of approved changes. For one of them I later see a search trace: it considered merging modules A and B, rejected the merge because it would have increased response time, and chose the reordering that kept response time flat. For the second planner I never see any trace. Even though the two planners' final outputs are identical, I now take myself to know something about the first planner's objective — that low response time matters to it — and nothing about the second's. Verdict: a search trace showing which alternatives were considered and rejected is evidence about the objective, even when final outputs match.

Case 6. A network planner consistently chooses paths that avoid a particular node. I cannot tell whether avoiding that node is part of the objective, a hard constraint, or merely a bias in the way candidate paths are generated — the chosen path would look the same in all three situations. Verdict: without knowing whether paths through the node were ever generated and rejected, or never generated at all, behaviour alone cannot separate objective, constraint, and search bias.

Case 7. A resource allocator's chosen allocations are, across a thousand randomly sampled environments, always the unique optimum of a linear cost function that weights throughput at 0.7 and fairness at 0.3. I infer that this weighted sum is its objective. Yet when I later vary one background variable that happened to be fixed in my sample, its choices bend in a way that no single linear weighting predicts. Verdict: I over-inferred; the objective was only determined relative to the sampled environments, and the new variation shows the system is tracking something else, perhaps a learned proxy.

I have been testing a rough principle against these cases, something like: infer as the objective whatever pattern of preference the system's choices are most robustly aligned with across the widest and most varied set of circumstances, and only assign a search space where I have traces or interventions rather than final outputs alone. But I do not think this principle handles the tension between Cases 1 and 5, or between Cases 3 and 6, very cleanly.

I have one further case without a verdict. Case 8. I observe a bidding agent that, in every auction I can witness, chooses the bid that maximises expected surplus given its own estimates. I can see its estimates of costs and competitors, but I cannot see the code that generated the bids. Should I say it is optimising expected surplus, and may I also say its search space is the set of legal bids, or should I withhold both claims until I have seen the internals? Please let the principle you offer rule on Case 8.
