Here's a variation on Hempel's raven paradox I keep chewing on. Standard setup: the hypothesis "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens," so by the usual instance-confirmation idea a green apple—a non-black non-raven—counts as a positive instance of the second claim and thereby confirms the first, which feels like a joke. The standard Bayesian reply is that the green apple does confirm but only by a sliver, because non-black things are everywhere and ravens are a small minority.

Now flip that background. Take a large but finite closed domain—a warehouse, a biosphere dome, whatever—where ravens are very common and non-black things are rare. You know the total number of ravens and the total number of non-black things, but not how those two categories overlap; that overlap is the very thing in question. Suppose you sample at random, with replacement if that keeps the arithmetic clean. Work through, with actual numbers or ratios rather than hand-waving, the condition under which seeing a green apple confirms "all ravens are black" more strongly than seeing a black raven does. Show the likelihood-ratio arithmetic. Then tell me what the flip condition is, and whether the Hempelian qualitative story survives it or whether it was always doing it's quiet work through the abundance assumptions nobody wrote down.

After that, the part I'm least sure about: say which step in your comparison is doing the most work and what change to the sampling story—or to the prior over possible worlds—would flip the ordering back. Does the defender of the view that a green apple confirms "at all, never mind how much" have a good reply, or is that response just hiding everything?
