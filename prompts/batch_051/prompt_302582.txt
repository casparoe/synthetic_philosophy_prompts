I want my expert-witness scene to actually hold up instead of just vibes, so please roleplay as an expert who works on neuroethics and the philosophy of mind — think carefully, and start by brainstorming two or three different ways to frame the issue before you land on the strongest one. In the book, there's a consumer device, something between earbuds and an EEG headband, that lets people type by silently mouthing words to themselves; it picks up the motor signals behind subvocal speech and decodes them into text. It started as an accessibility tool, but by the time of the story basically everyone wears one for hands-free everything. My protagonist, a data analyst, has used one for years. The company's terms of service say it can use "de-identified neural data" to improve the product, and like everyone else she clicked agree. But she discovers the company is also running a risk-flagging algorithm over people's subvocalized speech, and it's flagged her for "persistent anxious rumination" and shared a summary with her employer's wellness program. In another storyline, a flag about violent thoughts gets forwarded to the police.

Her lawyer wants to tell a jury that the company is, for all practical purposes, listening to her think. The company's lawyer says no — the data is just electrical signals from muscle and nerve patterns that would have fired if she'd spoken aloud; it's speech she chose to produce for the device, not her "real" thoughts; she consented; and anyway a search provider that flags concerning searches isn't doing anything so different.

What I'm trying to figure out: what's the philosophically serious version of the claim that what the company did was wrong, or is it actually okay? Is the core problem a privacy harm, a this-violates-some-basic-freedom-over-your-own-mind harm, a consent problem (because meaningful consent to something this embedded in daily life is basically impossible), or something about the line between thinking to yourself and speaking being much more fragile then the company's lawyer admits? Does the "it's just motor signals, not thoughts" move have any real bite, or is it a dodge?

Give me the strongest framing you'd put in my protagonist's lawyer's mouth, with the reasoning behind it, and then also give me the company lawyer's best reply, so that neither side in the scene comes off as the one who's just there to loose.
