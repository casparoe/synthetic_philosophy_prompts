I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I have been asked to run a 90-minute session at a civic-education weekend for about 24 adults — a mix of working journalists, lawyers, local officials, and interested citizens. The weekend's theme is "rules and their limits," and my slot is meant to be participatory rather than a talk. I want the group to feel, in their own decisions, what is distinctive about a constitutional gap or interregnum: the moment when the existing legal order has no answer to the question "who governs now?", so any answer that sticks must draw on something other than the rules themselves — acceptance, performance, habit, force, or the sheer need for a decision. I do not want to lecture them on legitimacy versus legality; I want them to run into the problem themselves and only afterward have it named.

Act as a professional philosopher working in legal and political philosophy who also has real experience designing experiential workshops, and think carefully before settling on a design. Begin by brainstorming two or three candidate mechanics for the session and briefly say why you reject the weaker ones, then specify the one you recommend: the fictional crisis and the specific constitutional silence it turns on, the roles and the interests each role has, the rules of procedure (including what participants are and are not allowed to do, and what happens if no one acts), a timeline, and any materials I would need to prepare. The debrief matters as much as the game: map particular moments in the activity — the first person who claims authority, the moment others comply or refuse, the attempt to invent a procedure after the fact — onto the underlying ideas, including the gap between legal authorization and de facto acceptance, the bootstrap problem of a first decision that cannot itself be authorized, and the way "who is in charge" is sometimes settled by performance rather than by rule. Pay attention to the order in which you introduce these ideas so that each name lands on an experience the participants have just had. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of a real interregnum the activity cannot reproduce and what I will need to supply in the debrief. No reading list, no citations, and no lecture up front.
