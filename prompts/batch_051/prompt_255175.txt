For the past several years I have been part of a small working group on the philosophy of mathematics and computation, and our recurring topic has been the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis. We have not moved an inch in that time. One camp treats the thesis as a definition or an explication: the effectively computable functions just are the Turing-computable functions, so the thesis is not the kind of claim that could be false; it can only be more or less apt for some purpose. A second camp treats it as an empirical hypothesis about the limits of what finitely describable procedures can compute — and, in its physical form, about what any machine in our universe can compute — and holds that the thesis's survival of every attempted counterexample is genuine evidence, not definitional fiat. A third camp treats it as a methodological constraint: nothing counts as a computability claim unless it can be cashed out as a claim about some Turing-equivalent formalism, which makes the thesis a norm governing how we theorize rather than a description of an independent domain of facts. Everyone in the room knows the same formal results and the same history, and no one denies the equivalence of the standard formalisms; what has not converged is the classification of the thesis itself.

My working hypothesis is that much of the impasse is verbal in a fairly precise sense: the three camps are using the phrase "the Church–Turing thesis" to name three different propositions, with different criteria of correctness and different kinds of vulnerability to counterexample. But I doubt that diagnosis dissolves the whole dispute, because there appears to be a residual, non-verbal disagreement about whether there is a fact of the matter concerning effective computability that outruns any particular formalism, and a further disagreement about what it would take to show that there is or is not. I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a survey of the positions or a verdict on who is right. Which parts are verbal or definitional? Which parts turn on standards of evidence — for instance, on whether the persistent absence of a counterexample counts as evidence? Which parts, if any, rest on an empirical question about the physical world that is being mistaken for a conceptual one, or on a conceptual question being mistaken for an empirical one? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress: a sharper distinction, a formal result, a changed question, an experimental finding — or should we expect no movement, and if so, why?
