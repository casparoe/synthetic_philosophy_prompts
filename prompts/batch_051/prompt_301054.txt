Here's the question the student was answering:

"A carbon tax is standardly described as a market-based instrument, as opposed to command-and-control regulation. Explain what that contrast amounts to, and then assess the degrowth objection that carbon pricing only addresses a symptom of ecological damage while leaving the growth imperative in place. Is the degrowth critique an argument against carbon taxes, or is it compatible with them?"

Here's the student's answer:

"A carbon tax is market-based because it doesn't dictate how firms cut emissions. Instead, it attaches a price to each tonne of CO2 so that emitters face the social cost of their emissions. This is the Pigouvian solution to the externality problem: once the price is right, the market coordinates reductions, and firms with cheap abatement options cut more than firms with expensive ones. That cost-effectiveness is the main advantage over command-and-control regulation, which forces everyone to meet the same standard or adopt the same technology regardless of local conditions. Cap-and-trade is the other main market-based instrument; it caps total emissions and lets the permit price emerge from trading, so it controls the quantity but not the price. A carbon tax controls the price but lets the quantity adjust. Under full information the two are roughly equivalent, but when abatement costs are uncertain the choice matters, and the right choice depends on whether it is more important to stabilize emissions or to stabilize the carbon price.

It's important not to stretch the 'market-based' label too far, though. A carbon tax is not really a regulation the way a technology mandate is; it's a price on a good, so it works through markets rather than through the state telling firms what to do. The 'market-based versus command-and-control' contrast is therefore a contrast between letting markets respond to prices and using legal compulsion.

Degrowth writers like Jason Hickel object that carbon pricing is a technocratic fix that leaves the growth imperative untouched. On their view, a carbon tax tells the economy to keep growing as long as it grows on cleaner energy, while the real problem is the overall scale of material throughput, which a price on carbon doesn't question. Efficiency gains get partly eaten by rebound effects, so emissions per dollar can fall while total emissions keep rising. This is why degrowth is often misunderstood: it is not a recession, which is an unplanned contraction with unemployment and hardship, but a deliberate policy of shrinking GDP as an end in itself, so that a smaller economy can stay within planetary boundaries.

In the end, I think the degrowth critique is not an argument against carbon taxes as such. Even a degrowth economy would need a high carbon price during the transition, to signal which activities belong in a low-throughput economy. The critique is better read as a warning that a carbon tax alone, inside a growth-dependent macroeconomy, is insufficient rather than misguided. So the two are compatible, but the tax should be paired with hard throughput caps rather than left to market forces alone."
