Here are the objections to the claim that computer-assisted proofs, such as the Appel–Haken proof of the four-colour theorem and Hales's proof of the Kepler conjecture, are genuine mathematical proofs on an equal epistemic footing with proofs that a human being can survey, that I have already collected:

(1) The apriority objection. Mathematical theorems are ordinarily taken to be knowable a priori, yet a proof whose force depends on the actual operation of a piece of physical machinery rests on contingent empirical facts, and thereby turns mathematics into something like experimental science.

(2) The surveyability objection. A proof must be surveyable: a competent mathematician should be able to take in the whole proof and check it step by step. Exhaustively checking thousands of configurations with a machine is not the sort of thing a single mind can take in as a perspicuous whole, and in practice no one surveys it, so it fails that requirement.

(3) The reliability objection. Between the intended argument and the printed theorem runs a chain of hardware, firmware, compilers, and source code, any element of which may fail silently. The warrant for the theorem therefore rests on engineering evidence of the sort that supports an instrument reading, not on demonstration. This is distinct from (2): a proof could be fully surveyable yet implemented by unreliable machinery, and a reliable machine could produce an unsurveyable output.

(4) The explanation objection. A proof ought to show why a theorem is true and yield understanding. An unsurveyable case-check can certify that the result holds whilst leaving its mathematical reason opaque.

(5) The social-practice objection. Mathematical proof is a communal practice: a proof counts as such only if it can be communicated, checked, and accepted by the mathematical community. When checking is delegated to a black box that no member of the community actually examines, mathematicians are reduced to accepting testimony rather than doing mathematics. This is distinct from (2): the worry here is not about a single mind's grasp but about the social labour of checking being displaced.

(6) The translation objection. A machine-checked formal proof establishes a formal statement, not the informal theorem that the mathematician cares about. There is an additional, non-trivial claim that the formalisation faithfully captures the original theorem, and certainty about the formal statement does not transfer automatically to the informal one.

I suspect these six already exhaust the standard objections, so I am looking for a seventh that is genuinely outside this list. It should not be a restatement, special case, or combination of any of them; for instance, pressing the reliability worry onto one component such as the compiler, or inflating (6) into an ever-higher regress of verification, would not count. Once you have supplied it, tell me honestly whether it is stronger or weaker than the objections above, and why.
