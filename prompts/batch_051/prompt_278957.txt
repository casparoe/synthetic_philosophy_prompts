In my philosophy elective this semester, we have been studying whether seeing an object is importantly different from seeing a picture of it. Our teacher gave us four criteria for when a person literally sees an object, rather than merely seeing a representation of it. First, the object must be part of the causal chain producing the person's current visual experience. Second, the person's visual experience must counterfactually track the object's visible properties: if the object's color, shape, or position changed, the experience would change correspondingly. Third, the experience must encode spatial information about the object as seen from a standpoint—angle, distance, occlusion—rather than merely describing or symbolizing it. Fourth, the process connecting object to experience must be "belief-free": no person's interpretation, memory, or imagination may do the tracking, the way an artist's mind does when making a sketch. The teacher used these criteria to contrast seeing a deer in the yard with seeing a pencil sketch of that same deer; the sketch fails at least the fourth criterion. But then she said something several of us are still arguing about: that photographs, unlike paintings, satisfy the criteria well enough that when you look at one, you literally see the thing photographed, even if that thing no longer exists. I have been turning this over using a photograph of my grandmother, who died when I was little. The photograph seems to pass the first criterion (her reflected light caused the exposure), the fourth (the process was optical and chemical, not guided by anyone's beliefs), and arguably the third (it preserves how she looked from a particular angle and distance). But the second criterion seems to fail: nothing about her can change now, so my current experience cannot track her. If she were still alive and changed her hair today, the photograph would not change. And that failure captures, better than anything else, why looking at the photograph feels like seeing a record of her rather than seeing her.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher of perception and work through this carefully. Start by briefly distinguishing the possible readings of the second criterion, since that seems to be where the cases come apart. Then go criterion by criterion for each of four cases: seeing someone in a mirror, seeing a hallway through a live video feed, looking at the old photograph of my grandmother, and looking at a hyperrealist painting made from life. For each case, commit to a verdict: do I literally see the object, or only a representation of it? Finally, address the main question head-on: does the photograph show that the four criteria need to be revised or supplemented—for instance, by adding a real-time condition, or by allowing the counterfactual condition to be satisfied at the moment the image was made rather than now? State which revision you actually endorse and why, and test it against all four cases so that it does not merely rig the result. Please give your answer in American English.
