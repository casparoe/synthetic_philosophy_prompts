Okay so i've fallen down this rabbit hole after a family dinner where my brother announced that agriculture was the worst thing that ever happened to us and my cousin shot back that hunter-gatherers were basically starving all the time. I've read enough since then to know they're both cherry-picking, but I still couldn't tell you what would actually settle the argument. What I want is the spec sheet: what does a theory of the shift to farming have to get right before I should trust it? I'm not after the usual "here's why farming won" stories; I want the requirements any such story has to meet, with the case or consideration behind each one. What cases must it handle — adoption in the Fertile Crescent but also in places with totally different crops and animals, sticking with farming even where the skeletons show people got shorter and more diseased, groups that never farmed or that went back to foraging, sedentary foragers before farming. What distinctions can't it be allowed to collapse — cultivating a bit versus committing to full sedentism, the first farmers versus the people farming was imposed on, having more food versus having more leisure (Sahlins's whole point), a region adopting farming versus a population simply being replaced by farmers. What empirical results can't it contradict — the health and stature data, the dates, the fact that inequality doesnt just automatically show up the moment grain does. And I want a verdict on which demands are non-negotiable and which are just the prejudices of a particular camp, plus the single requirement that does the most damage to the popular stories — Diamond's "it was a mistake" line, the population-pressure story, the "farming was obviously better so everyone wanted it" story. Keep the scorecard part short; the criteria are what I actually need, since I want to be able to judge new theories myself instead of just memorizing which one I'm supposed to like.
