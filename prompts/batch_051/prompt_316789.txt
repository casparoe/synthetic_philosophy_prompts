I've spent the last few years pulled between two positions about virtual worlds. A friend insists that losing something in a game is never a real loss: it is just a rearrangement of database entries, and the distress it causes is a category mistake. He adds that a virtual sword is not even the same object from one login to the next, since it has no continuous material substratum and is reinstantiated from storage; I suspect that is a red herring, but I cannot yet say why. I want to resist his conclusion, because it seems to ignore how people actually live now, but I also do not want to say that every in-game event is as real as its physical counterpart, since that would flatten the difference between a ruined evening and a broken arm. To work out where I stand, I have collected my verdicts on cases.

(1) A server rollback erases my teenager's summer-long city in a shared building game through nobody's fault; my kid is devastated. I think my kid lost something real, and the grief is fitting rather than confused. (2) A friend secretly deletes my 150-hour single-player save as a prank. That is a real harm and a real wrong; the fact that no one else ever saw the save makes the secrecy worse, not better. (3) In a VR social space, a stranger repeatedly moves their avatar's hands over my avatar's body after I ask them to stop. This is a genuine harm, closer to assault than to rudeness, even though no physical body was touched. (4) A player uses an exploit to remove a rare sword from another player's inventory in a game whose rules and economy treat that sword as owned property. The owner was genuinely robbed; the sword is real in the only sense that matters inside that world. (5) On a sandbox server whose rules explicitly permit griefing, a group destroys a castle that took two months to build. The builder's loss is real, but I hesitate to say the destroyers violated a right; still, I cannot shake the feeling that they did something wrong. (6) I burn down a village of non-player characters in a single-player game. Not a harm; there is no one there to be harmed, whatever the pixels show. (7) A virtual pet on my phone dies because I neglect it. My sadness is real, but I do not think the pet was harmed. This one troubles me, because if my 150 hours in case (2) grounds a real loss, I do not see why my months of feeding and caring for the pet should not ground one too. (8) A black-market trader is scammed out of a currency stack worth about four thousand dollars in real-money exchange. Obviously a real harm; the money settles it, though I would be uneasy if it were the only thing that made virtual loss real. (9) A bot network inflates an in-game currency and wipes out the value of a friend's hard-earned fortune. A real economic harm to my friend — a harm to the real worth of all those hours — even though the currency has no official real-money price.

I would like you to tell me whether there is one principle about the reality of virtual objects and virtual harms that captures these verdicts, or close to it. Please state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, as precisely as you can; say which of my verdicts it forces me to drop or revise and whether the revision is worth it; and apply the resulting view to a case I have not settled: I pay a real-money premium for a cosmetic item in a game because its whole value lies in expected exclusivity, and a month later the developer makes it freely available to everyone, breaking no rule and no promise. I feel cheated. Is that feeling tracking a real harm, a real loss in the value of an object, or neither?
