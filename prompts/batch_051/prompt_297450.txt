The classic "cyberspace as a distinct place" argument of the mid-1990s held that online life occurs in a borderless realm outside any state's effective jurisdiction, and therefore that geographically defined speech restrictions are both practically unworkable when applied to the internet and normatively suspect, while online communities may be left to develop their own governance. A later architectural development may have undercut the argument: internet speech is now mediated by a handful of global platforms and infrastructure providers — search engines, app stores, content-delivery networks, domain registries, payment processors — that can, and routinely do, enforce territorial law at scale through geo-blocking, takedowns, and de-listing orders. Does the "cyberspace as a distinct place" argument survive this development? Say whether it still goes through, needs repair, or is dead.
