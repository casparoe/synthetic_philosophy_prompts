Consider a cross-institutional practice: hospitals are reimbursed partly on patient-satisfaction surveys and readmission metrics, schools and teachers are assessed by gains on standardized tests, and content platforms tune recommendation systems to maximize watch time or engagement. The people designing these regimes typically concede that the metric is not the underlying good—health, learning, worthwhile attention—but they proceed as if the metric were a serviceable proxy for it. What view of the relationship between a quantitative measure and the value it stands in for does this practice presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose merely that the metric is correlated with the value ex ante, or the stronger claim that optimizing the metric will not systematically corrupt that correlation? Reconstruct the implicit theory of measurement and incentive, separate what the practice is actually committed to from what it is merely compatible with, and identify where rival theories could license the same practice. Then say what would have to change if the institutions instead treated Goodhart's law—that a measure made into a target tends to stop being a good measure—and specification gaming, where an agent satisfies the letter of an objective while defeating its purpose, as predictable effects of measurement under pressure rather than as occasional failures.
