My econ friend keeps telling me Marx's worries about machinery are overblown, and his whole argument hangs on an analogy: a factory machine is just a worker made of iron. Capital doesnt care about flesh versus metal; it just sees a task done for a lower wage, by something that never unionizes and doesnt get tired. So automation is basically the firm swapping expensive workers for cheaper ones, and anyone afraid of robots taking our jobs is confusing a normal labor-market adjustment for something sinister. Wages reallocate, new jobs show up, and a machine is just a better bargain.

I suspect he hasnt actually tracked what Marx says, but I want to pin down exactly how far his analogy holds rather than just hand-wave. Treat it as an audit. What genuinely maps between a worker and a machine under capitalism, where does the mapping break, and what does the "iron worker" picture quietly import that the target lacks? Then the payoff question: does his conclusion survive the disanalogies? I'd like an explicit table of correspondences, and a separate list of which disanalogies actually matter for his conclusion and which are just niggles.
