I am a doctoral candidate in early Chinese political philosophy, and I have been asked to help mark an undergraduate examination paper for a course on classical Chinese political thought. One of the questions concerns Han Feizi and the contrast between Legalism and Confucian rule by virtue. The question read as follows: Some interpreters hold that Han Feizi sought to replace rule by virtue with rule by incentives. Reconstruct the argument behind that reading, and then assess whether Han Feizi's system leaves any serious role for the virtue of the ruler or for the moral transformation of those governed. A student submitted the following answer.

Han Feizi is best read as replacing the Confucian project of ruling through moral example with a system of rule by incentives. The Confucians supposed that a virtuous ruler would transform the people through education, ritual, and personal example; Han Feizi regards that entire approach as unreliable, because most people, and above all ministers, respond to calculations of benefit and harm rather than to admiration for virtue. The remedy is to make obedience align with people's interests. Han Feizi therefore builds the state out of three elements: fa, the public standards of reward and punishment; shu, the techniques by which the ruler appoints, tests, and controls his ministers; and shi, the positional advantage that comes with office. The two handles of reward and punishment are the core of this machinery, and Han Feizi's standing warning is that the ruler must never allow these levers to pass into ministerial hands. He follows Shang Yang in thinking that, of the two handles, punishment is the only one that genuinely does the work: rewards are at best a soft supplement to be kept minimal, since fear of severe and certain penalties compels compliance more reliably than hope of gain. It follows that, for Han Feizi, law is simply whatever the ruler commands. Because the ruler is the source of law, his edicts need not be general, public, or stable in order to bind, and any attempt to constrain the ruler by his own laws misunderstands the voluntarist character of his position. The ruler's own virtue therefore drops out of the account; all that matters is that the apparatus of reward and punishment remains intact. Likewise, no moral transformation of the governed is even sought: subjects comply because it suits their interests, not because they have become good. That, the student concludes, is precisely the advantage of rule by incentives over rule by virtue.

For each point, indicate whether the student has rendered Han Feizi accurately, and where any claim is inaccurate or conflated explain precisely what is wrong and how serious the problem is for the argument as a whole. Please write your assessment in British English.
