I teach a graduate seminar on social conventions and the evolution of norms, and I am writing a short position statement for my students to critique. Here is the set of commitments I have been defending. First, I accept the central elements of David Lewis's account in Convention (1969): a convention is a regularity in behavior within a group in a recurring situation, where nearly everyone conforms and expects others to conform, and where there is at least one alternative regularity that the group would have settled on instead if it had become the expected pattern; that alternativeness is what makes the convention arbitrary. Second, I think many conventions, especially high-stakes ones like property and exchange norms, cannot be stable purely as solutions to coordination problems because they also involve partial conflicts of interest, so they require sanctions or internalized dispositions to punish violators, not just mutual expectations. Third, I believe conventions can acquire genuinely normative force over time: participants come to regard violations as wrong and deserving of censure, and this shift cannot be reduced to a prediction that violations will be punished. Fourth, I hold that norms evolve through a broadly selectionist process—variation, retention, and differential spread depending on how well they stabilize cooperation—and that this process is not guided by anyone's intention or by common knowledge of the whole pattern. Fifth, I think the arbitrariness of a convention undermines any claim that its content is intrinsically justified, but does not undermine the legitimacy of enforcing it once it is entrenched. Sixth, I endorse a path-dependence claim: once a convention is entrenched, it can persist even when a majority would prefer to shift to a different regularity, because no single actor can unilaterally bring about the shift and it remains common knowledge that everyone expects the old pattern. Go through these six commitments and tell me where the tensions are. For each pair that is in tension, say how severe the tension is and what the cheapest revision would be; then identify which apparent tensions are not genuine and why; and finally tell me which single revision would do the most to make the set coherent, and what that revision would cost me.
