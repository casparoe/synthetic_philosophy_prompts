I'm a grad student in philosophy working on a paper about curiosity as an intellectual virtue, and I keep running into a self-conflict I'd like you to help me think through. Act as a professional philosopher who works on virtue epistemology and the value of knowledge. Think carefully and maybe start by laying out the tension before taking sides.

Here's my situation. I have a rule with my own reading: I don't start a book, article, or even a long forum post unless I can tell myself, in advance, what question it's answering or what problem it will help me solve. If I can't state the cash-value, I put it down. I've justified this as protecting my curiosity from becoming intellectual hoarding—there's too much to read, and I'd rather know a few things deeply then have a million half-started threads. But I also suspect this is really a bias: I've been filtering out pretty much everything that isn't legible as "useful" from the start, and that seems to be exactly the attitude that kills the kind of curiosity that finds questions you didn't know you had.

What brought this to a head was rereading Leviathan chapter 6. Hobbes there distinguishes curiosity from other passions by saying, roughly, that curiosity is a lust of the mind, and what makes it differ from bodily lust is that its delight is not in any particular object but in the continual and indefatigable generation of knowledge. He even contrasts the short vehemence of carnal pleasure with the perseverance of delight in knowing. That is, he seems to be saying that curiosity's value, or at least its distinctive pleasure, lies in the process of coming to know, not in any finished product you can list in advance. If that's right, my rule is almost perfectly designed to weed out this kind of curiosity, because I'm always demanding the product before I allow the process to start.

So here's what I want you to help me figure out. Is my rule a defensible epistemic policy, or am I just rationalizing a loss of intellectual patience and calling it discipline? Can I restate the rule so it keeps the benefit—not drowning in low-ROI reading—without capping the process-curiosity Hobbes is describing? And be honest: maybe the rule is fine for a professional researcher but bad for a person trying to be intellectually virtuous. If so, how would I tell the difference from inside my own habits?
