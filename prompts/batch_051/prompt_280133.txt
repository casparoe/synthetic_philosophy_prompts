I'm not an academic, but I've been reading about a thought experiment called Death in Damascus and it keeps poking at me. The way I understand it: a merchant has to choose whether to stay in Damascus or go to Aleppo. He learns that Death, who is a perfect predictor, has already made an appointment to meet him in whichever of the two cities hell actually go to — if he'll stay, Death will be in Damascus; if he'll flee, Death will be in Aleppo. So whichever town he picks, he walks straight into the appointment. The part that bugs me is that his choice doesn't even cause Death to be there; the two are just perfectly correlated. Leaving Damascus looks like the obvious move, but that's exactly the move that puts him in front of Death in Aleppo. If he stays, Death is there too. There's no option that actually improves his outcome, even though one option obviously looks better than the other.

I've started to think this has implications for ordinary situations where the outcome depends less on what I do than on someone elses accurate prediction of what I'll do — like a parent or a boss who already knows I'll cave, or a friend who knows I'll take the path of least resistance. What are the most important implications of the Death in Damascus puzzle for how I should actually make decisions when I suspect someone can predict me? And please commit: should I sometimes do the thing that looks worse just because it's the thing a predictor wouldn't have expected? Is that genuinely rational, or is it just superstition about outsmarting a forecast? I want your actual position and your reasons for it, not a menu of options.
