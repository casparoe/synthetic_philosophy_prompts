I am trying to settle what I think in the debate between those who say all reasons must connect to something the agent already wants (internal reasons) and those who say some reasons bind an agent independently of her present motives (external reasons). Rather than start from the theories, I want to work from my gut reactions to particular cases, and I would like to know whether any one principle captures them.

Here are the cases.

1. A man has smoked for thirty years. He knows the health risks in a general way but has never cared about them; he says, sincerely, that he would rather die a few years earlier than give up cigarettes. I think he still has a reason to quit.

2. A woman has exactly one desire: to count blades of grass on the lawn outside her flat. It harms no one, she enjoys it, and she is good at it. I find it very hard to say she has no reason at all to go count grass this afternoon — though I also want to say that this reason matters very little.

3. A severely depressed man has, at the moment, no desires at all; he cannot bring himself to want anything. His doctor has told him that if he gets out of bed and calls the clinic, a treatment is likely to help. I think he has a reason to get out of bed and make the call, even though there is nothing in his present psychology pulling him toward it.

4. A young woman passionately wants to be a concert pianist and is genuinely miserable at the thought of not becoming one, but she refuses to practice scales and finds practicing boring and pointless. I think she has a reason to practice, because practicing is the only realistic means to what she already wants.

5. A businessman has no sympathy for others, feels no pull of conscience, and does not care whether his actions are just. He can embezzle from his clients without being caught. I still think he has a reason not to embezzle, though I am unsure whether to say it is a reason he has, or merely a reason that applies to him.

6. A friend of mine mocks me cruelly behind my back, but I do not know it and have no evidence of it. I want to say I have a reason to end the friendship, in the sense that anyone in my situation who knew the facts would have that reason; but I also want to say that, not knowing, I cannot be blamed for not acting on it.

7. A child does not want to learn to read and cannot see why it matters. I think he has a reason to learn, even though nothing in his present aims connects to literacy.

I can see that my verdicts pull in different directions. Cases 2 and 4 make it seem as if reasons depend on what a person actually wants; cases 1, 3, 5, and 7 make it seem as if at least some reasons are simply there, independently of the person's present wants; case 6 seems to be about knowledge rather than desire. Is there one principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures all, or nearly all, of these verdicts? State it precisely, and say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and whether I should revise those verdicts. Please also tell me what the principle says about a further case I have not listed: an adult who would want to stop drinking if he vividly understood what his drinking is doing to his family, but who deliberately keeps that understanding vague. Does he have a reason to stop now?
