I have been following the long-running controversy over whether culture can be treated as a cause in social explanation, and I have the impression that the same moves recur every generation without the argument actually advancing. The canonical case is Max Weber's Protestant ethic thesis: ascetic Protestantism, and Calvinism in particular, instilled a methodical, this-worldly discipline of calling, thrift, and reinvestment, which in turn helped generate the spirit of modern capitalism. The classical objections are familiar. Materialists argued that the causal arrow ran the other way, with commercial development creating or selecting the ethic rather than the ethic creating commercial development. Empirically minded historians countered that dynamic commercial life appeared in Catholic and Lutheran regions too, and that the fit between Calvinist theology and bourgeois behavior was looser than Weber allowed.

But the real impasse, as I see it, is not about seventeenth-century Europe. It is about whether values and norms can function as causes in the first place. One side treats culture as a legitimate explanatory variable: people internalize norms, norms shape preferences and habits, and behavior follows. The other side responds that culture is too vague to carry that load, that norms are often rationalizations or after-the-fact descriptions of behavior driven by incentives and institutions, and that a causal explanation needs something sharper. The fight then recurs under new labels. A generation announces that culture is decisive, the skeptics show that institutions and material interests explain most of the variance, and then the culturalists return with better definitions of norms and with natural experiments, insisting there is an unexplained residue. Each side ends convinced that the other has either defined culture into untestability or explained it away by fiat.

I would like a diagnosis rather than another adjudication of the Calvinism question. What kind of disagreement is this? Is it a disagreement about the historical facts of early modern Europe, about whether values can be causes in the same sense that prices or institutions are, about standards of evidence in the social sciences, about different senses of explanation, causal mechanism versus interpretive understanding, or are the two camps answering different questions under the single heading of culture? My working hypothesis is that the last of these does much of the work, together with an unstated disagreement about what a social explanation is supposed to deliver. But I want that hypothesis tested against what each side actually says. Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a research design, a formal result, a reframing of the question, or an argument that the dispute cannot be settled and should be left behind. Please answer in American English.
