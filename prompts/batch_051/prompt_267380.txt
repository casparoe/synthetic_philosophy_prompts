I retired some years ago from a career in the natural sciences and have only recently started reading moral philosophy in earnest, so I am probably the sort of amateur who invents a distinction that either already exists under another name or collapses under pressure. I would be grateful for an honest assessment of one such invention.

I have been thinking about cases in which a person uses an animal product without bringing any production about: eggs from genuinely rescued hens that would otherwise be thrown away, meat recovered from supermarket bins, leather from an animal that died naturally. It seems wrong to lump these together with ordinary purchases, but the usual term 'freegan' seems to me to cover several distinct questions at once. I have therefore started using a principle I call the Residue Test, which has two arms. The causal arm: a use is permissible only if the item is a genuine residue — had one abstained, it would have gone to waste, and one's taking it neither passes through a market that could register the taking as demand nor alters any decision to breed, confine, or kill an animal. The candor arm: the use must be one the agent can openly avow, without concealment, to people whose moral convictions the agent claims to share. My claim is that much of the disagreement about rescued-hen eggs and bin-recovered meat is a confusion between these arms: the causal arm answers the charge of complicity in animal suffering, while the candor arm answers the charge of quietly normalizing the use of animals as resources. The test seems to get the cases right: it permits eggs from rescued hens; it forbids buying cage eggs even where a single carton would not affect a farm, because the purchase passes through the market; and it explains what is wrong with secretly serving bin-recovered meat to guests who were not told its origin — the failure is candor, not complicity.

Please assess the Residue Test honestly: reconstruct it in its strongest form, say which existing ideas it most closely resembles (conceptually only — no bibliography or citations), and show where it breaks. Approach this as a professional moral philosopher, and before settling on an answer, think through the strongest reading of the test and the most damaging objections.

Then give me only a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the most charitable reconstruction of the test; (2) the closest existing ideas; (3) the deepest problem with the causal arm; (4) the deepest problem with the candor arm, including whether that arm is a moral condition or a merely strategic one; and (5) an overall verdict on whether the two-arm distinction is doing real work.
