In his 1929 book The Thing, G. K. Chesterton presents what has come to be known as the principle of Chesterton's fence: if a reformer finds a fence or gate across a road and does not see its use, that very failure is made into a reason to leave it standing until the reformer discovers the use it once had. Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France runs a structurally similar argument at the level of whole institutions: arrangements inherited from the past carry a presumption that they solve problems their present critics have not yet perceived. Notice a particular asymmetry in the way this argument is normally deployed. The person who wants to remove an existing practice is required to explain the practice's hidden justification before he acts; the person who wants to keep it is not required to produce that justification himself. He may simply insist that there must be one. Is this asymmetric distribution of epistemic burdens justifiable, or is it a built-in bias in favor of whatever already exists? Test it with a case in which a rule or institution is retained solely because nobody on either side can say what it is for, and with a second case in which the original reason is fully known and has obviously disappeared. In each case, state whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or abandoned.
