I manage the risk-assessment unit at a state environmental agency. We publish an annual Risk Outlook for legislators, county emergency managers, and the public, and every probability in it has to survive review by our general counsel and, occasionally, a legislative audit. The estimates we publish are a mixed bundle: the annual probability that a flood of a given magnitude occurs at a particular gauge station, computed from 90 years of records; the probability that a specific levee fails in the next 12 months; the probability that a nearby fault produces a magnitude 6.5 or greater earthquake within 30 years; and the probability that a major remediation project stays within its current budget. My analysts defend these numbers in different vocabularies. Some say probability just is long-run frequency in a reference class. Others say it is a degree of belief to be updated as evidence comes in. Still others speak of the physical chance of the system itself. Last year a reviewer objected that we had presented as an objective hard number what is actually expert judgment, and the audit committee nearly killed the report.

I want a reusable written procedure — a checklist or ordered set of tests — that my analysts can follow to decide which interpretation of probability should govern each published estimate, how to phrase the number given that interpretation, and when the honest answer is that reasonable analysts could disagree and the report must say so. Ground it in the real philosophical distinctions among frequentist, subjective, and propensity interpretations, but translate them into operational language for analysts with no philosophy background. The decisions recur annually, the stakes are high, and the constraint is that whatever we publish must be defensible by non-philosophers under adversarial review.

Work through this as a professional philosopher of probability who has also done applied risk assessment. Start by brainstorming the decision-relevant differences among the interpretations and the characteristic failure modes of misapplying each; then settle on a final procedure. Lead with the procedure's bottom line in the first sentence of your response, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Include worked applications to at least three of the four example estimates, including at least one in which the procedure forces my team to give up an authoritative-sounding number it would rather keep, and explicitly flag the steps where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and a human has to decide.
