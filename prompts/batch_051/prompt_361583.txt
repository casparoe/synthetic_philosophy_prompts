I have been working through the precautionary-style objections to gene drives and de-extinction, and I keep encountering a line of argument that is meant to deflate the entire objection. It goes like this. (1) If a proposed intervention could produce harms that are irreversible, that is a decisive reason against carrying it out. (2) Yet every ecological intervention — restoring a wetland, reintroducing a native predator, even fencing off a reserve — changes the system in ways that are strictly irreversible: the system will never return to exactly its prior state. (3) So if the gene-drive proposal is to be rejected because its harms might be irreversible, then these ordinary conservation practices must be rejected on the same grounds. (4) It would be absurd to reject ordinary conservation practices merely because they leave the system different from how it was. (5) Therefore, irreversibility cannot by itself give us a special reason to restrict gene drives.

I accept something like (2) and (4), and yet the conclusion strikes me as too quick, but I cannot locate exactly where the argument smuggles in the contested step. It feels as though the sense in which a drive that suppresses a population cannot be undone is quite different from the sense in which no intervention leaves a system exactly as it was, but I want a precise statement of the failure. Is the argument valid? If so, should we simply accept that irreversibility is doing no independent work? If not, identify the exact step — or the equivocation or suppressed premise between steps — that fails, and say how the principle should be restated so that it captures the distinct concern about gene drives without ruling out every form of ecological management.
