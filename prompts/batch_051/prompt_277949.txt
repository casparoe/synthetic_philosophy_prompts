I cover global labor and ecology, and I keep noticing the same conceptual move in two debates that are usually kept apart. In the sweatshop argument, the dispute turns on the baseline: measured against the worker's next-best alternative, the exchange is a mutual gain, but measured against fair terms or the bargain that would exist absent coercive background institutions, it can count as exploitative even though both parties gain. In ecology, the mutualism–parasitism distinction appears to pivot on the same choice of baseline — a symbiont is a mutualist if the host does better with it than without it, but "without it" might mean an uncolonized host, a host with a different symbiont, or a host in another environment, and the classification flips accordingly. Is this a deep identity of structure — one shared problem about benefit, exploitation, and counterfactual baselines — or is it only that both fields use words like "benefit"? If it is deep, what would have to be true for results to transfer between the two debates, and what does the analogy predict that turns out false?
