I am a doctoral student in philosophy, writing on the moral psychology of Adam Smith, and for a chapter on the impartial spectator I have been trying to work out how Smith’s mechanism fares when it is embedded in a concrete social world rather than treated as a piece of armchair psychology. I would like to read Kazuo Ishiguro’s The Remains of the Day — the 1989 novel, though the film may be drawn on as well — through Smith’s account of conscience in The Theory of Moral Sentiments. On Smith’s view, as I understand it, we judge the propriety of our own sentiments by imagining how a fully informed and disinterested spectator would respond to them, and conscience is the internalised perspective of that spectator. One of Smith’s central worries is that the actual people around us, whose approval we covet, can distort or displace this internal judge. Stevens, the butler, looks like a sustained study of exactly that failure: he subordinates his moral judgement to Lord Darlington, treats the suppression of spontaneous feeling as professional virtue, and only late in life comes to register what that deference cost him and Miss Kenton.

I would like a reading that is faithful in both directions, treating the novel as more than an illustration of the theory and the theory as more than a cudgel with which to judge Stevens. Where does the impartial-spectator model genuinely illuminate his self-deception, his unconvincing rationalisations, and his late regret? And where does Ishiguro complicate Smith — for instance, by suggesting that Stevens is not merely weak-willed, or too partial to his own interests and comforts, but that his capacity to occupy an impartial standpoint has itself been shaped by class, by the culture of service, and by a conception of dignity that equates self-command with self-erasure? I would be grateful for close attention to the scenes you think most revealing — the night of his father’s death, the smaller scenes with Miss Kenton in which feeling is offered and refused, and the final encounter on the pier at Weymouth — and for an account of what the novel adds to Smith’s picture that the treatise alone does not. Finally, please say how confident you are in the reading, and single out the point you are least sure of.
