Sorting out where I land on scientific realism and the pessimistic meta-induction. Here's the package, numbered for reference, not by how sure I am:

(1) I'm a realist: our best mature theories are approximately true, and their central unobservables (electrons, genes, fields) exist.

(2) I think the pessimistic meta-induction is the strongest argument against (1), and any adequate realism has to genuinely answer it, not wave it off. I don't mean refuting its premises; I mean explaining why the induction from past successful-but-false theories doesn't just transfer to our best current theories.

(3) I think the right fix is selective realism: be realist only about the parts of a theory that were indispensable to its novel predictive successes, and treat the rest as scaffolding. The aether wasn't carrying the load in the Fresnel equations; the structural part was.

(4) But I also think structural realism is right about what survives theory change: equations and relations carry over, while the nature of the relata keeps getting revised. So I don't want to say the electron is a little charged sphere; I want to say it's whatever fills that structural role.

(5) I also think there's no forward-looking test that separates working from idle posits in a current theory. We can only tell which parts did the work after the fact.

(6) I still think novel predictive success is our best forward-looking signal that a posit is on the right track, and much stronger than merely accomodating the data we already had.

(7) And I'm suspicious of the historical sample the PMI is built on. Theories get remembered precisely because they were successful and then overturned; ordinary successes that never got overturned don't make the list, so the sample may overstate the failure rate.
