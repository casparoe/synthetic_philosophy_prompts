I am a PhD student in cognitive science, and I am writing a dissertation chapter on conceptual change in science. I want to pressure-test the fluid-concepts framework — the family of ideas growing out of Copycat-style models of analogy and conceptual slippage — against a case that seems, on its face, to be both a textbook instance of the phenomenon and a counterexample to it. In that framework, concepts in a microdomain behave less like fixed definitions than like fields of constraints: near-miss instances exert pressure on an existing concept, and the concept stretches, splits, or shifts in response, often by analogy.

The case is the 2006 redefinition of "planet" by the International Astronomical Union. The salient facts, as I understand them: the discovery of Eris, a trans-Neptunian object comparable in mass to Pluto, forced the astronomical community to confront the fact that "planet" had no operative definition; an initial proposal that would have admitted several additional bodies was abandoned; and the definition eventually adopted required a body to orbit the Sun, to be rounded by its own gravity, and to have cleared its orbital zone, which placed Pluto in a new category, "dwarf planet." Treat this as a case study in conceptual slippage in a microdomain. Map the analysis carefully: what was "planet" doing conceptually before 2006, what made Eris a near-miss case, and what slippages did the community try before settling the matter by vote? Then run the analysis in both directions. Where does the fluid-concepts framework illuminate the episode — the pressure, the proposed stretches, the eventual split — and where does it misdescribe or fall silent, particularly given that the outcome was a legislated set of necessary-and-sufficient criteria rather than an organically shifted prototype? Finally, say what the episode shows about the framework: does it count as evidence for it, a case it handles only with strain, or something it was never built to cover?
