I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've fallen down a Maimonides hole. I've been reading the Guide of the Perplexed — skimming, honestly, its dense — and his account of animal sacrifice has lodged in my brain. The way I understand him, he thinks the whole sacrificial system was a concession: the Israelites were so steeped in a religion built around animal offerings (he blames the Sabians for a lot) that God did not simply abolish the practice. Instead he redirected it — same outward form, different object. God doesn't need the smoke or the blood; the point is a gradual weaning, and prayer is the more direct alternative that doesn't need any of the apparatus.

I'm working on a piece about cultivated meat, and I keep wondering how much of this logic carries over to current debates about eating animals. So here's the thought experiment I want your actual view on. If you accept that picture and run it forward, what would Maimonides make of two things that only exist now: industrial animal agriculture and lab-grown or cultivated meat? Would he see factory farming as exactly the sort of entrenched, habit-driven institution you have to wind down in stages rather than ban outright, or does the sacrificial concession just not transfer to eating animals? And on cultivated meat: is that a Maimonidean move — keep the outward practice (eating meat) while changing what it's made of — or is it a dodge that leaves the underlying attachment to animals-as-food untouched? Stick your neck out and give me a definite answer, with the reasons for it, and tell me the one step in the argument where you feel least sure. Don't give me three balanced readings and then stop.
