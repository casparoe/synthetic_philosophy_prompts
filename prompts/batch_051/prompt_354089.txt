I cover math and philosophy for a magazine, and I'm finishing a piece about mathematical structuralism — the view that the natural numbers are just positions in a single structure, and that set-theoretic codings of them (von Neumann, Zermelo, whatever) are all equally good because nothing makes one coding the "real" one. I've been interviewing a philosopher of math I'll call Dana, and she has very firm views about which questions about numbers are well-posed. I'm going to ask her a new question next week and I want to go in with a real prediction, because she gets visably annoyed when I make her restate her own view.

You're an expert philosopher of mathematics. Think carefully here, and start by brainstorming the different rules that could fit her record before you commit to a prediction.

Her earlier verdicts, from my notes, paraphrased:

1. Is 2 an element of 4? She waved it off. On the von Neumann coding — where 0 is the empty set ∅, 1 is {∅}, 2 is {0,1}, 3 is {0,1,2}, and 4 is {0,1,2,3} — it comes out true, because 2 is literally one of 4's elements. On the Zermelo coding — where 4 is just {3} — it comes out false. Since neither coding is forced on us, she says the bare question has no answer.

2. Is 2 + 2 = 4? Obviously yes; that's a real fact about numbers, not a coding artifact.

3. Is 0 an element of 2? She gave the same kind of dismissal as case 1: true on von Neumann, since 2 is {0,1}, and false on Zermelo, since 2 is {1} and 0 is not 1. So no coding-independent answer.

4. Is the number 2 identical to the set {0,1}? She said there's no absolute fact of the matter. Relative to the von Neumann coding it's true; relative to Zermelo it's false, because Zermelo's 2 is {{∅}}. The set {0,1} is fine as a stand-in, but no coding is privileged, so the identity doesn't hold of the number itself.

5. Is there exactly one natural number that isn't the successor of anything? Yes, zero, and she called that the good kind of question: the answer is the same in every admissible coding.

Here's the thing. I can read her as running at least two, maybe three, different rules. Rule A: set-membership (and set-identity) claims about numbers are never well-posed on their own; they only make sense inside a chosen coding, so the bare question is a category mistake and "no answer" literally means "ill-posed." Rule B: a bare set-theoretic claim about numbers is settled by checking the two standard codings; if von Neumann and Zermelo agree, the answer is just yes or no, and if they disagree, it's indeterminate. Rule C, which might be too cute: set-theoretic claims about numbers are ill-posed, with one carve-out — the claim that a number is an element of its successor, since both standard codings agree there, so "0 is an element of 1" and "1 is an element of 2" come out true.

All three seem to fit her five earlier verdicts, as far as I can tell.

The new question I'm planning to ask her is: is 0 an element of 1? Rule A says she'll call it ill-posed. Rule B says true, because both codings set 1 equal to {0}. Rule C also says true, but for a different reason. So which rule fits her record best, and what should I expect her to say? Also: is she maybe just a von Neumann reductionist in a structuralist costume, and does her denial in case 4 rule that out? And if her answer to the 0 ∈ 1 question somehow leaves it ambiguous between B and C, what's the next membership question I should put to her that would cleanly separate them?
