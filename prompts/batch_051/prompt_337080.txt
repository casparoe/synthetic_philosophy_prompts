I keep meeting the following argument against distributional word embeddings, and I want to know whether it actually works.

(1) Embedding models are trained on the principle that words appearing in similar contexts should receive similar vector representations.
(2) In such a model, representing two words with similar vectors just is representing them as semantically similar.
(3) 'Hot' and 'cold' appear in broadly similar contexts: each fits the normal temperature frames (the water was too __, it is __ outside, a __ bath) without affecting grammaticality.
(4) So the model should represent 'hot' and 'cold' with similar vectors.
(5) But 'hot' and 'cold' are not similar in meaning; they are opposites, denoting incompatible temperature ranges.
(6) Therefore the model treats genuine opposites as if they were semantically alike, and so misrepresents antonymy.

Is the argument valid? If not, tell me the first step at which it fails, or the point at which an illicit move occurs. In particular, be precise about what 'semantically similar' must mean for each premise to be defensible; say whether the argument can be repaired, and what exactly a charitably repaired version would show about what distributional embeddings do and do not capture.
