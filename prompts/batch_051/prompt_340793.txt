I have a standing rule for how I read the historiography of perpetrators of mass violence. When an account attributes the killing primarily to a deeply rooted national culture or collective character—the Goldhagen side of the Browning–Goldhagen dispute over German police battalions, roughly—I treat it as having to overcome a strong presumption, while situational mechanisms (conformity, obedience, career incentives, incremental escalation, division of labor, ideology acquired on the job) serve as my default. My worry is that the rule has begun to run ahead of the evidence: it may be screening out cultural explanations before they can be tested, and it may be as unfalsifiable as the essentialism it distrusts. Is the rule defensible as stated? If not, what restatement keeps its benefits without that asymmetry? And what would count as strong enough evidence for a cultural-character explanation to change the default itself, rather than just explain one case?
