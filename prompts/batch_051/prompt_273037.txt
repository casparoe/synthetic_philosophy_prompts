For a term paper in my high school philosophy class, I am writing about a practice I have noticed in the design of current AI assistants. The assistant I use displays a step-by-step explanation of how it reaches each answer, and the company that builds it describes these displayed steps as the model's own reasoning. The interface presents a longer running commentary as the assistant thinking out loud, and the company encourages users to read it that way. Yet the same company also says, in its public technical explanations, that the underlying model is not fully legible even to its own engineers, and that the displayed reasoning may not describe what actually produced the answer. The company nevertheless uses those displayed explanations to retrain the model and to decide when its behavior needs correcting.

I want to know what philosophical view of the mind, of self-knowledge, or of the relation between behavior and inner processes this practice presupposes. Does treating a generated explanation as a transparent report of the system's own reasoning commit the company to the idea that a language model has some reliable kind of introspection? Or could the practice instead be justified as a purely practical technique for shaping future outputs, without assuming that the displayed steps are causally faithful to what happened inside the system? Is there an inconsistency between the company's public description of the explanations and its engineering use of them, or can both be made sense of under one coherent theory? Please reconstruct the implicit theory the practice seems to presume, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change if the company replaced that theory with a strictly behaviorist view that treats the displayed steps as just more behavior rather than as reports of reasoning.
