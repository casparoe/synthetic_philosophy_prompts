In a jurisprudence seminar on the Hart–Fuller debate, a classmate of mine kept closing down discussion of a duly enacted statute that required citizens to report members of some disfavoured group to the authorities. Again and again he insisted that the matter was already settled by the maxim ‘Unjust laws are not laws.’ When challenged, he seemed to slide between very different thoughts: that the statute was not truly part of the legal system; that it created no obligation of obedience; that a judge should refuse to apply it; that ‘law’ in its proper sense only picks out rules that meet some moral threshold; and that even if it was law in some thin sense, it was not law in the sense that matters. I am not sure he saw that these were distinct claims.

I would like a careful disambiguation of that one sentence. What are the distinct precise theses it could be being used to assert, running from the nearly trivial to the very strong? For each reading, say whether it is plausible, contentious, or simply false, and what it requires a legal positivist to deny. Finally, tell me which reading or readings would genuinely support my classmate’s conclusion about what a judge or a citizen should do when confronted with a validly enacted but deeply unjust rule, and which readings would not.
