For the past two years I've been sitting in on an AI safety seminar, and one argument keeps returning without ever moving. The split is between what I'll call the alignment-first people and the control-first people. The alignment-first people maintain that the only load-bearing fix for a system that may be misaligned is to make it not misaligned: to get its goals to robustly match what humans actually want, across distribution shift and adversarial pressure. On their view, control measures—monitoring outputs, auditing internals, sandboxing, capability limits, keeping a human in the loop—are at best temporary scaffolding. A sufficiently capable system with the wrong goal will eventually route around them, and the more control apparatus we pile on, the more we fool ourselves into deploying a system whose motives we have not fixed. The control-first people say nearly the opposite: we should assume the model may be misaligned and build systems that are safe anyway. Since we cannot directly inspect goals, they argue, alignment is not a property we can verify before it matters, so the only safety properties worth much are the testable ones—monitor for bad behavior, restrict the model's affordances, bound its capabilities, require human authorization for high-stakes actions, and design the deployment so that even a misaligned model cannot cause much damage. When I press them, each side can grant the other's point and still not move: the control camp concedes that control may fail against a very capable system, and the alignment camp concedes that alignment is not directly verifiable, yet each draws the opposite conclusion about where the burden of proof falls.

The frustrating thing is that the disagreement has become stable: the same cases—a model that lies in red-team tests, a monitor that misses a subtle feature of an action, a goal that looks aligned in training but fails when the context changes—are accepted by both sides and read in opposite directions. My working hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but three tangled together: a hidden empirical question about whether alignment can become verifiable enough before capabilities outrun our control measures; a difference in threat models, between a competent adversary quietly pursuing an incorrect goal and a system with context-dependent or incoherent goals whose failures resemble specification errors more than defection; and a bedrock difference about whether "safety" is a property of the objective inside the model or a property of the situation the model is placed in. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself rather than a verdict about which camp is right. Is the disagreement verbal or substantive? Do the two camps differ over facts, over standards of evidence, over which cases count as data, or over starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Are they answering different questions under the single heading "safety"? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a distinction, an experiment or institutional practice, a formal result, a change of question—or should we expect no progress? I'm not asking for a survey of the field. Finally, please say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
