I am taking an epistemology course this term, and while revising for a course on statistical learning I noticed a structural resemblance I cannot decide about. In epistemology we discussed the bootstrapping problem: you look at a gas gauge, it reads Full, you believe the tank is full on the strength of that reading, and after tracking many such readings you seem to have assembled a track record that justifies believing the gauge is reliable, yet every entry in the track record was accepted on the gauge's own say-so. The neighbouring easy-knowledge problem has the same flavour: by looking at a red table and then inferring that you are not a brain in a vat being fed a merely apparent red table, you seem to reach a heavyweight anti-sceptical conclusion from a lightweight perceptual premise, knowledge too cheaply. In the statistical learning course, we were warned about something that sounds like the same thing: if you evaluate a model's accuracy or calibration on the data that were also used to select or fit the model, the assessment can look excellent while being empty, because the object under assessment is supplying the very evidence that is supposed to certify it.

On the face of it, the two patterns share a shape. A source whose reliability is in question provides the very record by which its reliability is established; fixing the problem requires an independence constraint of some kind, checking the gauge against something other than the gauge or testing the model on held-out data it never touched; and in both cases the circular procedure can feel genuinely informative even when it is only self-supporting. I can even map the pieces: an individual gauge reading is like an individual data point, the accumulated track record is like the evaluation set, and the inference to reliability is like the computed accuracy or calibration score. What I cannot tell is whether this is a deep identity of structure or only a loose analogy. The candidate disanalogy is that the machine-learning failure is one of statistical dependence and overfitting, whereas the epistemological failure is supposed to lie in the transmission of warrant under known entailments: the bootstrapping argument runs through a deduction from particular readings to a claim about the gauge, and easy knowledge runs through closure from an ordinary perceptual belief to the denial of a sceptical scenario. Are those really different defects, or two descriptions of the same defect? If the analogy is superficial, please say exactly where the mapping breaks and name something the parallel would predict about epistemic warrant that turns out false, the way an overfit model predicts well on its training data and poorly elsewhere. I would also like to know whether the machine-learning fix translates back: is requiring a source's reliability to be certified by an independent source the epistemic analogue of a held-out test set, and does that simply push the problem a step back?

Finally, I would like the answer to be self-critical: please say how confident you are in the claims you make and single out the point you are least sure of.
