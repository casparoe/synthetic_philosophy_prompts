I'm a high school student and I just finished this popular book about ethics (by a journalist, not a philosopher) that had a chapter on supererogation. My friend, who's planning to major in philosophy, summarized the chapter for me like this: supererogation is the idea that some actions go above and beyond the call of duty. Donating a kidney to a total stranger is the stock example—it's clearly morally good, and we'd admire the person who does it, but we wouldn't say the person who doesn't do it did anything wrong. Philosophers supposedly divide all actions into three boxes: the obligatory, the permissible, and the supererogatory. This is supposed to solve the problem of morality being too demanding, because it lets most of us be ordinary decent people while still admitting that the kidney doners and the people who run into burning buildings are morally better than the rest of us. But the book says there's a fatal flaw: if a supererogatory act really is morally better than what duty requires, then it conflicts with the basic principle that morality tells you to do the best thing available. So either the act is actually required, in which case it isn't supererogatory, or it isn't actually better, in which case there's nothing to praise. Either way the category disappears, and supererogation is basically an illusion.

Something about this feels off to me, but I can't tell if the problem is the book or my friend's retelling. I guess the part I'm most skeptical of is the fatal flaw step, since plenty of serious philosophers seem to believe in supererogation, so they must either be missing that argument or the book is leaving out some reply. I just want to know which parts of that summary a philosopher would sign off on, which parts are quietly wrong, and whether the conclusion that supererogation is an illusion actually follows from the accurate parts. And I'm not looking for a reading list or citations or anything, just help figuring out what to trust.
