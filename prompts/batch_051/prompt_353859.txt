In my work as a journalist covering artificial intelligence, I have been reading a great deal about how researchers try to make the internals of neural networks legible, and one practice has begun to seem to me as much philosophy as engineering. Researchers train simple classifiers, often called linear probes, on a model's internal activations; they claim to have recovered features for concepts such as a landmark, a programming construct, or a sentiment; they identify those features with directions in activation space; and they intervene by amplifying or suppressing those directions to see whether the model's behavior changes in the expected way. The practice appears to assume that there is a fact of the matter about which features a network has, that this fact determines whether an interpretability method has succeeded or failed, and that the features are part of the causal story about why the model behaves as it does — not merely a compressed description that an outside observer finds convenient.

Could all of the same experimental results be accommodated by an instrumentalist view, on which probes and features are just predictive fictions that earn their keep without representing anything? What would distinguish a feature the network genuinely has from a direction a probe can learn merely because the training data happened to contain a pattern? And does the day-to-day practice of interpretability strictly presuppose realism about representation, or is it underdetermined between several views — for instance, a view on which a representation must be consumed by some downstream process to count as such, and a view on which representations can be intrinsic properties of the network even if nothing read them? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of mind behind this practice, distinguish what the practice presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what in the methods themselves would have to change if the researchers dropped the realist commitment.
