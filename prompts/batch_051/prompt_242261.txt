I have been trying to work out what permits an observer to move from an agent's observable behaviour to conclusions about that agent's preferences, values or desires. In inverse reinforcement learning one tries to recover a reward function that rationalises observed trajectories through some state space; in revealed-preference economics one lets choice from menus disclose an underlying preference relation. Both enterprises seem to promise that behaviour, given enough of it and a correct model of the situation, can reveal what an agent wants. Yet my verdicts on particular cases keep pulling apart, and I cannot yet state the principle that sorts them.

Here are the cases. A colleague takes tea rather than coffee every time we share a staff-room break, and I am wholly willing to say she prefers tea. A visitor who has been told, falsely, that the main course contains an allergen he is trying to avoid selects the vegetarian option; I do not conclude that he prefers the vegetarian dish to the main course, only that he preferred what he took himself to be choosing. Had he known the menu was safe and selected the same dish, I would have drawn the stronger conclusion, though the menu and the bodily movements are identical. A woman who surrenders her handbag at knifepoint has not thereby revealed a preference for surrendering it. A patient with advanced dementia who can no longer state or compare alternatives, but who reliably brightens when a particular recording is played, seems to me to reveal something about what he values. A toddler who refuses a dish she has never tasted seems to me to reveal neophobia rather than a judgement about that dish's tastiness; I would not write "dislikes this food" into her utility function on that evidence alone. Two investors take identical concentrated positions in the same stock. One has studied the prospectus and seeks the high expected return; the other has misread the risk disclosures. I attribute a taste for risk only to the first, although the selections on paper are the same.

The pair that most bothers me is the allergy case and the investor case: in each, my willingness to attribute an underlying preference varies with the agent's beliefs while the observable choice is held fixed. That seems to commit me to the view that behaviour reveals preference only relative to a model of the agent's own representation of her options, not relative to the objective features of her situation. But I cannot state that principle with enough discipline to use it: which facts about the agent must be modelled before the inference is warranted (beliefs about the environment, beliefs about her own preferences, attention, self-control, computational limits), and which of my verdicts must be dropped if no simple principle can save all of them. What I would like is the best available principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures these verdicts, stated precisely enough that one could implement it in the reward-inference step of an inverse reinforcement learning system. Please also apply the principle to a case I have not listed: an autonomous system observes a pedestrian take a long detour on a hot day and must decide whether the pedestrian values the scenic route or is ignorant of a shorter one. And please say plainly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should revise or abandon any of them.
