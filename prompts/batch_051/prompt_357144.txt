I sit on a department curriculum committee, and a proposal to reduce the amount of hand computation in our calculus and linear algebra sequence has split the room. The defenders of the requirement appeal to a familiar argument: procedural fluency is not valuable for its own sake, but it is a precondition for conceptual understanding, because automating low-level symbolic work frees working memory for structure, and because the accumulated experience of computing many examples is what gives mathematical concepts their intuitive content. On this view, a student who has only watched machinery run has not yet understood anything.

That argument was formed under the assumption that the only place a procedure could live was inside the student's own head and hands. Most of our students now have reliable access to computer algebra systems and step-by-step solvers that will differentiate, factor, row-reduce, and explain the steps in natural language. If a procedure can be offloaded to a tool, the argument seems to acquire a new premise it never had to state before — that the automation must be internal to the student — and it is no longer obvious whether that premise is still true, or whether it can be replaced by a requirement that the student be able to predict, check, restrict, and interpret the machine's output.

I am trying to figure out whether the old argument is obsolete, repairable, or largely untouched by this development. Which of its premises does reliable external procedure execution actually bear on, and can a revised version preserve the original link between procedural fluency and conceptual understanding without requiring the student to be the one performing the procedure? Please address the argument itself, not the curriculum decision.
