Over the past year two people in my reading group have been arguing past each other about wether psychopaths actually know that what they do is wrong. One of them (the sentimentalist) says the psychopathy research shows moral judgement is fundamentally emotional: psychopaths can sort acts into wrong and not wrong, and will say "murder is wrong," but their blunted affect means they don't really grasp the moral content—they treat moral rules the way they treat arbitrary conventions, so calling what they have moral knowledge is a mistake. The other (the rationalist) says this conflates knowing with caring: psychopaths know the rule, can justify it, can apply it to hard cases that weren't on the original list, and what they lack is only the motivational push to act on it—you can know smoking causes cancer without wanting to quit, and its not obvious why moral knowledge should be different.

Draft the joint statement of their adversarial collaboration: the document they should have produced after a year of arguing past each other. It should include: (1) a numbered list of claims both can sign about what psychopaths can and can't do and about what moral knowledge would have to involve, phrased so neither side winces; (2) a disagreements table where each row has a neutral statement of the point at issue plus the wording each side would accept for its own view; and (3) a crux list—the subquestions a decent answer to which would actually move at least one of them—with, for each crux, a concrete test, case, or argument both agree bears on it and each side's prediction written down in advance. Be harsh about sorting the genuine disagreements from the merely verbal ones. I suspect one crux is whether the emotional responses are constitutive of moral understanding or just a normal accompaniment, and another is what it takes to count as "applying the concept to a new case," but don't take my guesses as the whole list.
