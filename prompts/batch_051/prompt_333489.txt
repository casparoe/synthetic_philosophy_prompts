So here's a thing that's been bugging me. In a lot of big multiplayer games, if some other player tricks you into trading away a rare sword, and you report it, the company will often actually undo the trade: they take the sword back from the scammer, put it in your inventory, and ban or suspend the scammer. They describe this as returning stolen property and making you whole. But the same company's terms of service say you don't own anything in the game — the sword is just a revocable liscense to see a certain arrangement of pixels, and the company can delete your account and everything in it whenever it wants without owing you anything. And if a bug accidentally creates a duplicate of the sword, they'd delete the duplicate without any of that stolen-property treatment.

So what theory of virtual objects does this practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that virtual objects are real in some sense — that a virtual sword is more like a bicycle than like a hallucination? Does it presuppose that virtual harms are real harms, so that being scammed in a game is genuinely bad for you? Or can the whole pattern be explained on purely license-based or fictionalist grounds, as if the company were just managing a shared fiction? Reconstruct the implicit theory behind the practice, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it's underdetermined — where more than one philosophical view could license the same behavior. And tell me what would have to change, either in how the game is run or in the terms of service, if you swapped in full-on realism about virtual items, or full-on fictionalism. Which of those would the current practice collapse into, if pushed?
