I'm a journalist who covers science-and-ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about the value of information. The spine of the piece is an old decision-theory result usually called Good's theorem: for a single expected-utility maximizer who updates by conditioning, cost-free information can never be expected to lower their best achievable payoff, since they can always ignore the information when it points the wrong way. The proof has a clean law-of-iterated-expectations flavor. But obviously real people decline information all the time. Most of those cases are boring for my purposes: the test causes anxiety, the result wouldn't actually change any decision, understanding it is costly, or the information comes with a fee attached. I want the non-boring version.

So here's what I want you to construct for me. A thought experiment in which a rational, emotionally ordinary person deliberately refuses information that is genuinely cost-free and genuinely relevent to a decision right in front of them — and their refusal is correct expected-utility thinking, not a mistake. Constraints: realistic (a used-car sale, a contract renegotiation, a clinic intake, whatever — not physics, no perfect predictors), one paragraph for the scenario itself, and the reason for refusing absolutely cannot be anxiety, computational overload, a taste for ignorance itself, or a hidden fee for opening the drawer. The refusal should fall out of the structure of the situation; I suspect the structural feature will be another optimizing agent whose response depends on what you know or on whether you looked, but I'll let you tell me. After the scenario, do the walk-through. State what Good's theorem seems to say here, pinpoint which assumption or premise of the theorem silently fails, and tell me which feature of the case is doing the real work. Then tell me what happens if you remove that feature — does the case collapse back into "always look"? And be honest about how a defender of the theorem would attack the case, probably by saying the information wasn't actually cost-free because looking changed the feasible actions or what the person could truthfully signal. Finish with a one-line takeaway I can steal for readers: when, if ever, can information hurt a rational agent without any psychological story.
