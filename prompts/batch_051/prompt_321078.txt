I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about what "random" means — it started with an argument over whether the digits of pi are random and kind of spiraled from there. I keep tripping over a puzzle I don't have a name for.

Take a binary string. One person says 0101010101... isn't random, because there's an obvious rule generating it. Another person points out that 0110100101... is also generated by a rule — the rule that says to print these digits in this exact order — so by that standard nothing is random, since any finite string can be described by a rule tailored to it after the fact. And yet people still talk about some strings being more random than others. The intuition seems to be about how short the rule has to be: if a string can be generated by a rule much shorter than the string itself, it isn't random; if the shortest rule is about as long as the string, it is. But for any finite string there's always some rule, and shortness is a matter of degree, so I'm not sure what property is actualy being picked out.

Is there an established name for this idea — that randomness is about the length of the shortest description? And how does it relate to the statistical idea of randomness, where each digit just has to be equally likely and independant? I'm not after a one-word label; I want to know whether this applies to finite sequences at all, or only to infinite ones, and where the idea breaks down. Lead with the bottom line and keep the whole thing under 400 words.
