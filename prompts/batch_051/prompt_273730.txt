Something I can't get to add up, and I keep turning it over: suppose a restaurant serves you a bowl of soup that, unbeknownst to you, had a brand-new, medically sterilized cockroach dipped in it for a second. No actual contamination, zero germs, you were never harmed. Now suppose the same restaurant has sloppy handwashing and you get food poisoning. When people hear these two stories, a lot of them treat the first as the bigger sin — like the restaurant violated something, not just made a health error. Similarly, burning a flag you own is morally repugnant to loads of people, but throwing an equally nice flag in the trash is just a shame, even if both are private acts that destroy the same object. In the food case, the version with actual physical harm gets the smaller moral reaction; in the flag case, the two acts have identical physical consequences and the only difference is symbolic — and that difference does all the moral work.

As a programmer I want to fit this into a utility function or a ruleset, and it doesnt fit cleanly. Is there a defensible principle that makes purity/sacredness/disgust a real part of ethics — such that 'harmless but impure' actions can be more wrong than 'harmful but ordinary' actions? Or are those judgments just bugs in human moral psychology that we happen to have dressed up in moral language? Give me the strongest case for purity being a genuine moral value, and then an honest verdict on whether we should keep these particular judgments or override them.
