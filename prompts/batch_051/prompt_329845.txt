Here's one that's been bothering me. People throw around "socially constructed" until it covers everything or nothing, and I want to pin down what the useful sense is — or decide that there are two or three useful senses and keep them apart. So here's the assignment.

Give me a working definition of "socially constructed" — or of the core sense at issue when people say that money, race, or gender is socially constructed. Don't survey the literature; I've read it. I want your sharpest cut at the concept.

Test it on these cases. Some are easy, some are maddening:

- a $20 bill
- money, as a kind of thing
- being unemployed
- the fact that a bishop in chess moves only diagonally
- a Higgs boson
- the sensation of a toothache
- race as it functions in mortgage lending, e.g., in the old redlining maps
- gender as recorded on a passport
- Pluto's being classified as a dwarf planet
- ADHD as a psychiatric diagnosis

I'm confident the bill, money, unemployment, and the chess bishop are constructed. I'm also confident the Higgs boson and the toothache — the thing itself, not our talk about it — are not. Race and gender feel constructed but in a different way than the chess move is, and I can't tell wether that's a difference of kind or just degree. Pluto and ADHD I genuinely don't have a stable view on.

One specific demand: I want the definition to say something informative about the sense in which a formal rule (like the bishop's move) is constructed versus the sense in which a category like race is constructed as it loops through institutions and self-conceptions. If you end up with two definitions, that's fine, but say which cases move between them.

Format: four sections, in this order and roughly this length:

1. "The definition" — about 120 words. Give the definition or definitions in a sentence or two, and flag any disambiguation you're making.
2. "Running the cases" — about 400 words. A table is fine, but give a one-line reason for each verdict.
3. "Where the definition strains" — about 150 words. Which cases did it classify wrongly, or only by force?
4. "Revise or defend" — about 200 words. Give at least one revision or a defense of a surprising verdict, and end by saying which verdict you are least secure about.
