Evaluate the following argument about the unity of the proposition that a is F.

(1) A proposition is a complex whole: it contains constituents—at least a and F—but it is not a mere list of them. Whatever accounts for the difference between the whole and the list must itself be present in the proposition.
(2) The only way for constituents to be combined in a whole is by a relation that relates them. So the proposition contains a relation R, predication, relating a and F.
(3) Once R is included, the proposition contains a, F, and R. By the same requirement, for these three to be one whole rather than three loose items, a further relation R′ must relate R to a and F.
(4) The same holds for R′, and so on without end: each relation introduced to unify must itself be related to the items it was introduced to unify.
(5) A whole whose unity requires an endless chain of further relations is never actually unified at all.
(6) Therefore the proposition that a is F cannot exist as a complex whole unified by relations. If a proposition exists, its unity must be non-relational.

Is this argument valid? If not, identify the exact step at which it goes wrong and explain how the failure blocks the regress. If it is valid, say which premise a defender of propositions should reject, and whether the regress in (4)–(5) is genuinely vicious or could be accepted as benign.
