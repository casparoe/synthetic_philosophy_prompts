One of my colleagues in the department works on moral psychology, and I've been keeping a running tally of how she rules on preference-versus-bias disputes — where the question is whether someone's moral reaction is a genuine difference of values (a preference) or a mistake of reasoning (a bias). Her calls so far: two people who disagree about whether to give to a malaria-net charity or the local food bank — preference. Someone whose judgment about a risky surgery flips when the same outcome is stated as 90% survive versus 10% die — bias. Someone who gives much more when an appeal names a specific child than when it speaks of thousands of children — bias. Someone who, across dozens of cases, consistently weighs their own child's interests far above several strangers' even when nobody is watching — preference. Someone whose fairness verdicts change depending on whether they state their principle before or after hearing the case — bias. Someone who opposes a new bus lane mostly because the street has been that way for years and hasn't actually worked through the consequences — bias.

The case for next week: a woman opposes an upzoning plan because it would lower the value of her house. She agrees the new units would relieve the housing shortage, she's reflected on it, and she still opposes it. Predict my colleague's verdict — preference or bias? I care less about the label than the rule she's implicitly running, the other two or three rules that fit her record just as well, which of her earlier calls is the closest analog, and which additional case would most cleanly seperate the candidate rules.
