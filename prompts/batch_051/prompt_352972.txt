I've got a pair of terms I keep using in my notes for the historical novel I'm writing, and I want an honest read before I build a whole plot on them. I call them *thin contingency* and *thick contingency*. Thin contingency: the tiny accident changes the how but not the whether. In my draft, a duke's horse puts a foot in a rabbit hole and the duke dies; if the hole hadn't been there, he'd have died of gout six months later, and the succession crisis was coming either way — only the order of betrayals changed. Thick contingency: the tiny accident changes the whether. Same rabbit hole, except the duke was the one man holding off a war, so the war never happens, the empire doesn't fall, and all the downstream events fork. My claim, which I'd like checked: the famous for-want-of-a-nail stories are almost always thin contingency dressed up as thick, because narrative needs a hinge. The nail is usually replaceable; if it doesn't fail, the same outcome comes in through a differnet door. But here's the thing that bugs me — maybe any thick case becomes thin in hindsight, since we redescribe the outcome until the accident looks trivial. So: is my distinction actually doing work, or did I just reinvent something obvious with new jargon? Is the claim about nail-stories testable, or does it only sound unfalsifiably wise? And does the hindsight problem (every thick event looks thin once narrated) kill the distinction rather than refine it?
