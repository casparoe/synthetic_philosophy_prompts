Here's the puzzle that's been eating at me, and I'd like a fresh pair of eyes. Sadness seems like the sort of thing that needs a mind — something to actually feel it — yet we say a tune is sad all the time, and we don't mean the composer was sad or that every listener ends up tearful. So how does a bunch of sounds get to be sad?

I've been collecting accounts, and so far I've got these:

(1) The arousal view: the music is sad because the normal response to hearing it is to feel sad, or something close to sad. The sadness is really in the listener, and the word gets transferred to the music as shorthand.

(2) The resemblance-to-behaviour view: sad music has the same audible shape as sad human behaviour — slow tempo, low pitch, drooping melodic contours, soft dynamics, the sound of sighing and laboured movement — and we hear that resemblance without needing to feel anything ourselves.

(3) The emotion-characteristics view: the music doesn't have to resemble human behaviour at all. A drooping willow looks sad without resembling a sad person, and a low, dragging, minor-key passage can simply sound sad in the same way. Sadness is an appearance property of the sound itself.

(4) The persona view: we hear the music as the expression of an indeterminate someone — a sort of virtual character implied by the music — and the sadness is attributed to that imagined person, whose emotional expression we're overhearing.

(5) The make-believe view: the music is a prop in a game of make-believe in which we imagine our own experience of the music to be an experience of sadness. The music is sad because it makes fictional that we, the listeners, are feeling it ourselves.

(6) The convention view: the link between minor keys, slow tempi and sadness is associational sediment. We've heard those features paired with sad lyrics and sad occasions so often that the music now simply means sadness by cultural convention, the way black can mean mourning.

I've deliberately set aside the old Romantic idea that the music is the composer's actual sadness poured into the notes and then tapped out of the listener — that just relocates the puzzle into the composer's head, and it says nothing about sad-sounding music nobody composed. So I'm not after that either.

What I want is another account, genuinly different in kind from (1)–(6) and from the composer-expression idea I just set aside. Not a rewording, not a special case, not a combination of items already on the list. If there is one, tell me what it is, say which of (1)–(6) it's closest to and where it parts company with it, and whether you think its actually any good as an answer to the puzzle.
