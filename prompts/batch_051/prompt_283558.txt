I have become convinced that Jeffrey conditionalization is the best available account of how to update on uncertain evidence. Unlike strict Bayesian conditionalization, which is silent unless I learn some proposition with certainty, it lets an ambiguous experience—a blurry glimpse, a charged tone of voice, a borderline test result—directly redistribute my credences over a partition (a set of mutually exclusive and exhaustive alternatives) and then propagate that redistribution across my other beliefs, while holding fixed my credences conditional on each cell of the partition. But I have a contrary conviction that an adequate theory of uncertain evidence ought to distinguish a redistribution caused by the evidence from a redistribution caused by me—by hope, fatigue, or bias—and Jeffrey's rule appears unable to draw that distinction. If my confidence that a colleague lied rises from 0.2 to 0.7 after a conversation whose decisive features I cannot articulate, the same numerical shift produced by wishful thinking would be propagated in exactly the same way, and the rule offers no guidance about which input probabilities I should adopt in the first place. Is the right conclusion that the theory is incomplete rather than false, that the intuition rests on a misunderstanding of what the rule is meant to do, or that a defensible theory of uncertain evidence must add something Jeffrey leaves out? Present the strongest case for each side and then give a negotiated verdict about what should be kept, amended, or abandoned. Please also indicate how confident you are in that verdict and single out the point you are least sure of.
