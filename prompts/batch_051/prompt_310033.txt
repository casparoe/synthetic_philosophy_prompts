I am a doctoral student in philosophy working on the formal theory of collective judgment, and I have been asked to run a one-hour session on the discursive dilemma for advanced undergraduates from philosophy and political science, none of whom have encountered social choice theory. The phenomenon I need them to grasp by the end is this: when a group votes separately on each premise of a decision and then on the conclusion those premises entail, majority votes can combine into a collective position that no individual member holds—for example, a majority supports the premise that a contract was valid, a majority supports the premise that it was breached, and yet a majority rejects the conclusion that the defendant is liable, even though every member's own judgments are perfectly consistent. I do not want the explanation itself; I want a plan for the explanation. Specifically, what must be in place before the dilemma can even appear to them—the consistency of individual judgments, the premise/conclusion relation, the idea of aggregating judgments rather than preferences—and in what order should these be introduced, with what depending on what? Which single worked case should carry the whole session, and what features must it have (a determinate entailment, a small panel, an uncontroversial voting rule) and what features should it lack? Which misconceptions should I expect—that the paradox exposes irrationality in the individual members, that more deliberation would dissolve it, that conclusion-based voting is obviously the correct repair, that majority rule has simply been refuted—and at what point in the sequence should each be headed off rather than answered afterwards? Should the standard contrast between premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation—voting on the premises and letting the entailment fix the conclusion, versus voting only on the conclusion—be introduced only after the dilemma has been allowed to bite, or do students need that contrast in advance to see why the result is surprising? What end-of-session question, posed without any worked example attached, would tell me whether they had grasped the structural problem rather than memorized the case? And what should I explicitly leave out at this level—say, impossibility theorems, strategic voting, formal logic beyond simple conditionals—and how should I announce those omissions so the session does not feel evasive? Give me the teaching sequence, not the lecture content.
