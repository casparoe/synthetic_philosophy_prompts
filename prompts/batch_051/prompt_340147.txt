I keep hearing a picture of the self in philosophy of mind — call it the narrative-gravity picture — where the self is like a center of gravity: real, not as a substance or homunculus, but as an abstract point that organizes a distributed story, and that's supposed to defuse the worry that there's no owner behind all the mental chatter. People lean on it to argue that talk of selves survives neuroscience and Buddhism-style doubts, just as center of gravity survives the fact that there's no little chunk of iron at that exact point. I want to know how far it actually carries. Give me the correspondence table: in the source, what maps onto the mass distribution, the gravitational field, the point itself, the fact that moving the parts moves the point; and on the self side, what are the "masses," the "field," the calculation nobody does consciously. Then tell me where it breaks, and more importantly which of those breaks matter for the intended use: does it smuggle in uniqueness (a body has one center of gravity, a person seems to have one authoritative narrative), stability, explanatory oomph, something else? Does the "don't panic, selves are real enough" conclusion survive the disanalogies, or was the analogy doing the heavy lifting?
