I've been trying to get clear about what formalism, intuitionism, and logicism actually disagree about, and I keep noticing an asymmetry in ordinary mathematical practice that seems to cut across the official positions of the schools. When a classical analyst proves an existence claim for a real number non-constructively—say, by assuming that no real number with some property exists and deriving a contradiction—the theorem is published, taught, and cited without any demand that the proof supply a witness. But when a computer scientist or mathematician claims that an algorithm solving some problem exists, and supports the claim only with a classical proof that a solution could not fail to exist, the result is ordinarily not accepted as an existence proof: the community demands an actual specification of the procedure, or at least a method for producing one. Both are existence statements expressed in the same classical language. Neither the formalist's official criterion—derivability in an accepted formal system—nor the logicist's criterion—reducibility to logical truths—appears to distinguish the two cases; only the intuitionist's constructive criterion seems to track the distinction. Yet the mathematicians who enforce this asymmetry are not intuitionists, and they would reject a global ban on non-constructive proof.

Candidate justifications present themselves: an algorithm is an artifact that must eventually be run, whereas a real number is an abstract object; an algorithm is something we have to write down and use, while a nondescript real is fine as a bare existence claim; a nonexhibited procedure gives us nothing to execute. But each of these seems to generate awkward pressure on the first case, or to imply that finite but astronomically large witnesses, or algorithms too long to write down, should shift the standard in ways most mathematicians would not accept.

Is there a principled, non-intuitionist line that justifies treating algorithmic existence claims as requiring constructive evidence while classical existence claims in analysis do not? Should the asymmetry be kept, reversed, or given up—and, in particular, is it a stable local scruple, or an inconsistency that should be resolved by moving either toward fuller constructivism or toward fully classical standards across the board?
