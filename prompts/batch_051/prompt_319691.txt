I am a novelist and I lead a reading group that pairs fiction with philosophy; this month we are setting Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day beside the Stoic dichotomy of control, in both its ancient form and its contemporary revival. The butler Stevens can look at first like a Stoic case study: he is exacting about his own conduct, indifferent to ordinary comforts, and disciplined about his feelings. I suspect the novel is less an illustration of the doctrine than a study of how the doctrine can be mistaken for emotional suppression, and of what it costs to misclassify what is one's own as something beyond one's power.

Write me a critical reading that moves in both directions. In one direction, use the dichotomy of control to diagnose Stevens: where does he correctly locate what is his own—his judgments, his professional standards, his chosen reticence—and where does he treat what is actually up to him, such as his assent, his grief, and his failure to speak, as though it simply happened to him or could not be helped? In the other direction, let the novel test the modern Stoic revival. Contemporary popularizers often flatten the dichotomy into a maxim about controlling the controllable, and some add a third category of partial control; is Stevens better read through the two-part or the three-part version, and does his case expose a weakness in either? Consider the scenes in which his self-command is most visibly on display, such as the night of the conference when his father dies, the episode of the dismissed maids, and the final meeting with Miss Kenton. In each case ask whether the narrative voice is dramatizing Stoic discipline or revealing self-deception that calls the Stoic ideal itself into question; Stevens names his restraint dignity, but the novel may invite us to see something closer to cowardice than to apatheia. Conclude by saying what a novel can show about the dichotomy of control that a handbook cannot, and where an honest critic should refuse to call the novel Stoic.
