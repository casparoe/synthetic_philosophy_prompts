Since retiring from a career in the natural sciences, I have been reading philosophy, and I have become puzzled by an asymmetry in the way ordinary conversation handles the same proposition depending on how it enters the conversation. Compare two remarks. One person says, "The new director is embezzling funds." Another says, "The new director regrets embezzling funds." If no embezzlement has occurred, we treat the first speaker as having said something false—perhaps lied—about embezzlement. But the second sentence presupposes that embezzlement while asserting only that the director regrets something; when the presupposition fails, we are much less willing to say the second speaker lied about the embezzlement, even though the speaker's move would, if unchallenged, install the same damaging claim in the shared record. Nor do we treat the second remark as perfectly fine: we call it infelicitous or loaded, or we correct the background assumption before answering. So presupposed content is both less imputable to the speaker and still policed, but under a different heading. Is this two-track treatment justified? I want to know whether there is a principled difference between asserting a proposition and introducing it as a presupposition that warrants our different practices of correction and blame, or whether we should hold speakers just as accountable for the presuppositions their sentences trigger as for the assertions they make. Please say how confident you are in your answer and single out the point you are least sure of.
