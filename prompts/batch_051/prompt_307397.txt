I direct a county vector-control district in a region where mosquito-borne West Nile virus is seasonal. Each March I must recommend to the Board of Supervisors whether to continue the previous year's level of larvicide treatment, expand it, or suspend it for the coming season. My two analysts (both M.S.-level, no formal philosophy) base the recommendation on the previous season's data: trap counts, infection rates in trapped mosquitoes, and human case reports. The inferential step that worries me is this: the data never choose between the regularity my staff projects — roughly, that the program keeps abundance low, so next season will resemble the one just observed — and a Goodman-style grue alternative, in which counts are low during the observation window that ends now and the very same observations equally support high counts in the coming season. I need a disciplined basis for trusting some projections and rejecting others, not just a statistical significance test.

Please write a reusable decision procedure, grounded in Goodman's new riddle of induction, for determining when an observed regularity in one season's data actually supports a projection to the next season. The procedure should be a sequence of questions with pass/no-pass outcomes that my analysts can run in about three working days. It should separate the projectability question — is the hypothesis lawlike rather than gruesome — from the strength-of-evidence question, and it should state clearly where it stops with "do not project" and where it must hand the decision to a person because no formal checklist can settle whether a predicate is projectible. Then apply the procedure to three worked cases, including one in which it rejects or blocks a projection the Board is inclined to make, and one in which the procedure cannot decide and a human must. Do not include citations or a reading list. Keep the entire response under 1,200 words; the procedure itself should fit on one page, with the worked cases following it.
