Okay, I need someone to sanity-check a position I've apparently been holding. I'm a historian—early modern, mostly—and I just got into it with a colleague over whether our department's methods sequence is teaching students to be too presentist. I ended up writing down what I actually believe, not the diplomatic version, and now I want to know whether it all hangs together. Here's the list:

- The questions historians ask will always be shaped by the present, and that's not by itself a defect; it only becomes one when present-day standards get smuggled into the evidence.
- A good explanation of past agents should reconstruct what they did in their own categories; explaining them in ours is the real sin of anachronism.
- But nobody ever fully reaches the past's own categories either; that's a regulative ideal, at most, because the historian is always speaking from somewhere.
- Moral condemnation of past actors is warranted when the standards it needs were already available in their own time—say, when some of their contemporaries were making the same criticism.
- Still, handing down moral verdicts isn't the historian's job; explanation and grading should be kept seperate.
- And yet a study of, say, a slaveholding society written in a rigorously neutral tone is doing something wrong; neutrality between enslaver and enslaved is itself a moral position.
- I also think the distinction between descriptive and normative presentism is doing real work here: present concerns can set the research agenda without supplying the yardstick.

So go through these and tell me which pairs are in tension, which tensions are only apparent, and rank them by how much they'd cost me to fix. If I can only revise one bullet to make the whole set coherent, which one should it be?
