I have recently been rethinking the way I was taught to talk about God. I grew up in a tradition that stressed divine transcendence—God as wholly other, beyond our categories, not contained by the world—but in the last few years I have found myself drawn to writers who speak of the divine as immanent, as the depth within experience or the life of the cosmos itself. At first I treated these as rival metaphysical pictures: either God is outside the world, or God is in the world. But the more I read, the less confident I am that this is a well-formed alternative. It seems to me that the very idea of transcendence already involves a relation to the world—something can be beyond the world only if the world is the sort of thing that can be transcended—and that the very idea of immanence already marks the divine as something other than the ordinary contents of the world. I am trying to work out an argument for the view that transcendence and immanence are not competing accounts of where God is located, but rather two different ways of describing how the divine is present to us: transcendence describes the limit or excess by which the divine escapes our grasp, while immanence describes the availability of that excess within experience.

I would like you to construct the strongest possible argument for this position, treating it as a philosophical thesis about the logic of divine presence rather than as a compromise between religious traditions. In particular, please address the objection that a truly transcendent God must be wholly separate from the world and therefore cannot be available in experience at all.
