I am a high school student preparing a paper on the distinction between doing and allowing harm, and I have collected a set of views that I would like to hold together. I want to know whether they form a consistent package, and if not, where the strain is. Please treat this as an audit of my commitments rather than a general introduction to the topic.

Here are the commitments:

1. The difference between doing harm and allowing harm is real and morally significant, not just a cognitive bias that makes certain harms feel worse to us.
2. Killing is morally worse than letting die when the circumstances are otherwise the same.
3. It is wrong to kill one healthy person and transplant his organs in order to save five dying patients.
4. If I see a child drowning in a shallow pond and can save the child at only a small cost to myself, I am morally required to save the child.
5. In the standard trolley case, it is permissible to turn the trolley away from five people and toward one person.
6. A doctor may give a dose of pain relief that will foreseeably shorten a patient's life, as long as the doctor's aim is to relieve pain and not to cause death.
7. We should generally try to reduce the total number of deaths, whether that requires acting or refraining, at least when the costs to ourselves are modest.

Which of these are in genuine tension with which others? Which apparent tensions can be dissolved by careful distinctions? Order the tensions by how serious they are, and say what would have to be given up or revised in each case and what that revision would cost. Finally, tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set coherent.
