I'm a journalist working on a piece about what political theorists call the boundary problem: if democracy means rule by the people, who counts as "the people"? It sounds pedantic until you try to draw a line around a decision—whether residents of a neighboring country get a say in your immigration policy, wether future people get a say in climate rules, or who exactly gets to vote on a local school board. I've been asking around, and the standard candidates I've collected so far are:

(1) The citizenship answer: the demos is whoever the law says is a citizen. Circular in an unhelpful way, but it's the default in practice.
(2) The all-subjected answer: everyone who is subject to the state's laws should have a say in making them.
(3) The coercion answer: everyone the state actually coerces gets a say, including people well outside the territory that it threatens with enforcement.
(4) The residency answer: everyone who lives in the place gets a say over its decisions, full stop.
(5) The all-affected-interests answer: everyone whose interests a decision affects should get a say, in proportion to how much it affects them.

Is there another candidate for who should belong to the demos that is genuinely different from any of these five? I don't mean a rewording of one of them, a special case of one of them, or some hybrid of two of them. If you've got one, give me the principle, explain how it differs from the five above, and tell me whether you think it actually dissolves the circularity or just relocates it. And since this is for a British desk, can you write the answer in British English?
