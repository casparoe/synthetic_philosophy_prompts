In the history course I am taking this year we have been studying the origins of the Cold War, and in a civics unit on tort law we covered how courts decide causation when more than one party contributes to an injury. I kept feeling that the two debates share a hidden structure, and I want to know whether the resemblance is deep or merely verbal.

In both cases the naive question — who started it, or who caused it — tries to locate the outcome in a single triggering act by a single party. But the underlying process is an escalation in which each side's conduct can be read as a response to the other side's previous conduct, so the search for a prime mover keeps sliding backward without settling. The law has a refined set of tools for this situation: the but-for test, proximate cause, intervening and superseding causes, and doctrines for apportioning fault between two contributing parties. The historiography of the Cold War seems to me to run on parallel tracks. Some accounts treat Soviet actions in Eastern Europe as the decisive spark that set everything else in motion — a but-for trigger. Others treat American actions, such as the atomic bombings, the Truman Doctrine, and the Marshall Plan, as the dangerous conditions or provocations that made a hostile Soviet response foreseeable — which sounds like proximate-cause reasoning. Still others explain the Cold War as following from the structure of the international system rather than from any particular decision, which resembles attributing an accident to the road rather than to either driver.

I want a careful verdict on whether this is a genuine structural identity, a loose analogy, or a confusion. If the parallel is real, do the legal distinctions map onto distinctions historians actually draw or ought to draw, and what would have to be true of both fields for the legal tools to transfer? If the parallel is shallow, where exactly does it come apart? I can see one obvious difference, and I want to know whether it is decisive: a court must end in a judgment against a specific party for a specific act, while a historian can answer who started it by rejecting the question as badly framed. Tell me, also, at least one prediction the analogy would lead someone to make that you think is false. Finally, say which argument or piece of evidence would be most likely to change your verdict about the parallel, and where your own reasoning is least secure.
