I'm trying to pin down what Williams is actually doing in the drowning-wife case, and I want you to rank some candidate readings for me. Quick setup: Williams asks us to imagine a man faced with two drowning people, one of whom is his wife, and he can save only one. Williams's line is that if the man first checks whether it is morally permissible to save her, or saves her only after reminding himself that spouses are allowed to prioritize each other, then he has taken a detour through an impartial principle that he should not have needed to take. Below are five candidate diagnoses of exactly what's wrong with that detour. Treat them as free-standing candidates; I don't care who may have defended each one, and don't go looking for attributions.

A. The fungibility reading. The detour casts the wife as a token of the type spouse, whose rescue is covered by a general permission. That makes her interchangeable with any spouse-shaped person, whereas the point of the attachment is precisely that she is not.

B. The deliberative-corruption reading. The problem isn't the content of the detour but the stepping back itself. First-personal practical reasoning in intimate cases is supposed to be spontaneous, and adopting the impartial point of view is not a neutral reflective move; it distorts the question being asked.

C. The over-moralization reading. The man assumes the rescue needs morality's permission to be legitimate. Williams's real target is morality's claim to be the final court of appeal: partial attachments may be normatively authoritative on their own, and requiring them to check in with an impartial system already gives morality too much authority.

D. The alienated-motive reading. If the extra thought is the man's actual reason, then he acts from a principle rather than from the relationship, which alienates his motive from his love; if the extra thought only shows up afterwards, the theory can only ratify the action by retrofitting a justification that played no part in moving him.

E. The integrity-unity reading. This is the integrity objection compressed into one example: making the man justify his rescue from the impartial point of view is the same move as making him treat his deepest commitments as things that can be demanded from him whenever an impartial calculation says so, so the two objections are really one.
