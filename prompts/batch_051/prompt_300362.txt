So I'm doing a piece on the way we use language to talk about things that aren't there — I've been reading Russell's "On Denoting" and I keep getting stuck on one passage. He says:

"A phrase may be denoting, and yet not denote anything; e.g., 'the present King of France.'"

I get the surface point: France is a republic, so there's no present king. But the more I think about it, the more I'm unsure what the actual stakes are supposed to be between Russell's theory and the views he's arguing against. It seems like there are several different positions one could hold about a phrase like that, and they don't all conflict in the same way. For instance, you might think the phrase has no denotation but still has a kind of meaning. You might think it denotes a nonexistent object, so it denotes something after all. You might think it denotes a merely possible object. You might think whether it denotes anything depends on context — maybe in 1620 it denoted Louis XIII, and in 1905 it denotes nothing. You might think it's not the phrase but the speaker who fails to refer. You might think the whole sentence "the present King of France is bald" is meaningful but neither true nor false. Or you might think, with Russell, that the logical form of the sentence is something like "there exists exactly one present King of France and whatever is present King of France is bald," in which case the sentence is just false.

What I want to nail down is: which of these are genuinely distinct positions, which collapse into each other, and which ones does the quoted passage actually rule out or fail to rule out? Also, I keep hearing people say Russell's theory "solves the problem of negative existentials." What precise problem is that, and which of the positions above would still have trouble with a sentence like "the present King of France does not exist"?

I'd like this laid out as a menu of readings or positions, with some verdicts about which are live options and which are dead ends, because I suspect the line "a phrase may be denoting, and yet not denote anything" is doing more work in these debates than it's usually credited with.
