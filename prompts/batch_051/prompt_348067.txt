I'm a faculty rep on our university's Academic Integrity and Conduct Panel, and we've got a case this term that has the panel split straight down the middle; I've been handed the job of writing the actual decision memo. A third-year student (call her Kay) was a research assistant in a biology lab, where she came to believe her PI had fabricated a figure in a manuscript they were preparing together. Separately, that same PI had failed her in a lab course the previous semester, which she'd always insisted was unfair. After the grade appeal process left the grade unchanged, she emailed the PI something like: change my grade or I send the fabricated figure to the research integrity office. The PI forwarded the email to the dean, the fabrication allegations are now being investigated as a seperate matter, and our panel only has to decide whether Kay's threat violated the conduct code.

The code has a clause, added a few years ago after some ugly incident involving a grad student and a dean, saying a student commits misconduct if they seek an academic benefit by threatening to disclose information that would harm the standing of another member of the university community. Four of our eight voting members think this is exactly the case the clause was written for: she tried to get a grade by threat, and the fact that the information was true (or that she had the right to report it) doesn't turn the threat into two innocent acts. Three others are deeply uncomfortable: they point out she had every right to report suspected fabrication — in fact the research integrity policy actively encourages it — and every right to appeal her grade. Each act alone would have been fine, so they worry we'd be punishing her for doing two permissible things in combination just because the combination feels ugly. They keep bringing up the general puzzle of why it's wrong to link a legitimate demand to a legitimate disclosure. The chair says she'll only break the tie if the memo gives her a principle she can actually defend.

So: draft the panel's decision memo. Recommend a finding (and, if responsible, a proportionate sanction), give the reasoning the committee can honestly stand behind, and deal squarely with the strongest argument on the losing side. If we keep the rule, also say what standard we should apply next time so the outcome isn't just gut feeling.
