Consider Exodus 33 in the King James Version. At verse 11 the narrator says, "And the LORD spake unto Moses face to face, as a man speaketh unto his friend." In the same context, at verse 20, God says to Moses, "Thou canst not see my face: for there shall no man see me, and live." A few verses later God appears to grant a partial exception: "thou shalt see my back parts: but my face shall not be seen." A reader committed to biblical inerrancy holds that each of these statements is true, because all are part of the inspired text. On a plain reading, however, verse 11 reports direct, face-to-face converse between Moses and God, while verse 20 rules out that very thing for any human being, and verse 23 splits the difference by allowing only a view of God's back. Moses is human and survives the encounter. Set out the strongest candidate reconciliations available to an inerrantist: for example, a distinction between ordinary relational presence and the vision of the divine face or essence; a distinction between "face" as an idiom of intimacy or favor and "face" as a literal countenance; a distinction between mediated manifestations (the angel, the cloud, the voice, the divine name, the back parts) and the unmediated sight forbidden in verse 20; and any reading on which one of the verses is better taken as hyperbole or as a description of what the encounter was like rather than a literal claim about visual perception. For each candidate, assess whether the distinction is genuinely in the text or whether it is special pleading introduced only to relieve the tension. Then give your verdict: which reconciliation is most faithful to the passage as it stands, and does the very need to supply such distinctions place a substantive constraint on how the doctrine of inerrancy must be formulated when it confronts apparent contradictions of this kind?
