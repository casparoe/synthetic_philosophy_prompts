A colleague of mine keeps a running file of candidate examples of what Wigner called the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics. For each entry she records a verdict: either the case genuinely deserves that name, or it has a perfectly mundane explanation. Her file contains five entries so far.

1. Conic sections and planetary orbits. The ellipse was studied in antiquity as pure geometry, with no thought of the heavens. It later turned out to be exactly the shape of a planetary orbit. Verdict: genuine case. Her note: the curve was there to be found, and the fit is exact.

2. Curved geometry and general relativity. The geometry was developed in the nineteenth century for reasons internal to mathematics, well before any physical application. General relativity later found it indispensable, and the resulting theory made novel predictions, including the bending of starlight, which were later confirmed. Verdict: genuine case.

3. Complex numbers in AC circuit analysis. The imaginary unit entered mathematics as part of the solution of cubic equations. Complex arithmetic is now the standard language for impedance, phase, and filters. Verdict: not a genuine case. Her note: a linear circuit is two coupled real equations, and complex notation only compresses what was already understood.

4. Fibonacci numbers in sunflower heads. The counts of the spirals follow the Fibonacci sequence. Verdict: not a genuine case. Her note: the numbers are a consequence of the packing geometry by which the florets are laid down.

5. Game theory in spectrum auctions. The mathematics of bidding works in practice largely because the auction rules were deliberately written so that rational bidders would behave as the mathematics assumes. Verdict: not a genuine case.

She must now rule on a new submission. The submitter proposes the presence of pi in the formula for the period of a simple pendulum: the period is two pi times the square root of the length divided by gravitational acceleration, T = 2π√(L/g). Pi, the submitter observes, was studied for millennia as the constant of circles, with nothing to do with clocks.

Predict her verdict if she is to remain consistent with her file. Identify the two most plausible rules that fit her five earlier verdicts, explain what each rule predicts for the pendulum case, give your best prediction of her ruling and defend the choice of one rule as her implicit principle, and name a further case that would settle which rule she is actually using if the pendulum leaves that open. If you think the earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine this case, say explicitly what principle she would be committing herself to under each available ruling.
