I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of games and aesthetics, and we have spent several weeks on a position I'd summarize as the aesthetics-of-agency account of games. On this view, what games contribute as an art form is not primarily their narrative, their visual surface, or even their rules as a formal object, but the temporary practical standpoint the player is invited to inhabit: the goals the game makes salient, the capacities it hands the player, the obstacles and trade-offs it arranges, and the distinctive way of being a practical subject that results. The position was developed largely by reflecting on relatively bounded games — chess, platformers, puzzle games, more-or-less authored role-playing games — in which a designer can plausibly be understood as deliberately sculpting such a standpoint, and it was offered in part as an answer to the old question of whether games are art at all.

My question is whether this argument survives the rise of engagement-optimized service games. A great deal of contemporary play is organized not around a designed practical standpoint but around systems whose explicit purpose is retention: daily login rewards, battle passes, variable-ratio loot schedules, and procedural or personalized variation tuned to keep the player in the loop. The goals a player pursues can then be byproducts of an optimization process rather than the articulation of an artistic intention; the designer is supervising a system that discovers whatever keeps players playing. A friend in the course insists this is simply game design done well and that the old view should stretch to cover it; another thinks the view was implicitly evaluative all along and gives a principled reason to call these games lesser art. I want to know what happens to the argument itself, not just what verdict we should reach about the industry. Which premise is actually under pressure — the claim that a designed standpoint is offered, the claim that the player takes it up as an agent, or the claim that this is where the aesthetic content lives? Does the account survive intact, survive only with repairs (and what repairs?), or does it become inapplicable to a large and growing part of the medium? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on this, and don't present a contested interpretation of the account as the consensus.
