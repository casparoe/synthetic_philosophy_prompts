I have a strict personal rule about voting: I never cast a strategic ballot. In any vote with three or more options — a ranked-choice city council race, an approval vote at our co-op's annual meeting, a straw poll among friends — I put down my preferences exactly as I hold them, even when I am fairly sure my first choice cannot win and the only realistic way to stop the candidate I most dislike is to promote my second choice to first. I followed this rule in last spring's ranked-choice primary: I ranked the long-shot candidate first and the electable moderate second, although I knew the long shot would be eliminated early. My brother says the rule is not honesty but a kind of principled self-sabotage. By refusing to use my ballot tactically, he says, I hand influence to voters who feel no such scruple. He claims that under every ranked voting system there will be cases where a voter gets a better outcome by misrepresenting her ranking, and he showed me a three-candidate version of our own primary in which my honest ballot helped the candidate I liked least reach the final round, while ranking the moderate first would have elected the moderate. I had to admit the arithmetic worked.

I still want to believe the rule is defensible, but I do not want to pay a needless price for a principle if my picture of the system is wrong. Is my habit defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as a virtue? Specifically: under what conditions does sincere voting amount to quietly giving away influence, and under what conditions is it harmless? Is my brother's claim too broad — does the fact that a voting method can be manipulated in some situations really mean that a sincere voter is always being exploited? And is there a restated version of my rule, something like "vote sincerely except when a rare and identifiable threshold is crossed," that keeps the honesty without the self-sabotage?
