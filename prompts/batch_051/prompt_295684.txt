I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a longform piece on the ethics of biopics and historical fiction — specifically, on what, if anything, the living owe to the real dead when actual lives are turned into drama. In my reporting I keep meeting two reactions that seem to be doing different moral work: a screenwriter who defended an invented scene on the grounds that the historical figure would have recognized the portrait as true, and the adult grandchildren of a different subject who felt a recent film slandered someone who can no longer answer back. I want the piece to include a "which position do you actually hold?" quiz that sorts readers by what they say about hard cases, not by which label appeals to them.

The instrument should have eight questions, each built around a concrete invented case — an invented speech, a composite character, a shifted chronology, a disputed allegation treated as fact, a novelist inhabiting a dead person's private thoughts — with two or three answer options. No question should simply ask readers whether they hold a named view. The fault lines I most need the quiz to capture are: whether the dead can be morally wronged at all; whether a work's status as fiction changes the duties compared with documentary; whether any duty runs to the dead themselves or only to living survivors, descendants, and communities; and whether invention is licensed when it preserves a deeper truth about the person. Build from the logical space first and then locate positions in it. The leaves must include at least a strict fidelity view, a posthumous-interests view, a survivors-first view, an artistic-license view, and a cultural-stewardship view; add one or two more only if the space demands them. For any unoccupied combination of answers, say whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves the instrument cannot really separate.

Please structure the response in four named sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths: The instrument (the eight questions and their options, phrased so an educated reader can answer without knowing philosophical labels; 700–900 words); Scoring key and leaves (how answer patterns map to the named positions, with one sentence for each leaf on its most awkward commitment; 350–450 words); Empty and contentious combinations (the unoccupied cells, with a one-line diagnosis of each, plus the inseparable pairs and why; 250–350 words); and Confidence note (how confident you are in the mapping and the taxonomy overall, and the single point you are least sure about; 150–200 words).
