In a reading group on Marx and technology, we have been stuck for several sessions on the *Grundrisse*'s so-called "Fragment on Machines." The passage that keeps dividing us is early in Notebook VII of the "Chapter on Capital," where Marx writes:

"However, the development of machinery along this path occurs only when large industry has already reached a higher stage, and all the sciences have been pressed into the service of capital; and when, secondly, the available machinery itself already provides great capabilities. Invention then becomes a business, and the application of science to direct production itself becomes a prospect which determines and solicits it."

The disagreement is not about what the words say, but about what the passage licenses. One side reads it as part of Marx's claim that capitalism increasingly subordinates science to accumulation, and that this subordination eventually makes direct labor time a poor measure of wealth. The other side says the passage is doing something narrower: it marks a distinction between two historical roads to mechanization—the conscious application of science, and the deskilling of workers through the division of labor—and it is a mistake to treat the Notebook VII drafts as if they had the same status as *Capital*'s published treatment of machinery. After a year of discussion, neither side has moved. I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is: Is it a dispute about how much weight to give an unfinished manuscript against Marx's published work? About whether the concept of the general intellect is doing theoretical work or just rhetorical work? About whether technological change can be separated from its capitalist form? Or is the debate stalled because the two sides are asking different questions under the same label? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a distinction, a test case, a shift in the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected? Please answer in American English.
