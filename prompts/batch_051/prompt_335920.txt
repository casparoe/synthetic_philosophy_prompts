I have been trying to sharpen a concept that tends to do unstated work in arguments about regional integration: what it is for a state to have genuinely joined a larger political body, as opposed to merely being party to a treaty among sovereigns. I want to avoid settling this with the cheap observation that sovereignty is a spectrum; that observation is only useful if it can specify which dimensions do the work. So I am going to state provisional criteria for being a confederation, then test them against a deliberately awkward case.

My provisional criteria are as follows. A group of states forms a confederation only if:

1. Institutional autonomy. There is a standing common organ, staffed or directed by persons expected to act for the body rather than as instructed delegates of their home states, that can take decisions binding on member states without each state's subsequent ratification, within at least one delimited domain.
2. Majority decision. At least some of those decisions are taken by weighted or qualified majority, so that a member state can be outvoted rather than possessing a veto.
3. External unity. In the pooled domain, the body acts externally as one actor — it negotiates and commits as a single unit — and member states are precluded from separate action in that domain.
4. Direct effect. The body's rules bind private persons inside the member states, and a common adjudicative mechanism can enforce them and override conflicting national decisions.
5. Intended permanence. Membership is an ongoing status from which exit is institutionally constrained, by long notice periods, continuing obligations during exit, or substantial exit costs, rather than a treaty denunciable at will.
6. Breadth. The body has competence in at least one domain outside the coordination of external trade — security or defence, monetary policy, free movement of persons, migration, or fiscal transfers to individuals — so that the pooling reaches something more than the mechanics of a common tariff.

Now consider this case. Three states, A, B, and C, establish a customs union. They adopt a common external tariff and a common commercial code, maintained by a Commission whose members are appointed by the three governments but are required to act independently. Tariff schedules and trade-remedy decisions are taken by weighted majority and enter into force without ratification. A Union Court hears complaints from firms against any member state or against the Commission; its judgments automatically carry the force of national law and can annul national customs decisions. The Union negotiates all trade agreements for the three states as a single bloc, and the member states may not conclude separate trade deals. Goods, services, and capital move freely, but persons do not. The Union's budget is capped at one half of one percent of the states' combined GDP, funded from a share of the common duties; it maintains a secretariat and a trade-defence office but makes no payments to individuals. Withdrawal requires four years' notice, during which all obligations continue to bind. There is no common foreign policy, no defence cooperation, no monetary integration, no common citizenship, and no common regime for the movement of people.

Does this arrangement qualify as a confederation under my six criteria? Work through the criteria one by one, saying for each whether it is satisfied, violated, or not precise enough to decide. Then answer the substantive question: have A, B, and C joined something best described as a confederation, as a treaty-based customs union with some confederal features, or as a case showing that confederation is a graded matter rather than a threshold one? Finally, say whether the case exposes a defect in my criteria — for example, a criterion doing more work than it should, a tension between criteria, or a missing criterion — and state the revision you would make.
