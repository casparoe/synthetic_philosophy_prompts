I find myself torn about delusions, and I want a disciplined adjudication rather than a verdict. On reflection, the two-factor account of how delusions form strikes me as close to right. On this view, a delusion requires two distinct failures. The first is a neuropsychological anomaly that supplies the raw material of the delusion: in Capgras syndrome, face recognition continues to work but the usual affective response to a familiar face is absent, so the patient is left with the insistent sense that someone who looks exactly like her husband is not her husband. The second is a failure of belief evaluation that keeps the patient from rejecting the strange hypothesis this experience suggests; without it, the anomaly would yield only a passing, though odd, thought. The account is economical, it explains both the content of delusions and their resistance to counterevidence, and it places delusions on a continuum with ordinary false beliefs formed in the grip of bad evidence and defended badly.

Yet the account seems to force a conclusion I cannot bring myself to accept: that the patient with Capgras believes her husband has been replaced by an impostor. When I actually imagine her—saying the words while continuing to live with the man, using his name, never calling the police, and sometimes behaving in ways that presuppose he is her husband—I cannot attribute a full belief to her. It looks more like an avowal that has detached from the rest of her cognitive and practical life, a thought that occupies her without commanding the inferences and actions beliefs normally command. If that intuition is right, then either delusions are not beliefs, or 'belief' needs a reading so accommodating that the claim that she believes is no longer the interesting claim.

Present the strongest version of both sides and tell me what it would take for one of them to give way. What would justify discounting my intuition as an artifact of a too-demanding, idealized picture of belief—one that many ordinary beliefs would also fail? What would justify instead abandoning the doxastic reading (the claim that delusions are beliefs) of the two-factor account, and what would that abandonment cost the account's explanation of why delusions persist? And is there a stable position on which both survive—perhaps by distinguishing what is avowed from what is believed outright, or by attributing to the patient a fragmented or partial doxastic state—and what would such a position commit me to? I am not asking you to settle the debate; I am asking what each resolution would cost, and how to tell which cost is worth paying.
