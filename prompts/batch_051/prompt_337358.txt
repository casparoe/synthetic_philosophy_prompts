I'm taking a graduate seminar in the philosophy of medicine, and my study group has been working through the debate over human enhancement. We kept running into the distinction between therapy and enhancement—roughly, therapy restores species-typical normal functioning while enhancement pushes a person beyond that normal range—and one member of the group eventually offered an argument that seems to show the distinction cannot carry real moral weight. We've now convinced ourselves of a conclusion none of us wanted, and we can't locate the mistake. Here is the argument as we've formalized it:

(1) An intervention counts as therapy if it aims to restore or preserve species-typical normal functioning, and as enhancement if it aims to move a person beyond that range. (2) So whether an intervention is therapeutic or enhancing depends entirely on where the boundaries of the normal range are drawn. (3) Those boundaries can be fixed in only two ways: as a statistical fact about how the relevant trait is actually distributed in a population, or as an evaluative ideal of how a human being ought to function. (4) If the boundary is merely statistical, then it is the wrong kind of thing to ground a moral difference: from facts about how common a level of functioning happens to be, nothing follows about which interventions are permissible, obligatory, or publicly fundable. (5) If the boundary is evaluative, then the distinction already presupposes the very moral judgment it is supposed to justify—it has quietly decided that departures from a favored norm are defects and that going beyond that norm is suspect—so appealing to the distinction cannot independently explain why therapy and enhancement should be treated differently. (6) Either way, the therapy/enhancement distinction is either morally arbitrary or circular. (7) Therefore calling an intervention therapeutic rather than enhancing adds no independent justificatory force; it marks, at most, a statistical fact or a concealed value judgment. (8) But that conclusion seems to prove too much, since medicine, sports regulation, and disability policy all place enormous weight on the distinction, and most of us still want to say that treating a child with strep throat differs from giving healthy students stimulants for exams in a way that cannot simply be settled by saying that one intervention maximizes expected welfare.

We've gone in circles about which step to give up. I'd most like to hold on to the conviction that there is a real difference in kind—not merely a difference in social convention—between repairing someone's functioning and augmenting it, but I worry that step (3) or step (4) has to be abandoned, and I don't know what giving one of them up costs. As a philosopher who works on enhancement and the concept of normality, could you diagnose the argument for us? Please begin by reconstructing it and brainstorming the possible break points, then identify the exact step or inference that fails, explain why that step looked plausible, and lay out what the main positions in the literature would have to pay to reject it. I don't just need to hear that the conclusion is too radical; I need to know where the chain of reasoning breaks and which alternative views remain standing once it does.
