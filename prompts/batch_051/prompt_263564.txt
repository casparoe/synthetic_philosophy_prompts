Exam question: Does Devlin or Hart have the better of the argument about whether the state may criminalise conduct purely because it is widely regarded as immoral?

Student answer: The Hart–Devlin debate came out of the Wolfenden Report, which recommended decriminalising homosexual acts between consenting adults. Devlin attacked the report for separating crime from sin. His central claim was that a society is held together by a shared positive morality, so that just as a society may punish treason because treason threatens the society's existence, it may also punish immorality, because departures from the shared morality loosen the bonds that make society possible. Hart replied that this social-cement claim is empirical and unsupported: there is no real evidence that changing sexual mores have ever made a society collapse, and Devlin moves from the fact that societies have shared moralities to the conclusion that enforcing those moralities is necessary for survival. Hart also drew a distinction between positive morality, the conventional rules a society actually holds, and critical morality, the standards by which conventional morality itself can be judged, and he argued that the law should be guided by critical morality, specifically Mill's harm principle. Devlin's reasonable-man standard is weak in the same way, because it lets disgust stand in for judgement: the reasonable man just is the man who is revolted by homosexual acts. Devlin's strongest point is that Hart's harm principle is thin, and misses the way shared norms underpin trust and cooperation. Still, Hart gets the better of it overall. On Hart's harm principle, Devlin is in trouble from the start: Devlin never shows that widespread moral disapproval is itself a harm. What he offers instead is really a form of paternalism, since the law ends up protecting people from their own self-corrupting choices, and that is precisely the kind of reason the harm principle rules out.
