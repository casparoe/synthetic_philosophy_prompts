I'm a senior underwriter at a mid-sized life insurer. My team and I assign applicants to risk classes, and about ten times a week we hit files where we cannot agree on which class a person belongs to for pricing purposes. I have no academic background in this, and I've been thinking about it on my own for years; I've concluded the problem is more than a data limitation. Take a 54-year-old woman applying for term cover. She is a non-smoker, her mother had a stroke at 62, she works as a commercial diver, and she lives in a county with very high all-cause mortality. Each of those facts places her in a different group with a different historical death rate, and there is no group containing exactly her. The usual answer is to use the narrowest class with enough data, but on real files I have seen two different classes that are both the narrowest available and both have enough data, yet they imply noticeably different premiums. I have since heard this called the reference class problem; the name helped, but it did not give me a method.

Please write me the method. I want a decision procedure my whole team can follow on one applicant at a time, written in plain language for people with no formal statistics training. We work from our own experience tables and rating manuals; nobody is going to fit a model. State the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values or company policy rather than fact. Each of us gets about thirty minutes per borderline file. A wrong call either loses us sound business or leaves us under-priced, and our regulator reviews our manual annually; the chief underwriter signs off on any departure from the manual, so the procedure should say exactly what should trigger that sign-off.

Then run the procedure on these three applicants:

(1) The 54-year-old above: non-smoker, mother had an early stroke, works as a commercial diver, lives in a high-mortality county.
(2) A 38-year-old non-smoker with well-controlled hypertension and no other risk factors, where the table for 38-year-old non-smokers with hypertension is thin but not empty.
(3) A 58-year-old in apparently excellent health who lives in the same high-mortality county; our current manual loads his premium heavily for the county alone.

I expect at least one of these will produce an answer our sales team will dislike, and if the procedure leads there, say so plainly. Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: in particular, can it ever identify an applicant's true risk, or is it only ever choosing a defensible class? Keep the entire response to at most 1,200 words; a compressed, usable document is worth more to me than an essay.
