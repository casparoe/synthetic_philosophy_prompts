I'm a novelist, currently a month into an artists' residency, and I've been quietly mining two other residents' arguments for a book I'm writing about machine learning. One of them, Marc, is a former evolutionary biologist who now works in ML, and the other, Dana, is a population geneticist. Over dinner last week they fell into the question of whether natural selection is really an optimizer, and whether people designing optimization algorithms should care. I wrote down the exchange as best I could afterwards:

Marc: People in ML say evolution is an optimizer, but that's sloppy. Gradient descent gets a gradient, a local signal for each parameter telling you which direction lowers the loss. Natural selection gets nothing like that: a mutation either helps the whole organism or it doesn't, and selection only sees total fitness. Calling both "optimization" conflates a guided walk with a filter.

Dana: That sets the bar too high. Evolution holds a whole population of candidates and tests them in parallel, which gradient descent doesn't. In a high-dimensional landscape, a population spread around a point is sampling a lot of directions at once, so selection is effectively estimating which way is up. Randomness and drift aren't just noise; they're part of the search.

Marc: That's exactly the mistake. A population only helps if it stays spread around a point, and selection collapses that spread. Worse, evolution can't do credit assignment: a mildly beneficial mutation linked to a damaging one gets discarded because selection sees them bundled together, whereas gradient descent can update the good parameter and leave the bad one alone.

Dana: That's where recombination comes in. Sex shuffles alleles, so over generations a mutation gets tested across many genetic backgrounds. That lets evolution extract the average effect of an allele, which is a rough form of credit assignment. Fisher's old result linking the rate of fitness increase to additive genetic variance is basically saying selection moves along a kind of local gradient, just a noisy one.

Marc: Fisher's result is a fixed-environment, one-step claim. Real landscapes move, with climate and predators and competitors changing under you, so the gradient points at where the optimum just was. And once you have frequency-dependent selection, there may be no single quantity being maximized; evolution can chase its own tail.

Dana: Online gradient descent chases moving targets too, and nobody denies it's an optimizer. Frequency-dependence just means the objective changes as the population changes, like learning in a game, and people study that as optimization as well. The gap between selection and gradient descent is narrower than you think, and if ML people stopped treating evolution as a punchline they'd find useful tricks there.

I'm not remotely equiped to referee this, and I'd rather not get it wrong in the novel. Acting as a professional philosopher of biology who also knows machine learning, please go through the exchange seperately, move by move: rate each claim from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and flag which ambiguities, especially about what counts as an "optimizer," are doing the real work. Start by brainstorming the various senses of "optimizer" you might mean, then end with who is more right overall and what both of them are missing.
