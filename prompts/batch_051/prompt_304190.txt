I work as a software engineer, mostly on machine-learning systems, and I have no formal background in philosophy. I have been trying to think clearly about whether a language model can ever be said to make an "intuitive leap": a genuinely novel insight that goes beyond its training distribution, rather than a clever recomputation of what is already latent in the weights. A colleague of mine is sceptical, and he laid out the following argument, which unsettles me because it seems to prove far too much. If it is sound, it also rules out human intuitive leaps, and I cannot accept that. Here are the steps as I understand them.

(1) A transition from what a system already knows to a new idea counts as a genuine intuitive leap only if the new idea is neither (a) determined by the system's prior information together with its fixed update and execution rules, nor (b) the product of mere chance.

(2) In a trained model, every output is either (a) determined by the training data plus the inductive biases built into the architecture, or (b), when stochastic decoding such as temperature sampling is used, a random draw from a probability distribution fixed by those same ingredients. Neither case meets the condition in (1).

(3) A human thinker is in the same position: any insight is either (a) determined by prior experience plus innate or evolutionarily inherited biases and fixed neural machinery, or (b) a random neural accident. Again, neither case meets the condition in (1).

(4) Therefore neither machines nor humans ever make genuine intuitive leaps.

(5) But that conclusion is absurd. Some human achievements look like paradigm cases of intuitive leaps: the first construction of non-Euclidean geometry, the idea that time might be a fourth dimension, or the conjecture of a deep mathematical structure before any proof of it existed. These are not merely reading off what was already believed.

(6) So at least one of (1) through (3) must be rejected.

I am most reluctant to give up (5). I am prepared to revise the early premises, but I want to know exactly what I am paying when I do. Where precisely does this argument go wrong? Which premise or step should be rejected? Explain why that premise looked as plausible as it does, and set out what a defender of each major alternative would have to concede. If the argument slides between two readings of one of its terms, state both readings and locate the slide. I also want the result to remain useful for assessing present-day systems: if something like a stochastic search with filtering turns out to be capable of a genuine leap, what exactly would a system need for that search to count as its leap rather than as luck? Please do not give me a reading list or point me to the literature; I want the analysis itself in your own words.

I would like you to answer as a professional philosopher working on the foundations of machine learning and the philosophy of mind. Before committing to a diagnosis, brainstorm two or three candidate failure points, then explain which you take to be correct and why. Think through the steps carefully and in order. Please write the final answer in British English.
