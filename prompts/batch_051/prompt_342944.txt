I co-teach an undergraduate seminar called The Ontology of Social Groups, about 16 students, 80 minutes. We're about to discuss whether generations—Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, whatever—are real social kinds or just journalistic shorthand. They've read Mannheim's old essay "The Problem of Generations," and they can mostly parrot the move: a birth cohort is just people born around the same time, but a generation proper exists only when a cohort is exposed to the same historical events during its formative years and actually works through them together. The trouble is they say this without feeling why it's hard. One student even said generations are astrology for college-educated people, and the rest kind of nodded, but I don't think they could defend that either.

I want a classroom activity—a game, role-play, sorting task, simulation, whatever works—that makes them discover for themselves that the same set of lives can be carved up in very different ways, and that treating "Boomer" or "Gen Z" as a real kind has to earn its keep rather than being assumed. The students should not hear Mannheim's theoretical vocabulary until the debrief. In the debrief, I want to map specific moments in the activity onto the difference between generation location and generation as actuality, and also onto the skeptic's worry that these labels are partly imposed by institutions, media, and retrospect. I haven't decided whether to use real historical events or invented ones, so please make a call on that and say why. You are an expert on the sociology of generations and on classroom design. Start by brainstorming two or three possible activity concepts, then settle on one and write it up as a runnable plan: exact setup, materials, rules or payoffs, timing, what the facilitator should and shouldn't say, what will predictably go wrong, and an honest note about which part of the idea the activity can't by itself produce.
