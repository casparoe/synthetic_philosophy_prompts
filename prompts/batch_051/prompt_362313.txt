I hold that rational credences satisfy something like Van Fraassen's Reflection principle: my present credence in a proposition should equal my present expectation of my future credence in that proposition, and more precisely, conditional on the proposition that my future credence in P will be x, my present credence in P should be x. I take this to be a substantive normative constraint on agents like us, not just a trivial consequence of how credences are defined. I want an honest account of what would count against it. Which assumptions does the principle rest on—about the reliability and authority of one's future self, the absence of forgetting or reasoning deficits, the continuity of the epistemic agent across time, and the direction of fit between credence and evidence—and what would the world have to look like for each of those assumptions to fail? Go beyond the standard cases of expected future drunkenness, bias, or memory loss and consider less familiar pressure points, such as cases in which the later credence is causally downstream of the present one, or in which the later self is arguably a different epistemic agent. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish escape routes that are live from those that are merely logically possible, and finish by assessing whether the principle's standard qualifications are principled or whether they insulate it into unfalsifiability—and whether that insulation is itself a problem.
