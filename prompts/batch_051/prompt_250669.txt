The exam question was: "Using Dennett's Chase and Sanborn scenario, explain what he takes the scenario to show about qualia and how it fits into his broader argument in 'Quining Qualia'."

The student's answer reads:

"Chase and Sanborn are two coffee tasters for Maxwell House who both discover, after many years, that they no longer like the company's coffee. Chase says his taste has changed: the coffee tastes exactly the same to him as it always has, but he no longer likes it. Sanborn says the opposite: his taste is unchanged, and the coffee itself has changed so that it now tastes different to him. Dennett's point is that no amount of introspection can tell which of them is right, and in fact there is no fact of the matter about which it is. A change in one's qualia is introspectively indistinguishable from a change in one's evaluative reaction to them. Since the traditional notion of qualia—as private, ineffable, intrinsic, and incorrigible—could not settle even this simple sort of case, Dennett concludes that there are no such things as qualia. On his view, all there really is is the subject's dispositions to report and behave, and qualia are just a theoretical fiction we should eliminate."
