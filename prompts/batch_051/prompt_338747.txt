I help run a monthly philosophy discussion circle at out local library — usually eight to twelve adults, none of whom have done any formal philosophy, and we get ninety minutes for a session. I read something recently that's been sitting with me, and I want to turn it into a session. The idea (the author called it something like the afterlife conjecture, but I might be mangling it) is that a huge amount of what we care about secretly depends on humanity continuing after we're gone, not on us personally being around to see it. The author asks you to imagine two situations. In the first, everyone alive now gets a full normal life, but no new people are ever born, so we quietly turn out to be the last generation. In the second, your own life is exactly what it would have been, except that thirty days after you die an asteroid painlessly kills everyone left. Nobody suffers extra, and in both cases your own life is unchanged. The claim is that in those situations, lots of projects — writing a novel, working on a cure for something, learning a dying language, keeping an old craft going — would start to lose their point in ways people don't predict ahead of time. And that's supposed to show that human extinction is bad over and above the deaths it involves, because it pulls a kind of rug out from under the things the living are doing.

I'd like to run this so the group discovers it themselves rather than me lecturing. Could you design a runnable activity — a game with small real stakes, a structured forced-choice exercise, or a role-play — that gets them to feel that pull before the idea gets named? I'm imagining the rules make them actually commit to projects or plans, and then the information about wether humanity continues changes partway through, so the pattern shows up in what they chose, not in anything I say. What I want them to leave with is the sense that a lot of what they care about is propped up, in ways they hadn't noticed, by the assumption that humanity keeps going. Then I'd like a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the pieces of the idea, says what will predictably go wrong when we run it (people refusing to take the sci-fi scenarios seriously, or just saying what they think they're supposed to say), and is honest about what the activity can't reproduce. For instance, we can't make anyone genuinely believe the extinction news, so at best they get a weaker echo of the feeling — you should say how much that limits the whole point, and what I'd need to supply myself. No lecture up front, and don't name the idea until after.
