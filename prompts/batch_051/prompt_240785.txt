I keep flip-flopping on laws of nature. The clean, engineer-y part of me is sold on the Humean story: the universe is just a long sequence of events, and a "law of nature" is a compact description of that sequence — basically the best compression you can do over all the data. No hidden glue, no spooky necessity, just patterns and the summaries of those patterns. That view doesn't postulate unobservables, and it avoids having to explain what a "power" is in the first place. But then I read the actual passage in David Hume's *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Section VII, "Of the Idea of Necessary Connexion," and I'm not sure it leaves any room for the thing I can't shake. He writes:

> When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other.

Hume is saying: all we ever observe is one event followed by another. And when I'm being strict, I believe that — all I ever see is motion, never "force" or "connection" as a separate ingredient. But when I shove a heavy desk, or watch a cue ball hit another, I have a stubborn intuition that the law isn't just a summary. It feels like there is something about the world that makes the second event happen, that explains why it follows, and that the future is more than just "it has always gone that way so far." A compression algorithm describes and predicts; it doesn't make anything happen. I don't want to lose the tidy Humean picture, but I also don't want to write the intuition off as an illusion left over from physics class.

So my question is the negotiate-the-truce one. What are my actual options if I want to keep both? Specifically: (a) is the intuition something I should debunk and drop — and if so, what would count as a legitimate debunking rather than just refusing to listen to it? (b) Or should the Humean picture be amended or abandoned, so laws are real governors or necessitators after all — and what exactly does that move cost in simplicity and empiricism, given that Hume's paragraph seems right about what we observe? (c) Or is there a reading on which "laws are compressed descriptions" and "laws do real work" are compatable, where the passage is true but the intuition survives? I want to know what would justify each move, not just which one is standard.
