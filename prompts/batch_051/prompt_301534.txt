I have been thinking about long-run empirical predictions, and I keep coming back to the phrase "right but early." I have verdicts about particular cases, but I cannot tell whether they hang together or whether I am using the phrase selectively. I want you to tell me what principle, or small set of principles, captures my verdicts; which of my verdicts it forces me to revise; and what it says about further cases I have not listed.

Here are the cases.

1. The early Malthusian argument paired an arithmetic claim—food output can increase only additively—with the claim that population tends to increase exponentially, and concluded that population would be held near subsistence by famine, disease, and war. Over the following two centuries, food output grew far faster than the arithmetic claim allowed, and the predicted global subsistence crisis did not occur as dated. I nevertheless hold that the abstract limit claim—endless exponential growth is impossible in a finite system—is true. My verdict: the theory as stated was falsified, and it should not be called "right but early," even though a weaker claim it never cleanly separated off was true.

2. A 1972-style simulation of world population, industrialization, and resources predicted overshoot followed by a sharp collapse beginning in the first half of the twenty-first century under business-as-usual assumptions. By 2000, several of its nearer-term paths—resource depletion rates, population for some regions—had already proved too pessimistic, but the collapse date itself still lay in the future. My verdict: I am unwilling to call the overall model falsified yet, but I count the failed nearer-term projections against it more heavily than its defenders do.

3. A specific forecast from the late 1960s predicted that within ten to twenty years hundreds of millions of people would die of famine in particular countries, and that some large regions would undergo population decline. Famines did occur in some places, but not in the predicted places or at the predicted scale, and the underlying agricultural trend changed in ways the forecast's framework did not anticipate. My verdict: the forecast was disconfirmed, and calling it "right about the risk but wrong about the date" would be special pleading, because the mechanism was misidentified rather than merely mistimed.

4. A geologist fit a logistic curve to historical production data and predicted that oil output in a particular major producing country would peak around a specified year. Production peaked within a few years of that date. The same method was less reliable elsewhere. My verdict: this is a genuine success for a long-run resource prediction, and it shows that a peak date can be predicted rather than only retrodicted.

5. A climate model projects that, under a specified emissions path, global mean temperature in 2050 will fall within a stated range. The year 2050 has not arrived. The model's physical mechanism is well understood, its hindcasts are accurate, and its shorter-term forecasts have been good. My verdict: I can rationally accept the 2050 projection now. A prediction is not merely unfalsifiable or unconfirmed simply because its date lies in the future.

6. A forecaster predicted collapse by 1998. In 1999 he said the theory was intact and only the date was wrong, and set the collapse for 2010. In 2011 he repeated this, setting it for 2025. My verdict: this sequence disconfirms the forecast and each of its dated successors. Moving the deadline back without changing the model is not being "right but early"; it is a confession that the theory as stated was false, and the bare undated residue is not itself a falsifiable theory.

7. Thermodynamics entails that the universe will approach a state of maximum entropy, often called heat death, on the order of 10^100 years from now. No one will ever observe this. My verdict: the claim is as secure as the thermodynamics that entails it. I am not bothered by the fact that the prediction itself can never be checked, because it functions as a consequence of an already well-tested theory rather than as an independent forecast.

8. Later versions of the Malthusian tradition incorporated demographic transition—the tendency of fertility to fall as societies grow richer—and endogenous technological change, so that limits show up as relative-price shifts, distributional crises, and delayed collapse rather than subsistence catastrophe. My verdict: this was a genuine theoretical revision rather than an ad hoc rescue, because the added mechanisms have independent empirical support and the revised theory makes new predictions the old one did not.

What I want is the principle. Which of my verdicts, if any, must I give up? In particular: when does a missed date count as disconfirmation and when as mere postponement? What distinguishes a legitimate "right but early" judgment from the date-shifting in case 6, and from the false-but-nearby-true situation in case 1? Is the distinction between the core of a theory and its timing assumptions doing real work, or is that distinction itself just a license for rescue? State the principle or candidates precisely, say which clause each case motivates, apply it to further cases I have not considered, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
