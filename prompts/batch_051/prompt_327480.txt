I'm a science and ideas reporter, and I've spent the last few months covering city-council fights about wages and rent, and I've realized my own instincts about price controls dosen't form one coherent view. After the hurricane a couple years back, when the corner store tripled the price of bottled water, I thought: that's not a market, that's a mugging, the state should stop it. When my city capped rent increases on older apartment buildings, I voted for it and still would; long-term tenants shouldn't be pushed out so a corporate landlord can catch up to "market rate." A $20 minimum wage for big fast-food chains? Fine, probably overdue. The same floor applied to the diner in the town I grew up in, population 3,000? Not fine; I know the owner and she'd cut a shift, and my cousin actually lost hours after the last increase. A cap on what hospitals charge for insulin? Do it yesterday, I don't care how many economists it annoys. But a cap on rents in brand-new apartment buildings, meant to keep them "affordable"? No; that's a slow-motion way to make the housing shortage worse.

So I catch myself liking a price floor in one labor market and disliking it in another, and liking a price ceiling on old apartments and insulin while rejecting a price ceiling on new apartments. The weird part is that a floor on wages and a ceiling on rent are supposed to be mirror images of the same thing—government putting a legal min or max on a price someone else would otherwise set—and I seem to like both mirrors while also making exceptions in each direction. Is there a principle that actually gets these six verdicts, or nearly? Half the time I think it's about short-run versus long-run supply, or about whether the seller has enough power that the buyer can't just walk away; the other half I think I'm just choosing whichever side can absorb the cost. Give me the strongest principle, or a small set of them, that captures my verdicts. Test it on cases I haven't sorted out—a cap on CEO pay, a wage floor for gig drivers, a freeze on rent increases during a pandemic—and tell me plainly which of my six verdicts it can't accommodate and whether I should drop that verdict rather than complicate the principle.
