Next month I am defending a paper at an affective science workshop, and I need a Q&A prep sheet. The thesis I will defend is that there is a class of ordinary negative emotions—call them recognition-responsive states—whose felt unpleasantness is not a cost attached to an informational signal but a constituent part of what it is to register that something one valued has been irreversibly lost. Because of this, interventions that damp these states, whether pharmacological, therapeutic, or app-based, are not improvements purely in virtue of how much suffering they remove, even when the person retains all the relevant factual beliefs. The audience is mostly empirical researchers in emotion regulation and positive psychology, with two psychiatrists from a mood disorders clinic and one philosopher of mind working on affect; they are not anti-emotion, but their working default is that a negative emotion earns its keep only as information, and once the information is extracted the remaining bad feeling is dead weight. They will be especially alert to anything that sounds like romanticizing suffering or eroding the line between ordinary grief and clinical depression. My argument runs roughly as follows: across choice and memory tasks, people whose recognition-responsive sadness is intact make more stable evaluative judgments about what mattered to them than people left with the same factual knowledge but dampened affect, and the best explanation is that the unpleasantness itself is doing the registering, not just adding a sting. For each, give a reply I could deliver in about two minutes and flag the exact place where that reply is softest—the thing I should be ready to defend rather than hope they miss.
