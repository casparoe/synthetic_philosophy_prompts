A friend and I got into it last week about whether the chain-of-thought a language model prints out is a faithful report of the computation that actually produced its answer, and I've been poking at it since. I keep circling around the same family of doubts, and I think they're all the standard ones:

- Post-hoc rationalization: the conclusion gets settled by whatever's going on under the hood, and the written reasoning is filled in afterwards to make it look sensible.
- Genre mimicry: the model learned from humans to produce the form of an explanation, without ever having introspective access to its own causal machinery.
- Coherence pressure: answer and rationale are generated as a package, so the reasoning gets quietly bent to support the answer, rather than the answer being the product of that reasoning.
- Distributed computation: the real computation is spread across millions of parallel operations, so it's not even clear there is a single token-level "reason" to report.
- Intervention evidence: if you ablate or edit certain individual chain-of-thought steps and the final answer doesn't move, that's evidence the steps aren't doing the work.
- Optimization for plausibility: training and reward models push toward rationales that look convincing or helpful to people, and "looking like reasoning" is not the same as tracking what happened.

These all look like the usual suspects to me. What I want is an objection to chain-of-thought faithfulness that is not on that list and not just a rewording, special case, or combination of things on it. Something with a different shape entirely, even if it's a bit weird. And then I'd like you to be honest about whether its any good — does it survive inspection, or does it collapse back into one of the six once you press on it?
