I recently started volunteering with a resettlement programme for people leaving prison, and I keep noticing a split in myself and among the other volunteers about what compassion actually does. Sometimes it seems obviously to be the thing that makes moral life possible: a person is met with mercy, and only then can they change. At other times the same attitudes seem to leave the person feeling pitied rather than respected, and the helper — if I am honest about myself — gets something from being the one who helps. A friend suggested this tension is an old one, worked out between Schopenhauer and Nietzsche, and I would like to think through it by reading a novel rather than only in the abstract.

I am preparing a reading of Victor Hugo's Les Misérables through the Schopenhauer–Nietzsche dispute about pity. On the one side, Schopenhauer holds that compassion (Mitleid) is the sole genuine moral motive: an action has true moral worth only when it is aimed directly at another's suffering or well-being, rather than at one's own advantage or at satisfying an abstract principle. On the other side, the later Nietzsche regards compassion as deeply ambiguous: he suspects it disempowers the sufferer by turning them into an object of charity, expresses the giver's hidden sense of superiority, and, in its strongest forms, is life-denying because it sanctifies suffering rather than strength or health. I want the reading to take both positions seriously rather than deciding in advance that the novel is on one side.

The obvious starting point is the Bishop's treatment of Valjean after the theft of the silver, but I would also want sustained attention to Fantine, to Valjean's decision to reveal himself at the Champmathieu trial, to his sparing of Javert at the barricade, and to Javert's death. Where does the novel present compassion as the foundation of moral life in something close to Schopenhauer's sense, and where does it present it as Nietzschean — a subtle form of power, a kind of self-abnegation, or a force that prolongs the very unhappiness it claims to relieve? I also want the honest reading that says where the text resists the framework, and an assessment of what the novel contributes to the philosophical debate that argument alone leaves out. Please write your answer in British English.
