An essay on machine learning and epistemology includes this passage: "The no-free-lunch theorems show that every learning algorithm performs equally well on average across all possible problems, so an algorithm is only ever as good as the biases it brings to the particular problems it faces. Induction, on this view, is simply one such bias: a standing preference for smooth or simple hypotheses over baroque ones. The theorems therefore imply that induction has no more rational standing than any other bias, and that its apparent success in science is not a sign that it tracks reality but only a lucky fit between our preferred hypotheses and the problems that happen to populate our corner of the world." I have seen this kind of gloss in debates about whether machine learning vindicates or undermines induction, and it sounds plausible, but I worry it moves from a formal result about averages to a sweeping epistemological conclusion. Is this a fair summary of the no-free-lunch theorems and their bearing on the problem of induction? Please separate what is accurate, what is subtly overstated or conflated, and whether the conclusions about induction's justification would survive the corrections.
