I am a PhD student in computational cognitive science. My working vocabulary is reinforcement learning: rewards, value functions, baselines and advantages, reward shaping, policies, defaults, exploration bonuses and penalties, and the distinction between the true objective and learned or misspecified proxies. My exposure to behavioral economics is mostly secondhand, and I want to know whether a debate I keep encountering has content my framework cannot already express.

Here is the debate as I understand it. The standard picture is that people evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point rather than in absolute terms: a loss relative to that point hurts considerably more than an equal-sized gain pleases. The related endowment effect is the tendency for someone given an object to demand far more to sell it than they would have paid to buy it moments earlier, as though assignment of the status quo itself shifts valuation. The contested question is whether these patterns are biases — quirks of the laboratory or of naive subjects, to be debiased or designed around — or legitimate preferences that policy ought to respect. The skeptical side argues that much of the measured willingness-to-accept/willingness-to-pay gap is an artifact of one-shot elicitation with inexperienced subjects, who may be confused, bargaining, or anchoring, and that the gap shrinks sharply with repeated markets, training, and incentive-compatible procedures. The defense counters that reference dependence survives in attenuated form after such controls and can be reconstructed as reasonable: if losses are real changes in welfare and the reference point tracks one's legitimate expectations, weighing losses more heavily need not be a mistake.

Please do two things. First, restate the debate — the phenomena, the skeptical critique, and the rational reconstruction — as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of reinforcement learning. For example, you might treat the reference point as a baseline or value function, loss aversion as an asymmetry in the reward or advantage function, and the endowment effect as a penalty for deviating from a default or previously committed policy; use whatever mapping is most accurate rather than most flattering. Second, give me a residue report: which parts translate cleanly, which parts translate only with distortion, and which parts have no counterpart in the RL vocabulary. In particular, I want to know whether my suspicion is right that from an RL perspective the whole dispute collapses into the question whether these reference-dependent terms are part of the true reward or are shaping bonuses and penalties bolted onto it. Is that reduction faithful, or does it lose the genuinely normative question the economists are asking — which revealed preferences count as the person's own and which count as artifacts of the choice architecture? I want to know whether my vocabulary is missing something real, or whether their distinction is less well-defined than it looks.
