Some years ago I began reading the philosophical debate about forgery in the visual arts, and I have been struck by how fixed its positions have become. The two camps agree on the descriptive facts: van Meegeren's forged Vermeers passed as genuine and were admired as such; a perfect copy of a painting would present an observer with exactly the same visible properties; and when a work is exposed as a forgery, the pigment on the canvas does not change. One camp holds that none of this matters to aesthetic value, which attaches to the experience a suitably situated observer can have, so a work and its indistinguishable forgery must be aesthetically equal. The other camp holds that the exposure does matter, because aesthetic value belongs to the work as a historically situated achievement, and an object with the wrong provenance is not the same object of appreciation. The argument has continued for decades without either side moving. I suspect it is stalled in part because the term 'aesthetic value' is being asked to mark two different things, but I am not confident of that diagnosis. What kind of disagreement is this, actually: a verbal disagreement about what 'aesthetic value' means, a difference about which properties are eligible to count as aesthetic, a hidden difference about whether appreciation may be genuinely cognitive, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether value resides in the object or in the encounter with it? And on the diagnosis you find best supported by what each side says, what would progress look like: a distinction that divides the question, a pair of cases that discriminates between the sides, an empirical result about how revelation changes experience, or an argument that the question as posed is ill-posed?
