In my department the utilitarians have a move they lean on constantly: social choice should work exactly like an individual choosing among lotteries over life-prospects. Picture an impartial observer behind a veil of ignorance—they could end up as anybody in the society that results, with equal probability—and they have to pick the social arrangement. The story is that Harsanyi's theorem then forces the choice to maximize a weighted average of individual utilities, because the observer is just a rational expected-utility maximizer and each possible "person they turn out to be" is one state of nature. So interpersonal tradeoffs get licensed by the same reasoning as one person's intrapersonal tradeoffs, with no separate social or egalitarian value smuggled in. I want an audit of that analogy, not a summary of the theorem itself. Give me the correspondence table: what is the veil, the gambler, the states, the prizes, the risk attitude, the utility function, and what is doing the work. Then mark where the mapping breaks: places where the analogy quietly imports equal chances or impartiality that the theorem does not itself establish, and places where a society choosing distributions over its members is genuinely unlike a single person choosing over their own future selves. Then commit. Once the disanalogies are on the table, does the impartial-observer argument still get you utilitarian aggregation, or was the analogy carrying the whole conclusion? If you think it does survive, tell me exactly which version survives.
