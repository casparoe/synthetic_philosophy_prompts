Ok so I keep running into the propensity interpretation of fitness as the standard reply to the tautology objection. The worry, as I understand it, is that if fitness is just differential reproductive success — the ones who leave more offspring are by definition the fitter ones — then the claim that natural selection favors the fitter is empty, its just saying the ones who reproduce more reproduce more. The propensity interpretation is supposed to block this by making fitness a probabilistic disposition: an organism's expected reproductive output given its traits and its environment, so a very fit organism can get struck by lightning, leave zero offspring, and the theory doesn't collapse. Good. But before I sign off on that fix I want the list of what any adequate account of fitness actually has to do.

Write me the desiderata. What must a theory of fitness deliver — which cases it has to get right, which distinctions it has to respect, what it has to explain or at least not contradict — with the case or consideration behind each one. I specifically want you to think about things like: drift (two equally fit types that diverge just from sampling error), frequency-dependent fitness where how well a type does depends on how common it is, heterozygote advantage where organism-level and allele-level fitness pull apart, and the reference-class problem for propensities (a propensity for what, in which environment, over what timescale?). Flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preferences. Then tell me, briefly, which of the requirements the propensity interpretation actually satisfies and which it chokes on. And if any pair of the desiderata looks like it cant be jointly satisfied, say so — that's the part I most want to see. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, not on scoreboarding the propensity view.
