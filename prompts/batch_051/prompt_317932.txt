Before I read any further philosophical or theological treatments of petitionary prayer, I want the evaluative standard itself made explicit. Assume the God being addressed is the God of classical theism: omniscient, immutable, and perfectly good. Write out the desiderata that an adequate account of petitionary prayer to such a God must meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it.

Make sure the account has to address at least these pressure points:

- praying for something already settled but unknown to the petitioner, such as a loved one's plane that has already landed or an election whose result has already been determined;
- praying for another person's free change of heart or conversion;
- praying for an outcome that would require overriding an otherwise reliable natural regularity, such as a sudden healing or a change in weather;
- praying for a particular good one believes God would bring about anyway;
- petitioning for a general good ("thy will be done") versus a specific, dated outcome.

Then tell me which of the candidate requirements are genuine adequacy conditions that any coherent account must satisfy, and which are merely commitments of one theological or philosophical camp. In particular, weigh the competing constraints that petitionary prayer must have some role beyond changing the person who prays, while prayer cannot be thought of as informing God, overcoming divine reluctance, or making a perfectly good being more willing to do good. Flag any pair or subset of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain whether that tension is real or merely apparent. Finally, identify which single requirement does the most work in eliminating otherwise familiar positions.

Keep the emphasis on the criteria and their motivating cases, not on surveying named theories.
