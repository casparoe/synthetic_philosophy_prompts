Something I've been chewing on: all the standard rationalist-friendly moves for saying that a "good enough" choice is actually optimal once you look at it the right way. The ones I've got:

1. Search is costly, so it's just optimal stopping — you stop when the expected value of another draw is below the cost.
2. Deliberation itself is costly; fold effort-aversion into the utility function and the satisficing option literally maximizes.
3. The full maximization problem is computationally intractable, so the best implementable algorithm is a satisficing heuristic.
4. Under uncertainty about the distribution of unseen options, settling for a known adequate option can be Bayes-optimal.
5. There's no scalar "best" to maximize, because values are incomparable or preferences are incomplete, so "maximize" isn't well-defined.
6. Satisficing is a robust heuristic in messy environments where exact maximization overfits to your model of the world.

I keep having the nagging feeling there's a seventh move that isn't just a rephrasing, special case, or combination of these six. What is it, and what does it actually add? And if you think the six really do exhaust the space, say so flat out and tell me why I keep feeling like theres a gap.
