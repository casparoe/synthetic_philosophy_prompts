So here's a thing I keep bumping into as a novelist. When dialogue is working, a line carries way more than its literal content, and I control that load through craft: subtext, what a character wants but won't say, what the point-of-view character notices or doesn't, free indirect style, repetition, omission. I don't have a theory of this, I have working habits. A friend told me Grice had a theory of exactly this — that a hearer assumes the speaker is being cooperative and uses a handful of maxims (quality, quantity, relation, manner) to bridge from what's literally said to what's meant, with conversational implicature as the gap. I want a translation exercise, and I want it honest rather than flattering. Restate Grice's machinery from top to bottom — the cooperative principle, the maxims, the difference between what is said and what is implicated — in the vocabulary I actually use: subtext, a character's want, the load a line carries in a scene, reader inference, free indirect discourse, withheld information. Tell me which joints map cleanly, which you had to bend or strap together, and which parts of Grice have no counterpart in what I do. Then flip it around: what do I work with every day — a line that tells the reader something the speaker doesn't intend, or implicatures that accumulate into a recognizable voice across a novel — that Grice's picture has no room for? Don't just tell me subtext is implicature. I want to know wether Grice is adding something to what I already do, or wether one of these vocabularies is doing work the other can't see.
