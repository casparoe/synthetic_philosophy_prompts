I spent thirty years doing experimental physics—mostly optics—and only got into philosophy after I retired. My reading group, mostly retired scientists with a couple of humanities people, has asked me to lead a session on the old Newton/Leibniz fight about whether space is a real container-like thing or just the network of distances and relations among bodies. I've read enough to know the textbook labels are substantivalism and relationism, and that Newton's bucket and Leibniz's shifting-the-whole-universe argument are the standard exhibits. But when I try to explain the distinction it comes out sounding like a verbal dispute, and I'm pretty sure its my fault rather than the distinction's. My own instinct is to start with coordinate systems and general covariance, because that's how I first learned to think about space, but I suspect that's exactly the wrong on-ramp. I also can't shake the feeling that in modern physics the gravitational field (or any field, really) is 'stuff' in a way that muddies the clean distinction—should I bring that in, or cordon it off? Most of my group will arrive thinking physics settled this a century ago. Plan the session's arc for me: what idea has to be in place before the question even makes sense; which single example should carry the session, and what does that example need to do and not do; what misconceptions this group will walk in with or form along the way, in the order they'll appear, and where in the sequence I should head each one off; and what to ask near the end to see whether they've got the question rather than just the labels. Also tell me straight out what to leave out at this level and why.
