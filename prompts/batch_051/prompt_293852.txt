Next week I'm doing a fifty-minute discussion section in an intro philosophy of religion course, first-years, and the topic is the exclusivism / inclusivism / pluralism distinction about salvation. I've never TAed before, so I want a plan for how to teach it, not an explanation to hand them. The students are mostly 18 or 19, some religious and some actively not, and I'd bet they walk in thinking the only real question is whether my religion is right or whether every other religion has to be wrong. I want them leaving with something more precise: that these three are answers to a narrower question about the scope and basis of salvation, not three personality types or three levels of tolerance.

So sequence the session for me. What has to go first? I'm guessing I need 'salvation' treated as a placeholder for whatever a tradition takes the ultimate good to be, and I need the class to feel the difference between 'are other religions true' and 'can people outside those religions be saved' before we define anything. Tell me if that's right and what depends on it. Then what order should the three positions go in, and what does each later one lean on from the ones before it.

Give me one carrying example to thread through the whole hour. I already know I dont want the blind men and the elephant, and I'd rather not start from 'your sincere Muslim friend,' because students just sort that one into tolerant-vs-intolerant and stop thinking. Tell me what the example has to have and what features would ruin it.

Then the misconception map: which wrong readings will they bring in or form midway, and at which exact point in the sequence I should head each one off. I can already imagine a few — exclusivism is necessarily smug or cruel, inclusivism is just pluralism with a loophole for friends, pluralism means all religions are equally true or just as good — but I need to know which of these actually show up, where in the order they show up, and any I haven't thought of. Also say what I should openly refuse to settle at this level, like universalism or the details of any one tradition's afterlife, and how to decline without making them feel dismissed.

Finally, give me one question for the last five minutes that would tell me whether they understood the distinction itself rather than just learned to stick labels on things. End your reply with a one-sentence summary of the session's throughline as the very last line, formatted as \answer{...}
