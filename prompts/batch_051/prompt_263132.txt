Writing in the debate about constitutionalism, a commentator claims that entrenched constitutional provisions should be understood as Ulysses contracts: by adopting them, a polity binds its own future majorities against anticipated myopia or passion, so that when a court enforces such a provision against a present majority it is giving effect to the polity's own earlier self-command rather than overriding democracy. A critic objects that the same facts can be redescribed without any self-binding: as a credible commitment that persists because reneging is costly, as a coordination device that persists because enough actors treat the old rule as a focal point, or as the dead-hand control of an earlier generation over the present. Suppose we want to know which description is most accurate for a particular entrenched rule, for example a balanced-budget requirement, a human-rights guarantee, or a fixed presidential term limit. How could one bring evidence to bear on the claim that such a rule functions as precommitment rather than as an external constraint?
