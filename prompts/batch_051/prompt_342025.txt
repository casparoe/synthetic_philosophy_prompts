I am writing a chapter on Fitch's paradox of knowability, and the core of the chapter will be a precise specification of what counts as an adequate solution to the paradox. For context, anti-realists about truth commonly hold the knowability principle that every truth can be known: formally, p → ◇Kp, where K means "it is known by someone at some time" and ◇ expresses the relevant kind of possibility. On modest assumptions — that knowledge is factive (Kp → p) and distributes over conjunction (K(p ∧ q) → Kp ∧ Kq) — this principle appears to collapse into actual omniscience. Take any proposition p and consider p ∧ ¬Kp: the claim that p is true and is not known by anyone at any time. If this proposition were knowable, there would be a possible situation in which someone knows it; distribution would then yield Kp ∧ K¬Kp, and factivity applied to the second conjunct would yield ¬Kp, contradicting the first conjunct. So p ∧ ¬Kp cannot be known. By the knowability principle, p ∧ ¬Kp therefore cannot be true; under classical logic this is materially equivalent to p → Kp for arbitrary p, so every truth would actually be known.
