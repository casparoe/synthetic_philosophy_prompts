I've been reading the old Greek stuff about oaths, and the one I can't get out of my head is swearing on the Styx. The standard line I keep seeing is that the Styx oath is the gold standard of bindingness because the penalty is built into the act itself: perjure yourself on it and it just hits you — no trial, no witness who has to decide whether to report you, no enforcer who has to be convinced to punish you, and once you've sworn there's no one to appeal to who can waive it. The oath is automatically self-enforcing, and that's supposed to be what makes it more than a strong promise.

But lately I've been thinking about smart contracts: escrow that moves money the instant a condition isn't met, staking systems that slash your deposit automatically, kill switches nobody can pause. These are also automatic and inescapable — the penalty executes, no court, no goodwill, no judge. And yet almost no one thinks entering one creates an oath. If anything people say the opposite: a smart contract gives you a prediction about future consequences, not a reason of the promissory kind. So did the old argument about why the Styx binds secretly conflate being forced with being bound? The argument seems to need at least two premises: that automatic, inescapable enforcement is what gives an oath its force, and that the Styx has this feature in it's purest form. Smart contracts seem to share both features but feel normatively thin. So I'm trying to figure out which premise actually breaks. Does the Styx argument survive smart contracts, does it need repair (say, by adding the idea that the enforcement has to come from a witness or a person-like will rather than a mechanism), or is it dead? And if it's dead, what's left that makes an oath an oath rather than an execution schedule?
