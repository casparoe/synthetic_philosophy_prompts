I have a set of gut verdicts about trying and effort that I cannot get to hang together, and I want to know whether one principle captures them. Lying in bed while telling myself to fall asleep, and monitoring whether I have drifted off yet, keeps me awake: the direct attempt at sleep is self-defeating. Trying to suppress the thought of a white bear by checking whether the thought is still present keeps the thought alive: again self-defeating. A pianist who decides mid-recital to play effortlessly, and monitors herself to be sure she does not force the notes, plays stiffly; yet when she attends only to the shape of the melody, the effortlessness she could not aim at directly arrives anyway. At a party, checking whether I am relaxed prevents relaxation, but deliberately slowing my breathing and loosening my shoulders brings it on. When I try to become a more generous person by performing generous acts while noticing myself being generous, the acts are real but the noticing makes the generosity feel staged, and I cannot say exactly what has been corrupted. The contrast that puzzles me most is between the pianist and this generosity case: she deliberately attends to the music and the unforced state arrives, while I deliberately attend to my own generosity and the unforced quality is spoiled. The case I keep going back and forth on is the one that prompted all of this: someone sets out to acquire the Daoist ideal of wu-wei, roughly acting well without deliberative forcing, through years of deliberate, effortful practice, and eventually acts in that easy, unforced way. Is the initial project self-defeating in the way the insomnia and white-bear cases are, or is it more like the pianist's training, which makes the unforced state available later? I half think the project works and half think the very idea of setting out to be effortless is incoherent. Please find the principle, if there is one, that distinguishes attempts that undermine the state they aim at from attempts that enable it, or tell me which of my verdicts have to go. State the principle precisely, tie each clause to the cases that motivate it, apply it to my undecided case and to at least one case I have not listed, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Answer in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the principle, (2) the clause-to-case mapping, (3) its verdict on my undecided case and on one unlisted case, (4) which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and (5) the main rival principle and what it would overturn.
