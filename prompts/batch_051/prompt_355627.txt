Okay, so here's the thing I keep going back and forth on. My housemate insists that physicalism is a substantive empirical claim — that "everything is physical" is the sort of thing we have evidence for and could get evidence against, not just a metaphysical stance. I keep hitting him with the obvious problem: what does "physical" even mean here? If it means "whatever current physics says exists," then physicalism is almost certainly false, since current physics is full of known holes (no unified story for QM and gravity, dark matter and dark energy unaccounted for, and so on). If it means "whatever the final, completed physics says exists," then the claim is just "everything is whatever the final physics says exists," which is empty — the final physics gets defined as whatever ends up accounting for everything. First horn: false. Second horn: trivial. Either way it look like there's no room for it to be an empirical thesis.

But sometimes I think I'm dodging, and that there has to be a way to make the claim testable enough to be worth arguing about. So I want you to actually design the test. Anchor it in the hardest case: phenomenal consciousness — the raw feel of being in pain or seeing red, the fact that there's something it is like for you. Concretely, what observation or measurement would you accept as evidence for or against the claim that phenomenal consciousness is physical? Say what would count as a positive result and what would count as a negative one, and most importantly how you're operationalizing "physical" for the test without just defining the problem away. Then tell me what the test would and wouldn't settle: after the results are in, what part of the physicalist claim is still untouched, and is the untouched part the part that actually mattered?
