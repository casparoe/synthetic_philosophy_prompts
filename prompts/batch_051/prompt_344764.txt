As a novelist I keep circling back to The Merchant of Venice because it seems to be doing something weirder than just "mercy good, revenge bad." In the second essay of On the Genealogy of Morality Nietzsche floats the idea that guilt and punishment started out as a creditor-debtor relation: the creditor could get repayment by making the debtor suffer, and that raw pleasure in someone else's pain later got moralized into conscience and justice. I'm wondering if the play is a sharper version of that story than the philosophy is. Could you read Merchant through that lens for me? Specifically: is Shylock's demand for the pound of flesh an outbreak of that old pre-moral, get-repaid-through-pain logic inside a Christian legal order, or has it already been moralized into revenge and the revenge just gets called justice by the other side? And whose guilt actually gets settled when the Duke pardons Shylock but confiscates his estate and forces his conversion—is that the community inventing its own good conscience by making him the debtor? I want to know where the play dramatizes Nietzsche's genealogy, where it complicates or undercuts it, and whether Shakespeare ends up giving a more honest account of how groups use economic force and call it morality than the Genealogy does. As a novelist I'm trying to write a scene where a community's kindness turns out to be a form of collection on an old debt, so I dont need a bibliography, just a real reading that takes both the play and the second essay seriously.
