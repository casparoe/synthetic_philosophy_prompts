I've been reading Albert Camus's "Reflections on the Guillotine" for a class on punishment, and a friend forwarded me the blurb a magazine ran next to an excerpt. The blurb says: Camus's essay is basically a deterrence argument—execution doesn't deter any better than life imprisonment, and states keep it only out of an atavistic taste for retribution. Its big rhetorical move, the blurb says, is the famous line about capital punishment being the most premeditated of murders, which Camus uses to show that the state commits exactly the cruelty it condemns. And his conclusion, the blurb claims, is that if ordinary citizens had to watch an execution, their stomachs would override their principles, so opposition to capital punishment is really just a prediction about what we'd feel if we looked.

But this passage from early in the essay seems to be doing something different—something about language, euphemism, and thoughtlessness rather than just deterrence or squeamishness:

"France shares with England and Spain the honor of being one of the last countries this side of the iron curtain to keep capital punishment in its arsenal of repression. The survival of such a primitive rite has been made possible among us only by the thoughtlessness or ignorance of the public, which reacts only with the ceremonial phrases that have been drilled into it. When the imagination sleeps, words are emptied of their meaning: a deaf population absent-mindedly registers the condemnation of a man. But if people are shown the machine, made to touch the wood and steel and to hear the sound of a head falling, then public imagination, suddenly awakened, will repudiate both the vocabulary and the penalty."

I can't tell whether the blurb is a fair compression or whether its quietly recasting the argument. Go through it claim by claim against this passage and the essay's actual line of argument: what's right, what conflates two things Camus keeps apart, and whether the gloss that it's psychological rather than moral is one Camus would accept. Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion, and devote the fifth point to that final gloss.
