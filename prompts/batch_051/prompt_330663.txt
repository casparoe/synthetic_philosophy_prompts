Question: "Sometimes people say that even if the probability that insects are sentient is low, the sheer number of insects gives us strong moral reason to care about their welfare. Explain this 'scale argument' and critically assess one main weakness of it."

Student's answer (reproduced verbatum):

"The core of the argument is an expected-value move. If there are roughly 10^18 insects alive at any time, then even a small probability p of sentience gives an expected number of sentient individuals of p × 10^18. With p = 0.01 that's 10^16 expected sentient lives, which dwarfs the human population, so expected insect suffering might swamp expected human suffering. The argument doesn't require us to know insects are sentient; the uncertainty just gets folded into p. The main weakness is that p is radically uncertain, so the conclusion depends on a number we can't estimate; but I think this cuts both ways, since uncertainty could also mean p is much larger than we thought. Overall, as long as p isn't zero, the expected number of sentient insects is so enormous that we should prioritize insect welfare over human welfare."
