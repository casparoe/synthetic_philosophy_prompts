I've been working through the first division of Being and Time alongside a poetry workshop, and it strikes me that Heidegger's distinction between readiness-to-hand and present-at-hand is better enacted than explained. I'd like you to write a fourteen-line poem in the shape of a sonnet, with an octave in strict iambic pentameter and a firm rhyme scheme (ABBAABBA will do), whose volta occurs exactly when a piece of equipment fails. The octave should render absorbed, unselfconscious work — hammering, planing, whatever seems right — so that the tool recedes into its use and the surrounding gear (the nails, the bench, the half-finished project) stays quietly in the background as a referential whole rather than as an inventory of objects. The verse itself should be smooth and transparent in the way a reliable tool is; the reader should be able to move through it without noticing its machinery. Then, at line nine, let the tool break, go missing, or refuse: the hammerhead loosens, the chisel binds, the work stalls. And let the form break with it. The meter should stumble; a rhyme should fail or turn conspicuously slant; the syntax should clog, so that the reader suddenly notices the machinery of the poem the way the worker suddenly notices the tool.

The sestet should then render the same object as present-at-hand: a bare thing with weight, grain, temperature, and edges, stripped of its embeddedness in the task, while the broken prosody keeps calling attention to itself instead of serving the sense. Do not name the philosophy, and do not editorialize; the formal rupture has to carry the thought. A reader who knows the distinction should recognize it enacted rather than merely described, and a reader who doesn't should still feel, in the movement from octave to sestet, the difference between a tool that disappears into the hand and a tool that has turned into an obstacle.
