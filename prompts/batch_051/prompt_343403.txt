I've been going back and forth on Kepler's Tertius Interveniens (1610), the pamphlet where he tries to hold a middle position between the people who wanted astrology banned outright and the people who accepted every last bit of it. There's one passage I keep returning to, from the Ken Negus translation, where he's explaining why even the clear-headed side shouldn't just discard the whole thing:

"It should not be considered unbelievable that one can retrieve useful knowledge and sacred relics from astrological folly and godlessness. From this filthy mud one can glean even an occasional escargot, oysters or an eel for one's nutrition; in this enormous heap of worm-castings there are silk-worms to be found; and, finally, out of this foul-smelling dung-heap a diligent hen can scratch up an occasional grain-seed -- indeed, even a pearl or a gold nugget."

If I take that at face value, the position is: most of astrological practice is superstition and bad technique, but buried inside it are a few genuine principles—about celestial configurations, rays, and sublunary nature—worth rescuing, and the only way to find them is to sift through the junk rather then reject or defend astrology wholesale. I know that's not how people usually frame astrology now; that's kind of the point.

Act as a philosopher of science who works on early modern natural philosophy. Before you organize anything, brainstorm the different kinds of defeaters for that position—empirical, conceptual, historical. Then give me a map of what would count against it: which results or arguments would be decisive, which would only be awkward, and what a thoughtful defender of Kepler's salvage program would themselves admit counts against it. End with an honest verdict on how exposed the position is, and specifically whether the sifting method insulates it from refutation (every failed prediction can be blamed on the "superstitious" side of the line), and whether that's a bug or a feature.
