I'm putting together a final exam for an upper-level epistemology seminar and I keep going back and forth with a colleague about it. The topic is the rationalism/empiricism fight over innate knowledge, and I don't want another question that just asks them to summarize Locke versus Leibniz, because half the class can rattle that off without understanding the actual issue. In discussion last week a student made a point I thought was sharper than most of the secondary literature: she said Locke's universal-assent argument mostly refutes a view nobody should hold, the view that whole propositions are sitting in the mind fully formed from birth and consciously available, and that he doesn't touch the more interesting rationalist idea that the mind has structures or concepts ready to go, which experience merely triggers but does not supply, the way veins in a block of marble determine what a sculptor can release from it. I want the exam question to force engagement with exactly that move and its best empiricist come-back, not just label-dropping.

So please write the exam question itself, then a model answer, then a marking scheme. It's a 72-hour take-home for upper-level undergrads, 1,200 to 1,500 words, 100 marks. The question should ask candidates to (a) rebuild the strongest version of the student's argument, (b) give the best empiricist rejoinder, and (c) say whether the dispute is substantive or partly verbal and what would settle the leftover parts. The model answer should distinguish innate as occurrent knowledge, as disposition, and as cognitive structure; show that the rationalist needs more than the point that experience merely triggers things; give the empiricist a reply that accepts hardwired learning machinery while denying it supplies concept content or justification; and end with an actual verdict. In the marking scheme, list the specific moves a top answer must make and the specific mistakes that should loose marks, for instance conflating innate concepts with innate propositions, treating hardwired mechanisms as if they automatically carried justification, or concluding it's all just a verbal dispute without saying what isn't verbal. No part of the question should ask for citations, page numbers, or outside readings; marks should come from reasoning with the course material.
