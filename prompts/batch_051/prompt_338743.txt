For my school's philosophy club we've been doing a unit on the imagery debate, Kosslyn vs. Pylyshyn, and for once I actually have opinions instead of just "both sides make good points." I'm supposed to come in Monday with a one-pager on where I've landed, and the older kids in the group are going to grill me, so I want to check it before I write it out. Here's what I'd put down:

1. I'm with Kosslyn: mental images are depictive, meaning the image itself has spatial features that matter for how quickly or easily you can do things with it.
2. The mental rotation results (people take longer to judge rotated shapes when the angle is bigger, in a pretty linear way) look like strong evidence for that.
3. I also think Pylyshyn's tacit knowledge objection is right: subjects can read the experiment's demands and produce exactly those reaction-time patterns without any actual spatial image, because they know they're supposed to be "rotating" something and adjust how long they take.
4. I think seeing and using imagery involve the same underlying representational format, basically the same kind of code.
5. I don't think there's a literal picture in the head or an inner homunculus watching a screen; that version of depictivism seems like an obvious strawman everyone should reject.
6. I think the format question matters, because the mind really is doing stuff over spatial layout and not just running descriptions of it.

I honestly can't tell where the load-bearing problem is, or whether these all fit together. Which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like it? Order the tensions by how bad they are and say what the cheapest fix would be for each — in particular, which of these I'd probaly have to give up or water down most.
