I have been developing a view about the Meno problem — the question why knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief, given that both appear to guide action equally well — and I would welcome a candid, and above all committed, assessment of it.

Let me first state the problem as I understand it, then the proposal. In the Meno, Socrates concedes that a true belief about the road to Larissa will get you there no less reliably than knowledge will, and more generally that true belief and knowledge have the same practical success record. The puzzle is to explain why we nonetheless treat knowledge as a distinct achievement worth more than true belief. I find the usual answers unsatisfying. An appeal to stability seems ad hoc, since a belief can be stable without being true. An appeal to justification or reliability seems to invite the swamping objection: if the point of reliability is to make true belief likely, then once the belief is true, the reliability has delivered everything it had to offer, and it is hard to see what further value it contributes.

My proposal is what I call the license view of epistemic value. The distinctive value of knowledge, I claim, is not that it gets you anywhere that true belief does not, and not that it makes the belief more useful to its possessor in private reasoning. Its extra value consists in a change in what one is entitled to do with the belief. A true belief, however confidently held, entitles its holder to act on it; but only knowledge entitles its holder to do any of the following: assert the proposition flatly, without hedging or marking the claim as testimony; use it as a premise in reasoning meant to persuade rather than merely to coordinate action; close inquiry into the matter; and teach the proposition — that is, transmit it to someone who aims not merely to believe truly but to know. To say that knowledge is better than true belief, on this view, is to say that knowledge confers a distinctive package of licences to perform certain social and deliberative acts that true belief alone never confers.

The view yields quick treatment of familiar cases. A tourist who guesses the way to the station and happens to be right may act on the guess — there is nothing improper in following it. But I say he may not flatly assert to another tourist that the station is that way; may not treat the matter as settled once he has arrived; and cannot teach the route, but only pass along a helpful guess. A local who knows the way may do all these things. The whole of the extra value of knowledge, I propose, is this: not a private epistemic good added to true belief, but a public normative status that opens up a class of acts otherwise unavailable without impropriety.

I recognize that this may sound like a version of the view that knowledge is the norm of assertion, or a cousin of knowledge-first approaches. But I intend something different. I am not claiming that one should assert only what one knows — a norm on a speech act. I am claiming that what we are valuing when we value knowledge over true belief just is the standing to perform these acts without impropriety. The direction of explanation runs from the norms governing social epistemic practice to the value of knowledge, rather than the reverse.

I would like you to give a committed assessment. Tell me, taking a definite position, whether the view is coherent; whether it collapses into something already recognized in the literature or is genuinely distinct; what it gets right; and where it breaks. Give me what you take to be the two or three strongest objections, and say whether any of them is fatal or merely uncomfortable. If the view fails, say exactly how you would revise or abandon it.
