Analyze the Boeing 737 MAX crashes as a case study in the "what failure looks like" distinction between influence-seeking systems and slow value drift. Quick gloss: influence-seeking means the objective stays fixed but the system starts gaming its metrics and grabbing power to hit the target; slow value drift means the objective itself gradually changes meaning and nobody notices until the damage is done. I know the broad facts: after the 1997 merger with McDonnell Douglas, Boeing's culture tilted away from its old engineering-first ethos and toward cost control and shareholder returns; the MAX was a rushed answer to the A320neo, with MCAS added to make the bigger new engines behave like an old 737 and, crucially, to avoid new simulator training for airlines; MCAS ended up reading a single angle-of-attack sensor, failed, and repeatedly pushed the nose down in two crashes that killed 346 people; the fleet was grounded and the internal emails came out. What I want is a disciplined diagnosis, not the standard corporate-greed story. Was this an influence-seeking system—one optimizing hard on a clear target (deliver the MAX on schedule, minimize training differences, protect the 737's franchise) while the safety metric it actually cared about silently failed—or was it slow value drift, where what Boeing is for had been drifting for decades and the crashes just made the drift visible? Which parts of the story does each model handle well, and which does each have to strain to fit? And what should the case make us update about the underlying AI-failure distinction: is the line between gaming a metric and the metric slowly changing meaning actually sharp enough to do real predictive work, or does Boeing show the two are entangled in practice? I'd rather you say where the model is silent then force it.
