I keep arguing with a friend about money pumps and diachronic rationality — he's convinced the whole problem is a product rather then a principle. Standard background, just so we're on the same page: if your preferences cycle or flip over time, a trader can sell you A for B plus a small fee, then B for C plus a small fee, then C for A plus a small fee, and you end up with A again but poorer. The standard line is that this shows preferences have to be transitive and stable, or at least that intransitivity is exploitable.

His proposal is a service — working name Hardened — that inverts the argument by letting other people profit from your inconsistency so that you have an incentive to stay consistent. You deposit money in escrow and keep a time-stamped "preference ledger" in the app: not what you wish you wanted, just pairwise tradeoffs you actually accept (caffeine vs sleep, job offers, whatever domain you pick). The ledger is visible to registered wardens, who pay a small subscription. If a warden finds a cycle or a reversal in your entries, the smart contract executes the cycle against your escrow at the recorded prices plus a fee: the warden keeps the pump profit minus a cut, and you're back where you started, minus the fees. You can append entries but never delete them, and you choose whether your ledger covers a narrow domain or something scary broad.

I think this is absurd, but I can't quite say how it would actually fail, so I want you to treat it as a mechanism rather than a moral position and walk me through it. Assume it launches and catches on with a certain rationalist-adjacent crowd. What does it incentivize among users, wardens, and the company? Where does it get gamed first — fake entries to bait wardens, reverse-baiting, refusing to log, engineered domains, something else? Does it make people more diachronically rational, or just make them hide or launder their changes of mind? And can the worst failure modes be patched without just turning it into an ordinary Ulysses contract?
