I've been trying to get clear on what Hilbert's hotel actually assumes, and I suspect the usual version smuggles in an actually infinite operation without saying so. Here's the variation I want to work through. The hotel is the usual one: rooms 1, 2, 3, ..., all occupied, and one new guest arrives. But now the manager is bound by a finitist rule: at any one time he may choose finitely many rooms and rearrange their current occupants amoung those same rooms (no doubling up), and he may do this over and over, even forever, but each single operation touches only finitely many guests. (i) Show carefully that no finite sequence of these operations ever leaves a room empty. (ii) Say exactly which step of the usual 'everyone move up one room' argument is unavailable under this rule. (iii) Now the limit version: call a schedule an infinite sequence of these finite operations applied cumulatively, and ask, for each original guest n, whether their room number converges. Is there a schedule such that, in the limit, guest n ends in room n+1 for every n? If so, describe one; if not, say what blocks it. (iv) On that schedule, does the new guest ever have a room on some particular day, or only 'at the limit'? And does the fact that (i) and (iii) point in different directions show that Hilbert's hotel really needs actual infinity, or does it show that the finitist objection is just impatience? I want the reasoning, not just a verdict.
