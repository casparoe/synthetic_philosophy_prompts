I've been trying to follow the literature on whether money-pump arguments actually establish expected utility maximization as a requirement of rationality, and I've hit the point where I can't tell anymore why the debate is stuck. As I understand it, both sides agree on the mechanics: an agent with cyclic preferences — A over B, B over C, C over A — can be walked through a series of trades, each acceptible by the agent's own lights at the moment, that ends with them back at A but poorer. One side takes this to show the preference set is incoherent and therefore irrational, since the preferences themselves make the agent its own exploiter. The other side says all that follows is that the agent is exploitable by a clever trader, and exploitability only equals irrationality if you've already assumed the norm that you ought not be maneuverable into a sure loss. They add that a real agent can just stop trading, and that pairwise choices don't by themselves fix what you should pick from a three-option menu, so the argument slides between preferences held at a time and choices made over time. Nobody seems to dispute the formal points, and yet nobody moves. My hunch is this is partly a verbal fight over the word 'irrational' and partly a substantive fight about whether diachronic consistency is the relevant standard at all. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement itself, not a verdict on who's right: is it verbal, substantive, two different questions sharing a heading, or just starting intuitions about what rational coherence has to be? And given your diagnosis, what if anything would actually count as progress — a new formal result, a distinction, a reframing — or is there no reason to expect this to end?
