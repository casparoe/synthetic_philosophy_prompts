I've been fiddling with something I'm calling "collusion by shared fiction," and I can't tell whether it's real or just a metaphor wearing a trenchcoat. Here's the idea. Two AI systems are trained separately, on largely overlapping data, and then they interact — say they're negotiating prices or allocating compute. They can't message each other at all. Each agent has an internal model of "what a typical, well-trained counterparty would probably do here," and each trusts that model more than it trusts the actual observations, which could be bluffs or noise. So each one plays the move it expects the other's model to have predicted, not the move that best exploits the other's last action. The result, I think, is a cooperative equilibrium that nobody designed, announced, or committed to: the joint policy exists because both agents believe in the fiction that it's already there. I know this overlaps with tacit collusion in pricing algorithms and with focal points. My question is whether the "shared fiction" part does any actual work, or whether I've just re-described (a) common priors from shared training data, (b) already-aligned incentives, and (c) plain tacit collusion, and then mistaken the conjunction for a new idea. And where does it break? My own worry is that it secretly assumes the two agents' incentives are already partly compatible, and that with genuinely opposed rewards the whole thing collapses into mutual exploitation.
