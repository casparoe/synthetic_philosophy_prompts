I'm taking a seminar on moral responsibility and free will, and the instructor has asked each of us to write down our current commitments about responsibility, blame, and the reactive attitudes so the group can test whether the set hangs together. Before I present mine, I'd like another pass at the audit. Here is the package I've ended up with:

(1) I'm a compatibilist about responsibility: determinism, if it is true, would not by itself make people less appropriate targets of praise and blame, and requiring libertarian freedom or ultimate self-creation demands more than any ordinary practice of responsibility requires. (2) I'm persuaded by P. F. Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes. Resentment, gratitude, and guilt are not conclusions we reach after evaluating a theory; they belong to the participant stance, the basic framework in which we take each other seriously as agents and hold one another to account. (3) Because of that, I don't think the practice of holding people responsible needs an external justification—say, in terms of its social utility—before we are entitled to engage in it, and the demand to justify the practice from outside it misunderstands the role the practice plays in our lives. (4) Nevertheless, when I blame someone for a particular wrong, I think the point of the blame is largely communicative and forward-looking: to make the person recognize what he or she has done, to express the moral demand, and to prompt repair or improvement. If blame could do none of that, it would strike me as pointless retaliation. (5) I think particular excuses track quality of will: we excuse an action when it does not reflect the agent's settled evaluative stance—through genuine factual ignorance, accident, or coercion—but we do not excuse merely because a passing impulse was hard to control, so long as the action was still the agent's own. (6) I also think the objective stance—treating someone as a person to be managed, predicted, or treated rather than reasoned with as a participant—is sometimes the right stance, and for children and for people with certain severe psychopathologies it is appropriate as a standing stance rather than a temporary retreat. For example, if someone with severe psychopathy harms another for amusement, I am inclined to regard the person as dangerous and in need of management or treatment, not as an appropriate target of the kind of resentment I would feel toward an ordinary wrongdoer. (7) Taking the objective stance can still be humane: it does not require coldness, cruelty, or indifference to a person's welfare; it just suspends the reactive attitudes.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which are only apparently so. Order the genuine tensions by severity, say for each what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost, and then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set coherent. I would rather have an audit than a defense of the package. Please write your answer in American English.
