Set at the end of a workshop on mental representation, after a talk that presented the mental-rotation and image-scanning results as if they were decisive. Write a dialogue between two cognitive scientists who disagree about the format of mental imagery. One defends a depictive account: mental images are supported by representations that encode spatial properties such as distance, size, and orientation in the representational format itself, rather than merely describing them. The other defends a propositional account: imagery is underwritten by structured, language-like descriptions whose content may be spatial without the format itself being spatial.

The dialogue should be rigorous and even-handed — neither character may serve as a strawman, and each must respond directly to the other's actual claims rather than changing the subject. Let them work through the classic findings that the depictive side treats as evidence: the roughly linear relation between angular disparity and reaction time in mental rotation, and the relation between scanning time and distance in imagery tasks. The propositionalist should press the objection that such response-time gradients may be produced by participants' tacit knowledge of how a physical object or a real picture would behave, so the findings do not uniquely pick out a depictive format; the depictivist must answer this rather than dismiss it. In return, the depictivist should press the question of what explanatory advantage a purely propositional account gains, and what it would have to add to explain the apparent spatial character of imagery. By the end, the two should face the deeper question of whether their disagreement is empirically decidable at all — what observation would force a concession from either side — and each should have been driven to refine or qualify their position at least once. The dialogue should end without an artificial resolution.
