I've been working through Augustine's City of God, and I hit a passage in Book XVIII, Chapter 43, on the authority of the Septuagint, that I can't fully parse. Here is the passage:

"For the same Spirit who was in the prophets when they spoke these things was also in the seventy men when they translated them, so that assuredly they could also say something else, just as if the prophet himself had said both, because it would be the same Spirit who said both; and could say the same thing differently, so that, although the words were not the same, yet the same meaning should shine forth to those of good understanding; and could omit or add something, so that even by this it might be shown that there was in that work not human bondage, which the translator owed to the words, but rather divine power, which filled and ruled the mind of the translator."

I've tried to steelman the inference and keep failing. The part I get stuck on is the move from the translators' freedom to depart from the Hebrew to the conclusion that their work is divinely inspired. Normally, a translator who adds, omits, or says something other than the source is making an error, or at best paraphrasing. Augustine seems to run the inference in the opposite direction: the very fact that the seventy were not bound to the words is supposed to show divine power rather than human weakness. Why would that be? And when he says the same Spirit could say "something else" through the translators, as if the prophet himself had said both, is he committed to the Greek text containing new content that was never in the Hebrew? If so, what is left of the Hebrew's priority, and why call the Septuagint a translation at all? I'd like help reconstructing the assumptions about meaning, language, and inspiration that would make this argument coherent, and an honest verdict on whether it actually works.
