I spent most of my working life calibrating instruments in an earth-science lab, so I’m much more at home with calibration curves, error budgets and drift corrections than with exegesis. Lately I’ve been reading Spinoza’s A Theologico-Political Treatise, and this bit from early in Chapter VI (Elwes translation) stopped me:

“The masses then style unusual phenomena, ‘miracles,’ and partly from piety, partly for the sake of opposing the students of science, prefer to remain in ignorance of natural causes, and only to hear of those things which they know least, and consequently admire most.”

I know that passage is not directly about interpretation, but it seems to me it sets up his whole naturalistic stance. Later in the book he argues that Scripture should be interpreted from itself alone, without importing meanings from outside. What I’d like is for you to restate Spinoza’s sola scriptura method in the vocabulary of my old trade: calibration standards, traceability, systematic versus random error, noise, drift, reference instruments, and the problem of measuring an instrument’s bias without an external standard. Treat the text as an instrument whose readings we’re trying to turn into reliable data. Then give me the residue report: which parts of his position translate cleanly, which parts you had to fudge, and which parts have no counterpart in calibration talk at all. I’m especially interested in whether the regress problem—who decides what the text means—really is like trying to calibrate a standard against itself, or whether the analogy hides something important about interpretive authority.
