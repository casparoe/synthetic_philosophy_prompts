Camus held that the most urgent question philosophy can ask is whether life is worth living, and that an honest answer must begin from the absurd: the standoff between the human demand for significance and the universe's refusal to supply it. On his account, the right response is not to dissolve that standoff by appealing to a cosmic guarantee but to live inside it — with lucidity, revolt, and the conviction that a life without cosmic meaning can still be lived in full consciousness. In his essay on Sisyphus, he presents Sisyphus, lucid about the futility of his labor, as the fitting model of this response: not a figure to be pitied, but one to be imagined as happy in his task.

In the decades after him, an empirical and philosophical literature has developed that treats meaning in life as an ordinary, measurable component of a good life. A standard model divides it into three components: coherence (life is experienced as intelligible and narratively organized), purpose (life is organized around valued goals), and significance (what one does matters beyond oneself). The model is deliberately neutral about whether the universe as a whole has meaning; meaning in life is studied as a property of individual lives, measured by self-report, and treated as something a life can have in greater or lesser degree. Researchers find that belonging, feeling that one matters to others, and telling a redemptive story about one's suffering predict higher meaning, while a pleasant but aimless life counts as meaning-deficient.

Suppose Camus could examine this program. Would he regard it as a belated empirical confirmation of his own account — a first-person description of revolt, lucidity, and engagement — or as a disciplined evasion of the absurd, one that replaces the question whether life is worth living with the question how well a person scores on a questionnaire? More pointedly: by the tripartite model, would Sisyphus — lucid, without hope, fully engaged in a task he knows is futile in the cosmic scheme — come out high or low in meaning? And could Camus consistently hold that the model is right about Sisyphus while denying that this vindicates the model's picture? Reconstruct Camus's likely position from his stated commitments, and argue the case.
