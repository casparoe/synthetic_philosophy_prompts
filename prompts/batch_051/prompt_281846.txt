A friend of mine works in city government and another one is a political theory grad student, and at dinner last week they got into an argument about a mayor who keeps telling crowds that crime is at an all-time high even after the city's own stats where published showing a decline. When a reporter pressed him, he said it's true because that's what people feel. The city-government friend said this is just normal campaign spin; politicians have always fudged numbers, and Arendt herself said lying is as old as politics. The grad student said no, Arendt's whole point was that there are two different things going on: the old political lie hides one particular fact, but the modern one tries to make the line between fact and opinion stop mattering, and "it's true because people feel it" is squarely the second kind. The first friend shot back that if a single false crime stat counts as an attack on factuality, then every fib in politics is an attack on reality, and the concept loses all its teeth. The grad student said the inflation actually runs the other way: people are so used to saying "all politicians lie" that they can't see the difference between hiding a fact and getting people to treat facts as just another opinion. The first friend said but the fact hasn't gone anywhere—the statistics are public, reporters are checking them, so what exactly has been abolished? Then they asked me to settle it, and I'd rather get a real verdict then just pick a side myself.

Can you go through their exchange and adjudicate it more or less point by point: who's right about which specific claims, whether the Arendtian distinction between hiding a fact and undermining the fact/opinion distinction can carry the weight the grad student put on it, and what, if anything, both of them are missing? Answer in American English, please.
