I keep flip-flopping on the rational herding stuff, and I want someone to talk me through the actual cost of each move rather than just pick a side.

The theory seems, like, airtight if you accept its setup. Suppose a line of people all have to guess which of two restaurants is good tonight, and each gets an independent private signal that's only a little better than chance. They see the choices of everyone ahead of them but not the signals. If the first two both happen to get the same wrong signal and pick A, the third person, seeing A A and a private signal for B, does a Bayesian update and rationally picks A—the public evidence from two prior choices outweighs one private signal. Then the next person sees A A A and faces the same arithmetic. Once that happens every later action is uninformative, the public belief freezes, and a whole crowd of individually-reasonable people can march into the worse restaurant. I find that argument genuinely compelling. It predicts a real phenomenon—cascades, fads, bursts of conformity—and it does so from a minimal set of assumptions about individual rationality.

And yet I don't believe it. Not the restaurants, but the lesson. I can't shake the intuition that a bunch of reasonable people with genuinely independent information shouldn't be that easy to herd off a cliff. If each of them really has a signal with some truth to it, the group ought to be able to do better than the second or third person's mistake, and a theory that says alot of extra information just gets ignored because actions are so cheap seems to be leaving something out. Is my intuition tracking a real flaw in the model, or is it an artifact of how I'm imagining the case? What would it take for the intuition to be the thing I give up, what would it take for the theory to get amended or abandoned, and is there a reading on which both survive? Don't just tell me which side wins. Oh, and answer in American English.
