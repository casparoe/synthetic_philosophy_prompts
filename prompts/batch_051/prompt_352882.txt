You are a political philosopher who specializes in distributive justice and the concept of desert, writing for an educated non-specialist readership. I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been collecting cases in which ordinary desert-talk and institutional reward come apart. Here is the asymmetry that most bothers me. Confronted with two students who submit equally good essays, one written over several painstaking weeks and one tossed off by a student with a natural gift for prose, most people will praise the first student as deserving and hesitate to say the second deserves the grade at all, since the gift simply happened to her. But the institutions that actually distribute goods — schools, universities, employers, labor markets — are built to ignore exactly that distinction: the two students receive the same grade, the same credential, and eventually the same pay for the same demonstrated output, and none of our formal reward systems is designed to detect, let alone differentially reward, the effort behind identical performance. Ordinary praise behaves as if effort were the true desert-basis, while the machinery of distribution behaves as if realized performance were, even when the performance is driven by native capacities nobody earned. I am not sure whether this split is a justified division of labor or an inconsistency we simply live with.

A passage in Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics sharpens the puzzle for me. In H. Rackham's translation: "This is also clear from the principle of 'assignment by desert.' All are agreed that justice in distributions must be based on desert of some sort, although they do not all mean the same sort of desert; democrats make the criterion free birth; those of oligarchical sympathies wealth, or in other cases birth; upholders of aristocracy make it virtue." Aristotle there treats the criterion of desert as politically contested, and my own two-track practice looks like a version of exactly that contest: a personal morality of effort sitting alongside an institutional settlement of output. Is there a principled reason to keep two standards, for example because praise is the right currency for effort while markets rightly track output for reasons of information and incentive? Or is one side the mistake — either our praise is sentimental, or our institutions reward what people merely happen to be? If you defend keeping the asymmetry, defend it against the limiting case: two employees in identical roles produce indistinguishable work at indistinguishable quality, but one finishes in an afternoon through a rare inborn ability while the other needs all week, and their pay and promotion track only what was delivered, so the difference in effort is invisible and uncompensated. Please think through the strongest justifications on both sides before you take a stand, and say clearly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up. Write in American English.
