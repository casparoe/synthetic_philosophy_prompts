I've recently been put on the disciplinary panel for a smallish professional body I belong to, and there's a case in front of us that I keep changing my mind about. The relevant bit of our code of conduct hasn't been revised since 1994 and reads:

"A member who makes an allegation of misconduct against a fellow member must do so in good faith. An allegation made maliciously, or made primarily to injure a colleague's reputation, is itself professional misconduct."

The facts, roughly. A woman in our field spent two years quietly collecting documents showing that a senior and well-liked colleague had been altering audit data to hide repeated errors. She then posted a long account of it on an anonymous account on a well-known industry forum — using his initials and enough identifying detail that people in the field clearly knew who she meant, without naming the organisation — and afterwards handed the same documents to our body. The documents are genuine, and several other people have since said they'd raised the same concerns informally years ago and got nowhere. So the underlying misconduct is real.

Here's the wrinkle. After she filed the formal complaint, she sent a message to a friend, and that message has ended up in front of the panel: "I won't pretend my motives were pure — I've wanted that man gone for years, ever since he humiliated me in front of a client and cost me a promotion, and I knew this would finish him." She has'nt denied writing it.

So the question before us is whether she's breached the good-faith clause. Walk me through what the plain words of the clause say, what the purpose behind it is likely meant to protect, what the people who drafted it in 1994 would probably have said about a pseudonymous forum post a quarter of a century later, and what a fair-minded member reading the code in good faith would make of it. Then tell me which of those approaches ought to control here and why. I'd like the answer in American English, and I'd rather you not just say it's complicated — I need something I can defend when the panel votes.
