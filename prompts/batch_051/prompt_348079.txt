I'm a clinical psychologist on an inpatient psychiatric ward and this comes up almost every week: a patient makes some bizarre claim, and the team starts arguing about whether the patient really believes it or is just saying it. Some of the nurses want to reality-test everything; others say dont challenge it, because its not a normal belief in the first place. I know the rough shape of the two-factor theory of delusions — one factor generates the strange experience or content, and a second factor is a broken belief-evaluation system that fails to reject it — and I know theres a philosophical debate about whether delusions are full beliefs, partial beliefs, imaginings, or more like avowals. I dont have a way to use any of this on a Tuesday afternoon.

So: write me a reusable intake procedure for classifying a single delusional report. This isnt idle semantics — the formulation drives medication decisions, whether someone gets CBT for psychosis, and sometimes whether they get leave, and the attending psychiatrist signs off on what I write. The procedure should take the patient's statement plus whatever I can observe and get from records in 20–30 minutes (no imaging, no experimental tasks), and output one of four verdicts: "full belief", "partial/unstable belief (double-bookkeeping)", "non-doxastic avowal" (the person says it but its more like an expressed image or empty speech act), or "stop — probably not a delusion; evaluate as a cultural/religious/subcultural belief instead." The verdict should determine what I do next, not just label the patient. The procedure should say which questions I have to answer before moving on, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a "no," and where it hands the decision to a human because the question is one of values or clinical judgment rather than something the theory settles. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about where the two-factor theory itself is thinnest — it fits neat single-idea delusions much better than sprawling paranoid systems, and the second factor is usually inferred rather than directly observed.

Give me the procedure itself as a JSON object. Schema: "steps" is an array of objects with fields "step" (integer), "question_or_observation" (string), "what_counts_as_sufficient_answer" (string), "principle_operationalized" (one of "factor_1", "factor_2", "belief_criterion", "cultural_exemption"), "if_yes_go_to" (integer or null), "if_no_go_to" (integer or null), and "judgment_call" (boolean). Also include "stop_rules" (array of objects with "condition" and "action") and "verdicts" (array of objects with "verdict", "operational_definition", and "recommended_next_step").

Then run the procedure on three cases and give me the outputs as a JSON array of objects with fields: "case_id", "presentation" (two or three sentences), "factor_1_hypothesis", "factor_2_evidence", "verdict", "recommended_action", and "where_procedure_misfires_or_hesitates". The cases: (1) a man who insists his wife has been replaced by an identical impostor, but still lives with her, makes her coffee, and seems more puzzled than terrified; (2) a woman with years of persecutory delusions about the FBI who has barricaded the door, won't eat food she didn't prepare, and calls me a collaborator; (3) a man who says he is being punished by a voodoo curse and that his family and church members confirm it. Include at least one place where the procedure gives an answer the team will not like, and finish by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do — about detention, competence, diagnosis, or whether the underlying philosophical debate is actually settled.
