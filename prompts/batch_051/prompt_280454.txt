I'm teaching a survey course that runs from roughly the fifth to the seventeenth century, and I've noticed I'm running a double standard about period labels that I can't quite defend. My official rule is that labels like "the Middle Ages" and "the Renaissance" are fine as library furniture — useful for shelf marks, course titles, and pointing students toward a century — but they're not allowed to do explanatory work. If I catch myself writing that something is "a characteristically medieval attitude" or that some shift "happened because of the Renaissance," I stop and rewrite the sentence in terms of specific institutions, networks, arguments, or dates. I think of this as intellectual hygiene: the labels are later ways of carving up the past, not causes in the past.

But I break my own rule all the time for "the Scientific Revolution." I plan a week around it, talk about a before and an after, catch myself saying that after it knowledge-claims had a different status, and treat it as a real boundary in a way I'd never let a student get away with for "the Renaissance." So I'm wondering: is my two-tier policy actually defensible, or is the exception just a bias I've built a rule around? Is there a version of the "labels are for finding, not explaining" rule that can survive the Scientific Revolution exception, or would the honest version be to drop all big period terms — and would that cost me anything as a teacher beyond more then convenience?
