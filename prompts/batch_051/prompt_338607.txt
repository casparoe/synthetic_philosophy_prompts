I have been reading about whether historical writing can be objective, and I keep running into the same three positions without any sense that the argument moves forward. As I understand them: Ranke held that the historian's task is to reconstruct the past as it actually was, without judging the past or enlisting it for present causes; if the sources are handled carefully, the past can simply be shown. Carr rejected this as naive, since every historian has to select which facts count as facts and which questions are worth asking, so the result is always a product of the historian's present standpoint — a dialogue between past and present, not a passive mirror. Hayden White takes the scepticism further by focusing on what happens after the facts are selected: the historian has to arrange them into a story, and the available story forms — tragedy, comedy, romance, satire — are literary structures that give the events a shape and meaning they do not have on their own.

I am not an academic, but I have been trying to figure out whether this is a substantive disagreement or mostly a confusion about the word objectivity. My working hypothesis is that the three of them are talking about different stages of historical work: Ranke is setting an ideal for source criticism, Carr is describing how a work gets framed by a researcher, White is describing how the final narrative is composed. If that is right, the argument seems stalled because one word, objectivity, is being asked to cover source handling, selection, and storytelling at once, and each side can win by choosing which stage matters most.

Is my diagnosis correct? If it is not, what kind of disagreement is this really — a hidden factual question, a difference about standards of evidence, a verbal ambiguity, or a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? And on your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here, or is progress the wrong thing to expect from this debate?
