My friends and I have been arguing for years about whether visitors have to follow the little unwritten rules of a place — tipping in the US, queueing in Britain, not jaywalking in Germany, that sort of thing. One side says when in Rome you do as the Romans do: just by visiting you sign on to the local conventions whether you like them or not, and breaking them is wrong in the same way breaking a promise is. The other side says thats way too strong. Those rules belong to the people who live there and keep them up; a visitor didn't grow up in the norm and never actually agreed to anything, so the most you can say is it would be polite or smart to go along, not that its a moral obligation. Every time we argue, we end up at the same walls. Is a convention a kind of implicit contract? Do tourists have to tip in places where the workers' wages depend on tips, even if tipping itself isnt really the local custom? We havent moved an inch in years.

My own hunch is that we're lumping together different kinds of unwritten rules. Jaywalking has actual safety behind it. Queueing is about fairness and coordination. Tipping in the US is kind of a wage system disguised as politeness. So maybe the debate feels stuck because we keep using the word obligation for all three, when they arent all the same kind of "should." But I'm not sure; maybe we really do just disagree about what makes any local rule binding on a person at all.
