I am a novelist, and for some time I have been working on an essay about coincidence in narrative: how much chance a story can carry before a reader stops believing it, and whether that stopping point is really a judgment about probability or about something else. I have been asked to state my settled position as a set of explicit commitments so that I can present it to the craft seminar I teach, and before I circulate it I would like a consistency check from someone who will not simply reassure me. Here is what I find myself maintaining, set down as five theses.

(1) A striking coincidence is often far more probable than it feels, because the event in question has an enormous number of opportunities to occur; I fully accept the standard birthday-paradox point that a coincidence specified only after the fact need not have been at all improbable beforehand. (2) Even so, I think a reader can be rationally justified in distrusting a novel or screenplay that turns on such a coincidence, even when the coincidence is, in the abstract, statistically unsurprising, because what is suspect is the author's decision to make the story hinge on it rather than the improbability of the event as such. (3) I also think first-person reports of uncanny coincidences are generally poor evidence about how the world works, since the report records a hit without recording the reference class that made the hit notable: the denominator of opportunities, near-misses, and banal non-events from which the teller selected it. (4) Yet I believe memoir and autobiographical fiction may use real coincidences that I would not accept in invented plots, because the reader's awareness that an event actually happened alters what the narrative is asking to be believed. (5) Finally, I hold the familiar craft principle that coincidence may set a plot in motion but should not resolve it, while also suspecting that this principle pushes fiction away from the way life actually proceeds; my own life contains chance resolutions that I would strike from a novel as cheap.

I would welcome an audit rather than a lecture. Please go through these commitments and tell me which ones genuinely pull against one another, ordered by how serious the tension is, and in each case say what would have to be revised or surrendered and what the cheapest repair would be. Then identify any pair that merely appears to conflict and explain why the conflict dissolves on closer inspection. Finally, tell me which single revision would leave the whole position most coherent, and what lingering discomfort it would leave in the essay's larger argument about when a story is allowed to be lucky.
