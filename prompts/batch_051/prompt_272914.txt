I have a departmental talk in political theory in three weeks and I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a rework of the paper. The view I'm defending is a qualified Walzerian one: that there are genuine dirty-hands cases where a political leader ought to do what ordinary interpersonal morality forbids (my running example is authorizing a raid to rescue hostages when the only route will very likely kill a child the captors have placed as a human shield), that the act remains in some real sense wrong even though it is the thing to do, and that the appropriate residue is not mere regret but a kind of guilt the leader is right to feel and wrong to treat as a private matter. The piece I expect to get shot at is that last part: the guilt requirement, not the existence of the dilemma itself.

The audience is a mixed political theory and international ethics colloquium, and it's the second half that worries me. There is a Kantian who will read the whole thing as a back door for exactly the kind of exception that, in her view, is how torture always gets rationalized; a hard act-utilitarian who thinks the guilt talk is moral theater because if the raid really is the best act, then feeling guilty about it is at best trauma and at worst confusion; a couple of Machiavellian-minded realists who will say I'm dressing up ordinary power politics in moral language and making leaders either weak or liars; and a historical-institutional person who will say the dirty-hands vocabulary has mostly been used to launder state violence, so my neat version is a distinction without a difference.

My argument runs something like: establish the dilemma case, insist that the ordinary prohibition doesn't simply vanish when state necessity enters, conclude that political wrongdoing is its own category rather than a failed test of ordinary morality, and then defend the guilt residue as the marker that the agent still tracks the prohibition instead of editing it away after the fact. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way each person talks (the Kantian says categorical, not deontological), which ones have real force, what I should concede up front and what I should hold, a short reply to each and where that reply is weakest, the one reply that has to land for the guilt requirement to survive, and the objection I should be praying nobody thinks to ask. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning; no preamble and don't restate the question.
