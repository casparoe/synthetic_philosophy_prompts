I run a small moral philosophy reading group and we've been stuck for months on the "should the numbers count" debate — Taurek's argument that if you can only save one person or five strangers, you aren't automatically required to save the five. His line was that the five don't combine into a single five-times-worse loss, because there's no subject who suffers all five deaths; each of the five has a claim as strong as the one person's, but claims don't add up. So he thought the respectful thing is to give each an equal chance, like flipping a coin. The people who defend aggregation have spent fory years trying to say why that's wrong without going full utilitarian: Parfit's stuff about how anti-aggregation leads to absurd conclusions, Kamm's pairwise comparison and balancing, Scanlon's contractualist justification for saving the greater number. We all know these moves.

The stall, as I see it, is that nobody in the group denies the facts of the cases or even the claim that each person matters equally. The split is between those who find "save the five" just obviously right — so obviously that Taurek's position reads as a reductio of itself — and those who find "equal concern requires a coin flip" just obviously right. And neither side can say much in favor of their starting point that isn't already the conclusion. The people on my side (I'm Taurek-ish) keep pointing out that aggregating the five treats them as a group rather than as individuals; the other side points out that we aggregate across time within one person's life without blinking, so refusing to aggregate across persons looks like special pleading.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A verbal dispute about what "worse" means? A clash of bedrock intuitions about the separateness of persons? A hidden formal question about whether you can derive aggregation from premises about individual claims? My hypothesis is that it's a bedrock intuition clash and that's why the literature has chewed on it for decades without convergence — but I'd like someone to check that diagnosis: is that right, and if so is there any real move that could count as progress, or is the disagreement just in the foundations where argument runs out? If my hypothesis is wrong, what's the better diagnosis and what would actually move things?
