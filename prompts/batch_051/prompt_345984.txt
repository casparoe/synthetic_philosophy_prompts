For a course on the ethics and economics of healthcare rationing, I am trying to reconstruct the theory of value implicit in quality-adjusted life year, or QALY-based, priority-setting. The practice I have in mind is the one used by health agencies that decide which treatments a public system funds on cost-effectiveness grounds. A year of life at full health receives a quality weight of 1, a year in a state rated 0.5 counts as half a QALY, and worse-than-dead states receive negative weights. Within a single patient's life, expected QALYs are computed by multiplying each state's quality weight by the time spent in it and summing over states. Across patients and interventions, expected QALY gains are added together and divided by cost, and treatments are ranked by cost per QALY gained, with those above some threshold normally denied funding.

What I want to know is what this practice presupposes about how values combine, and where it is merely an operating convention rather than a substantive commitment. Does the framework strictly presuppose that a year at quality 0.5 is exactly half as valuable as a year at quality 1.0 — in other words, that quality and duration combine multiplicatively within a life? Does it strictly presuppose that QALY gains are additive across persons, so that a large gain to one patient is exactly offset by small losses to many? Are those two commitments consistent? Could one keep the threshold-and-ranking machinery while adding prioritarian or sufficientarian weighting of QALY gains, or a lexical rule that life-saving always outranks quality-of-life improvement, and still be running the same kind of practice, or would that amount to abandoning its implicit theory of value? I am especially interested in what the funding practice would look like if the aggregation rule were made consistently multiplicative rather than additive across persons — for example, if the aggregate value of a set of QALY gains were their product, so that a fixed total gain scored higher when distributed more equally — or made consistently lexical, so that certain kinds of benefit would never trade off against others. Finally, the same systems sometimes depart from the threshold to fund expensive care for identifiable patients who would otherwise die, even when the cost per QALY is far above what is normally accepted. Should I read that as evidence that the practice never really presupposed additive maximization, or as a political exception layered on top of a coherent additive theory that the rest of the system continues to rely on?
