I've been reading the opening of Leviathan and got stuck on this bit, which I used to just skim:

"Nature (the art whereby God hath made and governes the world) is by the art of man, as in many other things, so in this also imitated, that it can make an Artificial Animal. For seeing life is but a motion of Limbs, the begining whereof is in some principall part within; why may we not say, that all Automata (Engines that move themselves by springs and wheeles as doth a watch) have an artificiall life? For what is the Heart, but a Spring; and the Nerves, but so many Strings; and the Joynts, but so many Wheeles, giving motion to the whole Body, such as was intended by the Artificer? Art goes yet further, imitating that Rationall and most excellent worke of Nature, Man."

What struck me is that Hobbes calls a watch an artificial life because it moves itself, but he also says it has an Artificer. Then a commonwealth is an artificial man, also with an Artificer. Fine. But it got me thinking about the sort of things people now describe as Moloch or multipolar traps, where lots of individual choices build something that then seems to run on its own, and there's no Artificer anywhere. I have these cases and my gut reactions, and I want to know if there's one principle behind them.

Here are the cases:

1. Open-plan office headphones. The office gets noisy, so people wear noise-cancelling headphones. Then people talk less, so the office gets quieter in a dead way, and more people wear headphones just to signal they're focusing. Everyone hates it, but nobody can stop wearing them without being distracted. Verdict: feels like a trap, but not a deep one; its more like an equilibrium nobody intended.

2. High school extracurricular arms race. Students do more and more clubs, sports, and "passion projects" because everyone else does. Each student cant unilaterally stop, since colleges compare them to peers. The result is anxious, sleep-deprived kids. Verdict: definitely a trap, and I blame the system, not the students or even the colleges individually, though collectively they caused it.

3. Bodybuilding with anabolic steroids. If one competitor uses, others must to keep up. The end state is bodybuilders who are all on drugs, with health risks, but the ranking is roughly the same as if no one used. Verdict: feels like a trap, but I also want to say the competitors still made a choice; they're not mere gears in a watch. Is that just me being moralistic?

4. Airline predatory pricing. One airline drops prices below cost to drive a rival out, the rival matches, both bleed, one exits, and the survivor raises prices. Verdict: feels like a race to the bottom, but it's not the same as the others because there's an identifiable strategist trying to kill a competitor. I don't think "no Artificer" is true here.

5. Social media outrage bait. Creators make more and more extreme content because moderate content gets less engagement. The result is a feed nobody actually likes, including the creators. Verdict: trap, but I blame the platform's algorithm more than the creators, so maybe there is an Artificer after all.

6. Standing at a concert. One person stands to see better, then the people behind stand, then everyone stands. The view doesn't improve for anyone, but standing becomes normal. Verdict: I don't think this is a trap at all, or if it is, it's harmless and not worth calling Moloch. Why does it feel different from the office headphone case?

My provisional principle is something like: X is a blind artificial machine (and a real race to the bottom) if each individual's move is locally rational given what others are doing, the resulting state is worse for everyone than some alternative, and no one intended the whole pattern. But that principle says the concert case is a trap and the predatory pricing case is not, and I'm not sure I believe both of those. Can you extract a better principle that gets my verdicts, or tell me which of my verdicts has to go? Also, what would your principle say about a case I haven't settled: a large city where everyone with a rent-stabilized apartment hangs onto it even when they'd otherwise move, causing young families to be locked out, but no individual landlord or tenant can fix it?

Oh, and please answer in American English.
