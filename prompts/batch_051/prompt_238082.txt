The question was: Lay out the core of the Lucas–Penrose argument that Gödel's first incompleteness theorem refutes mechanism, then identify and assess what you take to be the strongest objection. The rubric rewards accurate reconstruction of the argument and real critical engagement, so I'm more interested in comments at that level than in style points. A student wrote the following (I've just fixed up some typos and formatting, but the content is untouched):

"Lucas and Penrose argue that no consistent formal system can capture the mathematical abilities of a human mind. Take any candidate machine whose theorem-proving is given by a formal system F strong enough for elementary arithmetic. Since F is consistent, Gödel's first incompleteness theorem gives a sentence G_F which, roughly, says 'G_F is not provable in F.' The theorem tells us G_F is unprovable in F; but that is exactly what G_F says; so we can see that G_F is true. The machine, bound by F, cannot prove G_F. So the human mind can see a truth that the machine cannot. The main objection is that maybe F is inconsistent. But an inconsistent system proves everything and would not be a plausible candidate for a mind, so the argument only needs to consider consistent systems. Someone might object that we don't know our own minds are consistent, but that's irrelevant: we only need the consistency of F, not our own. Lucas's argument therefore shows that membership in any fixed, consistent formal system is not sufficient to capture human mathematical insight, and mechanism is false."
