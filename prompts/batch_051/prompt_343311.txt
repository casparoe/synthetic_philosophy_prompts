I recently retired from a career in the natural sciences and have been reading my way into the philosophy of law. In Maine’s Ancient Law, the chapter on legal fictions stakes out a position I keep coming back to: if I follow him, the legal fiction allows substantive legal change to occur while preserving the appearance of continuity with old rules—it quietly alters the law under cover of not altering it. Modern debate about the ontology of legal fictions seems to have stalled around the same examples. One side holds that a fiction is just a false proposition that officials agree to treat as true for institutional convenience, and that it is therefore eliminable in principle. The other side holds that fictions reveal something constitutive about law, because the mode of existence of a legal norm is being treated as valid, and the fiction differs from ordinary legal norms only in degree, not in kind. My working hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement about law at all but about what kind of fact could make a statement of legal reality true: one side is presupposing a naturalized or deflationary ontology, while the other is doing something closer to institutional ontology. Is that the right diagnosis of the impasse? If it is, is the underlying disagreement verbal, conceptual, or something harder to classify, and what would count as progress? If the diagnosis is wrong, what is the hidden crux that keeps the two sides from moving?
