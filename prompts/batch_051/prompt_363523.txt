I have been preparing a short unit on money for a group of non-economists, and I keep discovering that the textbook story and the recent behavior of central banks sit uneasily side by side. The classic argument says that when a central bank prints money, the result is inflation: more money chasing an unchanged supply of goods and services bids up the price level, and in the long run money is neutral, so the printing press cannot create real wealth. That argument seems to presuppose that newly created money enters the stream of spending, passes through bank lending, and eventually finances purchases of goods and services.

Yet under quantitative easing, central banks created vast quantities of reserves and paid interest on them, and much of that new money never financed ordinary spending at all. It sat on bank balance sheets and made itself felt mainly in financial markets: higher asset prices, narrower credit spreads, greater ease of buying and selling without fire sales. This looks like a changed setting for the old argument, in which the new money functions less as a medium of exchange for goods and services and more as a liquid asset on balance sheets. I would like to know whether the classic argument that printing money creates inflation survives this development, needs repair, or is simply aimed at the wrong object. Which premise does the change actually put pressure on, and is there a repaired version that still carries force? If the repaired version goes through, what precisely has been created: liquidity, paper wealth, real wealth, or something else? I am not asking for policy recommendations or an empirical forecast; I want the conceptual structure.

Please structure the answer as follows: (1) The argument restated, with the premises it needs to succeed, around 200 words; (2) What the shift to interest-bearing reserves and large-scale central bank balance sheets changes, and which premise it bears on, around 350 words; (3) The case that the argument survives in repaired form, around 300 words; (4) The case that it must be replaced rather than repaired, around 300 words; (5) Your own verdict on what printing money creates and whether any of it counts as wealth in a real sense, around 300 words.
