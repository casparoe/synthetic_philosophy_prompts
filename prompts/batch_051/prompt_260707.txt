I have been trying to work out what, if anything, quantum-level indeterminacy contributes to free will, and I find that my verdicts on particular cases are more stable than any principle I can state. I would like to know what principle, if any, captures my verdicts, or which of them I should revise. Here are the cases as I see them.

1. Ana deliberates for a week between two job offers, weighs the considerations, and chooses the one better by reflectively endorsed standards she already holds. Suppose the whole process is deterministic: the same total physical state always leads to the same choice, and a perfect predictor could have said in advance what she would choose. I still think Ana chooses freely and is responsible for the choice.

2. Boris sincerely cannot decide whether to report a colleague's minor misconduct or remain silent. He has reasons on both sides and is genuinely torn, so he lets a Geiger counter settle it: if the atom decays in the next ten seconds, he reports; otherwise he stays silent. I do not think the resulting decision is itself a free decision, even if his decision to use the device was free.

3. Cleo's brain is full of objective chance at the microphysical level. Chancy events toss up candidate urges, images, and framings, but a stable, reasons-responsive control system then accepts, rejects, or revises them with high reliability. I think Cleo can act freely even though there is real chance inside the causal chain.

4. Daniel is caused by a specific brain lesion to spend hours checking that the stove is off. He hates this, judges it pointless, tries to stop, and cannot. The causal route is deterministic. I do not think these checks are free actions or ones he is responsible for.

5. Eve's intrusive movements are caused exactly as Daniel's are, except that a genuinely random neural event triggers each episode. I hold, as with Daniel, that she does not act freely.

6. Consider two people whose actions flow from settled dispositions. Gita's disposition to keep a painful promise comes from values she has examined and endorsed; Hanna's disposition comes from a post-hypnotic suggestion planted by someone else, and it would not survive her ordinary critical reflection. I think Gita acts freely and Hanna does not, even though both actions are deterministic and both dispositions can be traced to causes outside the agent.

The pair that worries me most is 2 and 3. In both cases an objectively chancy event lies in the causal history of the action. The apparent difference is that Cleo's control system filters the chancy output while Boris merely goes along with the device. But I cannot say what filtering amounts to such that it blocks the inference from Cleo's case to a Boris who deliberately adopts the policy of letting chance break ties. I would like the principle to force an answer here rather than merely record my discomfort.

What principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures these six verdicts? State it precisely enough to be applied, and say which case motivates each clause. Then apply it to two cases I have not listed: (a) Ines, who is genuinely torn between telling a hurtful truth and lying to spare someone, and whose choice is ultimately settled by a genuinely random neural event, after which she acts without regret; and (b) a deterministic counterpart of Cleo whose candidate-impulse generator runs on pseudo-random noise rather than true quantum chance but is otherwise identical. Finally, tell me honestly which of my six verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether a simpler principle that overturns one or two of them is better than a complicated one that saves all six.
