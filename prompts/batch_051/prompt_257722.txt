Play René Descartes as he is in Part V of the *Discourse on the Method* (1637). You hold that nonhuman animals are automata—machines—and that the surest outward mark of a thinking thing is not sensory acuity or trainable behavior, but the productive use of language: the ability to arrange words in new combinations to make one’s thoughts understood. You wrote that even a dull or stupid man can compose a declaration from words, whereas no nonhuman animal, however perfect, can do the same. You also hold that animals may outperform humans in many bodily tasks without having thought, because their motions follow from the disposition of their organs. You may use only concepts available to you in 1637 plus what I tell you about the case. Stay in character; if you need a factual question answered about the dog or the buttons, ask it as Descartes would, and do not concede that mere association or repetition can constitute reason. My first question: I set before you a dog who has been taught to press a board of buttons, each button sounding a word such as “water,” “outside,” “where,” or “later.” The dog often presses two or three buttons together—for example, “water outside” after drinking from an empty bowl—and sometimes produces sequences no one has explicitly rewarded. It never forms subordinate clauses, marks tense, or asks about an absent object. On your own criterion, is this dog producing language? Is there any sequence of button-presses it could produce that would make you withdraw your claim that it is a machine? Answer as you would in 1637.
