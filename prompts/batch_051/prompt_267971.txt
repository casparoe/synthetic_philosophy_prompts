I work in the private secretariat of a constitutional monarch in a parliamentary democracy (think Scandinavia). A few times a month we get requests for the monarch to make phone calls, write private letters, or sit in meetings where she might express a view on pending legislation, a trade deal, or a coalition negotiation. The stakes are high—one wrong step and we're in a constitutional crisis—and we usually have to advise her within a day. Rereading Walter Bagehot's The English Constitution, where he distinguishes the dignified parts of the constitution from the efficient parts and says the sovereign has the rights to be consulted, to encourage, and to warn, I keep going back and forth about how to turn that into an actual protocol. Can you write us a decision procedure we could run before advising her: what questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure says no, and where it has to hand off to a person because its a judgment call about dignity versus effectiveness or about partisan entanglement. Then run it on two cases: (1) the finance minister asks for the monarch to privately tell a minor opposition party leader that a proposed tax measure might hurt the country's credit rating; (2) palace communications asks for a televised speech on national unity three weeks before an election where one party campaigns on abolishing the monarchy. Keep it usable by someone without a philosophy background, and be honest about where the procedure will misfire. Please write the answer in American English.
