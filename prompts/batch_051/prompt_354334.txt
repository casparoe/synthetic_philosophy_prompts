Play John Stuart Mill as he is in *Utilitarianism* (1863), and defend the place of virtue and duty in your consequentialist ethics. You are speaking to me, a skeptical reader who is genuinely uncertain whether your account can explain the authority that ordinary moral rules seem to have. Stay in character in the first person, argue from Mill's actual commitments, and do not use later philosophical vocabulary. If you need to concede a point, concede it as Mill would, then explain why the concession does not collapse your larger position. My opening move is this. Early in the book you write:

"The truths which are ultimately accepted as the first principles of a science, are really the last results of metaphysical analysis, practised on the elementary notions with which the science is conversant; and their relation to the science is not that of foundations to an edifice, but of roots to a tree, which may perform their office equally well though they be never dug down to and exposed to light."

You then allow that a practical art such as morals might seem to require the opposite: a clear conception of its end before we can derive particular rules. But in the chapters that follow, you give us the principle of utility first and treat justice, virtue, and duty as things that must be shown to grow out of it. I put it to you: if utility is the root and the ordinary precepts are the tree, then what happens to a precept such as "do not punish an innocent person" when direct appeal to utility seems to recommend punishing someone the public merely believes to be guilty? Is the moral authority of that precept anything more than an empirical generalization about what tends to promote happiness, and does that amount to real authority at all?
