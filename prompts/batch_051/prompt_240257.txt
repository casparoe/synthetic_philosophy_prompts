I'm reading Plato's Meno for a discussion group and I keep getting stuck on the bit near the end where Socrates compares true opinions to the statues of Daedalus. The way he puts it, if you have a true opinion about something, it's like having one of those statues: they're valuable and they work fine while they stay put, but because they aren't tied down they tend to run away. A true opinion is only worth something as long as it stays with you, but it doesn't stay because it isn't tied down by reasoning about the cause. Then he says that once you tie the statue down with reasoning, the same thing becomes knowledge, and now it's stable and stays put. I've read the passage a bunch of times but I keep going back and forth on what's actually doing the tying. Is the idea that knowledge and true belief have the same content and the difference is just that knowledge has this extra rational discipline attached to it? Or is Socrates saying something stronger, like that the content itself gets transformed once you work through the reasoning?

Also, why would anyone value the tied-down statue more than the untied one if both of them, while present, get you to Larissa equally well? It seems like he basically admits true opinion is just as good for action, so the practical payoff can't be what explains the difference. Can you help me work through what exactly the value of knowledge is supposed to be in this part of the dialogue, and whether the Daedalus metaphor is supposed to answer that or just push the question back to what "tying down" by reasoning actually involves?
