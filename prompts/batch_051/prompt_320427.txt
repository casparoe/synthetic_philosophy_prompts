I've been treating my own preferences like a legacy codebase and trying to reverse-engineer the decision rule they actually run on, and I keep hitting contradictions around risky choices. The rule I thought I had — I think people call it the independence axiom — was something like: if two options share a common chunk with the same probability, that shared chunk shouldn't matter, and I should compare only what differs. My gut keeps overriding it in a consistent direction, so either the rule is wrong or my gut is, and I want help figuring out which.

Here are my verdicts.

Case 1. A is a guaranteed $1,000,000. B gives a 10% chance of $5,000,000, an 89% chance of $1,000,000, and a 1% chance of nothing. I pick A without hesitating.

Case 2. C gives an 11% chance of $1,000,000 and nothing otherwise. D gives a 10% chance of $5,000,000 and nothing otherwise. I pick D. I know that going from A and B to C and D is supposed to be the same shared-chunk swap, but my gut doesnt care.

Case 3. E is a guaranteed $100. F gives a 10% chance of $500, an 89% chance of $100, and a 1% chance of nothing. I pick E.

Case 4. G gives an 11% chance of $100 and nothing otherwise. H gives a 10% chance of $500 and nothing otherwise. I pick H. So the pattern survives when the amounts are small.

Case 5. I is a guaranteed $1,000,000. J gives a 10% chance of $10,000,000, an 89% chance of $1,000,000, and a 1% chance of nothing. Here I pick J, and I feel good about it. The only thing that changed from Case 1 is the size of the uncertain prize, and it flips me from the sure thing to the gamble.

Case 6. K is a guaranteed $1,000,000. L gives a 10% chance of $5,000,000, an 88% chance of $1,000,000, and a 2% chance of nothing. I pick K even more strongly than in Case 1. But in the pair where neither option is sure, M gives a 12% chance of $1,000,000 and nothing otherwise, N gives a 10% chance of $5,000,000 and nothing otherwise, and I pick N. So a bigger chance of nothing makes me cling to the sure thing when there is one, but it doesn't put me off in the pair where neither option is sure.

I want to know whether a single principle can capture all of these, including my flip in Case 5. If the principle is something like: I put special weight on certainty, but a big enough uncertain upside can override that — can that be made precise enough to actually make predictions? What would it predict for the same structure with losses instead of gains, say a certain loss of $1,000,000 versus a 10% chance of losing $5,000,000, an 89% chance of losing $1,000,000, and a 1% chance of breaking even? And which of my verdicts would the principle have to reject as a mistake?
