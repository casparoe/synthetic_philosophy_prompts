You are a professional epistemologist who works on the epistemology of testimony, and you have been asked for a precise, structured analysis of a philosophical passage and a contemporary case. Think carefully and reason from first principles; before giving your final answer, spend time brainstorming the different interpretive approaches to the passage, the different features of the case that might matter, and how the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate about testimony bears on it. Then present a clear, organized diagnosis.

In An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, section X, part I ("Of Miracles"), David Hume writes:

"It will be sufficient to observe, that our assurance in any argument of this kind is derived from no other principle than our observation of the veracity of human testimony, and of the usual conformity of facts to the reports of witnesses. It being a general maxim, that no objects have any discoverable connexion together, and that all the inferences, which we can draw from one to another, are founded merely on our experience of their constant and regular conjunction; it is evident, that we ought not to make an exception to this maxim in favour of human testimony, whose connexion with any event seems, in itself, as little necessary as any other."

Hume is here offering what later epistemology calls a reductionist account of testimonial belief: trust in what people say is justified only insofar as it reduces to inductive evidence, drawn from our experience of a regular conformity between reports and facts. Anti-reductionists, by contrast, hold that there is a basic, default entitlement to believe what people say, one that is not assembled out of inductive inference.

Now consider a case Hume could not have had in mind. A junior policy analyst is drafting a report and, in addition to consulting the sources she has been trained to use, asks a large language model a series of factual and interpretive questions. The model answers fluently and confidently, with dates, proper names, and causal claims, sometimes true and sometimes false. The analyst knows that the model is not a person, has no perceptual contact with the events it describes, aims at nothing when it produces text, feels no shame when it is wrong, and stands in no practices of promising or being accountable. She spot-checks a sample of its claims and finds most, but not all, correct; on the strength of that, and of the model's general fluency, she goes on to rely on many of its unchecked claims in the report.

The question is how Hume's account, as stated in the passage, applies to this reliance. Work through the standard interpretive approaches: the plain words of the passage, the purpose for which Hume advances it, what Hume would most plausibly have said if asked, and what a reasonable reader now takes him to be committed to. On each approach, does the analyst's trust in the model fall under the passage, and is her justification the kind of justification the passage describes? Then step back and diagnose what kind of attitude this is: is it testimony, ordinary inductive inference to the reliability of a tool, or something that sits uneasily between the reductionist and anti-reductionist theories of testimony, both of which were built for human speakers? End by saying what the case reveals about the strengths and limits of a reductionism of the sort Hume states here, and what would count as progress in transferring the epistemology of testimony to machine-generated text.
