In the digression on the corn trade in Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations, Smith observes that the inland corn merchant — the dealer who buys grain when it is plentiful and cheap and sells it when it is scarce and dear — is regarded by the public with a hatred that Smith likens to old superstitions about witchcraft, even though Smith's own analysis concludes that such dealers perform an essential service by evening out the supply of grain. A merchant who imports corn from abroad to relieve a shortage is received quite differently, with gratitude rather than odium, although the two are supplying the same need. The same asymmetry survives in contemporary judgements: if someone buys up a stock of generators before a hurricane and resells them at a steep mark-up when the storm hits, they are called a price gouger, while someone who trucks in generators from outside the affected area and sells them at the same mark-up is more likely to be called a lifesaver. If the only difference is that the first bought up existing local supply in advance while the second added new supply, does anything justify treating the two so differently, or is the distinction a kind of superstition about causing scarcity rather than relieving it? Give me the strongest principle you can find that vindicates the asymmetry, test it against nearby cases (for example, a merchant who buys a stock of epinephrine auto-injectors and raises the price during a shortage), and then say honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
