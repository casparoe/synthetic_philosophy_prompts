A philosophy of religion seminar I attended made an argument that seems to prove too much, and I want to know where it goes wrong. Reconstructed, it runs:

(1) If a text is inspired, then its being inspired must attach either to its wording or to its content.

(2) If it attaches to wording, then no translation can be inspired, because a translation deliberately replaces the wording and preserves only what the words express. On this horn, calling a translation 'inspired Scripture' mistakes the object of the predicate.

(3) If it attaches to content, then any text that has the same content is inspired in exactly the same way.

(4) But content-preservation does not single out translations. A literal translation, a dynamic-equivalence translation, a scholarly paraphrase, and a careful sermon restating the passage can all preserve what the passage says.

(5) So if a translation is inspired in virtue of its content, then those looser restatements must also be inspired.

(6) That consequence is unacceptable: a children's retelling or a commentator's gloss of a passage is not thereby a revealed text. So the content horn overgenerates.

(7) One might reply that the translation is inspired because it is the authorized or received version of the community. But that either smuggles wording back in and returns us to (2), or makes inspiration a sociological property of being accepted rather than a property the text itself has. Neither seems to be what the relevant traditions mean.

(8) Therefore no text can be both a translation and inspired.

And yet the conclusion is too strong. Some ancient Jewish and Christian circles treated the Greek Septuagint as Scripture, and some later English-speaking Christians have held that a particular English translation, the King James Version, is itself the inspired word of God. Whatever else one thinks of these positions, their proponents did not take themselves to be making a category mistake. I would like to keep the thought that at least one translation can be inspired without thereby making every faithful restatement inspired. Which step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? I suspect the wording/content dichotomy in (1) is too coarse, but I do not know what the third alternative would be, or whether rejecting (1) by itself would be enough to block the slide from (3) to (6). Show me where the argument first goes wrong, and say what the least costly repair is.
