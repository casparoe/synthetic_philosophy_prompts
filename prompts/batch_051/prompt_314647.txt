I have been going back and forth between two things I believe, and I want help deciding whether — and in what sense — the numbers should count. On one side is an outlook I find almost irresistible in ordinary decisions. If I am the only one who can prevent one death or five deaths, and I have no special relation to any of the six, I should save the five. Each of the five matters as much as the one; death is equally bad for whoever dies; and choosing the five makes the outcome best by the only standard that treats everyone alike. The axioms doing the work are individually obvious: every person's life carries equal weight, more of the same kind of badness is worse than less, and one should choose the option with the better expected outcome for all affected. The view also has a tidy decision procedure — it runs on simple addition — and it does not flinch at scale: if the choice is between one and a hundred, the train of reasoning is identical and yields an answer at once. I notice how much I rely on this reasoning in policy, triage, and everyday risk.

On the other side is the Taurek-style rescue case and the intuition it sharpens. Suppose six people need a scarce drug and I have enough for five or for one. The one person is not an abstraction: he has a life of his own, plans, people who love him, and a way of mattering to himself that is not one-fifth of the five. If I unhesitatingly give the drug to the five, I treat his claim as having been outnumbered out of existence — as though multiplying the claims on the other side made his own claim vanish rather than leaving it unanswered. The firm intuition is that he is owed something like a real chance — a coin toss, or at least a serious hearing of his claim — and not merely the experience of being the smaller number. The deepest version of the intuition is not that five deaths are exactly as bad as one. It is that there is no entity for whom the five deaths are one combined loss: each death happens to one person, and the one's death is just as final for him as any of the others' is for them. So the real question is whether the five harms may be added together at all when deciding against him.

The two sides seem to pull in opposite directions, and I want a negotiated verdict rather than a one-sided brief. Tell me: can I keep the additive logic for evaluating which outcome is better while refusing to let it settle the decision procedure, or do the two levels have to answer to each other? Is there a principled way of counting the numbers that still gives each claim its due — weighted lotteries, thresholds, or some halfway structure — and what breaks in the limiting cases when you try to build one? Or should I instead debunk the Taurek intuition as scope insensitivity, status-quo bias, or plain deontic squeamishness — and if so, explain exactly what a debunking of it would need to show, so that this move is more than refusing to take an uncooperative intuition seriously. I care less about which side wins than about understanding what each side would have to surrender for a stable reconciliation.
