This one's been bugging me for a while. Ive always liked the Peircean picture of inquiry: you have to start somewhere, with a big mass of already-settled belief, but no belief is supposed to be closed off from revision forever. A colleague gave me this argument that those two commitments are actually inconsistent:

(1) You can't test all your beliefs at once. To test any one belief, you have to lean on background assumptions — that your instruments are working, that your logic isn't broken, that the observation wasn't a fluke.
(2) So in any actual episode of inquiry, at least some beliefs are treated as fixed.
(3) Fallibilism says that every belief is revisable in principle.
(4) But treating a belief as fixed just is treating it as not revisable in principle; that's what "fixed" means.
(5) So any actual episode of inquiry treats at least some beliefs as not revisable in principle.
(6) So no actual inquiry is fallibilist. Fallibilism turns out to describe a kind of inquiry that real inquiry could never satisfy.

I can tell something goes wrong around step 4, but I cant say exactly what move it is, and my colleague keeps saying it's airtight. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what's the fair way to restate the relation between background assumptions and fallibilism?
