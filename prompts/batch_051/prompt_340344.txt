I came across this summary of Hume's essay on suicide in a general-interest book my cousin lent me, and something about it strikes me as subtly unfair in a way I can't quite locate. The author writes: "Hume's essay is a straightforward argument that suicide cannot be wrong because each of us has a natural right to determine what happens to our own body. Against the religious objection, he points out that our actions can't really disrupt God's plan, since everything that happens, including what we do, is part of general providence — so if our lives belonged specially to God, taking medicine or stepping out of the way of a falling tile would be just as impious as ending them. Against the objection that we owe something to other people, he argues that someone who departs from life does no positive harm; he only stops doing good, which isn't the same as injuring anyone, so there's no victim. Hume does allow that suicide may be imprudent when it comes from a passing fit of melancholy, but he adds that this almost never happens, since nobody puts an end to a life they still regard as worth living. So his conclusion is that suicide is always permissible."
