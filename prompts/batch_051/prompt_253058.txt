Before I read any more theories of how the passage of time matters morally—why a wrong seems to become less answerable, or why it should not—I want the specification of what such a theory must deliver. Write the desiderata for an adequate account of the moral relevance of the time between a wrong and a response to it, adequate for saying when temporal distance matters, why it matters, and what exactly it changes. Treat statutes of limitations as the clearest legal expression of the phenomenon, but make the underlying moral structure the target, not any particular legal code. For each desideratum: give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement; mark it non-negotiable or negotiable with a one-line defense of that mark; and name the kind of view it embarrasses or destabilizes (for example, a pure retributive view, a pure deterrence view, or a simple view on which wrongs never expire). Organize the list by the phenomena to be accounted for, not by theory. Give a committed response: take definite positions and argue for them rather than laying out the options. Do not include citations or bibliographic references; the argument should stand on the concepts and cases themselves.

Make sure the list confronts at least the following candidates, unless you argue that one of them is not a genuine desideratum and justify that rejection. (a) The difference between a discrete wrong and a continuing wrong, and why the ongoing effects of a completed wrong must not automatically make the wrong continuing. (b) Why decaying evidence is the easy, purely epistemic part of the story, and what remains once evidence is stipulated to be intact. (c) The apparent asymmetry between ordinary wrongs where a time bar feels apt and large historical wrongs such as slavery or dispossession where it feels less apt—and whether one theory can respect the asymmetry rather than explain it away. (d) What exactly time is supposed to degrade: the wrongness of the act, the wrongdoer's desert, the victim's claim to complaint, or only the community's standing to punish. (e) Whether time matters intrinsically or only as a proxy for change in the wrongdoer (repentance, reform) or in the social world (reliance, repose). (f) Victim-side persistence such as trauma or continuing disadvantage, and why it cannot by itself keep every claim alive.

Then give two verdicts. First, which single requirement on your list does the most to exclude or destabilize the most popular positions, and why? Second, identify one pair of requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied without serious qualification; if you think all can be jointly satisfied, defend that view against the strongest tension you can construct.

Present the desiderata as a table with columns for the requirement, the case it must handle, whether it is negotiable, and what it embarrasses. Give the two verdicts in prose after the table. Make the very last line begin with Verdict: and state your overall position in one sentence.
