Here are my gut calls on a bunch of choice-related statements, and I'd like to know whether there's any single principle behind them or whether I should just stop trying to be consistent about set theory.

- The reals can be well-ordered: no. I can't make myself believe there's some first real, then a next one, and so on through all of them, even though I know "next" isn't the usual order.
- Zorn's lemma: totally fine, I've used it a hundered times and never felt weird about it.
- Every vector space has a basis: fine, even obvious the way the finite case is.
- Tychonoff's theorem for arbitrary products: fine, though I mostly use the compact Hausdorff version.
- A Hamel basis for R over Q: fine, actually kind of cool.
- Banach-Tarski: no. I know it's a theorem of ZFC, but it feels like a sign something went wrong upstream.
- Non-measurable sets: I'll say they exist in the abstract, but the Vitali construction feels like a party trick.
- Countable choice and dependent choice: obviously fine.

The one I keep flipping on is the plain statement that a product of nonempty sets is nonempty. For any family of sets I can actually point to, of course it is. As a claim about every family in the whole universe, I'm not so sure.

I've been toying with a principle like: choice is okay when you're choosing from sets that come with structure you can use to pick, or when you can describe the choice, but not when it's applied to arbitrary sets with no describable rule. Is there a real position in that neighborhood that gets most of my verdicts, and which of my gut calls does it force me to abandon? And can you tell me where that principle lands on a few things I haven't made up my mind about: every surjection has a right inverse, every field has an algebraic closure, and every filter on a set extends to an ultrafilter? Also, is my "obviously fine" attitude toward dependent choice defensible, given how much of the same nonconstructive picking it quietly lets through?
