You are an expert on the semantics of generic sentences and an unusually good explainer of philosophical ideas to non-philosophers. I am a philosophy professor, and next month I am giving a two-hour guest seminar to first-year PhD students in cognitive psychology. They work on categorization, concept learning, and social stereotyping, so they will arrive treating generics mainly as statistical summaries of categories: something like "ducks lay eggs" will seem to them to mean "most ducks lay eggs" or "ducks typically lay eggs," and the hard cases will seem like vague quantifiers or loose talk.

The one idea I want the session to teach is that this statistical picture cannot be right. Generic acceptability tracks not prevalence but something about the kind itself, and it tolerates exceptions in a principled, uneven way. My contrast cases will be these: "ducks lay eggs" and "mosquitoes carry malaria" are both accepted even though the relevant proportions are wildly different; "ducks are female" and "books are paperbacks" are not accepted even though their prevalence is comparable to, or higher than, some accepted cases; and "lions have manes" is not accepted even though the property is common among a salient subset. I do not need to settle which positive theory of generics is correct, but I do want them to leave convinced that the phenomenon is real and that prevalence alone cannot explain it.

Plan the session for me. I want a teaching plan, not the lecture itself. Specifically:

1. The order in which the components must be introduced, and what depends on what. The failure of statistical accounts should feel like a discovery they make, not an assertion I hand down.
2. The one example or minimal pair that should carry the session, with a note on what this example must have and what features would make it misleading for this particular audience.
3. The misconceptions these students are most likely to bring or to form partway through, in roughly the order they will surface, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be preempted or answered rather than deferred.
4. A specific end-of-session check that would tell me they have genuinely distinguished kind-based acceptability from prevalence, rather than merely learning new vocabulary.
5. What I should deliberately leave out at this level and say in one sentence why, and the one objection or complication I should explicitly flag as open rather than try to resolve.

Before you write the final plan, think through the dependency structure among the ideas and the audience's prior commitments, so that the sequence is ordered by what these particular students need, not by the standard map of the semantics literature.
