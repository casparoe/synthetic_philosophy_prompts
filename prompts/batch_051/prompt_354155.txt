And here's the student's answer:

"Moral generalism says that doing the right thing requires relying on a stable body of moral principles, while particularism says we can judge particular cases correctly without principles, because what matters is the specific shape of each situation. Particularists typically appeal to holism about reasons: a feature that counts in favor in one context can count against in another, and can be irrelevant in a third. For example, that an action would be enjoyable normally counts in favor of doing it, but it can count against when the enjoyment is cruel or undermines someone's trust. Given holism, any candidate principle like 'pleasure counts in favor' is false as a universal claim, and once you patch it with all the contextual qualifications it becomes too gerrymandered to guide action. So holism basically settles the debate. A generalist might reply that this only rules out exceptionless principles, not the pro tanto principles Ross described, which can be outweighed. But since Ross's prima facie duties are overrideable, Ross ends up being a particularist in practice, which shows how unstable the generalist position is once you accept holism."
