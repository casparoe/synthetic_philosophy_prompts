As a novelist, I spend much of my time with characters who must act under uncertainty, and I am inclined to think that the felt texture of uncertainty carries information. But I am also drawn to theories that impose discipline on a messy phenomenon, and subjective expected utility theory is the most disciplined account of rational choice I know. It shows that if a person's preferences satisfy a few simple axioms—among them the sure-thing principle, which seems almost impossible to reject on its own—then those preferences can be represented as maximizing expected utility relative to a single subjective probability distribution. It gives a definite answer in every case. Yet it seems to require collapsing my uncertainty into a best estimate, and here I hesitate. In the Ellsberg paradox, most people prefer a bet on a draw from an urn known to contain 50 red and 50 black balls to the same bet on an urn whose composition is unknown; many will name a winning color first and still prefer the known urn, even though their indifference between the two colors in the unknown urn suggests they treat each as equally likely. My own preferences fall the same way, and my intuition is firm that the source and quality of uncertainty matter, not merely as a bias to be purged. Present both sides at their strongest and give a negotiated verdict: should the intuition be debunked and dropped, should the theory be amended or abandoned, or is there a reading on which they are compatible? Be explicit about what would justify each move, rather than simply choosing a side.
