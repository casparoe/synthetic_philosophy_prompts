I'm a retired fisheries biologist (lakes, mostly—phosphorus budgets, winterkill, that sort of thing), and I've only started reading political philosophy and institutional economics since I stopped working. So please excuse me if I mangle the jargon. What I keep going back and forth on is the fight between the Hardin crowd and the Ostrom crowd over common-pool resources. I've watched both positions play out live on the lake where I live: our association tries to cap shoreline clearing and ice-fishing shacks, and the whole thing sometimes looks like the tragedy of the commons to me, and other times looks like a bunch of ordinary people quietly enforcing rules through gossip, dock-space norms, and the annual meeting.

Could you draft the joint statement a committed Hardinian and a committed Ostromian could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration? By Hardinian I mean the view that an open-access resource gets destroyed unless you either privatize it or hand it over to a strong outside authority; by Ostromian I mean the view that ordinary users can and do build local rules, monitoring, and graduated sanctions that sustain the resource. I'd like it organized as (1) a numbered agreed-claims section both sides would sign, including what they both now accept about Hardin's original pasture, the difference between open access and common property, and real cases of both success and failure; (2) a disagreements table, with each side's view stated in wording its own side would accept; (3) the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions where settling them would actually move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, a concrete test or argument both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If some of the fight turns out to be about words, especially the word "commons," say which part.

The thing I'd add, because it's what I actually know something about, is the biology of the resource. A fish stock that can double in a few years and an aquifer that fills over decades are both common-pool resources, but their institutions have to run on very different clocks. I'd like one crux to be whether the timescale and observability of the resource changes what kinds of rules can work: fish you can count and watch might be governable by a lake association, while a slow invisible aquifer might not be, or maybe that difference is overrated. I'd also like a crux on whether cheap monitoring—water-quality sondes, drones, eDNA sampling—changes the answer by making hidden resources observable and rule-breaking harder to hide, or whether it just makes outside top-down enforcement more tempting.

If it helps, use the two-lake comparison I've always wished somebody would fund: two lakes of similar size with the same phosphorus problem, one with a long-standing lake association that sets its own limits, one without, and ask what each side predicts after ten years. Don't tell me which side is right; write the document they could both put their names on, with the residual disagreement made exact.
