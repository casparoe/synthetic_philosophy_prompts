You are a privacy theorist who has also served on a university's data-stewardship committee. I want you to reason the way that committee would reason, not the way a generic corporate privacy team would. Start by explicitly reconstructing the candidate rules or principles that could explain the committee's record before you apply them to the new proposal, and think carefully about which distinctions the precedents actually require you to draw.

Below are the committee's published decisions.

1. Approved with conditions: a facilities researcher proposed analyzing one term of campus Wi-Fi association logs (building, access point, time) to map study-space usage. The committee required that names and device identifiers be stripped before analysis and that only building-level aggregates be published.

2. Rejected: the campus health center proposed transferring de-identified student health records (diagnosis codes, appointment dates, year of birth) to a pharmaceutical firm for market research. The health center cited a privacy notice students had signed saying de-identified data might be used for research. The committee held that students could not reasonably have expected commercial transfer of their medical records, that diagnosis plus dates plus year of birth made re-identification plausible in a campus population this small, and that the signed notice was too vague to count as informed consent for monetizing that information.

3. Approved: the library wanted to use individual borrowing histories to power an in-house recommendation display at checkout. Borrowers would be informed that borrowing data feeds recommendations, and the data would remain on library servers.

4. Rejected: a private tutoring company offered the university a share of revenue in exchange for student names, email addresses, and transcript data so it could target test-prep advertisements. The company pointed to a blanket consent students had given for sharing records with third parties for educational purposes. The committee held that marketing test-prep services is not an educational purpose and that the consent did not reach that use.

5. Approved with conditions: a psychology professor wanted to study first-year students' discussion-forum posts over a semester to measure how academic writing changes, presenting aggregate findings. The committee required that usernames be stripped, that no post be quoted verbatim, and that only aggregates be presented.

6. Approved: the dining service proposed installing ceiling cameras that count people in line and estimate wait times, with no face recognition, footage deleted after one week, and only aggregate statistics retained.

The new proposal is this. The registrar's office wants to sell a credit-modeling startup a de-identified dataset covering five years of individual student records: whether each student had a disability accommodation, whether the student ever used the counseling center, whether the student had any academic probation events, and broad demographic cells. The dataset would be prepared by a trained statistical unit to satisfy formal anonymization standards such as k-anonymity and l-diversity, would exclude free text and exact dates, and would be accompanied by a contractual ban on re-identification. Students were never told such a sale might happen. The only relevant consent on file is the routine registration release permitting sharing for educational purposes, which the registrar's office concedes does not cover this sale. The office asks whether it may proceed anyway on the ground that the data is properly de-identified.

Please give me the following, in order. (1) Two or three rival principles that each fit the committee's six precedents, stated precisely enough that they could disagree about new cases. (2) What each principle predicts about the registrar's proposal, and which precedent is the closest analog under each reading. (3) Your best recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with specified conditions — for consistency with the committee's own record, with the reasons you prefer that reading over the alternatives. (4) An honest statement of what principle the committee would be adopting under each possible decision. (5) A further case the committee could use to cleanly distinguish the live candidate rules, with each rule's predicted verdict on that case.
