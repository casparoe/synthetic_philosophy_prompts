I have been thinking lately about a result I came across called the Condorcet jury theorem, and I find myself unsure about what it is actually supposed to show. As I understand it, the theorem says the following: if a group has to settle a factual question with two possible answers, and every member independently has the same better-than-even chance of picking the correct answer, then a majority vote is more likely to be correct than any single member's vote—and as the group gets larger, the probability that the majority is correct approaches certainty.

If that is right, the theorem looks like a powerful mathematical argument for trusting large electorates more than small panels of specialists on any question that has a definite answer. Why, then, should we take seriously the view of a small committee of climate scientists on an empirical policy question, when a national referendum would be a vastly larger jury and, by the theorem, vastly more likely to reach the correct verdict? And when a large vote goes heavily in one direction, doesn't the theorem tell us that the majority's answer is almost certainly correct, so that the losing minority is being unreasonable if it does not simply defer?

I suspect I am misapplying the theorem somewhere, but I cannot see exactly where. Please keep your answer to at most 300 words.
