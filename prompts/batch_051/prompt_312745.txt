Novelist here. My brain lives in the craft of fiction: point of view, interiority, free indirect discourse, subtext, scene and summary, showing versus telling, the gap between what a narrator says and what the prose is doing — that sort of thing. I'm fluid in that vocabulary and semi-illiterate in the philosophy of music.

A playwright friend keeps bringing up Bernard Williams's writing on opera, which I havent actually read. As best as I can gather from her, Williams thought something like this: an opera is not a play that has singing layered on top of it, and the music is not a delivery system for the text but the thing that makes the form what it is. The music is not expressing emotions that are already there in the words or the story; it partly constitutes an emotional world that couldn't exist without it. Because of that, questions that come naturally to a novelist — whether a character is psychologically believable, whether a person would really behave that way, why they don't just say the thing in words — are in some deep way the wrong questions, or at best questions you can only ask after you've already accepted the conventions of the form. The dramatic life of an opera is in the way music and action make each other, not in whether the libretto would work as a spoken play.

I may be mangling Williams, so if my summary is off, correct me — but as a running correction while you do the actual job.
