I've been working through the MU puzzle from Gödel, Escher, Bach, the one where you begin with the string MI and try to reach MU with four rewrite rules. As I understand them, the rules are these: you may append U to any string ending in I; from any string Mx you may form Mxx, doubling the part after the M; anywhere in a string you may replace III with U; and anywhere you may delete UU. The standard solution is that MU can't be reached, and the proof is to jump out of the symbol game and count I's. The count starts at one. Doubling it gives two, four, eight, and so on, never a multiple of three; the other rules either leave the count alone or subtract three, so the count's remainder upon division by three is stuck, and since MU would have zero I's, MU can never appear. The lesson usually drawn is that you sometimes have to jump out of the system you're working in — translate the strings into numbers and let arithmetic do what the rewrite rules cannot.

While tinkering, I changed the second rule: instead of doubling the part after the M, my version triples it, so from Mx you may go to Mxxx. The change looks small, but it seems to dissolve the standard argument, because a single tripling move makes the number of I's divisible by three. In fact, I think you can then reach MU in two steps: MI to MIII by tripling, and then MIII to MU by replacing the three I's with U. What I'm trying to understand is how much of the original analysis survives this change. Specifically: is MU really derivable in the tripling variant, and is a two-step derivation the shortest possible? Does the jump-out-of-the-system strategy still work but with a different invariant — and if so, what invariant, and which impossible target string now plays the role MU used to play? And when you put the two systems side by side, is the real lesson of the original puzzle the specific arithmetic of doubling versus dividing by three, or is it the general habit of looking for some invariant, and what would show the difference? I'd like your answer in American English.
