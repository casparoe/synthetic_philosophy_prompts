I've been turning this over for a couple of weeks and I'd like some outside comments on an essay I'm sketching. Earlier this autumn I got sucked into a long online auction for a used record deck (the seller had underpriced it and, stupidly, I'd already bid three times before I realised). By the end I'd gone well past what the thing was worth, not because I wanted it that badly but because I couldn't stand the idea of losing after having come that far. My partner watched the whole thing and said it was basically the dollar auction, which is what got me reading about the game. In case you haven't come across it, the setup is that the auctioneer sells a dollar to the highest bidder, but both the winner and the runner-up must pay their final bids, so once you've bid, dropping out is costly and that's what drives the escalation.

Here's the essay idea, in rough notes. The standard line is that the dollar auction shows how sunk costs lead to irrational escalation: both bidders can end up paying far more than the dollar bill is worth, each one reasoning that one more bid will avoid losing what they've already put in. I want to argue that this is only sometimes right, and that the game actually bundles together three different mechanisms that usually get run together. First, there is plain sunk-cost reasoning, which really is a mistake. Second, there is a rational concern for your reputation as someone who doesn't get pushed around, and in repeated versions of the game that reputation can be worth more than the immediate loss. Third, there is uncertainty about when the other bidder will stop, which makes continuing rational in a way that isn't just a bias: if you genuinely don't know your opponent's limit, one more bid is a kind of costly information purchase, not just throwing good money after bad.

I'd like comments mostly on whether this is accurate, and whether the three mechanisms really are seperable or whether the third collapses into the first once you model it carefully. I'm also not sure the essay is interesting enough for people who already know the game, so please be blunt about whether the argument is obvious or novel. Where I suspect I'm weakest is the reputation point: in a one-shot anonymous game there is no reputation to protect, yet people still overbid, so maybe I need to say something about internalised norms or about the frame people bring to their behaviour in the lab. I'd rather not cite anyone; keep it about the reasoning itself. And American English, please, if you don't mind.
