A friend sent me this argument and I can't figure out where it goes wrong. It starts from an everyday case and ends up letting a stopped clock vouch for its own reliability, which has to be mistaken.

(1) When a tomato looks red to me, I'm justified in believing it's red.
(2) Since I'm also justified in believing it looked red to me, I can infer that my color experience was accurate on this occasion.
(3) Repeating this on lots of occasions gives me a justified track record: it looked red and was red, looked green and was green, and so on.
(4) From that track record I'm justified in concluding that my color vision is generally reliable.
(5) So I can become justified in believing my color vision is reliable using only color vision itself.
(6) But nothing in (1)–(4) is special to color vision. For any source, I could trust its outputs one by one, record each as a success because the source itself delivered the answer, and infer that the source is reliable.
(7) That would let a stopped clock be bootstrapped into being a justified source of the time, which is absurd.
(8) So something in (1)–(5) has to go.

I'd most like to keep (1) and (2), since giving them up feels like handing the skeptic a win. But I suspect the damage is in (3) or (4), or maybe in the slide from color vision to stopped clocks in (6). Which step do we reject, and what does rejecting it cost? And where does this go worng if I'm overstating the problem?
