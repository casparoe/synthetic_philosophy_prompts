I am a trustee of a small family foundation that funds civic organizations in my parents' home country, and I am the only trustee who grew up in that society; the other three are professionals with no personal ties to the place. We vote next week on whether to renew a grant to a grassroots civic-education group whose work we have all rated highly, and I need to circulate a short decision memo beforehand. The room is genuinely split, so the memo has to be something the board can act on rather than a set of general principles.

The sticking point is the group's proposed budget. It includes funds for what the executive director calls relationship maintenance, broken into three kinds of expense. First, modest holiday gifts to local officials who have overseen the group's permits in past years. Second, small cash payments to low-level clerks to make sure registration and venue applications are processed in time for a planned public event rather than sitting for months. Third, a part-time salary for a liaison whose main qualification is that he is the nephew of a senior official and whose value to the group is the access that relationship brings. On a call with us, the executive director was candid: each item is ordinary practice, and refusing would insult the officials involved and make the work impractical. The gifts, she said, are how one shows respect and keeps relationships; the cash payments are how underpaid clerks keep applications moving; and the liaison is how an outside organization comes to be trusted at all. She offered an example: after years of small holiday gifts, one official later expedited flood-relief permits at no cost.

Two trustees want to reject the renewal outright. They say the foundation's anti-corruption policy and the law in our country prohibit paying for what should be public services, and that calling it relationship maintenance does not change what it is; one of them added that if we accept the everyone-does-it argument here, there is no principled stopping point. Two other trustees want to renew without conditions. They say we would be imposing a Western standard on a society with different norms of reciprocity, and that a gift within a long-term relationship is different in kind from a payment for a specific favor, even where it later produces one. A conditional approval is on the table only if someone can defend a workable line between what should and should not be funded.

Please draft the memo I would circulate. It should recommend one course of action — renew, reject, or renew with conditions — give reasons the other trustees can apply when we vote, and engage the strongest argument on the losing side honestly rather than dismissing it. I am not an academic, and I need the reasoning plain enough to defend in the meeting without falling back on slogans.
