I'm a software engineer, no formal philosophy background, and I got weirdly into the textualism vs. purposivism debate because it's basically the argument we have at work about whether to implement an API spec literally or build what the spec author obviously meant. My partner's book club is doing The Merchant of Venice next month so I actually read it, and the trial scene has been living in my head. Shylock has a signed bond for a pound of Antonio's flesh, and his whole position is that the court just has to enforce the words as written — which sure sounds like textualism. The Duke and Portia first go the other way, appealing to the purpose of the contract, to mercy, to the kind of city Venice wants to be, and Shylock just refuses. Then Portia flips it by being even more literal than him: the bond mentions flesh but doesn't mention blood, so she rules he can take his flesh but must spill no blood, which makes the whole forfeit impossible. So the character who says enforce the text gets beaten by someone who enforces the text harder.

I'd like a reading of that whole conflict through the textualism/purposivism lens. Does the play actually take a side — does it treat Shylock's plain-meaning reading as the villain move, or does it admit that his legal position is the strong one and the spirit-of-the-law appeals are flimsy? Is Portia's blood point a textualist argument, a purposivist argument wearing a textualist coat, or just a loophole hack that shows why both sides are naive about how plain meaning actually works? And what would an honest defender of textualism say to the play: is the play unfair by making the text itself absurd, or does it expose that a text's plain meaning is never quite as plain as it seems once you have to specify what counts as flesh? I don't want to walk into book club with just Shakespeare prefers mercy to legalism; I want a reading that treats the play and the legal philosophy both seriously, and that's willing to say where the play cheats. No need for quotes or citations, I just want the reading to be rigourous without turning into a bibliography.
