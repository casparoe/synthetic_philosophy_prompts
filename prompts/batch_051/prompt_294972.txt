A stratigraphic commission's standing code contains the following rule: "An epoch shall be established only where a single, globally identifiable stratigraphic boundary can be demonstrated at a specified level in deposited strata, marking a change in the Earth system of sufficient magnitude and synchroneity to serve as a worldwide time marker." A working group now proposes an Anthropocene epoch with its lower boundary placed in the mid-twentieth century. The proposed boundary is identified by a layer of plutonium fallout, fly ash, and plastics in recent, largely unlithified sediment and ice; it is globally traceable, but the change it is taken to represent—the industrial transformation of the Earth system—began at different times in different regions and was at least partly a social event. The rule was written before any unit of geological time had been proposed on the basis of human activity. Does the proposal satisfy the rule? Work through the plain wording, the purpose of the rule, what the drafters would have said had they been asked about an epoch caused by one species within the past century, and what a reasonable geologist reading the rule today would take it to require. Tell me which approach should govern, and whether the Anthropocene decision is ultimately a scientific judgment or a political one.
