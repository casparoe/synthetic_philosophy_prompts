I have been trying to get clear about what exactly goes wrong, if anything, in a certain reply to Frankfurt cases, and I would be grateful for a precise diagnosis of the following argument. By a Frankfurt-style case I mean the usual setup: Jones decides on his own to shoot Smith, and Black, who wants Smith dead, has arranged things so that if Jones had shown any sign of wavering, Black would have intervened and ensured the shooting anyway. Black never has to intervene. The usual moral drawn from such cases is that Jones is morally responsible even though he could not have done otherwise.

Now consider this argument against that moral:

(1) Since Black never actually intervenes, the actual causal sequence running from Jones's deliberation to the shooting is exactly the same as it would have been in an otherwise identical case with no Black standing by.
(2) Therefore, the two cases are also exactly the same with respect to what Jones could have done.
(3) In the no-Black case, Jones could have done otherwise: had he decided not to shoot, nothing would have stopped him.
(4) Therefore, in the Frankfurt case, Jones could have done otherwise as well.
(5) Therefore, the Frankfurt case does not refute the principle that a person is morally responsible for what he has done only if he could have done otherwise; the case only appears to refute that principle if one illicitly lets a merely counterfactual intervener subtract an alternative from the actual agent.

I half-suspect the step from (1) to (2) is doing more work than it announces, but I can never make the diagnosis precise in my own mind. Are we entitled to accept this argument as sound? If not, locate the exact step at which it fails and explain what principle that step would require and whether the step could be repaired.
