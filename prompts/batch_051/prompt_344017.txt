For the past few months I have been reporting on how financial markets process information, talking to economists who treat prices as a kind of collective cognition and to traders who believe they see dispersed information get incorporated into prices every day. In the course of that reporting I have run into a collision between two things that each feel unassailable, and I would like help thinking through which one should yield.

The first is the no-trade theorem, which I find genuinely compelling as a piece of reasoning. Suppose you and I hold different private information about the value of an asset, and we are both rational and know each other to be rational. If you offer to sell to me at some price, your willingness to sell is itself a signal: it should lead me to lower my estimate of the asset's value. But you, anticipating this, should only have offered at a price that reflected that lowered estimate, and so on. The result is that unless something else is going on — hedging, a need for cash, or genuinely different prior beliefs — purely information-driven trade appears to unravel, because an offer to trade is itself evidence against the trade being good for the counterparty. The second is a conviction I have never been able to shake in my professional life: that markets are one of the most powerful knowledge-aggregation technologies we have. Prices seem to synthesize information dispersed across millions of people; stock, futures, and prediction markets routinely appear to know things no individual participant knows; and informed trading is, in practice, a real and profitable activity.

The obvious resolutions are too easy: dismiss the theorem as unrealistic and keep my faith in markets; dismiss my faith as survivorship bias and treat speculation as a form of collective irrationality; or say the theorem is true but simply irrelevant to the informational role of prices. I am not asking which side wins. I am asking what it would take to give up each one — what observations or arguments would show that my market-as-epistemology intuition is the thing that has to go, and what would show that the theorem is the thing that has to go — and whether there is a reading on which both survive: the theorem ruling out a narrow class of pure speculation while the epistemic function of markets remains intact. Please give the strongest version of each position and a negotiated verdict.
