Im a software engineer working on a hospital resource scheduler. The optimizer ranks schedules by summing predicted quality-adjusted life years across all patients and picks the schedule with the highest total. It'll happily shave a bit of physical therapy off one patient to give a much bigger gain to someone else, the way I'd trade my own Tuesday afternoon against my own future surgery. But patients aren't one person: there's no single life where her small loss and his big gain actually get combined. Meanwhile our consent flow is one-patient-one-veto—no amount of benefit to other patients lets us treat this patient without her sign-off. So what does the scheduler quietly assume about wellbeing, or about how different people's interests relate to each other? Does it actually need that assumption to do its job, or could a version with a hard floor on each patient's worst outcome, or one that treats each person's claim separately, still work? And what concretely changes in the objective function or the constraints if we drop the assumption?
