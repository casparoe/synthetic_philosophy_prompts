A philosopher I know has a settled, confident set of views about personal identity across various mind-uploading and replacement scenarios, but she will not state the underlying principle; she only gives verdicts on concrete cases. Her verdicts so far are as follows.

First, she says that a traveler who steps into a Star Trek–style teletransporter—scanned at the point of departure, destroyed there, and reassembled molecule-for-molecule at the destination—does not survive; the reassembled person is a new person with inherited memories.

Second, she says that a person whose biological neurons are replaced one by one over many years with functionally identical silicon neurons, while the person remains awake, conscious, and psychologically unchanged throughout, does survive; there is no point at which a new person appears.

Third, she says that a person does not survive a whole-brain swap in which the entire biological brain is removed in a single operation and a pre-built silicon brain is installed under full anesthesia with no brain activity; the subject who wakes is not the original person.

Fourth, she says that if a person is scanned without being harmed and a software simulation of her mind begins running while the original remains alive, the original survives and the simulation is a distinct person. After the original died of unrelated causes a week later, she said the original had died and the simulation remained a distinct person rather than becoming the original.

Fifth, she says that a patient who has undergone a complete split-brain operation, severing the corpus callosum, is still one person rather than two.

I now want to predict her verdict on a case designed to pull two candidate principles apart. A healthy man is awake and carrying on a continuous conversation while a swarm of nanomachines replaces every neuron in his brain with a functionally equivalent silicon neuron. The replacements are simultaneous, not sequential: each biological neuron is deactivated and its silicon counterpart takes over at the same instant, while the global activity of the network is sustained so that the man experiences no interruption in consciousness, memory, or personality. The whole conversion is over in less than a second, and no biological neurons remain afterward.

What would she most likely say about this last case: is the man at the end the same person, a new person, or a successor of the original? Lay out the distinct rules that fit all five of her earlier verdicts, state what each rule predicts about the new case, and give the best-supported prediction together with your reasons for preferring it. Finally, propose one further case that would most decisively settle which rule she is actually running.
