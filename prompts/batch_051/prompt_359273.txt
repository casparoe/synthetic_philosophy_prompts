I sit on the grants committee of a small foundation that funds interventions to improve the welfare of nonhuman animals. We score proposals with a single formula: each proposal is multiplied across three numbers — the expected improvement in welfare for the average affected individual, the number of affected individuals, and the probability that the intervention produces the intended result. A proposal ranks higher when it does more for more beings, adjusted for how likely it is to work.

Two of my colleagues have begun to disagree about the formula. One says it lets diffuse, low-grade benefits dominate: a project expected to give a nearly imperceptible improvement to an enormous number of barely sentient animals can outrank a project that would lift a smaller number of highly sentient animals from severe suffering to a decent state. She wants us to add a rule: insofar as a state involves intense suffering, preventing it takes absolute priority, and no quantity of mild improvement elsewhere compensates for allowing it. The other colleague says this is a mistake born of vivid stories. If a diffuse project really does produce more total welfare, he argues, the committee should fund it; refusing to do so because the improvement is barely perceptible is just a refusal to count the many small preferences of ordinary individuals.

I can see the force of both objections, and that suggests to me that our real problem is not the arithmetic but the structure of how values combine. I would like a reasoned recommendation: is our multiplicative scoring rule a defensible way to trade off the intensity of a welfare change, the number of beings affected, and the probability of success? Should any of these dimensions dominate the others — for example, should intense suffering be treated as a constraint that cannot be traded off at all? And if we add such constraints, what makes them more than an ad hoc way of protecting a conclusion we already like? Please do not give generic grant-making or management advice; I am trying to understand the underlying structural question about how values combine. And please say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different conclusion, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
