I have been trying to get clear on metaphysical grounding, and a friend showed me an argument that seems to prove far too much: it would show that no fact can be merely grounded, so every fact is fundamental. I would rather not accept that, but I cannot find the faulty step. The background is the familiar picture on which some facts hold in virtue of other facts. For example, the fact that this patch is red is supposed to be fully grounded in the fact that the patch is scarlet, and a grounded fact is supposed to be real without being an extra item in one's fundamental ontology — it is, as the literature sometimes puts it, nothing over and above its grounds. My friend's argument runs as follows.

(1) If a fact p is fully grounded in some facts G, then p is nothing over and above G.
(2) To say that p is nothing over and above G is to say that a world containing G and p contains nothing more, in the relevant sense, than a world containing G alone.
(3) But if p were distinct from G, then a world containing both p and G would contain something more than a world containing G alone — namely, p itself.
(4) So p is not distinct from G.
(5) If p is not distinct from G, then p just is G.
(6) Yet p is not G: the fact that the patch is red is not the fact that the patch is scarlet, and in any case grounding is asymmetric and irreflexive while identity is symmetric and reflexive.
(7) Therefore p both is and is not G; the notion of full grounding collapses into identity, and the only stable options are that every fact is fundamental or that there is no distinct grounding relation at all.

I would like to keep both the scarlet-to-red grounding example and the thought that a grounded fact does not add to the fundamental inventory of the world. Which premise or step should I reject, why does it look right, and what does rejecting it in the most defensible way commit me to? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
