In my conditionals seminar I've been reworking the usual version of Lewis's triviality argument, and I've gotten myself into the position where I can't say, with a straight face, which line I'd reject. Maybe you can talk me through it.

Let A→C be an indicative conditional and P a rational credence function. Take the Equation as a standing assumption: for any such P, where the relevant terms are defined, P(A→C) = P(C|A). And take it not just for your actual credences but for reasonable shifts of them too — in particular for P(·|C) and P(·|¬C). After all, if I learn the match was off, shouldn't my new probability for "if it rains, the match is off" still equal my new conditional probability that the match is off given rain?

Now pick A and C such that P(A∧C) > 0, P(A∧¬C) > 0, and 0 < P(C) < 1.

(1) Applied to P(·|C), the Equation says P_C(A→C) = P_C(C|A). Writing the left side as P(A→C | C), this gives P(A→C | C) = P(C | A∧C) = 1.
(2) Applied to P(·|¬C), the same move gives P(A→C | ¬C) = P(C | A∧¬C) = 0.
(3) By total probability, P(A→C) = P(A→C | C)P(C) + P(A→C | ¬C)P(¬C) = (1)P(C) + (0)P(¬C) = P(C).
(4) But applied to P itself, the Equation gives P(A→C) = P(C|A).
(5) So P(C|A) = P(C). Since A and C were arbitrary (modulo the positivity conditions), this says every indicative conditional's antecedent is probabilistically irrelevant to its consequent. But I have plenty of credence functions where P(C|A) ≠ P(C).

That's the argument that seems to prove too much. I'd like to keep the Equation if I can; it feels like the one piece of real data in this area. But I've stopped being able to see which of the other commitments I can live without, and what it costs to give one up. Where does the argument go wrong, and what do I pay for saying it does?

And since I'll be using this with students, I'd ask you to seperate what's genuinely settled from what's still contested among people who work on this, and not present a contested diagnosis as if it were the received view.
