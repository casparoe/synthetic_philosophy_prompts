I serve on a standing committee that decides whether simulation models should be admitted into the evidence base for our agency’s policy analyses. A friend and I have been arguing about whether our past decisions commit us to a particular view about unrealistic assumptions, and the discussion ended up relitigating Friedman’s old claim that predictive success, not the realism of assumptions, is what matters. My friend thinks our track record is basically Friedmanite: assumptions can be false so long as the model predicts well. I think our precedents are messier than that, and the distinction between “false but harmless” and “false in a way that matters” is doing real work.

Here are the committee’s rulings from earlier cases:

- We approved a fisheries stock-assessment model that treated all individuals in a stock as identical, because its annual catch forecasts had been accurate across a decade. The reviewers noted the assumption was false but said it did not affect the target variable.
- We rejected a second fisheries model that also assumed identical individuals but omitted spatial migration, because it failed to track stocks in the years after closed areas were introduced.
- We approved, with conditions, a housing-market model that used a single representative household and zero transaction costs, because its short-run price forecasts matched transaction data. The condition was that the authors run sensitivity checks on transaction costs before using the model for policy.
- We rejected an energy-demand model that assumed a constant price elasticity, because its long-run projections under high-carbon-price scenarios diverged from every other model and from the best available evidence. The authors could not show that the false assumption was harmless for the model’s intended use.
- We approved an epidemic model with homogeneous mixing, despite known contact heterogeneity, because it predicted attack rates for past outbreaks well and the question was limited to aggregate healthcare demand.

Now we have a new proposal: a labor-market model that assumes workers have complete information about vacancies and that search is costless. It fits aggregate unemployment-duration data very well over the past twenty years. The authors want to use it to evaluate how changes in unemployment-insurance generosity would affect long-term unemployment. My friend says our epidemic-model precedent settles the new case: false assumptions that do not hurt fit are acceptable, so we should approve. I suspect the new case is closer to the spatial-fisheries case or the energy-demand case, because something about search frictions is central to the policy question being asked. Are our precedents genuinely underdetermined by this case? What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, which precedent is the closest analog, and what principle would we be adopting with each choice?
