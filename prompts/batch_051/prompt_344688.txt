In my philosophy of biology group we've been going back and forth on the tautology objection, and we've managed to construct an argument that seems to show the standard fix — the propensity interpretation of fitness — doesn't actually fix anything. I keep feeling like I'm missing a step, but I can't find it, and I'd rather not loose a premise by accident.

Here's the argument as it stands:

(1) Natural selection can explain why a trait spread through a population only if fitness is defined independently of actual survival and reproduction. If being fit just means actually surviving and reproducing, then the claim that the fittest survive is just the claim that the survivors survived, which explains nothing.

(2) The propensity interpretation is the standard way out: it defines fitness as a probabilistic disposition — roughly, a trait's or genotype's expected number of offspring in a specified environment, like the bias of a coin.

(3) But a probabilistic disposition isn't directly observable. You estimate a coin's bias by flipping the coin a lot; likewise, the only way to estimate a trait's expected reproductive output is by observing actual survival and reproduction across trials, experiments, or populations.

(4) So the only evidence we can ever have for a claim of the form 'T is fitter than U' comes from actual reproductive output — which is the very fact the explanation was supposed to explain.

(5) The explanation 'T spread because T was fitter' is therefore supported only by the fact that T spread, and that makes it circular after all.

(6) So the propensity interpretation doesn't dissolve the tautology objection; it just relocates the circularity from the definition of fitness to the evidence for fitness.

I'm not asking for the history of who said what; I want the diagnosis. Which premise or step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep (2), because it still seems like the only way to make fitness a real property of organisms rather than a post hoc summary, but I can't tell whether that forces me to give up (4), give up (5), or weaken (1).
