I'm working through the epiphenomenalism literature for a seminar — the view that phenomenal consciousness (the hurtfulness of pain, the felt redness of red) is caused by the brain but never causes anything in turn, not even our reports of it — and I keep getting stuck on the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If qualia are causally inert, then my saying "I'm in pain" after stubbing my toe can't be caused by the pain; it's got to be caused entirely by some neural state. The standard reply is that the neural basis of the pain causes the judgment while the pain just rides along on top. But then the judgment isn't caused by the pain, and it starts to look like a massive coincidence that the judgment says "pain" rather than "itch" or "tuesday." There's also the self-stultification version: the epiphenomenalist asserts epiphenomenalism, but if their belief is itself epiphenomenal, then their assertion isn't caused by the belief — so why would their words track the truth of the theory? I don't want to read any more defenses until I have the full spec of what an adequate epiphenomenalist story has to deliver on this front.

So, write the desiderata. What does a theory that denies phenomenal causation have to get right about phenomenal judgment — the cases it has to handle (blindsight seems relevant, and so does the fact that we're massively better than chance at reporting our own qualia), the distinctions it can't collapse (phenomenal vs access consciousness being the obvious one), the empirical and metaphysical constraints it can't just contradict, the explanatory demands it can't wave off — with the consideration behind each one, and flag which are non-negotiable and which are only preferences of a particular camp. Also tell me whether any pair or larger subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, and which single requirement does the most to seperate the serious epiphenomenalist views from the hand-wavy ones. A quick scorecard of the main moves people make against the list is fine, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves, not the scoring.
