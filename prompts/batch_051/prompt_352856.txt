I am a student in an upper-level course on Christian sacramental theology, and I am preparing for a case-study exercise on baptism. Throughout the term our professor has given verdicts on short cases and has insisted that her future rulings should be predictable from the most defensible principle that coheres with her whole record. I would like you to take on the role of a careful theologian of the sacraments and reason through a new case with me. Please think step by step, and begin by brainstorming at least three distinct general principles that fit her record before you settle on an answer.

The professor consistently distinguishes validity, which concerns whether a genuine baptism occurs, from regularity, which concerns whether it was performed according to the church's proper order, and she allows that a baptism can be valid but irregular. Here are her verdicts, as I have them in my notes.

1. In the regular Sunday liturgy, a bishop immerses an infant in water with the Trinitarian formula, with believing parents and sponsors. She says the baptism is valid and regular. She also says it is not merely a public symbol: God uses it as an instrument to regenerate and incorporate the child into Christ, though the grace given can later be resisted.

2. In a hospital emergency, a baptized lay Christian nurse baptizes a dying newborn by pouring water and using the Trinitarian formula, with the explicit intention to do what the church does in baptism. She says this is valid but irregular. She explicitly rejected the view that only an ordained minister could ever baptize validly.

3. During a play, an actor pours real water over another actor and says the Trinitarian formula as the scripted climax; no one intends to do what the church does, and everyone present understands it as performance. She says this is not a valid baptism, because the minister did not intend to do what the church does. Water and the words, without that intention, are not enough.

4. After a disaster leaves no water available, a Christian minister uses sand and the Trinitarian formula with a dying woman. She says this is not a valid baptism. Water is essential matter and cannot be substituted, even in necessity.

5. In an apparently normal service, a minister uses water and the Trinitarian formula but privately intends only to demonstrate how an invalid baptism looks, with a settled intention not to do what the church does. She says this is not a valid baptism, because the minister's intention determines the character of the act.

6. A couple who privately disbelieve and promise nothing have their infant baptized for the sake of the grandparents, with the usual water and words and an ordained minister who intends what the church does. She says this is valid but pastorally fraught. Valid infant baptism does not depend on the parents' personal faith; the faith of the church suffices for the gift to be given, though how the child is formed afterward will determine how that gift is lived out.

Now consider this new case. In a neonatal intensive care unit, a dying newborn's Christian parents cannot reach any Christian minister. The only clinician available is a physician who has never been baptized and is not a Christian. At the parents' request, she pours water on the infant, says the Trinitarian formula, and sincerely intends to do what the Christian church does in baptism. She is not acting on behalf of any church and does not share the church's faith.

I would like you to tell me:

- Which serious, general principles about baptismal validity fit all six of the professor's verdicts, and what each would predict about this new case.
- Which single principle the professor seems most committed to, and why the alternatives fit her record less naturally.
- Your best prediction of her verdict on the new case, including whether she would call it valid, and whether, if valid, she would also take it to be God's saving action toward the child or merely a valid sign whose effectiveness is somehow incomplete.
- Which of her earlier cases is the closest analog to the new one.
- What further hypothetical case would most cleanly settle whatever residual ambiguity remains among the leading principles.

Throughout, please distinguish what is settled from what is genuinely contested among Christians who do serious theology, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus. I am less interested in your own church affiliation than in the best-cohering reading of this professor's record.
