I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm stuck on a scene in my next book. The conceit is that Leibniz gets to spend an evening with a present-day cosmologist, and at one point she explains the many-worlds interpretation to him: roughly, that every quantum branch is equally real, that nothing selects among them, and that all physically possible histories get actualized somewhere. I need Leibniz to respond the way he actually wouldve, given the positions he really held — that there are many possible worlds, each composed of compatible possibilities, but that God, by his wisdom and goodness, chooses the best one; that nothing happens without a sufficient reason; and that unchosen possible worlds exist only as ideas in God's understanding, not as concrete worlds. On those commitments, what would he make of the claim that every possible world is equally actual? Would he hear it as a refutation of his optimism, as a naive confusion, or as something weirdly close to his own view? Where exactly would he resist, and which of his own principles would do the most work? I need enough of the back-and-forth to write the scene without making him a caricature; dont bother with what the cosmologist thinks, just give me his reasoning.
