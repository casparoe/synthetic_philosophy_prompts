I'm putting together a one-year internal fellowship proposal at the lab where I work, and I've got a half-formed idea I need turned into something a review committee will fund. The topic is training models to be understandable rather than merely correct—what I've been calling legibility. I got onto this after watching a customer-facing assistant we fine-tune become much better at giving clear, step-by-step justifications for its answers while its actual success rate on messy edge cases barely moved, and sometimes moved backward. My suspicion is that we treat legibility and correctness as if they were the same target, or at least as if they always pulled in the same direction, and I doubt that. It seems at least possible that a model can be more correct but less legible: the reasoning that actually got it to a right answer on a messy case might be a pile of brittle heuristics that can't be summarized in plain English. And a model can be more legible but less correct: if you penalize it for giving confusing justifications, it may learn to produce clean, confident-sounding rationales that no longer track what actually drove the answer. If that tradeoff is real, a lot of the current "just train it to explain its reasoning" work might be quietly trading one for the other without noticing.

I want you to write the proposal itself (one to two pages, no citations—I'll add those later). It should state the research question precisely, say why the existing train-it-to-explain-itself approaches don't settle whether legibility and correctness can come apart, lay out what I would actually do month by month, and state what would count as the project failing. Also, because I know one reviewer will ask, include a short line somewhere on why a quasi-philosophical question about what it even means for an output to be understandable deserves engineering resources rather than being dismissed as not our job.
