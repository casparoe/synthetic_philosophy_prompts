Last night a friend and I argued at length about the unilateralist's curse: the phenomenon whereby a group of people, any one of whom can bring about some irreversible act on their own, becomes increasingly likely to take that act as the group grows, even when each individual is cautious and the group's average assessment opposes it. Our disagreement was specific. He held that the bad outcome is nearly built into the standard setup, so that the only real remedy is to remove someone's unilateral power, for example by requiring consensus or centralized approval. I held that the outcome depends on a structural feature of the decision situation, one that can be changed while leaving unilateral power fully intact. I would like a case built to settle this.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications.

Set the case in a single, ordinary professional setting — a hospital, a newsroom, an open-source software project, a regulatory office, or something similar. There are at least six agents, each of whom can unilaterally take a costly and irreversible action whose value depends on uncertain facts. Each agent receives a private, noisy but unbiased estimate of the action's value, and each uses a modest, defensible threshold for acting: no one is reckless, overconfident, or knowingly irrational. In fact, the action is worse than doing nothing, and the average of the agents' private estimates is negative, so an aggregate assessment would recommend against the act. Arrange the estimates and thresholds so that, if the agents decided simultaneously and privately in the standard unilateralist's-curse setup, at least one agent would act — that is, the most optimistic private estimate exceeds its holder's threshold. In the full decision situation you describe, nobody acts.

There is one restriction on how you may achieve this. You may vary only a single structural feature of the decision situation: its timing, the order in which agents decide, what each agent can observe of the others' choices before the act becomes final, or something of the same kind. You may not remove or weaken unilateral power, reduce the number of agents, make the act reversible, alter the private signals or make them more accurate, lower any threshold, change the true value of the act, or make the average estimate positive. The point is to keep every standard ingredient of the unilateralist's curse in place and still prevent the bad act.

After the scenario, show your work. First, walk through the reasoning of the most optimistic agent and explain why that agent nevertheless does not act. Second, identify the structural feature that is doing the work, remove it while holding everything else fixed, and show that the curse returns: at least one agent acts, and the act is bad in expectation. Third, state honestly what a defender of the standard unilateralist's-curse analysis would say to disqualify the case, and whether that objection succeeds.
