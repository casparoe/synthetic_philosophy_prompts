I am a graduate TA for an introductory philosophy course with a short unit on language, and next week I have one fifty-minute section to teach Austin's idea that saying is doing. The assigned material is an excerpt on performative and constative utterances, and I already know from the students' response papers roughly what will happen: they will latch onto the performative/constative split as though it were the finished theory, treating I promise or I now pronounce you as a special class of magic words and everything else as ordinary description, and then they will be mildly annoyed to find that Austin spends the rest of the excerpt dismantling the very distinction he opened with. What I want them to leave with is the sense that the performative/constative contrast is a ladder Austin kicks away, and that the replacement machinery — locutionary, illocutionary, perlocutionary — is where the real account lives and why it is an advance rather than a retreat.

Don't explain the theory to me; plan how I should teach it. I need the order of moves: what has to be in place before I can even motivate abandoning the performative/constative line, and what depends on what. I want a single carrying example — a bet, a promise, a christening, an apology, something else — with an argument for why that example rather than the others: what it must let me show at each stage of the sequence, and what it must not quietly suggest. Then map the misconceptions in the order they will actually arise. I suspect, for instance, that students will think performatives cannot be assessed for truth or falsity, that illocutionary and perlocutionary are just two names for an utterance's effects, and that if constatives turn out to be actions too then the original distinction was empty and nothing was ever at stake. Tell me where in the fifty-minute sequence to head each of these off, so I am not cleaning them up afterward.

Finally, give me one question or micro-exercise for the last five minutes that would show whether they have actually grasped the three-level distinction between saying something, doing something in saying it, and doing something by saying it — and tell me explicitly what to leave out at this level, such as Searle's taxonomy, the full doctrine of infelicities, or implicature, and how to say so without making the truncated treatment feel evasive.
