I am a retired population biologist who has come to philosophy late, and the units-of-selection debate has been bothering me because the same word "unit" seems to be doing several jobs at once. Over the last few years I have worked out a diagnostic I call the residual test, and I would like an honest assessment of it rather than an endorsement.

The test runs as follows. Choose a candidate level—gene, individual, kin group, social colony. Identify a trait that varies at that level and appears to track differential persistence or reproduction at that level. Then ask how much of the change in that trait's distribution can be reconstructed from variation, heredity, and fitness differences at the next level down. If all of it can be reconstructed, I call the higher level a statistical unit of selection: it enters the bookkeeping only as a convenient summary. If a residual remains—an association between the higher-level trait and higher-level persistence that cannot be written as any weighted aggregate of lower-level processes—then I call the higher level a causal unit of selection.

My central claim is that much of the units-of-selection dispute would dissolve if this distinction were enforced. Genes emerge as causal units almost automatically, since their dynamics cannot be reconstructed from any lower level. Organisms count as causal units only when their trait-fitness relations cannot be partitioned into additive lower-level effects. Groups count as causal units only when a group-level effect survives after controlling for every lower-level confound; in the social spiders and colonial ascidians I have used as test cases, it usually does not. I realize this sounds like statistics rather than metaphysics, and that is deliberate: my proposal is that the unit of selection is not something selection is "about," but something a correct variance decomposition cannot eliminate.

I have three questions. First, is the distinction coherent, and has it already been worked out under some name I have not yet encountered? Second, what does the residual test get right, and where precisely does it mislead? Third, what kind of biological case would cause it to misclassify the level of selection? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
