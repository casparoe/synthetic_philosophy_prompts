I run a monthly discussion group for fourteen adults with no formal background in philosophy — people who enjoy thinking slowly about small corners of everyday life, and who would not respond well to a lecture. We have ninety minutes, and I want a session built around something I have been turning over on my own.

The thing I cannot shake is this. A person says, "I'll have it to you by Tuesday," or "I'll be there at six," and then they deliver on Wednesday, or they arrive at 6:20. From the inside, the lapse hardly feels like a broken promise: the thing did get done, the meeting did happen, and the lateness sits in a separate, softer category. From the outside, though, the lateness is the experience. The person waiting has bent their own schedule around a fixed point and ended up holding an unplanned stretch of time they never agreed to absorb. And the same person who shrugs off their own lateness would usually object if a bank, a repair person, or a colleague made them wait in exactly the same way. I also notice a third thought that tends to slip by unstated: perhaps what is damaged is not the promise itself, but the other person's willingness to rely on you next time.

So there seem to be at least three pictures of one small event. On the first, a small promise is still a promise in the strict sense, and being late breaks it just as cleanly as refusing to do the thing, only smaller. On the second, lateness is not a broken promise at all but a failure of something distinct — consideration, perhaps, or leaving a person free to make other plans. On the third, the real harm is to reliability: to what others can expect from you going forward.

I want the group to come away seeing the first picture as a live option rather than as a harsh pose: that a promise's pull does not shrink in proportion to the stakes, and that one's own lateness looks very different from the receiving end. I do not want them to end up at a slogan like "lateness is bad." I want them to feel how easy it is, from inside the lapse, to honestly believe nothing much was owed, and how different the same event looks when you are the one waiting.

Design a structured activity — a game, a role-play, or a staged exchange — that runs within the ninety minutes and requires no outside reading and no real money. Give me the rules, the materials, and a payoff scheme built from points, chips, or clock time itself. Arrange the task so that each person is, at some point, the one who is late and, at another, the one kept waiting, without that symmetry being announced in advance. Include a timeline for setting up, running, and debriefing the exercise, and a debrief that maps what happened back onto the three pictures above — but no lecture up front; the idea should arrive through the activity and only be named afterward.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with fourteen adults in ninety minutes, and how I should handle it in the moment, not merely afterward. And be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce: plausibly a game with points cannot carry the real weight of a promise made to someone who genuinely needs the thing, or the way a real promise stays in force across days rather than minutes. Tell me what I will have to supply in the debrief, honestly, instead of pretending the game did all the work.

Finally, if the framing is confused — if it is wrong to run lateness and promising together, or wrong to treat "I'll be there at six" as a promise at all, or wrong to think a game with points can capture any of this — say so plainly. I would rather be told the task is badly posed than receive a polished activity that smuggles in my conclusion. If that is the case, tell me what the closest honest exercise would be, or how the session should be rethought.
