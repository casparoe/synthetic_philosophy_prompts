Act as a professional philosopher of cognitive science with a specialty in the unity of consciousness. I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've been trying to figure out what makes an information-processing system count as one conscious subject rather than several, or none. Split-brain cases look like the right stress test. Please think through what follows carefully, begin by brainstorming candidate principles before you commit, and then work back and forth between candidate principles and my concrete verdicts until they hang together as well as they can—and tell me honestly where they will not.

Here are my gut verdicts on a set of cases.

Case 1 (full split). A patient whose corpus callosum has been fully severed is shown a word in the left visual field, so the input goes only to the right hemisphere. Asked what they saw, they report seeing nothing. Seconds later, with the left hand hidden behind a screen, they select the object named by that word from among several distractors. My verdict: the right hemisphere consciously saw the word, but the speaking person in front of me did not. I want to say there were two simultaneous streams of consciousness in that head, one of which could not talk.

Case 2 (blindsight). A patient with damage to primary visual cortex reports being completely blind on one side, yet can reliably point toward a light flashed on that side and can guess the orientation of a line well above chance. My verdict: there is no visual consciousness there at all, not even a weak or hidden one. The channel guiding the pointing is unconscious processing. This feels importantly different from Case 1, even though both combine a verbal report of not seeing with accurate non-verbal behavior.

Case 3 (alien hand). A patient buttoning their shirt with the right hand keeps being undone by their own left hand, which unbuttons the same buttons. The left hand's motions look purposeful rather than random, but the patient insists the hand will not obey them and feels foreign. My verdict: there is still exactly one person and one stream of consciousness. The left hand is a recalcitrant subsystem, not a second subject.

Case 4 (highway driving). A healthy person drives thirty minutes in traffic, changing lanes and braking appropriately, while completely absorbed in planning a work project. Afterward they remember almost nothing of the drive. My verdict: they were still conscious of the driving, but in a degraded or peripheral way, and there was still only one stream.

Case 5 (slow partial transfer). Imagine a patient with only part of the callosal fibers cut, where visual information can still get from one hemisphere to the other, but only slowly and unreliably. A word shown to the right hemisphere can sometimes be named after several seconds of effort, but only about half the time. My verdict: this is a genuinely intermediate case. I'm pulled to say there is a single heavily degraded stream rather than two streams connected by a weak link, but I would not defend that hard.

I tried to capture all of this with one candidate principle: a content is conscious just in case it is available to a single global workspace for reasoning, report, and the control of action. That seems to handle the blindsight verdict and perhaps the driving verdict, but it appears to conflict with Case 1, since the word is not available for report by the speaking subject. So I suspect I am missing a distinction.

My instinct is that at a few bits per second the hemispheres are still two subjects, and that with a high-bandwidth naturalistic link they would become one.
