I'm a philosophy professor prepping a unit on the School of Names for an ancient philosophy survey, and I want to make sure I actually understand the White Horse Dialogue before I try to teach it. I'm not a China person, and this passage keeps throwing me. It's from Gongsun Long, White Horse Dialogue (in the translation on Wikipedia):

[Advocate] "If one seeks a horse, yellow and black horses may all be delivered. If one seeks a white horse, yellow and black horses may not be delivered. Suppose a white horse were [just] a horse. Then what is sought [in the two cases] would be the same. If what is sought were the same, then 'white' would not differ from 'horse.' If what is sought does not differ, how is it that yellow and black horses are acceptable [in one case] and unacceptable [in the other]? That acceptable and unacceptable are mutually opposed is clear. Therefore yellow and black horses are the same in that they can answer to 'there is a horse,' but cannot answer to 'there is a white horse.' This is a white horse's not being a horse: it is certain."

What I'd like help with: is the advocate actually offering an argument here, or just playing with two senses of "horse" and "same"? The bit about seeking a horse versus seeking a white horse looks like it's doing the real work, but I can't tell whether it's a genuine point about how terms pick things out or just a rhetorical trick about what counts as "answering to" a request. If I wanted to give my students the most charitable reconstruction, what exactly is the conclusion and what are the premises? And where does the argument go wrong, if it does?

Also, please seperate what's actually settled in the scholarship from what's genuinely contested, and don't present one interpretive camp as the consensus. I'd rather tell my students "here's the live debate" than teach them a neat answer that specialists would fight about.
