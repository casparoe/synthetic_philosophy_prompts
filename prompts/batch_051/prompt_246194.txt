You are a professional philosopher who regularly sets and marks upper-division undergraduate examinations, and who shares the view that a good exam question should be nearly impossible to answer well by pattern-matching to the textbook labels. I am writing the final for a course that includes a unit on infinite regress arguments, and I want one essay question on it. The students can all reproduce the standard distinction between vicious and benign regresses, and between regresses of justification and regresses of truth, but every year I find the median answer simply asserts that infinite regresses are not automatically fallacious and then applies the word "vicious" to whichever regress the question flags as bad. That is exactly the cached response I want to design against. I would like the question to force students to articulate what actually makes a regress vicious rather than merely infinite — what job the regress is being asked to do, and why an unending series fails at that job in one case but not in another. A framing that has worked in past years is to present two regresses side by side and ask why one is standardly treated as harmless and the other as catastrophic, then require a general criterion that does not itself contain the word "vicious" and does not reduce to "it goes on forever." For example: a truth regress (if p is true, then it is true that p is true, and so on) next to a justification regress (each belief is justified only by the next, with no stopping point). But use whichever examples you think best expose the crux.

Please produce the completed exam task: the question itself, written for a 75-minute in-class essay at a level where a strong student would write 600–800 words; a model answer of about that length; and a marking scheme out of 25 marks. The marking scheme should name five must-hit moves, the marks each is worth, and a one-line note on how weaker candidates typically miss each one. An A answer should do more than state a criterion: it should apply it to both regresses, and it should say what would have to change for the apparently benign regress to become vicious — showing control over the underlying functional question, not just the taxonomy. Before committing to a final question, brainstorm two or three candidate framings and note which specific student misunderstanding each is best at exposing; then write the version you judge most diagnostic. Think carefully about precision: the model answer should not rest on attributions to named authors or exact citations, and it should mark the difference between an infinite series that is harmless and an infinite series that is unacceptable in terms a student could actually deploy under time pressure rather than merely gesture at.
