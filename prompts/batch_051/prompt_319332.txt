I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've somehow gotten myself booked to give a seminar in a cognitive science department next month. The talk is a defense of a claim Ive been developing while covering AI: that today's large language models are best classed as Popperian creatures in Dennett's old ladder, but not Gregorian ones, and that the distinction is doing real explanatory work rather than just labeling. For context: the ladder goes Darwinian creatures (design fixed by natural selection), Skinnerian ones (behavior shaped by reinforcement), Popperian ones (internal models that let bad hypotheses die instead of the organism), and Gregorian ones (creatures that use external thinking tools like words and diagrams to do their thinking for them). My argument in one breath: LLMs do a version of generate-and-test that fits the Popperian rung, but they don't actually use external mind-tools in the Gregory sense, they only simulate tool-use in text inside a single trained network, so calling them Gregorian stretches the idea until it stops meaning much. The room is going to be mostly cognitive scientists and philosophers of mind: two of the faculty are serious Dennett fans who know the ladder inside out, three or four think the whole thing is a folk taxonomy that cognitive science outgrew in the nineties, and a couple of grad students do interpretability work and will want me to say something about what's inside the model, not just what it does. I dont want a defense of my view, I want a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'm actually going to get from this specific room, in roughly the order theyll come at me, phrased the way those people would put them, with what I should concede and where I should hold my ground, and a note on which objections land hardest. Also flag the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices. Skip the generic objections; give me the ones this particular audience would raise.
