I've gotten weirdly obsessed with the old debate over whether markets make people better or worse — the idea that commerce softens manners versus the worry that it turns everything into a cold calculation. It stopped being abstract for me six months ago when I started selling my leatherwork online, and I've actually felt my own character shifting in both directions at once.

The criteria I've been using for saying market activity civilizes a person are: (1) it forces you to deal repeatedly and non-violently with strangers, (2) it channels self-interest into serving other people's wants, (3) it rewards honesty, reliability, and reputation, and (4) it pushes you to see things from the customer's point of view. The corruption criteria I'd use are: (a) making you treat relationships as transactions, (b) training you to put prices on things that shouldn't be priced, and (c) making you see other people, and yourself, as instruments.

Here's the borderline case, and it's me: I've become noticeably more patient and dependable — I answer the same dumb question without sighing, I hit every deadline, I've stopped assuming buyers are out to scam me. But I also caught myself the other day converting a favor my neighbor did for me into what it saved me in hourly rate, and Ive started dodging hangouts with people who aren't useful to the business. So: does my case qualify as the market civilizing me, corrupting me, both, or neither? Act as a philosopher of markets and character, think carefully, and go criterion by criterion. Flag where my criteria underdetermine the verdict, and say whether the case shows the criteria themselves need redrawing.
