Suppose a country passes a law loosely inspired by Dennett's worry about counterfeit people. (Dennett's concern, more or less, was that the damage from an AI passing itself off as a person isn't just the one-off deception; it's that it erodes the standing background assumption that anything addressing you in humanlike ways has an accountable human somewhere behind it.) The law says: any AI system that interacts with the public in text, voice, or video must be registered in a public ledger with its operator; must announce in its first message that it is an AI; and must embed a cryptographic watermark in every output. There's a safe harbor: as long as the system says the exact words "I'm an AI, not a person" at the start of each conversation and repeats it whenever a user asks, the operator can't be fined for that interaction. Synthetic media—deepfakes, cloned voices—must be watermarked as well, and distributing unwatermarked synthetic media is a offense. Treat this as a mechanism rather than a moral position. Walk through how it actually plays out: what companies do with the placement and wording of the disclosure once it becomes a legal checkbox, how users and scammers route around it, what happens to AI companions and to services that keep a low-wage human in the loop mostly to preserve the legal fiction of a "person," where the first serious fraud shows up, and which failure modes can actually be fixed without gutting the scheme.
