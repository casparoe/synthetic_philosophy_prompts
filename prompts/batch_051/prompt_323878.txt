I am drafting a historical novel set in early sixteenth-century Italy, centered on a condottiere who seizes a small city-state, holds it for several years, and then loses it. My drafts keep oscillating between two renderings of the same events. In one, his rise reads as pure mastery: he reads the city's factions, strikes in the brief interval between two plots against him, and breaks his word only when keeping it would leave him exposed. In the other, the same events read as little more than weather: the rivals he feared die of fever, a hostile league dissolves over an unrelated dispute, and the one miscalculation that undoes him was invisible to anyone at the time. I do not want the novel to become either a sermon about the all-powerful agent or a tale of blind chance. Is there a defensible way to think about the relation between a person's qualities and their fortunes that lets me show his ability and his luck as both real, with neither one explaining the other away? What would that view look like in the actual construction of scenes and in the shaping of the character's rise and fall?
