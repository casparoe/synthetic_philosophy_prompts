I've noticed a split in my own head, and it seems to be in a lot of people's heads too, between how I treat infinity in math and how I treat it in the world. In math class I don't blink at being told that their are exactly as many even numbers as natural numbers, because you can pair each natural number n with 2n. I also accept that a proper part of an infinite set can be the same size as the whole—that's just how Cantor's arithmetic works, not some trick. But the second the same idea gets dressed up as a hotel, I reach for the brakes. Hilbert's Hotel has infinitely many rooms, all occupied, and the manager supposedly makes room for a new guest by shifting the person in room 1 to room 2, room 2 to room 3, and so on forever. My gut says no: if every room is full, a new guest can't fit without two people sharing or someone leaving, and maybe an actually infinite hotel can't exist at all.

So the asymmetry is this: I'm a Cantorian about sets and basically a finitist about hotels. Is there a principle that justifies treating those two differently—say, that actual infinities are allowed as abstract objects but impossible as concrete or physical ones—or is the hotel just a case where my finite intuitions haven't caught up to math I already accept? I want the strongest principle you can find for the split, and an honest verdict on whether I should keep the asymmetry or give up one side of it. If you defend the split, make sure it handles the nearby cases: an infinite past, a queue with infinitely many people where everyone steps forward one spot, and a library with infinitely many books. If it can't, tell me straight.
