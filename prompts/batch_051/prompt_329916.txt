I'm in a philosophy of mind course and I've basically talked myself into epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness — the view that qualia are real but causally inert. The thing everyone throws at me is the paradox of phenomenal judgment: my saying "that hurts" sure seems to be caused by the pain, but if pain is epiphenomenal it can't cause anything. I take the standard reply seriously: the underlying physical state causes both the quale and the report, so the correlation doesn't show phenomenal causation. I want to stress-test this, not just rehearse the stock objections. Act as a professional philosopher of mind who's sympathetic to epiphenomenalism but honest about its weak spots. Start by brainstorming candidate defeaters, then map what would have to be true for the view to be false: which findings, arguments, cases, or introspective observations would be decisive, which would be merely akward, which ones a committed epiphenomenalist would conceed as counting against the view, and whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability (for instance by always saying the common-cause story handles any apparent case of phenomenal causation). Also say what, if anything, epiphenomenalism predicts that interactionist or illusionist rivals don't, and whether it risks collapsing into illusionism once the contents of phenomenal reports are explained without qualia. For what it's worth, I'd find it most persuasive if someone showed that the content of phenomenal judgments — not just their occurrence — can't be accounted for by the underlying physical states alone.
