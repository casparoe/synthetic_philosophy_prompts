I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about why the same desire for more life is treated as sensible in one corner of medicine and as a problem in another. She kept calling the whole thing "deathism" — the way people moralise a normal lifespan — and by the end I had to admit I couldn't pin down what view was actually doing the work.

Here's the contrast that's been bugging me since. On the one hand, a health service will treat a forty-five-year-old who wants to live to a hundred as perfectly reasonable, and will happily fund research that pushes life expectancy up; nobody tells a middle-aged patient to come to terms with their mortality. On the other, in palliative and hospice care a "good death" is very often described in terms of acceptance: the patient stops fighting, lets go, finds peace. A dying patient who keeps pressing about what else might be tried, or says outright that they want more time, is likely to be talked about as "in denial" and steered gently toward acceptance. So the desire for more life is rational at one age and something to be managed psychologically at another, and the line looks like its tracking a natural or normal lifespan rather than whether any further good life was actually on offer.

What I want to know is what that pattern presupposes about the value of death and the shape of a good life. Does it commit us to the idea that there's a right time to die, after which death stops being a deprivation and becomes something healthy people welcome? Or is the whole arrangement compatible with the plain deprivation view — death is bad whenever it costs you good life you would otherwise have had — with the hospice emphasis on acceptance being just a pragmatic response to treatments no longer working? And if we really dropped the presupposition, say if we thought a patient's wanting more time was rational all the way down even when we couldn't grant it, what would end-of-life care have to stop doing? If I'm lumping together two practices that aren't really one thing, or if "presupposes" is the wrong lens and I've misdescribed how palliative care talks, say so plainly; I'd rather be told the question's broken than get a tidy answer built on a wobbly premise.
