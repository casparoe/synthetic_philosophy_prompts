So I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and after a bunch of interviews with game developers and a couple of philosophers about whether anything you do inside a game can be genuinely wrong, I've landed on a homemade idea I want someone to kick the tires on before I build a long feature around it.

I've been calling it accrued standing. The thought is that a game character's moral status isn't fixed by what the character is—code, fiction, whatever—but grows as a player routes their agency through it. The procedurally spawned bandit you knife in hour one and never think about again is morally weightless. But the horse you named, fed, and kept alive for two hundred hours, or the town you built house by house in a management sim—by the time the game lets you neglect it, sell it, or kill it off for convenience, the choice has a moral texture the bandit never had. My claim is that this extra weight is real, and it attaches to the relationship rather then just to you (your character, your habits, the sunk cost in your head). The character has become the kind of thing you can wrong—not because it gained sentience or interests, but because your agency has been routed through it long enough.

Two examples I keep returning to. First, a creature in a raising sim that you deliberately abandon versus an identical creature you never adopted: most people feel a difference, and I want to say the feeling is tracking something, not just nostalgia. Second, killing a story NPC the game made you bond with as a mechanic versus killing the same NPC model in an optional rampage with no history behind it.

So: is this a real concept or just a metaphor with a name and a vibe? Has someone already worked it out under another label? I keep bumping into the gamers dilemma, but that seems adjacent rather than the same thing. And where does accrued standing break—what does it count as a wrong that plainly isn't, and what does it fail to count as a wrong that plainly is? Be as candid as you can; if the whole idea is just virtue ethics in a trench coat, say so.
