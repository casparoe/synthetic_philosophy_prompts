Among the people I know who take strong longtermism seriously—the view that shaping the long-run future is a dominant moral priority because of the potentially astronomical number of lives at stake—the simulation argument commands a kind of attention that other radical skeptical hypotheses do not. Several of them say that Bostrom-style reasoning makes it appreciably probable that we are living in an ancestor civilization's simulation, and that this should temper far-future ambitions: our apparent descendants might never come to exist, the simulation might be shut down or redirected, and the future that matters might be decided entirely outside our reach. Yet the same people do not treat the brain-in-a-vat hypothesis, a Cartesian demon, or the possibility that the world was created five minutes ago with false records as reasons to revise their far-future priorities. They call those bare skeptical scenarios, too unconstrained to act on, while treating the simulation hypothesis as concrete enough to affect cause selection.

I have been trying to state the principle they are tracking, and I cannot make it come out clean. Is the whole difference that the simulation argument, unlike the vat or the demon, purports to assign a conditional probability to its scenario on the basis of assumptions about civilizations and computation? If so, can that difference carry the normative weight it is being asked to carry, or is it a somewhat superficial feature of one vivid scenario that happens to arrive with numbers attached? Perhaps the simulation hypothesis differs more deeply because it offers a positive mechanism by which the apparent world is produced and controlled, rather than a bare failure of evidence. Give me the strongest principled defense you can find for treating the simulation hypothesis as specially relevant to far-future projects, and then offer an honest verdict: should this asymmetry be kept, or is it an inconsistency that far-future-oriented thinkers have learned to live with?
