I cover machine learning and philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and lately I've noticed that 'there's no free lunch' gets made to do very different work depending on who's saying it. A researcher told me it means no model beats every other model on every data set, so choosing a model is really choosing a bias, not finding the best one full stop. A philosopher I interviewed then used it to say induction can't be justified from the data itself, because any learner that succeeds on some worlds has to fail on others, so the problem of induction is inescapable in some formal sense. An economist later claimed it means all optimisation algorithms perform equally well when averaged over all possible problems, which struck me as either trivially true or obviously false and I couldnt tell which. I'd like a menu of the distinct claims that slogan could be expressing, from the precise mathematical ones to the loose metaphor, with a note on which are platitudes, which are subtle and defensible, and which are just wrong. And for each of the three people, which reading of the slogan would actually support what they claimed? When someone uses the phrase to dismiss the hunt for a universal method or a presuppositionless induction, which reading is doing the real work?
