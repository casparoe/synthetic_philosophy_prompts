A close relative of mine is in the middle of a long and uncertain course of treatment, and I have been struck by how differently the people around us talk about hope. One side treats sustained optimism as a duty, something we owe to the patient and to each other, and suspects that my habit of following the numbers wherever they lead is a form of coldness. The other side treats cheerful expectation as an epistemic failure, a way of protecting ourselves with wishful thinking, and suspects that the family's hopefulness is making us worse at absorbing what the doctors are actually telling us. I find both reactions intelligible, and I keep moving between them. The disagreement feels deeper than temperament; it looks like a disagreement about which kinds of norms ought to govern a hopeful person's mind.

I would like you to write a dialogue between two invented philosophers, each defending one side of this disagreement at its strongest. One should defend the view that hope of the relevant kind — understood as a disposition to form optimistic expectations about outcomes that matter — is best classified as an epistemic vice, because it systematically degrades inquiry. The other should defend the view that hope is a moral virtue, because it sustains the agency, fidelity, and care that despair would extinguish. Both characters should be equally capable, and neither should be a strawman; they should respond to what the other has actually said rather than trading position statements.

Make the dialogue work through at least the following turns. First, whether hope requires optimistic belief at all: the virtue-side may argue that hope is desire plus uncertainty, so the evidentialist objection — roughly, that one's confidence should track one's evidence — never lands, and the vice-side should reply that this either hollows out the virtue or relocates the problem to the question of which desires one permits oneself. Second, the self-fulfilling prophecy argument: hopeful expectation sustains the action that makes good outcomes more likely; the reply should press whether that is a practical reason to act hopefully rather than an epistemic reason to believe hopefully — unless one accepts something like pragmatic or moral encroachment, the view that how much evidence is required can depend on what is at stake, in which case the next turn should ask whether high moral stakes would license any comforting belief in high-stakes contexts and where the principled limit lies. Third, the interpersonal dimension: what we owe to others — hope or honesty — and whether respecting someone can require withholding the so-called realistic outlook. Do not let them settle early on a definitional truce; if they come to suspect that their disagreement is partly about what hope is, that should arrive only at the end and should feel earned.
