Since I retired from a career in chemistry, I have joined a reading group that is working through mid-century philosophy of science, and last month we read Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions alongside a selection of Popper. Our discussion turned into a long argument between two members of the group that none of us could referee, and I would like your help going through it carefully.

The question was whether science remains rational during a scientific revolution. Margaret, a former geologist, argued that on Kuhn's account it does not — at least not in the sense of "rational" that Popper and the logical empiricists had in mind. She said the decision to abandon one paradigm for another cannot be settled by the old paradigm's own standards, because those standards are partly what is being abandoned, and the new paradigm's standards are not yet authoritative; from inside the transition, she claimed, the choice presents itself more like a conversion than an inference. David, a former engineer, replied that Popper had already secured rationality at exactly that point: the old theory has been falsified, so rejecting it is rational, and the new theory is accepted provisionally insofar as it survives severe tests. Margaret answered that this falsificationist machinery breaks down precisely during revolutionary change, because what counts as a decisive observation and which auxiliary hypotheses one holds fixed are themselves in dispute; a defender of the old paradigm can absorb an anomaly, and an advocate of the new one can dismiss an apparent failure. David responded that this objection proves too much, since even normal science requires such judgments about auxiliaries and the severity of tests, yet no one denies that normal science is rational. He added that Kuhn himself grants that scientists across paradigms share values such as accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, and fruitfulness, and that theory choice is rational because it is guided by those shared values even though they do not determine a unique answer. Margaret rejoined that this was exactly her point: once a list of shared values underdetermines the choice and different scientists weight those values differently, what remains is judgment rather than rule-following — so either one is quietly smuggling back in a Popperian rule that no longer exists, or one is stretching the word "rational" until it covers something much thinner than what Popper defended.

I have been turning the exchange over since the meeting. I suspect the dispute is partly about what "rational" should mean in such cases — rule-governed by shared standards, guided by shared values, or merely non-arbitrary and corrigible — and partly about whether rationality belongs to the individual scientist's decision or to the community's long-run filtering. But I could not settle it, and I would rather not referee by intuition. Would you please go through their exchange point by point, say which specific claims hold up and which overreach, and then say who, if either, is more right overall? I would also appreciate it if you would say how confident you are in that verdict and identify the point on which you are least sure.
