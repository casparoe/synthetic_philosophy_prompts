my reading group has been going at each other for a year about how big social norms actually die, and two people in it have finally agreed to do one of those adversarial-collaboration exercises where the output is a document both of them have to be able to sign. one of them is basically an Appiah person: her view is that practices like dueling and slavery are held in place by honor, and a moral revolution happens when the honor code flips—when a practice stops conferring status and starts bringing shame, so dueling becomes faintly ridiculous and slave-owning becomes dishonorable rather than respectable. the other is an Eric Williams person: he thinks the honor talk and the high-minded antislavery arguments are mostly epiphenomenal, and the real engine is material interests. on his telling, British slavery ended because the West Indian sugar economy stopped paying and a rising industrial-capitalist order wanted free labor and free trade. they use two cases: the withering of the aristocratic duel in Britain over the first half of the nineteenth century, and the abolition of slavery in the British empire under the 1833 Act. each of them can tell a smooth story about one case and a much more patched-together story about the other, but neither of them will volunteer which one their worried about.

Write the joint statement a good-faith collaboration between these two should produce, with these parts: (1) a numbered agreed-claims section—not just boilerplate facts, but substantive things both camps can sign about what actually needs explaining (the timing, who changed, what participants said versus what they did); (2) a disagreements table in which each row states the exact remaining disagreement in wording each side would accept for its own view, with no strawmen; (3) at least three cruxes—sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them—and for each crux a concrete case or piece of evidence both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. there's a third person in the group who keeps insisting the moral arguments themselves did the real work, and both of these two are tired of him—so keep the document between the two, but if you notice that some of their agreement only holds because they're both defining themselves against him, say so. If part of their dispute looks verbal—say, different senses of 'explains,' or one side offering a necessary condition while claiming it as sufficient—point out which part and why. Don't adjudicate who is right; the goal is to make the residual disagreement exact while keeping everything they can actually co-sign.
