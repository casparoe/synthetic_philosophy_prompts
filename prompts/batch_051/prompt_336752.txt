I direct an upper-level undergraduate ethics seminar, usually fourteen to eighteen students, and we have a ninety-minute session coming up on the ethics of intimate relationships. I want a structured, heavily choreographed in-class activity centered on one question: whether sexual exclusivity is best analyzed as a convention of a particular form of relationship, revisable by the parties under conditions they themselves specify, or as a moral requirement that binds independently of any agreement. I would like the students to feel the grip of the view they are inclined to reject before they formulate a position. Concretely, my plan is to have small groups draft a short "constitution" for a hypothetical committed relationship — default rules about exclusivity, a procedure for revising those rules, and criteria for what counts as breach — and then to push each group through a simulated renegotiation crisis in which one party follows the agreed procedure to open the relationship and the other party objects. The point is to get the groups to notice where their own reactive language stops tracking their stated theory: they can explicitly treat exclusivity as a revisable default and yet condemn a procedurally compliant revision as betrayal. I want that gap to surface in what they say, not in a lecture from me.

Please give me a runnable design: how to form the groups, what materials to distribute, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, the exact fact pattern for the renegotiation crisis to hand each group, and a debrief that maps the moments of the activity onto the distinction between conventional and moral norms while also pressing on why agreement might or might not generate obligations, why a default being revisable does not make revising it costless, and how to tell whether a resentment of the outcome is aimed at the procedure or at the substance. The students should be given nothing up front that names the distinction or the thesis; the vocabulary should come only after the exercise. Be candid about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — vague constitutions, groups that treat it as a joke or refuse the premise, the risk of the activity invading students' own relationship choices — and explain how to absorb each failure into the debrief. I also want an honest account of which element of the phenomenon the exercise cannot reproduce; my suspicion is that simulated stakes cannot recreate the phenomenology of actual jealousy or felt betrayal, and that the facilitator will have to supply that texture from the students' own reported experiences without making the session confessional. Tell me how to supply it carefully.
