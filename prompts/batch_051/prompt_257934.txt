I've developed a rule over the last few years, and my partner thinks its kind of harsh. When someone says they "really wanted" to do something but didn't — go to the gym, apply for the job, finish the essay, call their mom — I don't file it under weakness of will. I treat the non-action as the better evidence of what they actually valued at that moment, and the later "I really wanted to" as a story the long-run self tells to avoid admitting the preference flipped. I apply it to myself too: if I keep not meditating despite saying I want to, I conclude I don't actually want to very much, rather than that my will is weak.

My partner says I'm basically doing freshman-year revealed-preference theory on everyone and ignoring hyperbolic discounting: the Monday self that plans to go to the gym and the Wednesday self that stays on the couch are both real, and my rule writes off the Monday self every time. Last week we argued about whether my rule would have said her dad was "revealed not to care" about quitting smoking, even though he did eventually quit. But I don't want to go back to treating everyone's stated intentions as the truth either, because people are constantly lying to themselves about what they want.

So is my heuristic defensible? When is the non-action actually better evidence than the stated intention, and when does the rule break down? Is there a restatement that keeps the no-excuses part while not pretending a genuine preference reversal never happened?
