I'm taking an upper-level seminar this term on promising, trust, and the ethics of everyday commitment, and last week's discussion ended in a standoff I haven't been able to break. Two classmates were arguing about a friend of ours who is chronically five to fifteen minutes late to casual one-on-one arrangements—coffee, library work sessions—after saying things like "See you at three." No one thinks the stakes are high; the question is what kind of moral failure, if any, the lateness is.

Dana argued that it is the same kind of thing as breaking a small promise, just repeated. Saying "see you at three" gives the other person an assurance that they reasonably rely on, even if only in a small way, and arriving late without warning disappoints an expectation the speaker deliberately created. That makes each instance a small wrong; a pattern of such wrongs doesn't become harmless, any more than repeatedly shortchanging a customer becomes fine once everyone knows the shopkeeper does it. Amir argued that casual scheduling is not full-blooded promising. In that register, he said, "see you at three" functions more like a coordination proposal than an undertaking to be somewhere punctually no matter what. People in this friendship do not actually treat ten minutes as a betrayal unless they have explicitly staked something on punctuality—a job interview or a train connection—and that is exactly what distinguishes those cases. He added that once a relationship's track record of lateness is common knowledge, the reasonable content of the utterance shifts: it comes to mean something like "I'll aim for three and may well be a bit late," and so the same words no longer create the entitlement Dana wants to ascribe.

Dana replied that this lets a bad track record rewrite the norm that condemns it. If the content of an assurance is hostage to the speaker's own history of failing to honor it, then repeated disrespect weakens the standard rather than triggering it, and that gets the direction of explanation backwards; moral obligations are supposed to judge our patterns, not be eroded by them. Amir answered that Dana was conflating two things: a person's reliability is a matter of character and can be criticized as a vice, but promissory obligation is different—it tracks the rights we have actually given others. His view doesn't excuse lateness, he said; it classifies it as a failure of reliability rather than a breach, except where punctuality was made a condition of the arrangement.

The seminar ended before anyone worked out what general distinction would sort the casual case from the formal one. I'm not asking whether the friend is blameworthy in some vague sense. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: which claims are right, which trade on a conflation, and what principle or distinction would most cleanly settle the underlying disagreement.
