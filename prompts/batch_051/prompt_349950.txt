I run an after-school philosophy and game-theory society at my school, with about sixteen members aged 16–18, and I have a sixty-minute slot. I want to run a session on program equilibrium, but I would rather not open with a miniature lecture, since most members have not met the Prisoner's Dilemma beyond the basic two-by-two table, if at all.

The idea I want them to feel, without hearing its name first, is the following. In an ordinary one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, defection dominates: whatever the other player does, you fare better by defecting, so two well-informed self-interested players land on mutual defection even though both would prefer mutual cooperation. The situation changes if the players stop choosing bare actions and each submit a decision rule that can examine the other player's rule before deciding what to do. A rule of the form "I cooperate just in case the other player's submitted rule is identical to mine" makes mutual cooperation self-enforcing: if both sides submit it, both cooperate and receive the reward; if one side secretly switches to a rule that always defects, the other side notices the difference and defects in response, so the switcher receives the punishment for mutual defection rather than the temptation payoff. In other words, once choices become transparent, mutually readable conditional rules rather than fixed actions, cooperation can become an equilibrium. The takeaway should be structural rather than moralistic: the move from fixed actions to transparent conditional rules changes what a self-interested player has reason to do, without any appeal to altruism or reputation.

Please design a runnable classroom activity and debrief that makes this shift tangible. It should have concrete rules, groups or roles, a payoff scheme that needs no real money, and two phases: first a phase in which students choose bare actions and are likely to converge on defection, and second a phase in which groups submit written decision rules that may refer to the other group's rule, so that the cooperative possibility emerges from the mechanics and, ideally, groups discover something like the matching rule above for themselves rather than having it handed to them.
