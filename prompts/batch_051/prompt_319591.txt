In my course on intergenerational justice, we keep circling around the discounting of future costs and benefits, and I am trying to pin down exactly what the standard practice commits an institution to. When government agencies do cost-benefit analysis of regulations, they routinely apply a positive annual discount rate to future costs and benefits, so that a benefit arriving fifty years from now is entered into the analysis as worth only a small fraction of an identical benefit arriving today; a rate of three percent per year shrinks a benefit fifty years off by a factor of roughly four, and a rate of seven percent shrinks it by a factor of roughly thirty. Yet many of the same agencies also issue sustainability pledges and statements about responsibility to future generations.

What I would like to know is what philosophical view of time and value this practice actually presupposes. Does applying a positive discount rate to future harms and benefits commit the agency to pure time preference, the view that later welfare matters less simply because it occurs later? Or can the practice be read as presupposing only an opportunity-cost claim about what the resources could earn if invested, together with some empirical expectation that future people will be richer? Please reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with, and assess whether the different parts of the practice—discounting dollars, discounting lives or well-being, and using the same or different approaches over short and multi-century horizons—presuppose consistent things. Finally, if the analysis were instead built on the view that the mere timing of welfare has no value significance in itself, what exactly in the standard practice would have to change, and could cost-benefit analysis still function without it? Please write your answer in American English.
