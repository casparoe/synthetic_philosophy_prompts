If a friend says "I know leaving him is the right thing to do, and I'm not going to do it," we all treat that as an obvious rational failing — textbook akrasia. But if the same friend says "I believe my flight leaves at seven, even though I know my evidence really doesn't support that," or "I'm convinced he's being honest, though I can tell my reasoning is just wishful thinking," philosophers are genuinely divided: some call this epistemic akrasia and think it's every bit as irrational, while others think it can be perfectly rational, even required, because you should follow your first-order evidence rather than your beliefs about it. Is there a principled difference between the action case and the belief case that earns the asymmetry — maybe that belief aims at truth while action aims at value, or that you can't believe at will anyway — or is the asymmetry a mistake? If you defend it, stress-test it against the case where someone accurately says: my evidence says p, and I'm going to believe not-p anyway.
