I'm a philosophy professor, and I've been canvasing my own gut reactions about a basic question that keeps tripping me up: when exactly should I defer to what my future self will believe? I have a loose starting principle and a bunch of cases. I'd like you to test the principle against the cases, repair it if you can, and tell me which of my verdicts, if any, I should abandon.

The loose principle is: your present credence in a proposition should always equal the expected value of your future credences in it. In the abstract that sounds right, but my cases seem to pull against it.

Case A. I've just followed a proof and now assign 0.95 to the theorem. I know that by next week the details will have faded and I'll be back to 0.4, with no new evidence. I think present me is right. I don't think I should drop to 0.4 now just because I'll forget the proof.

Case B. I'm running on four hours of sleep, and I find myself thinking a leaked email discredits a well-established theory. I know rested me will think it doesn't. I defer to rested future me; in fact, I'd like to already have his credence.

Case C. Next week I'll hear a talk by someone I know to be charming and rhetorically effective but who never gives new information. I know from experience that I'll leave the talk substantially more confident in his conclusion. I don't think I should defer now to post-talk me; I'd rather discount the effect ahead of time.

Case D. Tomorrow I'll learn the result of an experiment that's genuinely informative about H. I don't know what future me will believe, but he'll be either very confident in H or very confident in not-H, depending on the result. Here the loose principle feels exactly right: my current credence in H should be a weighted average of those possible future credences, weighted by how likely I now think the different results are.

Case E. Suppose I'll be given a pill next month that will make me considerably more confident in whichever answer I'm initially inclined to give, on any question put to me, without changing my information. I don't think I should defer to pill-me. But this case troubles me: I can't state a clean difference between Case B and Case E, since in both cases the change between me and future me is just some neurochemical difference. Is my tendency to trust rested-me but not pill-me defensible, or is it just gerrymandering a principle around whatever feels like my "normal" state?

Case F. I can predict, mostly from watching older colleagues and from noticing my own drift, that at 75 I'll hold somewhat more conservative political views than I do now, even though I can't point to any particular evidence I expect to acquire. I don't think I should defer now to 75-year-old me. But I also don't think this is just another predicted irrationality like the pill; he'll be at least as careful as I am now, maybe more careful. So if the repaired principle only lets me refuse deference when I expect future me to be less informed or less rational, isn't this case a problem? And if it forces me to defer here after all, that feels wrong too.

So what should I think? Improve or replace the loose principle, stated as precisely as you can, and say what it predicts for a couple of cases I haven't listed—say, a future self with less evidence but noticeably better judgment, or a future self I expect to be more rational but also expect to disagree with me. And be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accomodate, if any.
