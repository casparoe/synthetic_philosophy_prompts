I serve on an advisory working group that has been asked to comment on a draft constitution for a country rebuilding its political institutions from scratch. One article in the draft attempts to combine the accountability of a parliamentary system with the fixed-term stability of presidentialism. Under the proposal, the president is directly elected for a fixed six-year term and can be removed on broad political grounds only by a three-fifths vote of the lower house. If such a vote passes, the president leaves office immediately, a presidential election must be held within ninety days, and the lower house is automatically dissolved, with fresh legislative elections held on the same schedule. The distinctive clause is the cost it imposes on the removers: every member who votes in favor of removal is personally barred from seeking any elected national office—the presidency or a seat in either house of the legislature—for the next ten years, while members who vote against or abstain face no such bar. The drafters expect this to guarantee that a house will remove a president only when a durable majority is willing to stake its own political careers on the judgment, and not for routine political advantage.

I would like you to treat this as a mechanism rather than take a position on whether it is a good idea. Walk through how it would actually operate. What does the personal bar do to when and why removal votes are attempted, to who joins the pro-removal coalition and who abstains, and to whether the three-fifths threshold is ever realistically met? How does it reshape the president's relationship with her own party, with the opposition, and with individual legislators who are nearing the end of their careers or eyeing appointive office? What strategies would emerge in the snap election campaign, and where would the rule be gamed first? Are the main failure modes fixable without gutting the underlying idea, and what does a realistic version of this mechanism buy a constitutional designer that ordinary no-confidence votes and ordinary fixed terms do not?
