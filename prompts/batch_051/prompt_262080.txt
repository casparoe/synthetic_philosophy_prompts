I'm taking a course on statutory interpretation, and I've been trying to sort the absurdity doctrine cases we've read into three different doctrines that all happen to go by one name. I'd like an honest assessment of whether this distinction is coherent, whether I'm inadvertently re-creating a standard view, and where the taxonomy breaks.

The three kinds I have in my notes are these.

First, what I call incoherence-absurdity. The literal reading is not merely bad policy but self-defeating or internally inconsistent: it commands two things that cannot both be done, or produces a result that defeats the only intelligible point of the provision. For example, a provision read literally might require disputes to be resolved by a body that is also one of the parties, or might contain two clauses, one of which requires an act and the other forbids it. Here the court seems to be repairing a broken instruction rather than overriding the legislature.

Second, purpose-defeating absurdity. The literal reading is perfectly coherent, but applying it would produce the opposite of the statute's evident purpose. The paradigm is the old labor-law pattern in which a statute banning the importation of foreigners under contract to perform labor or service gets applied to a church bringing over its minister, even though the statute was plainly aimed at cheap manual labor. The court departs from the letter to protect the point of the enactment.

Third, repugnance-absurdity. The literal reading is coherent, and it may even be faithful to the legislature's purpose, but it yields a result so contrary to some background legal or moral commitment that the court refuses to follow it. A statute read literally might, for instance, require a court to order something that violates a constitutional value, or to produce an outcome the legal system clearly treats as a grave injustice, even though the statute's own point does not rule that out.

My working view is that these are genuinely different, and that calling all three absurdity lets courts borrow the legitimacy of the first kind for the third. The second looks to me like purposivism in disguise, while the third seems to license judicial imposition of external values in a way the first does not. I'd like to know whether the trichotomy actually tracks what courts are doing, whether the second and third collapse into the first once a statute's purpose and context are specified carefully enough, and whether the third kind is defensible as a distinct ground for departing from the text at all.
