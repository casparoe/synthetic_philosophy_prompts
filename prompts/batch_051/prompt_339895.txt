I read an long explainer about "ergodicity economics" — the time-averages-versus-ensemble-averages stuff — and I wrote down what I take the piece to be claiming, but I'm not sure where the popularization runs ahead of the actual result. Here's my summary of it:

"Standard economics evaluates gambles by taking an ensemble average over many parallel copies of an agent, but a real person lives one path through time, so the relevant average is the time average. The usual coin-flip example makes this vivid: each round you either multiply your wealth by 1.5 (heads) or 0.6 (tails), with equal probability. Expected value says the expected multiplier per round is 1.05, so taking the bet is a no-brainer, and anyone who hesitates is just exhibiting irrational loss aversion. But because wealth compounds multiplicatively, the average that matters is geometric: a typical round multiplies wealth by only sqrt(1.5 × 0.6) ≈ 0.95, and since that's less than one, a bet with positive expected value in the ensemble actually ruins almost everyone in the long run. The upshot is that economists have been maximizing the wrong quantity: what an individual should maximize is the time-average growth rate of their wealth, which is equivalent to maximizing expected log wealth. So ergodicity economics shows that logarithmic utility isn't just one preference among many — it's the objectively correct one."

The part I'm most suspicious of is that last leap, but I'd like to know if the earlier stuff is shaky too.
