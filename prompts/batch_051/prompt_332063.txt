You are helping a colleague grade a take-home exam in an upper-level moral philosophy course, and they have asked for a careful second opinion rather than a quick score. Assign a letter grade to the answer reproduced below, and support it with the specific comments that justify that grade: what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong or conflates, and what a better answer would have said. The question the student answered was: A regional council is deciding whether to designate Maple Street, a residential street in a small town, as an approved through-truck route between a quarry and a regional highway. The designation would save roughly two thousand drivers an average of seven minutes per day. It would also raise the annual risk of a fatal pedestrian or cyclist collision for each of the twenty thousand people who live along Maple Street, from one in one million to one in one hundred thousand. Over the lifetime of the policy, according to the council's projections, a number of deaths will be attributable to the additional through traffic, although no individual can be identified in advance. The council will not seek actual consent from every affected resident or driver, and no compensation scheme is proposed. Under what conditions, if any, is the council morally permitted to impose the additional risk on the residents without their individual consent? In your answer, evaluate whether the policy can be justified by actual informed consent, by hypothetical consent behind a veil of ignorance, by aggregate welfare maximization, or by reciprocity among participants in a cooperative scheme, and state which, if any, of these would be sufficient.

The student's answer was: Strict actual consent cannot be a condition on every policy of this kind, because then almost no public infrastructure or safety regulation could ever be legitimate; we never obtain every affected driver's actual consent before setting a speed limit. The right test is therefore hypothetical consent. A reasonable person behind a veil of ignorance, not knowing whether they would be a resident or a driver, would approve the designation, since the aggregate expected benefit of the time savings is large while the added individual risk is only nine in one million per year. And once a risk is rational to accept from the ex ante perspective, no one has a legitimate complaint ex post when it materializes: the eventual fatality is simply the ex ante risk made concrete, and it has already been taken into account in the calculation that justified the policy. Nor are the residents being singled out, because everyone in a modern society accepts small reciprocal risks in order to participate in a system of roads and mobility from which all benefit; some burdens therefore fall on some people as a matter of fair play. The individual added risk is also so far below the risks people already accept in ordinary life that it is de minimis and should count as no real harm at all. Hence the policy is permissible, though the council would do well to offer compensation as a matter of courtesy.
