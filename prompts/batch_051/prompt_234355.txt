I'm a science journalist preparing a feature on how mathematicians use heuristic arguments to make predictions long before proof is available, and I've found myself stuck on one case that seems to reveal something general about the method.

The standard probabilistic heuristic for the primes begins with the thought that, once the obvious local obstructions are set aside, whether a large integer n is prime behaves rather like a random event with probability about 1/log n, and that such events at nearby integers can be treated as approximately independent. From this presumption of independence, the probabilistic tradition associated with Cramér and with Hardy and Littlewood has produced conjectures about twin primes, prime gaps, and rarer constellations that have matched numerical evidence remarkably well.

Then came the 2016 result of Robert Lemke Oliver and Kannan Soundararajan: they found that consecutive primes are strongly biased in how they arrange themselves among residue classes. In base ten, a prime ending in 1 is substantially less likely to be followed by another prime ending in 1 than by one ending in 3, 7, or 9, and analogous biases become more pronounced for larger moduli. A naive independence heuristic appears to predict something close to uniformity among the allowed residue classes, so at first pass the result looks like a decisive failure of the whole probabilistic outlook. But the same authors argued that the full Hardy–Littlewood k-tuple conjectures—themselves products of that outlook—predict these very biases accurately.

That is the knot I want to unravel. Does the standard heuristic argument survive this development, or does it need repair, and in either case which premise exactly is under pressure: the probability assignment itself, the independence assumption, or only the casual way independence gets applied once one conditions on a known prime and asks what the next prime does? What, if anything, can be salvaged in a weaker or more careful form, and what does the repair teach us about what a heuristic argument is actually claiming when it assumes independence? I am interested in the conceptual mechanics of the argument, not in a bibliography; I want to be able to explain to a general reader why the 2016 discovery felt like bad news for the heuristic but might instead be the heuristic vindicating itself in a subtler form.
