Im writing an essay on self-deception and I keep coming back to Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, the novel (the film is fine too). You know the basic puzzle: "deceiving yourself" looks contradictory, because it seems to need one person who both knows the truth and hides it from himself — believing p and not-p at once. The usual moves are Sartres bad faith, the intentionalist idea that you rig your own evidence-gathering, and the deflationary line that its just motivated bias with no intention behind it. Stevens feels like the perfect test case. For decades he tells himself Lord Darlington is a man of principle while sliding past every bit of evidence to the contrary — the two Jewish maids, the Nazi guests, the appeasement crowd — and something parallel happens with Miss Kenton. So my question is: read the novel through this debate. Which account of self-deception actually captures what Stevens is doing, and do they all miss something? Does the novel show him simultaneously knowing and not knowing, or is that the wrong description of someone who just looks away and then quietly rewrites the past? Are there places where his manner of narrating is itself the self-deception, not just the content? And does the long retrospective first-person form create trouble for theories built around a single person at a single time? I also wonder whether he's a case of straight self-deception (believing what he wants to be true) or something darker where what stays buried is the fact that the whole project his self-deception was protecting turned out to be wasted. I want a reading that is honest both ways: the novel isn't just a case study, and the theory doesn't get a free pass where Stevens resists it.
