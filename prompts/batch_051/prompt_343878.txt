Someone in my reading group keeps insisting self-deception isn't really possible, and the more we push him on it the more his argument turns out to ride on an analogy with ordinary lying. His line is this: lying requires the liar to believe not-p, to intend that the target come to believe p, and to bring that about knowingly. So 'lying to yourself' would require one person to simultaneously believe p and not-p, or to intend to surprise yourself with something you already know, which is impossible. Therefore what we call self-deception is at best sloppy belief formation, biased attention, or an after-the-fact story. Other members push back by taking the same analogy at face value: a con man hides the evidence from his mark and the mark ends up believing what the con man wants; in self-deception, the same person just fills both roles, with one part wanting the comfortable belief and the other part getting got. They say that's why the phrase stuck around, and we have plenty of everyday cases — ignoring a strange charge on the credit card, not reading the follow-up, convincing yourself the project will ship on time.

We've been going in circles because neither side separates which parts of the analogy are doing work from which parts are just color. I'm not convinced the impossibility conclusion survives unless the analogy is a lot tighter than it looks, but I don't have a clean way of saying where it come apart. Audit the lying-to-yourself analogy for self-deception. What maps to the liar, the lie, the mark, the intent, and the act of concealment? Where does the mapping break, and which breaks actually matter for the question of whether self-deception is possible? Does the analogy smuggle in a single, transparent, unified self that the phenomenon itself is calling into doubt? And if the impossibility claim is just an artifact of the analogy, what should we put in its place?
