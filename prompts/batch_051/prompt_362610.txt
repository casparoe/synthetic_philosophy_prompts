I've landed on the view that the badness of pain is entirely in its affective/motivational side, not in the sensory-discriminative side. What makes pain bad is the ouch part, the I-mind-this-make-it-stop part; the location, intensity, burning-versus-stabbing character, and so on is just neutral information unless the affect attaches to it. Pain asymbolia is my favourite evidence: people with it can still identify a stimulus as painful, describe where it is and what it feels like, but they don't find it unpleasant and often seem untroubled or even amused by it. If you can have the full pain sensation without the suffering, then the suffering is doing all the moral work and the raw sensation is doing none.

I want to know what would count against this. What observations, cases, arguments, or implications should make me back off the clean seperation? I vaguely know some people read asymbolia differently—maybe asymbolics don't actually have normal pain sensation, or they have some kind of attention/response problem that isn't specific to pain. Draw those alternative readings out as genuine defeaters, not footnotes. Also, tell me whether my view is committed to things I should find uncomfortable: for instance, that a sensation of a nail through your hand isn't bad at all if the affect is missing, or that two pains with identical affective intensity but wildly different sensory intensity are equally bad. I'd like the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and an honest verdict on whether I've made the view unfalsifiable by defining 'sensory' and 'affective' however I need to.
