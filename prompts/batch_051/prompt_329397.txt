For the past several months I have been living with two very different uses of probability, and I have become unable to say why I treat them differently. A surgeon has told us that there is an 85 percent chance that a particular operation on my relative will succeed. The claim concerns a single patient, on a single morning, with no exact precedent; nevertheless, everyone involved—the surgeon, the nurses, my relative, the rest of the family—treats the number as something one could get right or wrong, not as a report of the surgeon's private feeling. A few weeks ago, by contrast, another relative told me he was 80 percent confident that a third family member would keep a difficult promise, and I heard myself replying that probability does not apply to things like that.

I have since noticed that the line I drew is unstable. A roulette wheel's probability of landing on 7—one in 37 on a standard European wheel—looks objective because the wheel can be spun again and again and shows a stable frequency. But the operation cannot be repeated, and neither can the promise. Yet I want to place the surgeon's number with the roulette wheel, as probability in the world, and the promise-number with subjective opinion. I also treat weather forecasts for a particular day and engineering failure estimates for a particular structure as objective, even though those events are unrepeatable too. The uneasy intermediate case is legal guilt: a probability of guilt in a specific trial seems neither clearly in the world nor clearly a mere attitude.

So the asymmetry I cannot justify is this. We treat some probabilities of unique events—medical prognoses, tomorrow's weather, the failure of a specific structure—as objective facts that competent judges can estimate correctly or incorrectly, while we treat other probabilities of unique events—whether a promise will be kept, whether this defendant is guilty, whether I will finish a project by June—as subjective degrees of confidence with no fact of the matter. Is there a principled account of probability that draws this line where ordinary practice draws it, or is the asymmetry simply an inconsistency we live with? If objective probability requires a stable chance setup or a long-run frequency, then the surgeon's 85 percent seems to lose its objectivity too; if every probability is only a degree of belief, then the roulette wheel's probability is as much in our heads as the promise. I would like the strongest principle that can justify the asymmetry—one that preserves the objectivity of the roulette wheel and, if possible, the surgeon's figure, while leaving the promise-probability on the subjective side—tested against the hostile nearby cases. And I want an honest verdict: should the asymmetry be kept, reversed, or given up?
