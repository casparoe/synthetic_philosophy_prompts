Had a long argument with a friend about the absent-minded driver puzzle—the one with a two-exit highway, payoffs 0 for taking the first exit, 4 for the second, and 1 for driving past both, where the driver can't tell the exits apart once he's at one. The usual analysis says the ex ante best plan is to exit with probability 1/3 at each exit, and then there's all this wierdness about whether that plan survives once he's actually at an exit. I want to try a variation that's less standard. Suppose that at the first exit only, there's a sign indicating it is the first exit, but the driver only notices signs 50% of the time—the second exit never has a sign. So if he notices the sign, he knows where he is and just continues; if he doesn't notice a sign, he's in the murky information set of first-exit-with-sign-missed or second-exit. He knows the notice probability, so it's a known noisy signal, not a mistake.

Work out the ex ante optimal probability of exiting when no sign is observed, and then check whether the action-stage reasoning still agrees with that same probability or whether the usual ex ante/action-stage gap gets better or worse compared to the standard puzzle. Also, how does the optimal probability behave as the notice probability increases—does it hit 1 at some threshold, and if so where? I'd like the actual algebra, not just a verbal conclusion.
