I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about how early modern arguments about suicide look when placed next to later empirical work on the mind. I want to focus on a specific paragraph in David Hume's essay “Of Suicide.” He writes:

“The providence of the deity appears not immediately in any operation, but governs every thing by those general and immutable laws, which have been established from the beginning of time. All events, in one sense, may be pronounced the action of the almighty: They all proceed from those powers, with which he has endowed his creatures. A house, which falls by its own weight, is not brought to ruin by his providence more than one destroyed by the hands of men; nor are the human faculties less his workmanship than the laws of motion and gravitation.”

Hume's argument, as I understand it, is that if the deity governs the world through general and immutable laws rather than by intervening in each particular event, then a human being's choice of death is no special intrusion into God's jurisdiction; it is simply one more operation of the very powers the deity has embedded in nature. On this view, the theological objection to suicide dissolves, and what remains are secular questions about one's duties to other people and to oneself.

Here is the new development I would like to test against it. Over the past several decades, clinical research and population-level studies have converged on the finding that many—likely a majority—of serious suicidal acts occur during acute, short-lived crises rather than after long deliberation. People who survive a near-lethal attempt often do not repeat it, and many later describe the act as having been undertaken in a state of transient mental pain or panic, frequently connected to conditions that are treatable. In other words, the act is often less like a considered rearrangement of one's life and more like a short-circuit of agency.

My question is not about what policies this justifies. It is about whether this development undermines Hume's argument specifically. Does the empirical picture of suicidal behavior as often transient and non-autonomous break the analogy on which his passage depends—the idea that choosing death is just one exercise of the same faculties that can divert a river or demolish a house? Or does it leave the structure of Hume's argument intact, merely shifting the relevant “general laws” from divine providence to neurobiology and then raising a new question about which acts count as autonomous? Identify the precise premise the development bears on and say whether Hume's argument can be repaired or must be limited.
