I've been arguing with a coworker about why mutation makes programs so much harder to reason about than pure functions, even for people who've written imperative code for a decade, and I'd like to sneak in some metaphysics and get a probability out of you rather than another opinion. My pet story is that people are intuitive endurantists about objects: we track a thing over time as one persisting substance wholly present at each moment — same car, new tires. A destructively updated variable doesn't actually offer us that. `x = x + 1` doesn't change a persisting x the way a car persists through a tire change; it overwrites a memory location with a new value, and the old one is just gone unless something else happens to reference it. So the idea is that mutable state clashes with our default object metaphysics: the syntax invites us to treat x as an enduring entity, while the semantics only give us stages and overwrites, and we burn a chunk of our error budget trying to hold together an object that isn't there. I'm not talking about novices who dont know the language; I mean competent programmers whose code gets big, aliased, or concurrent and suddenly their reasoning breaks. I'm at something like 0.45 that this endurantist-mismatch explains at least half of the distinctive difficulty; my coworker is at maybe 0.1 and says its just aliasing and combinatorial blowup.

Decompose the number. Use whatever terms you think belong — I'd expect something like: the probability that untrained humans are intuitive endurantists about ordinary objects; the probability that this habit transfers to how people read assignment statements; the probability that it produces errors or confusion above and beyond aliasing, concurrency, and raw state-space size; and the probability that its contribution clears the 50% bar. Give each term a number and a sentence of justification, show how you combine them, and then say which term you're least confident in and what evidence would move it most.
