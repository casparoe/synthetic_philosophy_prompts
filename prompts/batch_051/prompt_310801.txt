I got roped into this jokey "philosophy for engineers" reading group at work and we're doing Hume on miracles. I don't have any formal philosophy background, so I leaned on a summary one of the guys pasted into our Slack from some philosophy explainer site. The summary goes roughly: Hume proved that it's never rationally OK to believe a miracle on the basis of testimony. A miracle is a violation of a law of nature; laws of nature are established by uniform past experience; so the evidence against any miracle is as strong as evidence can get. Testimony, by contrast, comes from people who are known to be mistaken, credulous, or deceitful. So a rational person should always prefer the explanation that the witnesses lied or were fooled -- no matter how many witnesses there are or how honest they seem. The post ends by saying this means even seeing a miracle yourself wouldn't make it rational to believe it happened, because your own senses are also just one more fallible source against the whole background of nature.

I'll be honest, I can't tell how much of that is Hume and how much is the blog's editorializing. Could you go through the summary piece by piece and mark which parts a careful reading of Hume would accept, which parts are overstatements or distortions, and whether the "even seeing it yourself" conclusion actually follows from the accurate parts? And if there's a more careful version of the argument that keeps the punch but doesn't overclaim, I'd love to see it stated cleanly without jargon.
