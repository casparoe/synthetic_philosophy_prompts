I am a PhD student in political philosophy, and I have been trying to work out an account of terrorism by seeing which candidate principles survive contact with the verdicts I actually give about particular cases. For a while I have been working with the following rough principle: an act is terrorism just in case it is violence, or a threat of violence, directed at civilians or non-combatants, with the intent of intimidating or coercing a wider audience or a government into some political course of action. I once included a further clause requiring the perpetrator to be a non-state actor, but two of the cases below led me to abandon it.

Consider the cases. A separatist group detonates a car bomb outside a government office at rush hour, killing passersby, with the stated aim of forcing the government to enter negotiations; I have no hesitation in calling this terrorism. A state's air force, during a conventional war, deliberately bombs a residential district far from any military objective in order to break the population's will and force the enemy government to surrender; I now call this terrorism, even though the perpetrator is a state. A state secret police abducts and kills the child of a prominent dissident, deliberately letting the killing become known in order to deter other dissidents; I call this terrorism too. By contrast, when uniformed soldiers of one state attack an enemy naval base during peacetime and announce that they will continue unless the enemy withdraws from a disputed island, I do not call it terrorism; I call it aggression or an undeclared act of war, even though it is coercive political violence. When animal-rights activists, after checking repeatedly that a university laboratory is empty, set fire to it at night and leave a message saying the attacks will stop only when the university ends a particular line of research, I lean toward calling that terrorism, even though no person was harmed or directly threatened and the target was property rather than persons. When a lone gunman assassinates a cabinet minister as revenge for a massacre, announcing publicly that the killing was retribution but making no demands and no threats against anyone else, I do not call it terrorism. The case I keep going back and forth on is this: insurgents under a foreign occupation attack a barracks at night while soldiers are sleeping, aiming to make the occupation too costly and force a withdrawal. Sometimes that seems to me an act of irregular war; sometimes it seems like terrorism.

My principle handles the first three cases and the assassination without much strain, but it appears to exclude the arson, since no civilian or non-combatant was attacked, and it cannot settle the barracks case, since the sleeping soldiers are combatants of a sort. Is there a single principle that captures all or most of these judgments? Which of my verdicts would it force me to revise, and what would it say about cases I have not listed, such as a cyberattack that shuts down a civilian hospital in order to pressure a government, or a strike on a legitimate military target that predictably kills civilians as collateral damage?
