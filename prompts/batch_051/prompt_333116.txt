I'm a novelist, and the book I'm working on is built around a fight in a small town over whether to keep holding a memorial service for a factory fire that killed nineteen workers back in the 1980s. Doing research, I sat through town meetings and argued with people on both sides, and again and again the conversation got closed down by somebody saying "we can't just forget" or "never forget." What struck me is that the slogan wasn't one claim so much as a placeholder for several very different ones: keep the records in order, keep showing up in early October, stay angry at the company that owned the building, stay sad about the people who died, refuse to let the town become the kind of place that moves on. I want help seperating these out. Could you lay out a menu of the distinct claims "never forget" might actually be making, from the weak and near-trivial readings (something like: don't let the facts be lost) to the strong and demanding ones (something like: the living owe it to the dead to keep them present, maybe forever), and for each one say whether it's defensible, which are probably false or impossible, and who the "never" could plausibly bind — individuals, families, institutions, the whole town? And I especially want the other side, because I keep running into the thought that a duty to remember might be the very thing preventing the town from having a future, or that some kinds of remembering are a kind of disloyalty to the living. Is there a reading that supports the people using it to shut down debate, and also a reading — taken seriously — that makes forgetting a duty or at least a right? I'd rather have the menu than one clean interpretation, because honestly I can hear my characters' voices in the different readings.
