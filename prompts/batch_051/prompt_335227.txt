While reporting a story on the mind–body problem, I interviewed a computer scientist who rejected Ryle's diagnosis of Cartesian dualism—the immaterial mind steering the body—as a category mistake. His argument was that Ryle dismissed what he called the ghost in the machine too quickly: a running program really is a ghost in the hardware. It is real and causally effective, not identical to any set of circuits, and not readable from the hardware alone; so the computational picture of mind shows that the picture Ryle mocked is coherent after all. I want an audit of how far this analogy actually carries. Spell out the correspondence: what maps onto the mind, the brain, the ghost, the machine, causation, and dependence? Which disanalogies matter for his conclusion and which are merely rhetorical? What features of software does the analogy quietly import that a Cartesian mental substance would lack, and what does it quietly drop? Finally, say whether the audited analogy overturns the category-mistake charge, merely relocates it, or does neither, and whether some adjusted analogy could do the work better. Please keep your answer to at most 750 words.
