I'm on the board of a regional community safety partnership, and we have to decide next Tuesday whether to commission a £40,000 forecasting tool from a consultancy that uses cliodynamics. The pitch is that history has broad mathematical patterns — secular cycles — driven by population pressure and something they call elite overproduction: too many educated young people chasing too few elite jobs, rising inequality, and then a crisis phase every couple of generations. They don't claim to predict exact dates, but they say the model gives a probabilistic early-warning signal, and they want us to use it to time our spending on youth services and community mediation, putting more money in during the predicted run-up to a crisis phase.

Half the board thinks this is astrology with equations — history doesn't repeat and each crisis has its own causes. The other half says it's no different in principle from economic forecasting or flood-risk modelling, and we'd be irresponsible to ignore an early-warning tool. I've been asked to draft the short decision memo for next weeks meeting. I left university at 19 and have only been reading around this on my own, so write it in plain English but don't dodge the hard bit: can a model of long historical cycles really tell us anything reliable about what's coming here and now?

Give us a clear recommendation: commission it, reject it, or approve a smaller pilot with conditions. If you recommend a pilot, say how long it should run and what would count as success. And deal fairly with the strongest point on the losing side, because whichever way we go, someone's going to want to know their view was taken seriously.
