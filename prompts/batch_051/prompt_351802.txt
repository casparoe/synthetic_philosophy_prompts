I want a reasoned counterfactual analysis of the Mytilene episode of the Peloponnesian War. Here is the setup. In 427 BC, after Mytilene surrendered to Athens, the assembly first voted, on Cleon's motion, to execute the adult male population and enslave the women and children. The next day, after a debate in which Diodotus argued that a standing punishment of massacre for revolt would teach rebels that surrender was worthless, the assembly narrowly reversed itself; historically, the second trireme overtook the first and arrived in time to halt the execution. For the counterfactual, suppose the second trireme had been delayed enough — a damaged hull, contrary winds, or exhausted rowers — that it arrived a day late, and the first decree was carried out in full, with no further reconsideration possible.

I would like you to reason forward over the following ten to fifteen years, focusing on the Delian League's cohesion and the internal politics of Athens. Please make the causal chain explicit rather than telling a story. In particular: (1) How should other allied and subject cities update their expectations about revolt and surrender? Does the credibility of Athenian threats deter further revolts, or does removing the option of a negotiated surrender make rebellion more desperate and more expensive to suppress? Which effect dominates, and what would have to change for the balance to reverse? (2) How does the event reshape the assembly's decision-making? Does Cleon's diagnosis gain authority, and does the practice of revisiting a decree the next day come to be seen as a safety valve or as a weakness? (3) How does Sparta exploit the massacre in its appeals to Athens' allies? For each of your main conclusions, say whether you regard it as robust or as a fragile guess, and specify the background condition whose failure would overturn it. I am less interested in a single all-things-considered verdict than in the mechanisms and the confidence you attach to each link.
