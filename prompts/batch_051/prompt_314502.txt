One of the other students in my philosophy of cognitive science group defends a strong form of aetiological teleosemantics. On her view, the content of a representational state is fixed by its proper function, where proper function is understood aetiologically: a token state has a proper function only if previous tokens of that type were selected in an ancestral lineage because they performed some task, such as carrying information about food or predators. I am sceptical: the view seems to me either an untestable claim about history or a way of dressing up content we have already assigned on behavioural grounds. Is there an experiment that would put genuine pressure on this claim? Set out the strongest test you can design, using organisms or artificial agents with controlled selection histories. The test should specify: two histories for an otherwise identical internal state that differ only in the selective role played by earlier tokens of that type; the behavioural or neural outcome, measured in advance, that would favour the teleosemantic account; the outcome that would favour a rival account, such as a causal-role or similarity-based semantics; and a careful statement of what a positive result would and would not settle. In that last part, address whether any behavioural evidence can distinguish the constitution of content from our attribution of content, and whether the experiment shows only that selection history is necessary for content or the stronger claim that selection history determines which content the state has.
