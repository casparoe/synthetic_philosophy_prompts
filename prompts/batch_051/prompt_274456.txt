I have been teaching a graduate seminar on territorial jurisdiction over speech, and I keep coming back to a hypothetical I am considering for my final examination. A small jurisdiction—call it the Republic of Adia—enacted a Public Speech Act in 1985, before the internet was in view. Section 4 of the Act defines publication as follows: "A statement is published within the State when (a) it is uttered to an audience physically present within the territory; (b) it is broadcast by radio or television from a transmitter located within the territory; or (c) it is distributed in printed or recorded form from an address within the territory, whether by sale, gift, display, or delivery." Assume the statement in question is one the State may constitutionally regulate provided that publication occurs within its territory.

Now suppose a news organization incorporated in the neighbouring state posts a video on its website, whose origin server is in that neighbouring state. To serve Adian readers quickly, the organization contracts with a content delivery network that maintains an edge server in a data centre in Adia's capital. When an Adian visitor requests the video, the edge server inside Adia transmits the file to the visitor's device. No employee of the news organization or the network has decided to publish anything within Adia; the file was copied onto the Adian server automatically, weeks earlier, by a caching algorithm anticipating demand. The prosecutor argues that the edge server distributes recorded form from an address within the territory under clause (c). The publisher responds that the drafters wrote those words for print publishers and broadcasters, and that treating a cache as a place of publication would be a legal fiction.

I would like you to work through the problem in four stages: (i) what clause (c), read literally, does with the edge server; (ii) what the Act's evident purpose, and what its 1985 drafters would most likely have said if informed about content delivery networks, would do; (iii) which interpretive approach should win and why; and (iv) what the case reveals about the wider question of whether a territorial conception of publication can still determine whose law governs internet speech. I care less about the practical outcome than about what the case exposes in the idea of a "place" of publication.
