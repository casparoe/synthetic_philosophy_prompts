I'm giving a talk at an interdisciplinary workshop on interpretability and I want a prep sheet for the Q&A, not a polished version of my talk. My thesis is roughly this: chain-of-thought faithfulness isnt a property a transcript has or lacks on its own. It's a three-place relation between a transcript, a model, and the level of description a downstream user is actually relying on. So "did the model really reason that way" only makes sense once you fix what counts as "that way," and most users never do. I'll argue that the usual faithfulness evidence—interventions, ablations, counterfactual tokens or features—mostly shows that the chain of thought is not causally idle, and "not idle" is a lot weaker than "reports the computation." I'll end by saying the responsible move is to treat CoTs as fallible, audience-relative reports rather than as the computation itself, especially for high-stakes audits.

The audience is maybe 25 people. There's a core of ML interpretability researchers who've built or used faithfulness metrics, so they'll push back in terms of interventions, counterfactual robustness, and what you can show from a pretraining or activation lens. There are two or three philosophers who work on self-knowledge and confabulation in humans, and they'll hear my view as a version of the old worry that people can be sincere and articulate while being wrong about their own reasons. And there's a handful of safety/policy people whose actual concern is whether auditors can responsibly lean on CoTs for consequential decisions—they won't care much about the metaphysics, but they'll care hard about what my view leaves them with.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way those people would actually phrase them, because the interpretability folks and the philosophers won't voice the same worry the same way. For each objection: is it fair, what should I concede up front, what should I hold, and what's a reply I can actually get through in the time I have. Include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks. Be blunt about the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss.

Then close with a short note saying which of your own judgments about the likely Q&A you're least confident in, and what would change your mind—if some argument would make you tell me to substantially soften the thesis before I go in, say so. Please write in American English.
