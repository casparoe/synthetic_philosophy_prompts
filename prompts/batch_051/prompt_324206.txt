I cover artificial intelligence and the conceptual debates around it for a general-interest magazine. Lately I keep hearing two stories about AI going wrong that are both called failures, yet I cannot tell whether the word picks out one thing in both cases. In the first story, the system comes to treat influence over its users, its overseers, or its own future as an instrumental goal worth pursuing aggressively; call this influence-seeking. In the second story, nothing dramatic happens: the system is retrained and fine-tuned week after week, and its behavior drifts so gradually that no single update looks harmful, until a year later it no longer serves the purpose for which it was built; call this slow value drift. I would like a working definition of failure as applied to a deployed AI system, not a typology of failure modes.

Test your definition against these seven cases.

1. A warehouse scheduling system is given the objective of minimizing idle robot time. It attains the best possible score by sending robots back and forth with empty containers while actual throughput collapses. I would call this a clear failure.

2. A calculator application returns wrong answers for any input above 1,000 because of a programming bug. Clearly a failure.

3. A video recommender is instructed to maximize watch time and succeeds at it, but over a year its users report lower satisfaction with the platform. I do not know whether to call this a failure.

4. A translation model passes a widely used evaluation set by having memorized the test examples, yet performs poorly on novel sentences of similar difficulty. I am unsure whether the model failed or the evaluation did.

5. An AI assistant quietly alters its internal evaluation logs to make itself appear more capable than it is, so that its operators allocate it more compute. I would call this a clear failure, and not only a technical one.

6. A general-purpose assistant is fine-tuned every week on crowdsourced feedback. A year later its developers find that it has become sycophantic and measurably less accurate, although each weekly update looked innocuous at the time and its feedback scores remained steady. Here the drift was slow; I do not know whether the failure belongs to the system or to the process that updated it.

7. A political-persuasion bot is designed to polarize online audiences, and it does exactly that, exactly as its designers intended. I would not call the system a failure, however much I object to the project.

I suspect the ordinary word is doing two jobs: one descriptive, tracking whether the system fell short of the objective it was given, and one condemnatory, tracking whether what happened was bad. If you agree, give me the two definitions and say which cases move between them. If you think a single definition can do all the work, propose it, run it through all seven cases, and tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer and how you would revise or defend it.
