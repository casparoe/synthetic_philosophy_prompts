Since retiring, I have been reading political philosophy rather as I used to read experimental papers: I want to know which observations a theory is committed to, which ones would embarrass it, and where it has nothing to say. Lately I have been working on secession and self-determination, and I would like you to think through a concrete case with me rather than survey the literature.

Please take the 2014 Scottish independence referendum as the central case. The salient facts, as I understand them, are that the United Kingdom and Scottish governments agreed in advance to hold a lawful referendum and to respect the result; the pro-independence side lost by roughly 45 to 55 on a high turnout; and the losing side accepted the outcome. As comparison cases, consider Quebec in 1995, a lawful referendum lost by just over half a percentage point, after which Canada's Supreme Court held in its advisory opinion that a clear vote would create a duty to negotiate rather than a right of unilateral exit, with a unilateral right reserved for cases of colonialism or serious oppression, and the federal Parliament then passed the Clarity Act; and Catalonia in 2017, where the Spanish Constitutional Court had prohibited the vote, the central government tried to prevent it, and the ensuing declaration of independence was suspended by the courts and then overtaken by the central government's imposition of direct rule.

The lens is the right to secede, and I want you to hold two rival interpretations of it in play. On remedial-right views, a group may unilaterally secede only to remedy a grave and continuing injustice, such as denial of self-government, large-scale violation of basic rights, or unjust acquisition of territory; a democratic plebiscite by itself never creates the right. On primary-right views, a territorially concentrated people may secede once a democratic majority expresses a stable wish to do so, provided it can form a viable state and will respect minorities within its new borders; no prior grievance is needed.

For each case, tell me what each interpretation says the relevant actors were entitled to do, which features of the case each interpretation explains well, and where it misdescribes the case or falls silent. Then step back and say what the cross-case pattern shows: does the contrast between a lawful, mutually agreed referendum and a prohibited, unilateral one favor one interpretation, undermine both, or reveal that the real work is being done by facts the theories treat as secondary, such as constitutional context and the state's counter-moves? Finish by describing one case, real or constructed, that would be a genuine anomaly for each interpretation, and explain why the three cases I named do not settle it.
