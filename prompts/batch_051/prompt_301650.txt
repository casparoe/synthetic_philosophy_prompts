Hey, I'm a classics PhD student and I co-host a tiny podcast where we do these anachronistic long-table interviews with figures from old texts — not the real historical people, but the characters as their authors stage them. Next episode is Cato the Elder as he appears in Cicero's Cato Maior de Senectute. The hook is that Cato is defending old age against the four standard complaints Cicero has him list: that it pulls you away from public business, weakens the body, strips away nearly all pleasures, and sits close to death. His replies are the famous-ish ones: the old still steer the state through counsel even when they can't row; bodily decline matters less than advertised; losing sensual pleasures is a gain because pleasure is bait for vice; and death is either annihilation or the soul's translation to something better, so a man near it shouldn't be frightened. I don't want a soft interview that lets him restate the position. I want eight to ten questions that actually pressure the view, with at least one on each of the four complaints and each with a follow-up that anticipates the reply Cato-as-Cicero would most likely give. For instance, push on wether the counsel-not-rowing move just relocates old age's value into being useful to the young rather than good for the old themselves; on whether the anti-pleasure line proves too much and indicts youth along with old age; on whether the death-is-either-annihilation-or-something-better move is a false dichotomy he never argues for. Maybe also one question on how the dialogue form itself is doing work — an 84-year-old Cato speaking at length and wittily is supposed to be evidence for the thesis, and I want you to make him face the possibility that it just shows Cicero's craft, not old age's resources. Keep the questions specific to the joints of his argument, not generic podcast filler such as asking what drew him to public life. Can you draft the list?
