I've been trying to steelman hedonism, and the thing that keeps tripping me up is the heterogeneity problem: if "pleasure" covers the buzz of a back scratch, the click of finally getting a proof, schadenfreude, and the feeling when a headache stops, what do these actually have in common besides the label? I've been using a working definition with four criteria for whether something counts as a pleasure. (1) It's a conscious state, there's something it's like to be in it. (2) It has a positive felt quality, not just the absence of a bad one. (3) The person is at least weakly motivated to stay in it or seek it again for its own sake. (4) On reflection, the person endorses it as good for them, not just as something that happens to feel nice.

Now the borderline case. A friend of mine is an exhausted new parent. The baby finally goes down at 1 a.m., and she sits alone in the dark kitchen for ten minutes. She calls it the best part of her day, but when I press her she says it doesn't feel like anything positive exactly — it's more that the noise and the demands stop. She says she can't tell whether the quiet itself has a positive taste or is just the absence of the bad stuff, and she describes the whole thing as a bit hollow. She also says she'd be furious if someone interrupted it, she arranges her evenings to protect it, and she does think it's genuinely good for her, not just a pause in the bad parts.

Is she experiencing a pleasure? Go through the four criteria honestly and say which ones she meets, which ones she fails, and which ones just don't say anything either way. Then say whether the case shows my criteria need fixing, or whether it shows that "pleasure" was never one kind of thing to begin with. And does hedonism need her case to count for its story about well-being to survive?
