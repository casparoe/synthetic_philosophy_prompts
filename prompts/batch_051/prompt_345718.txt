Take Thomson's lamp: it starts on, and you switch it off after half a minute, on after a quarter of a minute more, off after an eighth, and so on — infinitely many switches by the one-minute mark. The usual analysis says the final state simply isn't fixed, because the on/off sequence has no limit. Now tweak it: the switch is slightly faulty, in that each time you try to throw it there's an independent one per cent chance it sticks and the lamp stays exactly as it was (and we know this in advance). What does the standard analysis say now? I'd have thought we at least get a determinate probability — and that for any failure rate strictly between zero and one hundred per cent the limiting probability of being on is 50/50, regardless of which of the two states it started in. But if that's right, there's something odd: the answer is one half for every tiny positive failure rate, yet at exactly zero it collapses back to there being no fact of the matter. Does the original conclusion — that the final state is underdetermined — survive this tweak, or was it an artefact of a perfectly reliable switch? And is the half in the faulty version a genuine fact about the lamp at the one-minute mark, or is it just the same indeterminacy wearing a probabilistic costume?
