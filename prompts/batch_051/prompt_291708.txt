I am a retired natural scientist, and since leaving the laboratory I have taken up the study of philosophy. One of my old professional habits was to infer general rules from a body of cases, and I have been applying that habit to the published decisions of a truth commission in a country emerging from a civil war. The commission operates under a statute modeled on the amnesty-for-testimony procedure used by South Africa's post-apartheid truth commission: it may grant individual amnesty from prosecution to applicants who fully disclose the politically motivated crimes they committed during the conflict, and it publishes reasons for its decisions.

I have abstracted five earlier decisions.

(1) Amnesty was granted to a low-ranking soldier who publicly confessed to shooting two detainees during a massacre. His account matched the forensic record, and he accepted responsibility for the killings.

(2) Amnesty was denied to a soldier from the same unit who confessed only after a survivor's diary implicated him and whose account omitted that he had ordered new recruits to fire.

(3) Amnesty was granted to a mid-level officer who had ordered an attack on a village under written orders from his superiors. He produced the orders, named the superiors, and said that he had obeyed out of fear rather than political conviction but now accepts that obeying was wrong.

(4) Amnesty was denied to a militia commander who confessed to killing a neighbor during the conflict; the commission found that the killing was a private quarrel over land with no connection to the hostilities.

(5) Amnesty was granted to a radio broadcaster who had incited attacks in his programs. He read out a complete list of the broadcasts and their content, described himself as a willing instrument of the militia's propaganda campaign, and repudiated that campaign.

A new applicant is a prison doctor who, during the conflict, signed authorizations transferring healthy detainees held as suspected opponents from the prison clinic to an interrogator he strongly suspected would torture them. He never beat or interrogated anyone himself and did not know in detail which detainees were tortured. He now offers full and apparently truthful testimony about how the transfer system worked, and he describes that system as part of the military's counterinsurgency apparatus. But he insists that he was merely following hospital procedure and does not accept that he bears responsibility for the detainees' subsequent treatment.

I can see at least two principles that would reproduce the first five decisions, and they appear to require opposite answers in the doctor's case. What are the candidate principles, and what does each require here? Which of the earlier decisions is the closest analogue to the doctor's case? What is your best prediction of the commission's decision if it is following its own precedents, and which principle would it be committing itself to in deciding the case either way? Finally, what further case would most cleanly separate the rival principles?
