I work as a product manager at a mid-size software company. Every few weeks we decide whether to run one more round of research before shipping a feature — sometimes a two-week A/B test, sometimes a batch of usability interviews, sometimes just a couple days of reading analytics — and the default is usually to run the test because it feels safer. I need a reusable decision procedure, ideally a checklist in words I can run in about twenty minutes before the planning meeting, for when to gather more information and when to stop and ship. Ground it in Good's theorem on the value of information (the rough idea: for an expected-utility maximizer who updates correctly, the expected value of free information is never negative; its zero when the information can't change what you do, and positive in expectation when it can) along with the reasons that theorem doesn't simply license endless research: information isn't free, attention and sprint capacity are scarce, and my team is not a single coherent expected-utility maximizer. The procedure should force me to ask, in order: how would my decision actually change if this test or study came back in each possible way, and what is that change worth; what does the delay and the team's confusion cost; can I get rough numbers for the expected value of the information, and what do I do when I can't; and when should I expect information to be actively bad to have even if its free — for instance because knowing creates legal exposure, locks us into commitments, saps motivation, assigns blame, or because the people who'll see the result won't update the way they should. Apply the procedure to three made-up but realistic cases: one where it tells me obviously don't test, one where it says don't buy the expensive research even though it would be genuinely nice to know, and one where it says run the cheap test even though the leadership wants a decision now. Then flag the steps that are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure and say honestly what this checklist can't do for me. End with a one-sentence summary of the whole procedure as the final line.
