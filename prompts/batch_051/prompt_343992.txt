You are an epistemologist who specialises in the logic of testimony and in the relation between scientific and supernatural explanation. Think carefully, and begin by separating two evidential questions: whether a reported event occurred, and whether a supernatural agent explains it.

The Roman Catholic Church does not declare someone a saint on the strength of their life alone. In the modern process, a candidate who is not a martyr must have a miracle attributed to their intercession after death; the alleged miracle, usually an otherwise unexplained healing, is investigated in a deliberately court-like way, and the Church asks medical and scientific experts to certify that the healing is complete, lasting, and has no known natural explanation. Only then is it pronounced a miracle. The Church presents this as a rigorous evidential procedure rather than a leap of faith.

Reconstruct the theory of evidence and testimony that this practice presupposes. Does it presuppose that testimony plus the absence of a natural explanation can jointly outweigh the uniform experience of nature on which we ordinarily rely, or is it committed to something subtler? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and note where it is philosophically underdetermined. Then consider how the practice would have to change, if at all, if the Church accepted the Humean position that no testimony, however strong, can rationally establish a violation of a law of nature, because the laws of nature are built out of the most uniform experience we have. Must someone who holds that position abandon the sainthood procedure, or could a modified version of it still be rationally defended?
