I've been going back and forth on the symbol grounding problem for a while now — one friend who works in ML keeps telling me it's basically solved, because a vision-language model that can reliably answer "where is the coffee cup" from a camera feed has, he claims, grounded the symbol "coffee cup" in the only sense that matters. Another friend, more enactivist, says nothing counts unless the categories came out of the system's own sensorimotor history, so no amount of text (or even offline images) is grounding. I've realized they're running different checklists and I don't actually know what the checklist ought ot be. So before I read another paper or blog post about it, I want the specification itself.

Act as a philosopher of cognitive science who has been in these debates too long. Think carefully; maybe brainstorm the candidate requirements before committing. Write the desiderata that a theory of how symbols in a computational system acquire meaning must meet to count as solving the symbol grounding problem — not just "attach labels to clusters," but deal with the Searle/Harnad worries. For each requirement, give the case or argument that forces it (the Chinese room, the paper-slip version where the person executes the whole rulebook, a pure-text LLM that passes every verbal test but has never interacted with anything, a robot whose "coffee cup" is just a tuned boundary in its sensor data, a human and an AI passing the same tests while only one has the relevant causal history, etc.), and mark which requirements are non-negotiable vs. which are really preferences of one camp (internalist vs. externalist, representationalist vs. enactivist, grounding-requires-biology vs. any-transducer-with-the-right-causal-profile-works). Then the part I most want: state whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — whether the problem as usually posed is already unsolvable — and which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate solutions. Only after that, briefly score the main families of solutions (Harnad-style grounding through learned sensory categories, distributional semantics as partial grounding, agents bootstrapping shared symbols through interaction, enactivist accounts, and pure-text LLM training as grounding) against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, not the scorecard; I'll fight my friends about the scorecard later.
