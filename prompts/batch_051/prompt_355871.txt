Yesterday evening I had a long argument with a friend about the heat-of-passion doctrine in criminal law, and I still cannot decide whether his criticism was right. In many common-law systems, a person who kills while in the grip of sudden passion—for instance, upon unexpectedly discovering a spouse's infidelity—is convicted of manslaughter rather than murder and receives a substantially lighter sentence. But the mitigation is carefully bounded. The provocation must be of a kind that would cause an ordinary person to lose self-control, and the defendant must not have enjoyed a cooling-off period long enough for such a person to regain composure; where the reaction is disproportionate, delayed, or shows forethought, the mitigation is withheld.

My friend argued that the doctrine is philosophically incoherent because it treats the same emotion under two incompatible descriptions. On one side, the law treats the defendant's passion as a non-rational surge that temporarily overwhelms or suspends the capacity for self-control—something that happens to a person, like a fever or a reflex, and therefore partly excuses. On the other side, the tests of adequate provocation and a reasonable cooling-off period treat the passion as answerable to reasons: the fact-finder is asked whether the emotion was a fitting or proportionate response to the situation and whether a reasonable person would still have been in its grip. He maintained that one cannot consistently hold both that an emotion bypasses a person's judgment and that it can be appraised against how a reasonable person would respond.

I find the inconsistency he identified persuasive enough to take seriously, but I do not know which half to abandon or what abandoning it would force us to give up. What account of the emotions—as judgments or appraisals, as bodily feelings, as perceptions of what matters, or something else—does the heat-of-passion doctrine actually presuppose? Is it strictly committed to incompatible accounts in its two halves, or is there a single theory of emotion that can make sense of both the excusing element and the reason-evaluating element? And if the law were rebuilt around a consistent theory of emotion, how would the practice of distinguishing murder from manslaughter have to change?
