I have been working out a principle I call the Welfare Gradient Rule for comparing the net welfare of ecosystems. The rule says: for any habitat, start with a threshold of animal welfare that constitutes a life worth living, then evaluate the habitat by taking the number of sentient organisms alive at each moment, weighting them by how far above or below that threshold their moment-to-moment experience falls, and summing over time. On this measure, a thriving ecosystem is not necessarily good. The overwhelming majority of animals in a rich ecosystem are small, short-lived, high-fecundity animals whose offspring mostly die before maturity; the standard causes of death are predation, starvation, disease, and exposure. So for every adult that lives above the threshold, there may be thousands of organism-hours spent below it. This has led me to the uncomfortable conclusion that many healthy ecosystems are net welfare sinks, and that a sudden and complete destruction of a habitat—setting aside the acute terror and pain of the dying individuals themselves, which by hypothesis is brief relative to the suffering that would otherwise be generated—could be welfare-improving, because it prevents future generations of net-suffering organisms from coming into existence. I have been calling this the "merciful clearcut" implication, and I do not endorse it. I want an honest assessment of the principle, not the policy. Is the Welfare Gradient Rule coherent enough to reason with, or does it collapse under scrutiny because the threshold is undefined, or because one cannot simply sum welfare across individuals that way, or because it ignores the value of existing lives and not just future ones? Where exactly does it break, and is there a weaker version that preserves the core insight—that abundance is not the same as welfare—without licensing the monstrous conclusion?
