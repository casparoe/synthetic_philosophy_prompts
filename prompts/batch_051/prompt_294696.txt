Spent most of last night at the pub with my mate going round in circles about why industrialisation happened in Britain and not in China, and he eventually set out an argument that the question is badly posed. I can't shake it, but I'm not sure he's actually shown what he thinks he's shown. He laid it out like this:

1. A contrastive question of the form why X and not Y is only well posed if X and Y were on the same trajectory before whatever is being explained made them diverge.
2. The question why industrialisation happened in Britain and not in China is exactly that sort of contrastive question.
3. Britain and China around 1700 were plainly not on the same trajectory: wages and fuel prices were different, accessible coal sat near population centres in one and not in the other, one had an Atlantic empire while the other ruled a continental one, and their state structures were nothing alike.
4. So the question rests on a false presupposition, namely that the two cases were on the same trajectory.
5. Therefore the question is ill posed, and the realy sensible questions are narrower ones, like why the Yangzi delta's early modern commercial growth didn't tip over into industrialisation the way Lancashire's did.

My gut says step 1 is doing something sneaky, but I cant pin down what, and he says if I drop step 1 I'm just refusing to let the question go. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails and how? If the problem is step 1, give me the strongest reading of it that's true and tell me whether the argument still goes through on that reading. And separately, if I wanted to defend the original question, would I have to say that Britain and China actually were on the same trajectory?
