I'm grading an exam for an upper-level course on concepts and categorization, and I'd appreciate a second opinion on one answer. The exam question read as follows:

Locke writes, in Book III, Chapter III, section 7 of *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*:

"Afterwards, when time and a larger acquaintance have made them observe that there are a great many other things in the world, that in some common agreements of shape, and several other qualities, resemble their father and mother, and those persons they have been used to, they frame an idea, which they find those many particulars do partake in; and to that they give, with others, the name man, for example. And thus they come to have a general name, and a general idea. Wherein they make nothing new; but only leave out of the complex idea they had of Peter and James, Mary and Jane, that which is peculiar to each, and retain only what is common to them all."

(a) Reconstruct the account of concept formation Locke is giving in this passage. (b) Briefly characterize the three main contemporary alternatives — prototype theory, exemplar theory, and the theory theory of concepts. (c) Which of these, if any, is the closest heir to Locke's abstractionism, and which is furthest? Defend your answer.

Here is the student's answer:

Locke is describing how general ideas are produced by abstraction. The child begins with particular ideas — of the nurse, the mother, Peter, James, Mary, Jane — and then forms the general idea 'man' by leaving out whatever is peculiar to each individual and retaining only what is common to them all. Concepts, on this picture, are not copies of particulars; they are representations the mind manufactures by subtraction. That makes Locke the ancestor of prototype theory. Prototype theory, associated with Rosch and her colleagues, says a concept is a summary representation of the typical features of a category, weighted by how frequently or centrally those features occur; that is why it handles typicality effects, such as the fact that a robin is judged a better bird than a penguin. Exemplar theory, by contrast, denies that we store any summary: the concept just is the set of remembered instances, and a new item is classified by its similarity to those stored instances. The theory theory, developed by Medin and Schaffer, says concepts are embedded in folk theories and get their coherence from causal-explanatory knowledge rather than from similarity alone. On balance, Locke is closest to prototype theory and farthest from exemplar theory, because he, like the prototype theorist, thinks the mind keeps a single general representation and discards the particulars; and he is not a theory-theorist, because abstraction requires no causal-explanatory framework.
