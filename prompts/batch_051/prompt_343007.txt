Two friends of mine, Maya and Tom, got into a long argument at dinner over whether "better than" is transitive. Maya opened with a spectrum case. Consider 101 employment contracts, C(0) through C(100). C(0) pays $50,000 a year for a 40-hour week. Each step raises the annual salary by $2,500 and adds 20 minutes to the required weekly hours, so C(100) pays $300,000 for a 73-hour-and-20-minute week. Their exchange went as follows.

(1) Maya: Take any adjacent step from C(n) to C(n+1). It adds 20 minutes of work per week and pays $2,500 a year for it, which over a fifty-week year is roughly $150 per added hour. At each of the 100 steps, considered on its own, that is a favorable trade, so step by step C(n+1) is all-things-considered better than C(n). But when I set C(0) next to C(100) directly, I judge C(0) better than C(100). If "better than" is transitive, the 100 stepwise judgments force the exact opposite of the endpoint judgment. Something must give. I would give up transitivity before I gave up either the stepwise judgments or the endpoint judgment.

(2) Tom: Then you have mislocated what must give. A relation that lets you climb from C(0) to C(100) by 100 improvements and still leaves the first job better than the last is not functioning as a comparative ranking at all. If you really judge each step an improvement, you have already committed yourself to C(100) being better than C(0). So I would convict one of the adjacent judgments instead: at some point, for a particular person, 20 extra minutes a week stops being worth $2,500. The point is hard to name, but that is ordinary vagueness in "worth it," not evidence that "better than" is intransitive.

(3) Maya: Your reply purchases transitivity with a boundary that no reflection can locate. And the comparison with vague thresholds is misleading. For a chain like "bald," there is a single underlying dimension, so a vague cutoff is perfectly plausible. Here the adjacent comparisons and the endpoint comparison are doing different work: the stepwise judgments ask whether 20 more minutes is worth $2,500 to this life, while the endpoint judgment asks whether the whole package—hours, money, rest, the texture of the week—is better. Those can pull apart precisely because "better than" weighs several dimensions at once. The bad step is a phantom, and only your commitment to transitivity forces you to believe in it.

(4) Tom: If the local, stepwise comparison and the holistic, package comparison really use different standards, then you do not have one relation called "better than" running through the whole argument; you have two, and transitivity was only ever a claim about the single all-things-considered relation. Either each adjacent step is a genuine all-things-considered improvement, in which case your endpoint judgment is simply the false one, or your stepwise judgments are merely marginal and cannot generate the chain you want. Which is it?
