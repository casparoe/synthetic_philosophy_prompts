I've spent the last year reporting on the forecasting world for a feature I keep threatening to write: prediction markets, superforecasters, the election models that put a number on everything. So here's the thing I'm stuck on. The narrative-fallacy critique of historical explanation says that after something happens we tell a story that makes it look as if it had to happen, and then we convince ourselves it was predictable all along. That argument was built in a world where what was knowable in advance was mostly a guess, so you couldn't really check a post-hoc story against a real ex ante prediction. But now you can, at least for a bunch of things: some forecasters logged probabilities before elections, coups, recessions, pandemics, and a few of them turned out to be genuinely well calibrated. Some events really were forecastable, not just narratable.

Before I write the piece I want a clear line from you. Does the narrative-fallacy critique survive this development, or does it need a repair? Which premise does the forecasting work actually bear on: the psychological claim that hindsight inflates perceived predictability, the epistemic claim that retrospective stories can't be checked, or something else? And is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still does the real work, something like the fallacy isn't telling a story after the fact, it's telling it with a certainty no forecaster had at the time? Reply as continuous prose, like an email back to me: no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables.
