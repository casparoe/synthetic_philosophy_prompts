Decision theorists have spent years disagreeing about whether to pay in counterfactual mugging. In the standard version, Omega flips a fair coin; if it lands tails, Omega asks you for $100; if it lands heads, Omega pays you $10,000 if it predicted you would have paid had it landed tails. Some say you should refuse in the tails branch: the coin has already landed tails, Omega's prediction is fixed, and handing over $100 buys nothing causally. Others say you should pay: the ex ante best policy is to pay in all tail branches, and a rational agent should remain faithful to that policy even after the coin has landed. A closely related dispute arises in XOR blackmail, where the harm-versus-payment connection depends on the parity of multiple agents' decisions, so no single agent's act determines the outcome by itself. Both debates seem stalled, with each side treating the other as irrational rather than merely pursuing different values. My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement is not about the formal machinery but about whether the object of rational evaluation is a token act in the world as it turned out or a policy covering counterfactual branches, with the formal apparatus downstream of that choice. Is this right? If not, what kind of disagreement is it—verbal, empirical, formal, or foundational—and what, if anything, would count as progress rather than another round of the same arguments? I want a committed diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of positions.
