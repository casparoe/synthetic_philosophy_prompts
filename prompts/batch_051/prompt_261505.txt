You are a philosopher of emotion who works on Hellenistic ethics and Stoic moral psychology, with a side practice in literary criticism. I am writing an essay on Kazuo Ishiguro's 1989 novel The Remains of the Day, read against the ancient distinction between equanimity and apatheia. Begin by working slowly through the philosophical concepts and the novel's shape before you commit to a reading; do not rush to a thesis.

To prime the discussion, make the following rough distinction. Apatheia in its Stoic form is not numbness, repression, or a stiff upper lip: it is the absence of the passions through correct judgment about what is and is not within one's control. The Stoic sage replaces the passions with well-reasoned affective states and may undergo involuntary first movements without assenting to them, so undisturbedness is supposed to be compatible with feeling, attachment, and moral agency. Equanimity, by contrast, is a broader and less theoretically loaded ideal: a durable calm, an unflappability, the kind of state the Epicureans and Skeptics sought as ataraxia and that ordinary moral psychology treats as a trait rather than as the conclusion of a system. The novel, I think, presses on both ideals. Stevens, the butler, has spent decades perfecting a discipline of composure he calls dignity: he subordinates his own judgment to his master's, stays at his post while his father is dying upstairs, refuses to notice or answer what Miss Kenton offers him, carries out orders he privately thinks badly served rather than judge them, and only near the end lets himself register that this composure has cost him a life. Yet I read the book as refusing to make him simply a fool or a cautionary tale: there is something genuinely admirable in his discipline, and his closing turn is not grief but a resolution to make the best of what remains.

Write me a reading that does justice to both the novel and the philosophy. Specifically:

1. Does Stevens achieve anything properly called apatheia, anything properly called equanimity, or only a counterfeit of each? Where exactly do the two ideals come apart in his case, and why does that difference matter?
2. Consider the cost question. The Stoics argued that apatheia does not destroy one's humanity or moral agency but completes them. The novel arguably suggests the opposite: that Stevens's composure is tangled up with moral failure, above all in his loyal service to Lord Darlington's appeasement politics and in his silent compliance with an unjust dismissal. Does the novel show that undisturbedness can function as a vice, or as a servant of vice, rather than a good? Or is the Stoic reply available that Stevens failed precisely because he surrendered his judgment and therefore never had apatheia at all?
3. Identify the scenes that carry the philosophical weight and say what each one contributes, but also say where the novel resists the Hellenistic reading, so that an honest critic must admit the book is not an allegory of Stoic or Epicurean ethics.
4. End by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical debate about whether freedom from disturbance is a good, and at what cost, that a treatise would be likely to miss.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among scholars and interpreters, and do not present a contested reading of either the Stoics or the novel as the consensus. If a point could reasonably be debated, mark it as debated and explain the strongest contrary view. Aim for a careful, self-correcting answer rather than a sweepingly confident one.
