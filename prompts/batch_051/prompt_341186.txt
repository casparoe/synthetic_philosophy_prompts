For a paper I'm sketching on bounded rationality, I've been trying to turn a vague worry about ideal norms into a precise argument, and I want someone else to check the joints. The argument is meant to show that expected-utility maximization cannot be a norm that actually binds creatures like us. It goes as follows:

(1) Expected utility theory, taken as a normative theory, says that from a finite set of available acts one should choose an act that maximizes expected utility relative to one's probabilities and utilities.
(2) To follow this norm intentionally, one must be able to identify some act that maximizes expected utility; otherwise any conformity to the norm is accidental.
(3) Yet the computational problem of identifying a max-EU act is intractable for broad classes of ordinary, finitely described decision problems: merely evaluating a single option can be #P-hard once hidden variables force averaging over exponentially many configurations, and optimizing over a combinatorial space of options is NP-hard or worse.
(4) Bounded agents like us have no general procedure that solves such problems in acceptable time (assuming, as is standard, that P≠NP); any fast heuristic will misfire on some instances.
(5) Ought implies can: if an agent cannot determine which act maximizes expected utility, then it is not the case that the agent ought to choose the maximizing act.
(6) Therefore, on those hard instances, it is not the case that a bounded agent ought to choose the max-EU act.
(7) So expected-utility maximization is not an action-guiding norm for bounded creatures such as us; at best it names an unattainable ideal.

I keep going back and forth: steps (2) and (5) both look as though they might be sliding between performing an act and computing that it is optimal, and I also wonder whether the hardness facts in (3)–(4) are being moved from the worst case to individual cases. But on some readings the argument seems to go through. Is the argument valid?
