Write a ghazal in English, seven or eight couplets, on Wittgenstein's family-resemblance account of how a general term such as game applies: there need be no essence common to everything under the word, only a criss-crossing network of overlapping similarities, as among the members of a family. Let the ghazal form carry the argument rather than merely decorate it. In a ghazal, each couplet should stand on its own as a complete image or episode; the couplets are bound together not by narrative or inference but by the radif, a repeated phrase at the end of each second line, preceded by the qafia, the rhyme. Make each couplet a different game or a different use of language — a board game, a lover's teasing, a courtroom move, a child's pretend rule, a ritual exchange — such that every couplet shares something with a neighbor or two while no single trait, no single sense of game, runs through them all. The refrain itself should function as the family resemblance: it returns in every couplet and gives the poem its continuity without supplying a definition, a moral, or a common core, and it should not smuggle one in. Let the final couplet step back and include the poem itself, or the word carried by the refrain, as one more member of the family the poem has assembled. The result should satisfy both readers: the specialist should recognize the Investigations in the poem's very structure, and a reader who has never encountered Wittgenstein should feel what it is for many things to hang together without a center.
