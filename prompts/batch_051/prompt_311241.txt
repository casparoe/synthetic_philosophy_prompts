Consider the following pair of practices. In a seminar, a student with no adequate grounds for believing the time of the next train asserts flatly that it departs at 10.12, as though he knew. The assertion happens to be true, but the tutor would normally treat the guess as a failing: the student should not have asserted what he was not in a position to know. If he adds that he was only guessing, the confession makes matters worse, not better. The next day the same student sits an ordinary multiple-choice paper on the railway timetable, with no penalty for guessing, and again selects 10.12. Because 10.12 is the correct option, he is awarded full marks; even if he tells the examiner that he guessed, the mark stands. Conversely, a student who gives a careful, well-supported but mistaken answer in discussion is normally treated with some indulgence, while the same careful error on the multiple-choice paper is simply marked wrong. In the one setting the answerer is assessed for something like the warrant behind the answer; in the other, only the correctness of the answer itself appears to count.

The same tutor, often within the same week, applies both standards. Is there a single principled rationale that generates both, or is one practice simply out of step with the other? I want the strongest justification you can find, tested against nearby cases—an oral thesis defence, a long-answer examination, a consultant's diagnosis offered on a ward round, a courtroom cross-examination—and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up. If you think the two settings differ in a way that dissolves the problem, I need the exact difference in the act or its aim that is doing the justificatory work, and why it warrants such a marked difference in treatment. Please write your answer in British English.
