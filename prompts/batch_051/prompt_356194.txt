**The Sunk Cost Fallacy Is Not a Fallacy About Sunk Costs**

When people are told to ignore sunk costs, they are given a piece of decision theory that is almost undeniable: a cost already paid cannot be recovered, and a forward-looking decision should turn only on the prospective difference between available options. But exactly because that reasoning is so airtight, we should suspect that many real-life cases of "honoring sunk costs" are not actually counterexamples to it. I want to suggest that the fallacy label is often applied to decisions that are rational once we look carefully at what the original expenditure actually did.

A sunk cost is not merely an amount subtracted from some mental account. Expenditures have causal consequences. Time spent learning a technique is unrecoverable as time, but it produces skills that remain. Money spent on a project may produce partially completed components, institutional knowledge, or simplified next steps. In such cases, citing the past cost is perfectly consistent with ignoring sunk costs in the strict sense: the agent is responding to current assets the past cost created, not to the cost itself.

More interesting are cases in which the past cost created no asset yet still seems rationally relevant. Consider three channels. First, the cost may be evidence. Having spent heavily on a project, I know how strongly I once believed in it; if I now feel like abandoning it, I should ask whether my present mood is a noisier signal than the considered judgment that led me to invest. The past cost is not a reason in itself; it is a proxy for a past assessment. Second, the cost may have shaped the reliance of others to whom I am accountable. If they have planned around my completion, their reliance is a present fact with future consequences. Third, and most controversial, the cost may have changed my own preferences or self-conception: I may now value completing the project partly because I value being the kind of person who finishes what they begin. That valuation is a current fact, and abandoning the project would frustrate it.

Each of these channels allows an agent to take the past cost seriously without violating the injunction to ignore sunk costs. What the injunction really forbids is a fourth response: treating the unrecoverability itself as a reason to continue, as though continuing could retroactively redeem the expense. That is the genuine fallacy, and I suspect it is much rarer than the popular literature implies.

The upshot is a deflationary inversion. Rather than saying that people who honor sunk costs are irrational, we should say that the sunk-cost fallacy is, in many actual cases, not a fallacy about sunk costs at all, but a failure to articulate the forward-looking channel through which the past is relevant. The difficult boundary case is an agent who has no asset, no evidence, no third-party reliance, and no preference change, yet still continues because stopping feels like waste. Is such an agent as rare as I claim? And is there any principled way to rule out a pure waste-aversion preference that would make even this case rational in a broad preference-satisfaction sense? I am unsure about both.

---

I would particularly value comments on the following: (1) Are the first three channels genuinely distinct, or do they collapse into one another? (2) Is the formulation of the fourth channel as the "genuine" fallacy too strong? (3) Is there a familiar class of cases in which a past cost seems to do rational work without running through any of the first three channels? (4) Would the essay be stronger if it conceded that pure waste-aversion can itself be a legitimate normative attitude rather than a mistake?
