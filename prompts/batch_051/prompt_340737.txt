You are a professional philosopher working in social ontology and the metaphysics of kinds, comfortable with debates about natural kinds, social construction, and self-fulfilling classifications. Think carefully rather than reaching for a verdict: begin by brainstorming the most charitable reconstruction of my view, and only then test it.

Last night a friend and I argued for two hours about whether generations — Boomers, Gen X, Millennials, Gen Z, and the rest — are real. My friend's line was that they are essentially fake: arbitrary fifteen-to-twenty-year slices through a continuous stream of births, kept alive by consultants, HR departments, and listicles, with no more unity than a set of people whose phone numbers end in the same digit. I found myself resisting this, but not on the usual grounds that generations share formative experiences. By the end of the argument my position had hardened into something I want checked. Let me set it out in my own words.

I kept wanting to distinguish two ways a cohort label could be real. A "property kind" is real because its members share a robust cluster of traits that a common formative experience produced — people who grew up during a famine, say, or combat veterans of a particular war. Generations, I think, rarely qualify as property kinds: their supposed shared traits are weak, the trait lists are usually reverse-engineered after the label already exists, and the birth-year boundaries do no independent work. But I wonder whether generations are real as what I'll call "contrast kinds." A contrast kind, as I mean it, is a group that comes to have a weak but real existence through a sustained practice of reciprocal stereotyping with its named neighbours. Once labels like Millennial and Boomer circulate, people use them to interpret their own behaviour and the behaviour of the adjacent cohorts; they come to experience their cohort as a "we" partly because there is a named "them" on either side. The label does not merely track an independent kind; it helps bring one into being. The shared property is not a first-order trait but a second-order one: members jointly occupy a position in an on-going scheme of generational comparison, and they typically know it. On this view a generation is real roughly the way a rivalry or a border is real — dependent on representation, but not merely an illusion, because the representation changes expectations and conduct.

The central claim, then, is that generations are largely not discovered by demographers; they are stabilized and made real by contrast with adjacent cohorts, and their causal power flows from people orienting to the labels rather than from any independent property cluster. My clearest case is the Millennial/Boomer contrast: the two labels are maintained less by evidence than by the usefulness each side gets from having the other as a named explanation of its frustrations — housing costs, workplace culture, the online jeering in both directions — and yet, after two decades of that practice, the labels arguably do correlate with real differences in self-presentation, consumption, and political expression, because people have been modelling themselves in relation to them.

I realize this sounds close to the familiar idea that classifications of people can loop back and change the classified, and to the older sociological idea of the self-fulfilling prophecy, and I do not mind if the answer reduces to "a looping effect with a generational hat." But I specifically want to know whether the contrast mechanism — the idea that the kind is generated not mainly by classification from above but by lateral comparison between adjacent cohorts — is a distinct mechanism or just a special case. So please do four things. (1) Reconstruct my view in its strongest form, separating what is definitional, what is a causal hypothesis, and what is an empirical prediction. (2) Say what already exists under another name and where my formulation differs, if at all — name the positions or mechanisms, but do not give a bibliography. (3) Present the best objection my friend could have made, the one that survives my replies, and then the best defence I could still mount. (4) End with a clear verdict: are generations real, in exactly which of my senses, and what evidence would most change your mind?
