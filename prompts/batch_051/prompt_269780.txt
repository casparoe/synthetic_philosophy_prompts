I'm in a historiography seminar this term and we keep circling back to counterfactuals — the professor will say something like, had Franz Ferdinand's driver not taken that wrong turn in Sarajevo, would the war still have happened, and half the room jumps in. I've developed a policy about this and I want to know whether it's sound. My rule is: the moment an argument goes counterfactual, I stop granting it any evidential weight. Not that I think the question is strictly meaningless; more that I think any answer is hopelessly underdetermined, because once you alter one event you have to hold the rest of the world fixed somehow, and there's no principled way to do that. You just smuggle in whichever story you already believed. Even the supposed safeguards — keep the rewrite small, make the alternate course historically plausible, don't violate what we know about how the world worked — feel like rules of thumb that do the real work for the author ahead of time. So in discussion I basically tune out until we get back to what actually happened.

But the policy has a cost. Sometimes the counterfactual is doing genuine explanatory work, like isolating which factor mattered, and if I refuse to engage I cant tell the difference between "the war was overdetermined" and "it really did hang on a few contingent decisions." And I also worry my rule isn't neutral. I've always found inevitability arguments sort of comforting — they make history feel less absurd — and I think I might be using "counterfactuals are unfalsifiable" as a way to protect a soft determinism I already hold. It would be awfully convenient if the one kind of reasoning that could refute inevitability claims was exactly the kind I've declared out of bounds.

So: is this policy defensible epistemic hygiene, or is it bias wearing an epistemology costume? How would I tell the difference from the inside, given that I'm the one with the motive? I'd like a committed answer: lead with your verdict — keep the policy, amend it, or drop it — in the first sentence, and then give your reasons without restating the question.
