I have been turning over whether the old rule that simpler explanations are more likely to be true has any real backing, and I keep ending up at the same tangle. The usual informal defense of Occam's razor is that simpler theories are preferable because they are somehow more likely. There is also a mathematical version of this case, usually called Solomonoff induction, which I understand roughly as follows. Measure the simplicity of a hypothesis by the length of the shortest computer program that would generate its predictions, and assign each hypothesis a prior probability that shrinks as its program gets longer. Shorter-program hypotheses then start out more probable, and with enough evidence the procedure is supposed to converge toward the truth for any computable regularity. But I have since learned that the simplicity score depends on which universal programming language one uses to write the programs. The standard reply is that changing languages only shifts all the scores by a constant, but that constant can be made as large as one likes, so which finite hypotheses count as simple is not settled by the mathematics itself. The scheme is also uncomputable, since taking every possible program into account is something no finite procedure can actually do. And then there is the development I cannot stop thinking about: the artificial systems that currently generalize best in practice are deep networks with enormous numbers of parameters, which look like extremely long programs, exactly the sort of thing the razor was supposed to penalize, yet they plainly do more than memorize their training data. My question is whether the mathematical case for Occam's razor survives these developments. I do not want a survey or a history of the idea. I want you to pinpoint which premise of the original argument is under the most pressure, say whether it can be repaired into something weaker that still explains why simplicity works when it does, and then give an honest verdict on whether we should go on treating simplicity as a guide to truth or instead as a practical convenience of searching a large space of hypotheses. Please write your answer as continuous prose, like a letter or an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
