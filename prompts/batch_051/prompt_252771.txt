I'm a novelist, and I have spent the past year trying to take Tolstoy's infection theory of art seriously rather than dismissing it as moralizing. The harder I try, the more I get tangled in the following argument, which seems to show that on his own criterion no work is ever art. I would like you to go through it step by step and tell me whether it is valid, and if it is not, exactly where it fails.

Here is the argument:

(1) For Tolstoy, art is a transmission of feeling: an artist who has lived through a feeling arranges words, sounds, or images so that an audience is infected with that feeling.
(2) Transmission requires that the audience come to have the same feeling the artist had.
(3) Feelings are individuated, at least in part, by their objects and histories: my grief for a particular person, in a particular situation, with a particular history, is a different feeling from your grief for a different person in a different situation.
(4) An audience member never shares the whole of the artist's situation and history, and often does not know the particular object of the feeling.
(5) So the audience never comes to have the same feeling the artist had.
(6) So, by Tolstoy's criterion, no work is ever art.

Tolstoy plainly believed that some works are art, and so do I, so something must be wrong. My suspicion falls on (2) or (3), but each time I try to amend them I seem to lose the idea of transmission that makes Tolstoy's view distinctive. Please say whether the argument is valid; if it is not, name the exact step or steps that fail, explain why each looked true, and state what the corrected version of the criterion would be. If you think the argument is valid, say what a defender of Tolstoy would have to give up instead.

Also, please tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis and which point you are least sure of. Please write in American English.
