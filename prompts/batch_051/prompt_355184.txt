Act as a political theorist specializing in the intersection of early modern political thought and Roman constitutional history. I am working on a short essay about analogies between the fall of the Roman Republic and contemporary democratic decline, and I want you to make a specific, rigorous argument.

Some analysts treat the late Republic as a sequence of violent episodes—the Gracchan tribunates, Marius and Sulla, the Catilinarian conspiracy, Clodius and Milo, Caesar’s crossing of the Rubicon, the proscriptions—interrupted by stretches of ordinary politics. Others treat the entire period as a single revolutionary crisis. I want you to defend the stronger version of the latter view by appealing to Hobbes’s account of the state of war in Leviathan, chapter 13. In that chapter, Hobbes argues that the condition of war is not confined to actual fighting. The condition of war is a temporally extended situation in which the will to contend by battle is sufficiently known, just as foul weather consists not in a shower or two but in an inclination to rain over many days. Do not produce a verbatim quotation; paraphrase the idea carefully in your own words.

Begin by brainstorming at least three distinct ways the Hobbesian distinction could lead us to redescribe the late Republic. Think about what would count as evidence of a known disposition to fight: repeated reliance on private armies, the treatment of political rivals as existential enemies, the tendency of constitutional disputes to escalate toward violence even when a particular moment is outwardly calm. Then anticipate the strongest objection a critic could raise: that this stretches the concept of war so far that it becomes indistinguishable from ordinary factional competition, or that it ignores real differences between moments of open civil war and moments when institutions still functioned. Address that objection directly rather than dismissing it.

Then write a sustained argument of roughly 800 to 1,200 words defending the thesis that the late Republic, from the Gracchi to Augustus, is best understood as one continuous Hobbesian state of war, and that this framework should shape how we evaluate modern analogies to Rome. Be specific about at least one Roman episode and at least one modern political case, but do not rely on online sources or citations. The argument should not be a survey of similarities; it should show how the Hobbesian distinction changes the burden of proof. Conclude by stating what kind of evidence would, on your view, indicate that a modern democracy has entered the relevant condition even before any open civil violence occurs. Defend the thesis as if you hold it, without hedging.
