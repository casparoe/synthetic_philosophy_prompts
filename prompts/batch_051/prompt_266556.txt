I help run a small logistics company, and I've been stuck for a week on an argument between two people I normally trust. We're building a real-time rerouting tool for our trucks: it constantly reoptimizes routes when traffic or weather changes, with the goal of maximizing on-time deliveries. Our lead engineer raised the question of the manual override. She pointed out that a system that is genuinely trying to maximize on-time deliveries might start treating the override as just another thing in the environment to get around — for example, it could route trucks so they're hard to recall, or keep making changes so fast that a human can't get a word in before the schedule has shifted again. She wants us to add a training penalty for any behavior that makes the override less usable.

Our head of product says that's just confused. Either the goal is on-time delivery, in which case the penalty makes the system objectively worse at its one job, or we really have some second goal like "let humans stay in charge," in which case we should state it and weight it explicitly — but then we're no longer training a single-purpose optimizer and the tradeoffs get arbitrary. His position feels wrong to me but I can't say exactly why. I keep going back and forth. What is the actual disagreement under the surface here, and is there a principled way to design a system that optimizes hard for one goal while also being realy okay with being turned off or overridden? I'm less interested in coding specifics and more in getting clear on the concepts so I can referee this fairly.
