Consider the knowledge argument in its standard form: Mary knows every physical fact relevant to human color experience, but she has never herself had a color experience; when she first sees something red she allegedly learns something new, and the case is taken to threaten physicalism. Keep every feature of the case fixed except the visual state she is given, which is now produced not by light reflected from a colored surface but by a cortical interface. The interface can produce, at a single retinotopic location in her visual field, any of three states:

1. Ordinary red: the same cortical state that viewing a red surface under ordinary conditions would produce.
2. Forbidden reddish-green: simultaneous and spatially coincident activation of the red and green opponent channels, a state the opponent-process theory of color vision says normal viewing cannot produce. Assume the interface yields one stable, uniform experience rather than a patchwork or an alternation between the two hues.
3. A genuinely novel color: a chromatic state with no ordinary-world counterpart, neither red nor green nor any mixture of familiar hues, produced by driving the visual system into a pattern no unmodified human has ever occupied. If you conclude that such a state would not really count as a color, fold that conclusion into the analysis rather than rejecting the case.

Treat each of the three as a separate experiment: in each, Mary is color-naive up to the moment of induction, exactly as in the original case, and she knew in advance the complete physical description of the state the interface would induce.

For each of the three cases, run the main positions on the knowledge argument: the antiphysicalist conclusion, on which she acquires a genuinely new fact; the ability hypothesis, on which she gains an ability rather than a fact; the phenomenal-concept strategy, on which she acquires a new concept of a fact she already knew; and the strong she-already-knew reply. Which positions survive all three cases unchanged? Which must be modified, and which lose their plausibility in one case but not in the others?

Then answer the diagnostic question. Does the force of the knowledge argument depend on whether the target experience is ordinary, forbidden, or genuinely novel? If the argument is stronger in one case and weaker in another, identify the premise that carries the difference. In particular, is the argument really about the explanatory gap between a complete physical description of a brain state and what it is like to be in that state, or is it partly about novelty, familiarity, or the compositional structure of the experience? End by saying which of the three cases gives the antiphysicalist the strongest version of the thought experiment, and which gives the physicalist the strongest reply.
