I run an undergraduate philosophy club with about sixteen students, mostly second- and third-years, and we have one eighty-minute meeting. I want a game that makes them feel the force of what is usually called Parfit's hitchhiker and the associated idea of rational irrationality, and I want them to run into the problem themselves before anyone names it. The idea I have in mind, roughly, is this: a stranded hitchhiker can get a ride only by promising to pay the driver afterward, but both know that once he is in town he will prefer not to pay and the driver cannot enforce the promise; so the hitchhiker may arrange in advance to make his later self keep the promise even though keeping it will no longer be in that later self's interest. The puzzle is whether it can be rational to make yourself act against your own future self's better judgment. By the end of the session the students should see three things: why a bare promise creates no trust in a one-shot interaction when the promisor will later prefer to break it; how a visible restriction on or change to one's future self can make both parties better off; and what is still uncomfortable about calling that arrangement rational.

Please design the activity end to end: roles, rules, materials that need no real money, a point payoff scheme, and a timeline that fills the eighty minutes and lets everyone play at least twice. The rules and incentives should do the pedagogical work; the debrief only names and organizes what the players already felt. The hitchhiker must have, before the driver chooses, some visible pre-arrangement option, and I would like two versions of it run side by side across rounds. One version should work by penalty: if he later refuses to pay, he forfeits something already put up. The other should work by changing his future self: he takes a potion whose effect is that his later self keeps the promise. Let the students see that both get him the ride, then make the difference between the two versions the hinge of the debrief rather than announcing it during play.

The debrief should map particular moments in play onto backward induction, credible commitment, and rational irrationality, and should press the students on whether the two versions are rationally different: the penalty seems to change what it will be in the hitchhiker's interest to do at the moment of choice, while the potion seems to leave the later act against his interest but change him. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle those failures without lecturing: I suspect players will keep promises out of social norms even when the points say not to, and drivers will stop out of niceness, which hides the trap from view. Also be honest about what the game cannot reproduce: it models the hitchhiker's later self as a rule or a separate player rather than as the same person genuinely deciding with changed beliefs or desires, so the facilitator will have to supply the questions of whether one can really choose to become irrational and which self is being benefited. Do not announce the name of the idea until the debrief.

Keep the complete plan, including the timeline and debrief, to at most 850 words.
