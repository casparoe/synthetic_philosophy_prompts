I edit the history-of-science notes in a scientific reference work. Most weeks I have to approve or revise a sentence of the form 'X is named after Y, who discovered it in Z'; I have at most a morning per entry, and the entries are widely trusted, so a false attribution is a real error but so is an entry that hectors readers about naming. Stigler's law — roughly, that scientific discoveries are almost never named after their original discoverer — has made me distrust these stock attributions, but it cannot by itself tell me which eponyms to keep. Please write me a reusable decision procedure for this. It should set out the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; when the procedure returns a definite instruction — keep the eponym, hedge it, or say that the name is misleading — and when it must stop and hand the decision to a senior editor because the step is a judgement call rather than a question of fact. Give me the procedure you would actually adopt, with its choices made and defended, not a menu of options. Then run it on two cases: (a) a reaction named after a chemist who published the first clear and correct account, although an unpublished notebook by a contemporary describes the reaction twenty years earlier; and (b) a syndrome named after a physician who merely popularised it in a widely used textbook, while the first correct description came from someone else and is now little known. I expect at least one application to yield a verdict I would rather not publish. Finally, say explicitly what the procedure cannot settle and which remaining steps are judgement dressed up as method.
