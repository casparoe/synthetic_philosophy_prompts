I cover science and ideas for a living, and a claim from Thomas Metzinger's self-model theory has stuck with me. Metzinger argues that the self is not an inner thing that has experiences; it is a representational structure, a self-model the brain continuously builds and updates. In ordinary waking life, he says, this self-model is phenomenally transparent: the system does not experience the self-model as a model. The machinery of self-modeling is invisible from the inside. What shows up in experience is only the model's content — a body felt as one's own, a spatial point of view, a sense of authorship over one's actions — so the world and the body are experienced as simply given. Transparency, on this view, is part of why a solid, non-constructed self feels so undeniable: the construction itself never appears.

The claim I want you to test, as far as it can be tested, is this: transparency is a real and graded property of the self-model, usable as an empirical variable, not merely a metaphor for the fact that we sometimes overlook our own information processing. If that is right, then transparency should fail or degrade under the right perturbations, and the difference between transparent and opaque self-modeling should be observable from outside.

Design the strongest concrete test of that claim. Treat it as an operationalization problem: propose an experiment or battery (variants of the rubber-hand illusion, full-body ownership illusions, agency misattributions, sensorimotor conflict, depersonalization or dissociative states, or something better), say exactly what you would measure and what comparison would do the work, and state what pattern of results would count as a genuine failure of transparency rather than a mere change in what the model represents. Then say what a positive result would and would not establish, what a negative result would establish, and what part of the claim — plausibly something about phenomenal character itself — would remain beyond any third-person test even in principle. Lead with the bottom line: give your proposed test in a sentence or two before elaborating. Keep the entire answer to 800 words.
