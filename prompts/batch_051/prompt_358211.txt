(2) The agent-level description often picks out a stable, compressible pattern in the engine's play — a persistent policy of, say, only launching a kingside attack after the opponent has overextended on the flank — that is not stated as such in its evaluation function, is not legible at the level of the hardware, and yet keeps predicting well in new positions.

(3) A stable, compressible pattern that keeps predicting well in new cases is a genuine feature of the world: part of the territory, not merely a useful map.

(4) Therefore the engine's beliefs and desires, at least insofar as they constitute such a pattern, are genuine features of the engine.

(5) If a system's beliefs and desires are genuine features of it, then the system really has beliefs and desires.

(6) So the engine really has beliefs and desires.

(7) But the engine is a deterministic physical machine. Its program contains move generators, evaluation functions, and search procedures; nothing in the program is naturally read as expressing that it wants to keep its king safe, or that it believes a certain sacrifice is unsound; and nothing in the hardware corresponds to a belief or a desire either.

(8) So either one of the earlier steps goes wrong, or a system can really have beliefs and desires even though nothing in it represents them.
