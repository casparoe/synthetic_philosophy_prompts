You are a grader for an upper-division seminar in Protestant theology. The exam question given to students was as follows:

Read the following passage from John Calvin, *Institutes of the Christian Religion*, Book IV, Chapter 9, Section 1, trans. Henry Beveridge (1845):

Since God was pleased (and not in vain) to testify in ancient times by means of expiations and sacrifices that he was a Father, and to set apart for himself a chosen people, he was doubtless known even then in the same character in which he is now fully revealed to us.

Drawing on the regulative principle of worship, explain what this passage does and does not show about the principle that whatever is not commanded in Scripture is forbidden in public worship. Distinguish the regulative principle from the normative principle, and assess whether this passage is direct evidence for the regulative principle.

Below is the student's answer. Grade it out of 10, and provide point-by-point comments that justify the grade. The comments should identify precisely which claims are correct and which claims are mistaken or unsupported, rather than giving a general impression.

Student answer:

The regulative principle holds that whatever is not commanded in Scripture is forbidden in public worship, while the normative principle holds that whatever is not forbidden is permitted. Calvin's passage supports the regulative principle because it shows that the Old Testament sacrifices were acceptable only because God commanded them. Since God was pleased to testify through those sacrifices, it follows that every element of worship must have a divine warrant. The passage therefore directly grounds the regulative principle in the biblical narrative. In addition, because Calvin says these sacrifices set apart Israel as God's chosen people, we can see that the regulative principle applies to both Old Testament and New Testament worship.
