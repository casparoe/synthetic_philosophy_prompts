I spent my career as a research scientist in the physical sciences, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy, chiefly decision theory and formal epistemology. Please adopt the role of an expert in those fields and work through the following problem carefully. Before you commit to an answer, brainstorm the three or four strongest candidate justifications for the asymmetry I will describe, state the strongest objection to each, and only then defend the position you think is best.

Here is the puzzle as I experience it. A sensible homeowner pays several hundred dollars a year to insure against a house fire whose annual probability the insurer puts at something like one in a thousand; the premium is roughly equal to the expected loss plus administrative overhead, and almost no one considers this irrational. The same person, however, will decline without a second thought a stranger's demand for twenty dollars to prevent a catastrophe the stranger claims he can avert—say, the torment of ten billion future people—while conceding that the probability he can really do such a thing is perhaps one in a trillion. This is, I have since learned, a version of what is called Pascal's mugging. Taken literally, expected-value reasoning seems to say that the second payment is overwhelmingly a better bargain than the insurance premium: the expected harm averted is astronomically larger. Yet our ordinary practice is to trust the tiny probability produced by an insurance company or a public lottery, and to dismiss the tiny probability asserted by a stranger, even when the asserted cost of acting is trivial and the asserted stakes are vast.

So the asymmetry is this: we regulate our conduct by small probabilities when they come packaged with familiar institutions—actuarial tables, regulatory oversight, repeatable sampling, competitive pricing—but we refuse to regulate our conduct by small probabilities that are merely asserted, no matter how large the stakes are asserted to be. Is there a defensible justification for that asymmetry, or is it a rationalization for our reluctance to honor the demands of expected-value reasoning when the probability cannot be measured?

I want you to test the candidate justifications severely. If you defend the asymmetry by appealing to the measurability, calibration, or verifiability of institutional probabilities, explain whether this is a matter of principle or merely a practical heuristic, and say what the principle or heuristic dictates when a claim is unverifiable but the asserted harm is so large that even an extremely small probability would overwhelm any ordinary cost. If you defend the asymmetry by saying that rational agents may set a lower bound on the probabilities they will take seriously, or an upper bound on the values they will assign to outcomes, show how those bounds are motivated without being tailored to reach the comfortable result. If you think the asymmetry is unjustified, say what a consistent practice would look like: should we hand over money to every stranger with a coherent story of immense expected value, or should we revise our willingness to pay insurance premiums and buy lottery tickets against tiny, well-calibrated probabilities? Give me your own verdict on whether the ordinary asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned, and answer the strongest objection to that verdict.
