You are a professional philosopher working in decision theory and the theory of intention; please take your time and reason carefully rather than reaching for a memorized answer. I have been working through Kavka's toxin puzzle for a seminar paper on instrumental rationality, and I suspect I have already encountered every standard move, so I want your help finding something genuinely new.

Here is the puzzle, briefly, so we are working from the same version. An eccentric but trustworthy billionaire offers you one million dollars, to be deposited in your account tomorrow morning, on the condition that at midnight tonight you *genuinely intend* to drink a mildly unpleasant but harmless toxin tomorrow afternoon. The billionaire has an infallible intention detector, and you know this. You will be paid before the time for drinking arrives, and you are not required to drink in order to collect; the intention alone suffices. You know that, once the money has cleared, you will have no reason to drink the toxin and only mild discomfort to avoid, so your future self will not drink it. And you know this about yourself at midnight. Since you cannot rationally intend to do what you are certain you will not do, it looks as though you cannot form the intention — even though you would gladly pay for a pill that gave it to you.

Here are the standard solutions or diagnoses I already know, and I do not want any variant of them repeated: (1) Kavka's own deflationary diagnosis, on which the case shows that reasons for intending are not reducible to reasons for acting, and a rational agent may simply be unable to meet the condition; (2) the voluntarist response, which denies the premise that intentions cannot be formed at will and says you can just intend to drink, collect the money, and revise the intention afterward; (3) the precommitment response, which creates an independent reason to drink — a side bet, a penalty clause, an enforcer — so that drinking tomorrow becomes rational and the intention tonight becomes honest, though critics call this changing the example rather than solving it; (4) the dispositional response, on which a rational agent can cultivate and maintain a standing disposition to follow through on intentions or commitments, and can then intend to drink because drinking is an instance of preserving that disposition; and (5) the planning-theoretic diagnosis, on which intentions are plan states governed by norms of consistency, means-end coherence, and stability, so the puzzle is an illustration of why you cannot rationally intend to drink, not a counterexample to planning theory.
