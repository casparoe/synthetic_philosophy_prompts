Your colleague in a seminar on existentialism offers the following deflationary reading of Sartre. "Bad faith is just the ordinary phenomenon of lying to oneself. When someone tells himself a comforting falsehood — that a failing relationship is fine, or that a promotion he was denied was not worth having — one part of his mind does the deceiving while another part is kept in the dark. Once we grant that the mind contains such partitions, the phenomenon is no more puzzling than an ordinary lie between two people. Sartre's apparatus — pre-reflective awareness of freedom, a fundamental project of self-deception, anguish at the effort to flee one's freedom — is therefore unnecessary." The colleague is leaning on the analogy with an interpersonal lie to conclude that bad faith raises no special problem for a single, unified consciousness, and that Sartre's account of radical freedom can be set aside as overwrought.

Audit this analogy. Make the mapping explicit first: in the model of an ordinary lie, what is supposed to correspond to the liar, the dupe, the truth concealed, and the act of concealment when the object is bad faith? Then show where the correspondence breaks. Pay particular attention to what the analogy quietly imports from the interpersonal case — above all, two numerically distinct centers of awareness and a clean division of epistemic labor between them. Consider whether the colleague can repair the analogy by replacing the two persons with two parts of one mind, or whether that repair merely relocates the difficulty (for example, in a censor that must itself know what to hide). Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the colleague's deflationary conclusion from those that are incidental. Finally, say whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table; if it does not, say what better analogy, if any, does the work the colleague wanted without importing the same problems, and what that substitute analogy can and cannot carry.
