A handbook chapter I was assigned summarizes the debate roughly as follows. Kosslyn and his collaborators are said to have shown that mental images are effectively little pictures laid out in the brain: when you rotate a figure in your head you spin an inner picture, and an inner eye scans or inspects it, which is why reaction times track rotation angle and scan distance. The chapter treats the classic behavioral results—longer response times for greater mental-rotation angles and for scanning across greater imaged distances—as direct demonstrations that images have a spatial, picture-like format. It then describes Pylyshyn as the skeptic who denies that people really have mental images and treats reports of imagery as something subjects produce because the task implicitly tells them to. Finally, it concludes that neuroscience has settled the matter in Kosslyn's favor, since imagining activates early visual cortex, the same tissue used in seeing, and therefore the representations involved must be depictive.
