Write a deadpan piece of corporate satire in the form of a “Truthful Communications Compliance Manual” — roughly 700 words, formatted as seven or eight short headed policy clauses — for an invented company that sells customer-analytics subscriptions to small businesses. The manual, introduced by an invented Chief Integrity Officer, trains sales staff to reliably induce false beliefs in customers while remaining within a rule that forbids only literal falsehood. The target is the position that misleading is morally safer than lying: the claim that a speaker is answerable only for the truth of what she explicitly asserts, and that the false conclusion a hearer draws is the hearer’s own doing. The humor should be earned rather than broad, and should come from the manual’s getting the philosophy right. In character, it should work through at least three of the following: the gap between what a sentence literally says and what it conversationally implicates; the difference between falsely implicating a proposition and merely allowing the customer to infer it; how a carefully chosen literally true sentence can communicate a false proposition in context; how customers rationally rely on a speaker’s whole communicative act rather than on truth conditions alone; and the point that a literal-truth-only rule does not eliminate the lie, it only relocates it upstream into the selection, ordering, and framing of true facts. Never have the manual admit that staff are deceiving customers; give it euphemisms, such as “assist the client toward a suboptimal inference.” The manual should quietly reveal the weakness of its own position at least once — for example, by having to explain why these techniques may be used on customers but not on internal investigators, or by conceding that the company’s own lawyers regard a customer’s reasonable inference as something the company is answerable for. The piece should be funny to someone who knows the lying-versus-misleading literature and readable to someone who does not.
