Here's something I can't get straight. Most of us would say a judge who sentenced a car thief to be tortured for even a single day was a sadist, and any legal system that tortured a petty criminal forever would be an abomination. We reject punishments as unjust when they're wildly out of proportion to the crime, and we do this even for genuinely terrible crimes. But many of the same people—maybe most people who have ever lived—hold that an ordinary sinner who dies unrepentent is punished forever, tormented without end. The crime was finite: a few decades of selfishness, or even just unbelief. The punisment has no limit. That looks like exactly the disproportionality we would never tolerate from a human court, turned up to infinity and applied to everyone.

So I'm asking whether anything actually justifies treating the divine case differently from the human case, or whether it's just that the tradition has made one of them feel normal. The candidate justifications I hear are: the offense is against an infinite being and so is itself infinite; the damned freely chose hell and so it is self-inflicted, not imposed by God; and our ordinary ideas of proportionality simply don't reach the afterlife, because the relevant goods and harms are incommensurable. I'd like you to take the strongest of these (or supply a better one), defend it against the nearby cases that seem to break it—a human king who tortures a subject forever for a single insult, a cult that gets members to sign a contract consenting to endless servitude, a god who just annihilates the wicked instead—and then tell me honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept, or whether our revulsion at the human case should leak over to the divine one, or the other way around. And say a bit about whether annihilationism and universal salvation are best understood as attempts to fix exactly this proportionality problem rather than as independent views.
