I spent much of last evening arguing with a friend who lived in France for a decade and defends a fairly strict version of laïcité. He kept pressing one principle: the state, and its institutions, must be scrupulously blind to religion, because any official acknowledgment of a citizen's faith — positive or negative — amounts to the state marking citizens by their religion. I resisted the principle, though I could not state cleanly what I would put in its place. Here are my actual verdicts on a range of cases we discussed or invented. (1) A state primary school sends a nine-year-old home for wearing a small headscarf: I think this is wrong; the punishment falls on the child rather than protecting the school's neutrality. (2) A municipal swimming pool bans full-body swimwear of the kind some observant Muslim women wear: I think this is wrong too; the pool is not issuing a statement about Islam. (3) A state secondary school refuses to let a teacher teach while wearing a face-covering veil: I side with the school; a seminar is partly face-to-face in a very literal sense. (4) A school canteen drops its non-pork option and tells Muslim children to take the vegetarian meal or go hungry: I think this is wrong, though I would rather the school fix it with a universal menu change than by formally labeling halal and non-halal lines. (5) A town hall keeps a nativity scene up through December: I do not much care, but I would accept a rule against it if the rule were applied even-handedly to every group. (6) Public money follows a child to a religious school, provided the school teaches the full common curriculum, admits students of any faith or none, and is audited: I think this is fine. (7) A public university sets aside an otherwise unused room for Muslim students' midday prayers: I think this is fine, so far as the room is bookable by any group for analogous use. (8) A national law bars every public employee from wearing any religious symbol while on duty: I find this too blunt, but I am less outraged by it than by case (1), because adults take jobs under known rules.

Is there a single coherent principle that gets all of these, or close to all of them? I am especially bothered by the pairs (1) and (8), and (3) and (1), which feel different to me but may not be defensibly different. Can you state the best available principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it forces me to revise, and tell me what it implies about a case I keep going back and forth on: a civil registrar who refuses, on religious grounds, to officiate at a same-sex wedding — should the state accommodate the refusal, discipline the registrar, or something else entirely?
