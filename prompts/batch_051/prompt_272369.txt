I have spent a long time drawn to a Popperian demarcation criterion: for a body of claims to count as empirical science, it must stick its neck out with risky, falsifiable predictions, and a framework that can be bent around any observation is a metaphysics—interesting, perhaps, but not science. At the same time, I cannot bring myself to say that Darwinian natural selection is not science. The conflict is live for me, because natural selection really does look exposed to the falsificationist challenge: the core principle can be stated so broadly that it comes close to a tautology about differential reproduction, and particular adaptationist explanations of a trait are notoriously post hoc—if one story fails, a skilled biologist can supply another without abandoning the framework. Yet I would bet heavily on common descent and on the modern synthesis, which seem to me as well-confirmed as anything science has produced. Popper himself at one point classified Darwinism as a metaphysical research programme rather than a testable theory; he apparently later softened that verdict, and I half-suspect the earlier Popper had his finger on something real.

I don't want a verdict; I want a map of the costs. Take each of these moves in turn and give its strongest version: (1) my intuition that Darwinism must be science is misdirected and should be dropped, so that I can say without embarrassment that natural selection is a metaphysical research programme in the falsificationist sense; (2) the falsificationist criterion should be revised or replaced, with Darwinism as the case that forces the revision—and an account of what remains of demarcation afterwards; (3) both survive on a disambiguated reading of "Darwinism" or "science"—and whether that disambiguation is substantive or an equivocation. Finish by saying which move you would endorse and what it forces me to give up.
