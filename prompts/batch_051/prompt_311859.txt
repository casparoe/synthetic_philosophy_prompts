I am a novelist — three published books, none of them about philosophy — and in three weeks I am giving a ninety-minute session at a residential programme for writers and artists. The advertised title is "Why does 'that's disgusting' feel like 'that's wrong'?" The room will be roughly thirty novelists, memoirists, and painters: bright, argumentative, no formal training in philosophy or moral psychology, and highly practiced at making an audience feel revulsion without having analyzed whether revulsion tracks any real moral difference.

I am not asking you to write the talk. I am asking you to plan the teaching. The session should do one piece of conceptual work: move the audience from using "disgusting" as a near-synonym for "wrong" to holding apart two claims — that human beings moralize purity and sanctity (descriptive moral psychology), and that this family of judgments deserves a place in a defensible ethics (normative question). By the end I want them to be able to say, without prompting, what marks "degrading" or "sacrilegious" as different in kind from "unjust" or "harmful"; to treat disgust and the sense of the sacred as two poles of one evaluative axis rather than unrelated moods; and to state the strongest reason for granting disgust and sanctity some ethical authority and the strongest reason for refusing it. I want the session to leave them with a live, precise disagreement rather than a debunking or a vindication.

Plan the explanatory arc in the way a novelist would care about: one carrying image or scene that does most of the work, with the ideas introduced in the order that makes the distinction feel inevitable rather than imported.

In particular, your plan must do the following.

Choose the single carrying example and say why it can carry the whole session. It should come from the class of harmless taboo violations — private, victimless, fully consensual acts that predictably call up moralized disgust — so that the audience cannot slide back into harm or consent language. It should not be primarily sexual, not obscure, not merely a case of physical grossness, and it must sit exactly where revulsion and moral judgment begin to come apart.

Give the order of ideas as numbered stages, each with its dependency: what must be in place before the audience can even hear the normative question, why that piece must precede the next, and where the turn from descriptive moral psychology to normative ethics happens. Build in the separation I care about most: "these judgments are widespread, deep, and not reducible to harm" does not entail "these judgments are justified." And rejecting the second should not force anyone to treat the whole domain as a pathology.

Map the misconceptions. The audience will arrive with, and generate, predictable mistakes. I expect at least these four: that purity is simply sexual purity or ritual cleanliness; that the feeling of disgust is evidence of wrongness; the reverse, that disgust is too primitive to count so all purity norms are disguised power or prudery; and that if a purity judgment cannot be backed by a harm it has been refuted. Tell me which further misconceptions will form, at what point in the sequence each is most likely to arise, and where exactly to head it off — with what question, distinction, or example.

Tell me how to check understanding. Give me one question or task for the end of the session, and say what I should count as evidence that they have grasped the distinction rather than merely picked up the vocabulary.

Tell me what to leave out and how to say so. At this level, which tempting digressions should I refuse (for example, the cross-cultural and experimental literature, the evolutionary origins of disgust, the neuroscience, the legal-application question, or the assessment of particular religious rituals), and what sentence should I use when someone pushes toward one of them?

Work through this carefully rather than pattern-matching to a standard lecture. Answer as a moral psychologist who has taught philosophy to working artists and who understands that a novelist needs the plan to have the economy of a piece of fiction. Make definite choices throughout: do not give me a menu of possible sequences or examples. Pick one through-line, one sequence, one carrying example, and one misconception map, and defend each choice against the nearest alternative — I would rather have a specific, defensible plan than a survey of possibilities. Where a real controversy should remain open, say which side the session will leave open and give me one sentence to mark it honestly.

Structure the answer in exactly these sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

1. Working notes (~150 words). The candidates you considered for the carrying example and for the sequence, which you rejected, and why.
2. The through-line (~150 words). The single idea the session exists to land, stated as an understanding rather than a slogan, and the endpoint the final minutes should reach.
3. The carrying example (~350 words). The one case or scene, why it can do the whole job, what it must have and must not, and how to introduce it.
4. The order of ideas (~600 words). The numbered stages, each with the dependency that places it after the previous stage and before the next.
5. The misconception map (~400 words). Each misconception, when it arises, where in the numbered sequence you head it off, and what defuses it.
6. Check for understanding (~150 words). One final question or task and the standard for success.
7. What to leave out (~150 words). The digressions to refuse at this level and a sentence to refuse them gracefully.
