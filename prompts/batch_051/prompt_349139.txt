For the past several years I have followed the running dispute between Niall Ferguson and Shashi Tharoor over the moral balance sheet of the British Raj, and I am struck by how much of the disagreement is not really about the facts but about whether the balancing exercise itself is legitimate. Ferguson proceeds as if the Raj can be assessed by drawing up a ledger: railways, irrigation, a common legal order, and the suppression of practices such as sati and thuggee on one side, and famines, deindustrialisation, violent repression, and humiliation on the other; one then asks whether the net is positive or negative. Tharoor proceeds as if the ledger is itself the error, because the benefits were incidental, extractive, or self-serving while the harms were systemic and the point of the enterprise rather than a side cost of it.

I would like you to read E. M. Forster's A Passage to India as a third party to this dispute, not as an illustration of either camp but as a novel that may have its own view about whether imperial goods and evils can be weighed on a common scale at all. Please give me an extended critical reading along the following lines. First, identify the episodes and relationships in the novel that each side would most naturally claim as support, and say whether a close reading actually bears those claims out or quietly undercuts them; give special attention to the trial of Aziz, the echo in the Marabar caves, the bridge party, and the failure of the Aziz–Fielding friendship. Second, take a position on the methodological question the two historians are really fighting over: does the novel suggest that a moral balance sheet for the Raj is possible but badly computed, or that the very idea of such a ledger misdescribes what empire is and does? Third, mark the places where the reading strains, such as the limits of Forster's social scope or his irony, where the novel resists being conscripted into the debate's terms; I want the honest critic, not the advocate. Finally, say what the novel adds to this dispute that historical argument and counter-argument alone do not.

Please write your answer in British English.
