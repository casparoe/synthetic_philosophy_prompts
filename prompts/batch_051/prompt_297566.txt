I have been arguing with colleagues about how far expected-value reasoning is supposed to reach, and the argument keeps exposing an asymmetry in the way we actually let it govern choices. In ordinary cases we are happy to be moved by tiny probabilities of very large stakes: a driver buckles a seatbelt for a journey on which the chance of a fatal crash is, say, one in a few million, and we think that plainly rational because the stake is a whole life; we pay a modest premium to insure a house against, say, a one-in-a-thousand annual chance of fire; we evacuate a town when a hurricane has a ten per cent chance of making landfall. But when the same form of reasoning is pushed outside that familiar range, nearly everyone refuses to follow it. Suppose a politician must choose between a programme that will save one actual person for certain and a programme with one chance in 10^30 of saving 10^40 lives. Expected-value reasoning, which seemed to do all the work in the seatbelt and insurance cases, now says the second programme is overwhelmingly better, since its expectation is 10^10 lives against one. Yet most of us would judge it fanatical, reckless, or simply wrong to let an astronomically improbable, astronomically large outcome dominate a certain rescue. Ordinary practical judgement is therefore asymmetric: we let expected value rule while probabilities and stakes sit in an ordinary range, and we decline to let it rule when both are inflated together. This is the tension that discussions of expected-value fanaticism are about.

Before I take a side, I want the strongest principled defence of that asymmetry you can construct — or a frank argument that it is just an inconsistency in our practice. Give the candidate principle in a precise, applicable form: a probability threshold below which possible outcomes are ignored, a rule about the diminishing practical significance of very large numbers of lives, a risk-sensitive or bounded-utility story, a constraint against treating astronomical expectations as concrete reasons, or something in that space. Then test it against hard cases. If the principle involves a threshold, say exactly where the threshold falls and whether that still preserves the seatbelt, insurance, and hurricane judgements while excluding the 10^30 case. In any event, show how the principle treats a ten per cent chance of saving ten lives, a one per cent chance of saving a thousand lives, a one-in-10^8 chance of saving 10^9 lives, and the original one-in-10^30 chance of saving 10^40 lives — noting that the middle two have the same expected value, ten lives, though their probabilities differ by a factor of a million. Finally, give an honest verdict: should we keep the asymmetry, accept expected-value reasoning all the way down, or reject both horns for something else? Please write your answer in British English.
