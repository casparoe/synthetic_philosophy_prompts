Could you give it a grade out of 10, and write the point-by-point marginal comments I should give—which moves are right, which are wrong, and why? I'd apreciate the feedback in American English.

Exam question:

"State the doctrine of double effect and its conditions. Then say how someone who accepts the doctrine should rule on each of the following, and note at least one place where its defenders disagree: (a) switching a runaway trolley away from five workers onto a side track with one worker; (b) pushing a large bystander off a footbridge to stop a trolley before it reaches five; (c) the loop case, in which the side track loops back onto the main line and the trolley is only stopped by the one worker's body before it can reach the five; (d) giving an agitated, dying patient enough morphine to control his pain even though it will suppress his breathing; (e) injecting a lethal dose to end the same patient's suffering; (f) tactical bombing of a munitions plant that is expected to kill nearby civilians, versus bombing civilians to break the enemy's morale."

Student answer:

"The doctrine of double effect says an action with a good and a bad effect is permissible if the bad effect is foreseen but not intended, whether as an end or as a means. So the switch case is permitted, because the death of the one worker is a foreseen side effect, while pushing the bystander is forbidden because his death is part of the plan for stopping the trolley. The loop case is contested: some would permit it because what is needed is the worker's body rather than his death, while others would call the death a means because the trolley is only stopped by hitting him; I lean toward permitting it. The morphine case is permitted because the doctor only allows death rather than causing it, so long as his aim is pain relief. Lethal injection is forbidden because death is intended as the means to relieve suffering. Tactical bombing is permitted if the civilian deaths are proportionate; terror bombing is forbidden because the deaths are intended to cause fear. A place where defenders disagree is the hysterectomy/craniotomy pair: some allow a hysterectomy on a pregnant woman to remove a cancerous uterus because the fetal death is a side effect, but forbid a craniotomy during obstructed labor because crushing the skull is intended as a means of delivery. I don't see a stable difference between those two, but I know it's controversial."
