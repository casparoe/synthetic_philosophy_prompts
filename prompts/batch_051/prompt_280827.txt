I have been trying to work out what principle, if any, governs my decisions when I am uncertain between moral views rather than merely uncertain about the facts. I have written down my honest verdicts on several cases, and I would like to know whether a single account can capture them, which verdicts it would force me to drop, and what it implies about cases I have not yet decided.

1. When I choose between two charities that fight childhood disease, one supported by strong randomized-trial evidence and the other by promising but contested evidence, I split my donation rather than giving it all to the better-evidenced charity. I am confident this is right: factual uncertainty calls for hedging.

2. When I choose between that childhood-disease charity and a charity that reduces farm animal suffering, and the animal charity is comparably important only if a moral view I give around 30 percent credence is correct, I do not split the donation. I give the whole sum to the childhood-disease charity, and I do not regret it. I realize this looks inconsistent with the first case, since I treat empirical uncertainty differently from moral uncertainty, but both verdicts feel correct.

3. My father is incapacitated and I must approve his care plan. My best-guess view holds that comfort and quality of life are what matter most; a minority view I give 25 percent credence holds that life must be preserved for as long as possible even with substantial suffering. I do not simply follow my best-guess view. I choose a plan that prolongs his life moderately while adding strong palliative care, an option that is ideal under neither view but acceptable under both. This seems right, and it seems different from the charity case, because here following my best-guess view would mean doing something the minority view condemns.

4. I will not eat meat from animals I believe were raised in severe confinement. I hold no corresponding confidence that animals have the same moral status as humans; the animal-inclusive view is the same 30 percent one from the charity case. Yet refusing the meat feels more like case 3 than like case 2: the animal-inclusive view condemns eating the meat, whereas in case 2 it merely said my money could do more good elsewhere. I cannot shake the sense that condemnation and comparative importance are doing different work, but I cannot state exactly how.

5. A charity proposes a project with an extremely small probability, on the order of one in a hundred thousand, of preventing a catastrophe that would affect an astronomical number of future people. The expected-value calculation says this project dwarfs the childhood-disease charity. I give to the childhood-disease charity anyway, and I think I am right to do so. I simply do not trust a calculation whose result is driven by a tiny probability of a payoff I can barely make sense of. This seems to contradict the spirit of the first case, where I let uncertain probabilities guide me, and I want to know whether I can have both verdicts.

6. When the candidate actions all pass the test set by cases 3 and 4, that is, when no moral view I take seriously condemns any of them, I follow the view I find most probable and try to do the most good by its lights, treating my moral uncertainty as settled. That is what I did in case 2, and it still seems right there.

My in-progress principle is roughly this. First, I may not perform an action that any moral view I take seriously, above some threshold of credence, regards as seriously wrong, unless every alternative would be seriously wrong under some such view as well; among the actions that survive, I follow my best-guess view and maximize expected value by its lights. Second, factual uncertainty about which action best serves the surviving views is handled by hedging, exactly as in case 1. Third, I cap the role of tiny-probability, astronomical-payoff calculations, as in case 5, though I worry this cap is just a patch that saves case 5 at the price of unprincipled arbitrariness.

The case I keep going back and forth on is this. A close friend is getting married abroad, and attending involves a long flight. A moral view I give perhaps 15 percent credence says the per-person emissions from such a flight amount to a serious wrong, while my best-guess view says the trip is permissible. It matters greatly to the friendship that I attend, and the harm is diffuse and statistical. Does my in-progress principle require me to attend, forbid me, or ask for some compromise? And is there a precise principle, not a list of patches, that handles all of the cases above? Please state the candidate principles precisely, say exactly which clause each of my verdicts motivates, give their verdicts on the wedding case and on any further cases you think are diagnostic, and be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, they cannot accommodate.
