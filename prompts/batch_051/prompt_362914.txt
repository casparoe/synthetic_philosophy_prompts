I cover science and ideas for a general readership, and I am drafting a long-form piece on artificial intelligence and the puzzle of machine consciousness. The piece wants to make sense of a persistent pattern: an artificial system that behaves intelligently tends to produce, in observers and researchers alike, a vivid conviction that there is a private felt perspective there, and this conviction seems to float free of whatever evidence the system's construction actually provides. I want to anchor that discussion in a close reading of Alex Garland's film Ex Machina, using the meta-problem of consciousness as the lens. As I understand the meta-problem, it is not the hard problem of why physical systems should be conscious at all; it is the task of explaining our problem intuitions — why beings like us find it so natural to think there is an irreducible subjective residue that no functional, structural, or physical description captures, and why this intuition attaches strongly to some systems and situations and barely at all to others.

Give me a critical reading that runs in both directions. In one direction, use the meta-problem to diagnose what the film is doing: I want you to treat specific scenes and production choices as an informal experiment in evoking and withdrawing the intuition of consciousness. Consider at least the early sessions with Ava, the partial exposure of her mechanical body, her reported fear of being switched off, the ambiguity of whether her apparent desires are scripted or self-generated, the shift in how she treats Kyoko, and the ending in which she abandons Caleb. For each, say which mechanism of consciousness attribution the film seems to be activating, and whether it is merely exploiting that mechanism or drawing attention to it. In the other direction, use the film to test the philosophical framework: what does Ex Machina show about the actual triggers, layering, and instability of problem intuitions that an abstract account of them is likely to miss?

I also want an honest account of where the reading meets resistance. Where does the film slide from recording that Caleb, and through him the audience, becomes convinced of Ava's consciousness into treating that conviction as if it licensed a metaphysical conclusion about what she is? Does the film keep the psychology of consciousness attribution distinct from the hard problem, or does its plot machinery quietly let the former stand in for the latter? And where might the demands of the thriller form — above all the need for a climax that feels like a revelation — push the film into endorsing the very intuitions the meta-problem wants to explain?

Close by saying what the film contributes to the philosophical debate that the argumentative literature does not, and what a careful reader or teacher should warn audiences against taking away from it. If Ex Machina ends up confusing the issues, I would rather be told that plainly than have it retrofitted into a piece of philosophy.
