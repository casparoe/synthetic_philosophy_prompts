I'm putting together a ranking exercise for an advanced undergraduate seminar on philosophical approaches to lexical meaning, and I want a worked example to hand out. The organizing question for the week is whether words have meanings at all in a robust sense, or whether "meaning" is better located in utterances, speakers, or particular interpretive episodes. To force the issue, I've isolated four broad pictures of what a word meaning is, none of which I want you to assume is uniquely correct:

(A) Dictionary-inventory view: each phonological form is associated with a list of discrete senses; homonymy and polysemy differ at most in the historical relatedness or overlap of those senses; context mainly selects among rehearsed entries.
(B) Single-schema view: each word has one highly underspecified semantic representation, a kind of semantic potential, and the apparently distinct senses are surface elaborations of that single schema generated in composition.
(C) Eliminativist/occasionalist view: words contribute only constraints or pointers, while actual meanings are ad hoc concepts built in context; the word itself has no stable semantic content worth positing.
(D) Core-plus-pragmatics view: each word carries one thin literal convention, and nearly everything that looks like polysemy is pragmatic enrichment or modulation, not part of the word's semantic contribution.

The exercise asks students to test these views against a battery of data-patterns that any account should handle, and I'd like your scoring of the matchup. The patterns are: (i) ambiguity diagnostics, such as "Anna and Ben both went to the bank," which is smooth if both went to a riverbank but strained if Anna went to a financial institution and Ben to a riverbank; (ii) regular polysemy, such as "chicken" for the animal and the meat, or "newspaper" for the physical object, the institution, and the day's content; (iii) coercion, such as "Sam finished the book," which seems to mean finished reading, writing, proofreading, or editing it, with the verb imposing an activity on the complement; (iv) copredication, such as "The lunch was delicious but took forever," where one use predicates a sensory quality and a duration of what looks like an event; and (v) adjective-noun interaction, such as "fast car," "fast lane," "fast friend," and "fast color," or "good knife," "good watch," "good dog," and "good weather."

Please sort A through D into tiers by how well you think each handles this battery overall, with a sentence or two per view identifying its best feature and its most serious failure. Where the data genuinely don't discriminate between two views, say so rather than inventing a distinction. Since the seminar ultimately cares about philosophical adequacy rather than just predictive convenience, add a brief note on which view does the best job of respecting the difference between what a word contributes and what a speaker merely conveys on an occasion. I'm not looking for citations; I'm looking for a defensible ordering and the reasons for it. Finally, please say how confident you are in the ranking you've given, and single out the one sub-judgment or claim in your answer you are least sure about.
