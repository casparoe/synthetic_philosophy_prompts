I'm in a philosophy of religion course and last week we got stuck, for the whole session, on whether religious people are actually morally better than nonreligious people. One side kept bringing up studies on charitable giving and crime rates, the other side kept saying those numbers don't even touch what "better" means, and then a third person pivoted to the claim that secular societies can't sustain trust because they lack the shared moral vocabulary religion provides. It got heated and we never even agreed on what the target was. So I want to step back and treat the measurement problem as the real assignment: write me the desiderata (requirements list) that an account or measure of "being more moral" would have to satisfy before the sentence "religion makes people better" even has a determinate truth value. What cases must it handle: rule-following vs. genuine concern, in-group vs. out-group behavior, stated values vs. costly action, short-term vs. lifetime behavior, behavior in high-trust vs. low-trust settings? Which distinctions can't be collapsed along the way, like frequency of prosocial acts vs. the quality of the underlying motivation, or individual virtue vs. group-level coordination? What findings or confounds from psychology and sociology does it have to be consistent with (religiosity tracks age, income, community membership, and so on)? And flag which single requirement does the most to exclude the naive measures people actually cite in these debates.

Then, briefly: if a measure met all of these requirements, would the original question survive as one question, or would it break apart into several different claims with probably different answers? I dont want a bibliography or a survey of the empirical literature; I want the criteria and the reasoning behind each, with the ones you think are negotiable marked as such.
