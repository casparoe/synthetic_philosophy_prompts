Suppose the few AI labs that control most large-scale training compute get worried about the competitiveness constraint — the fact that a lab that spends extra on safety gets outcompeted by one that doesn't — and decide to replace it with a managed alignment tax. The rule: any training run above a cutoff (say, 10^25 FLOP) owes a 12 percent surcharge on its compute bill, but the lab can pay it in cash or in kind by doing tasks off an approved safety menu — evals against a fixed battery of misbehavior tests, adversarial training, interpretability passes, red-team time with outside firms. Each lab's in-kind payments get logged in a quarterly 'alignment ledger,' and at the end of the quarter the member labs vote on whether each member's ledger was real work or just box-ticking. A three-quarters majority can declare a member's work hollow, which triples that lab's tax for the next quarter; seperately, a lab can buy down to half its tax by paying into a shared fund that subsidizes safety hires at any member lab.

Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral position: what does it actually incentivize the labs, the safety researchers, and the eval/red-team vendors to do; how do labs game the vote, the approved menu, and the buyout; where does it break down as labs differ in size, secrecy, or in-house capability; and are the failure modes fixable, or does the competitiveness pressure just move somewhere else?
