I'm a retired physical chemist, got into philosophical logic a couple years ago mostly on my own, so I may mangle the vocabulary. I help out with a math circle at the county library: about a dozen high-school kids, mostly 16 or 17, 70 to 75 minutes a session. I want to run a session on a liar-style paradox that doesn't actually use self-reference.

The version I have in mind is a row of sentences S1, S2, S3, ... going off to the right forever, where each Sn says 'every sentence to the right of me is false.' No sentence mentions itself and there's no last sentence, and yet there's supposed to be no consistent way to mark each one true or false. That's the strangness I want the kids to feel. There's also a cousin paradox, Curry's, where a sentence like 'if this sentence is true, then the cafeteria serves pizza on Fridays' lets you prove the pizza thing using just the ordinary logic of conditionals, no fancy negation moves. I'd like the main event to be the first one, with maybe Curry's as a short coda in the debrief if you can make it fit without forcing it.

Could you design a runnable activity for the math circle? A game or staged task with cards, slips of paper, roles, whatever works. I want the kids to bump into the idea before I name it: ideally they discover that assigning true or false becomes a mechanical procedure from the right end, and then realize the procedure only works because there's a right end. I want them to come away seeing that the liar-ish trouble doesn't depend on self-reference, and that the finite version quietly leans on having a last item. No lecture up front; the rules or materials should do the work.

For the format, give me the whole design as a JSON object with these fields, and nothing else:
- materials: a list of concrete physical items to prepare
- setup: a step-by-step arrangement of the room, cards, and kids
- rules: the instructions exactly as I should read them to the kids
- phases: an array of objects, each with time_minutes, what_happens, and what_i_say_or_ask
- predicted_sticking_points: an array of objects, each with moment, what_kids_will_likely_say, and how_i_should_respond
- debrief: an array of objects, each with activity_moment and the philosophical_point it maps to
- what_the_activity_cannot_show: what a finite group can't literally reproduce (the actual infinity, the missing last sentence) and exactly how I should hand that over in the debrief without pretending the game already contained it

Keep the voice usable with teenagers, not a seminar paper.
