I am a professor of philosophy, and I have agreed to give the address at my department's graduation ceremony this year. My own work is on Sartre, and I want the speech to be an exercise in radical freedom and bad faith rather than a talk about them.

Please write the speech, about eight hundred words, under the following constraints. It may not comfort the graduates with appeals to a fixed nature, a true self, innate gifts, destiny, temperament, or the idea that their choices followed inevitably from who they already were; Sartre would treat all of that as bad faith. It also may not overcorrect by treating their circumstances — family expectations, financial constraints, the job market, their records — as if those simply dictated what they may do; facticity constrains but does not determine. The room will be mixed: some faculty and graduate students know Sartre well and will notice every evasion, while most family members are hoping for a warm ceremony and will tune out a technical lecture. The ideas therefore need to be enacted rather than explained. In particular, the speech should find a way to congratulate the graduates without turning them into finished products, since congratulation of that kind is itself bad faith. It should also, at some point, acknowledge the speaker's own temptation to take shelter in the role of the wise professor addressing the young, as if a role could spare him the burden of choosing each word.

Give as the very last line of your response a one-sentence summary of the speech's controlling idea, written in the form \answer{...}.
