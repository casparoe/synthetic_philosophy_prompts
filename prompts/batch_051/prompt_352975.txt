I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing an interactive session for my newsroom. The session is for about 14 reporters and editors who cover science, health, and technology, and it will run for 75 minutes. Its theme is the unilateralist's curse, and I would like you to design the session.

Here is the idea the participants should come to see from the inside rather than be told. In many newsroom-relevant cases, any single member of a team can, on their own judgment, take an action that affects everyone and cannot easily be undone: publishing a sensitive dataset, reporting an unverified allegation, describing a dangerous method from a preprint, or revealing identifying details about a source. If such an action happens whenever at least one person judges it net positive, the group's effective decision rule is to act if its most optimistic member says act. Under noisy private evidence, that rule is far more permissive than the threshold almost any member would endorse in advance, and this remains true even when everyone shares the same values and is reasoning in good faith. The point is that the outcome need not trace back to anyone's recklessness; it can be produced entirely by well-intentioned individuals whose only failure was to act in parallel.

Please design a structured game or role-play that makes participants feel that mechanism, runnable in 75 minutes with ordinary materials and no real money. Use a fictional but realistic publication decision, one unlikely to trigger partisan defensiveness. Each participant should receive private, unreliable evidence about whether releasing the information would be net good or net bad, and should decide simultaneously and secretly whether to release. If anyone releases, the information has been released, and the round is scored against the facilitator's hidden truth. Keep any upfront remarks to logistics only; do not preview the concept.

The payoff structure should be common to the whole group, for example a single public-interest score, because the curse assumes shared values. Do not add scoop bonuses or status payoffs; those introduce a different problem. Include at least three rounds: a baseline round with fully private parallel decisions; a round after a minimal suggestion that participants may coordinate if they wish, with no imposed rule; and a round under an explicit collective rule, such as release only by consensus or supermajority. Vary the hidden truth across rounds. Choose the private-evidence distribution so that a reasonable person who receives a positive signal is only mildly in favor of releasing, and so that, with 14 people, the anyone-says-yes rule produces a clearly visible false-release rate in the baseline round.

The design should include roles, rules, materials, a 75-minute timeline, and a debrief. The debrief should map what happened onto the mechanism above; show how an imposed consensus or supermajority rule changes the group's implicit decision threshold, and whether it overcorrects; and distinguish the curse from groupthink, from discussion-induced shifts toward risk, and from ordinary individual recklessness. The name unilateralist's curse should not appear until the debrief. Please end with an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce—real irreversibility, career stakes, and newsroom incentive conflicts come to mind—and what the facilitator must instead supply in words.

You are an expert in group decision science and science communication. Before settling on a final design, think carefully and brainstorm three different activity skeletons in two or three sentences each, reject two of them with stated reasons, and then develop the winner in full. Finally, include a section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to fix each failure.
