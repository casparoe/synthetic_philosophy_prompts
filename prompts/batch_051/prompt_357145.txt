I keep encountering a particular move in discussions of infinite processes, and I would like to know whether it has a standard name and, more importantly, how its boundaries should be drawn.

The first place I noticed it is the classic Thomson's lamp puzzle. A lamp is to be switched on and off repeatedly at the times 1/2 minute, 3/4 minute, 7/8 minute, and so on. One side says that at the one-minute mark the lamp can be neither on nor off, because every time it is switched on it is later switched off, and every time it is switched off it is later switched on. The other side says the setup does not determine the lamp's state at the one-minute mark, but the lamp may still be either on or off, consistently with everything that was specified. What strikes me is that both sides proceed as though the infinite sequence of switchings must leave behind a definite state at the finish, and the only question is how to deduce that state from the finite stages. Yet the original rule governs only each finite step; it says nothing about a state after all of them. The debaters seem to add a rule of their own for reading off such a state.

I saw the same habit in a second example. Imagine a point that at each stage of an infinite process is moved halfway from its previous position to a fixed target. Since after every finite stage the point is still short of the target, one person concludes that the completed process does not reach the target. Another person points out that the successive positions get arbitrarily close to the target and concludes that the completed process does reach it. Here too, the movement rule tells us only where the point is after each finite stage; it does not specify the result "after all stages." Each side supplies a different extrapolation from the finite stages and treats it as though it were already part of the process.

I am inclined to think this is one identifiable pattern: treating a completed infinite process as if it must have an outcome at the end, and as if that outcome must be fixed either by the last stage, when there is no last stage, or by what the finite stages are tending toward. But I can see that someone might say I am running together several different principles.

My questions: Is there an established name for this move, or for the family of moves that includes it? If so, what is its standard definition, and which of my two examples does it cover exactly? If there is no established name, I would like a proposal for one, together with a definition that separates the pattern from each of the following: legitimate reasoning with mathematical limits, the observation that a description may simply be incomplete, and arguments that supertasks are impossible. I am not satisfied with a bare label; I want an account of what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
