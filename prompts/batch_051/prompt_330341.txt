I am a PhD student in cognitive science, and I work in a lab that studies visual awareness. Our lab's pre-registration template contains the following clause, which was written before I joined and which everyone treats as settled methodology:

'A stimulus S shall be scored as consciously perceived at time t only if (i) S was physically present at t, and (ii) in a subsequent unspeeded forced-choice task the participant reports at least one task-relevant feature of S with above-chance accuracy.'

We are now planning a control condition based on the color-phi phenomenon. A red disc is flashed at position A; after a brief blank interval, a green disc is flashed at position B. Observers routinely report that they saw a single disc move from A to B and change from red to green roughly halfway along the path, which is before the green disc was physically present. The people who drafted the clause were thinking of near-threshold flashes and ordinary masking, where the physical stimulus and the perceived stimulus are close in time; they plainly did not anticipate a case in which the most salient conscious content seems to be supplied by a stimulus that had not yet occurred.

I would like you to work through this case in detail. First, apply the clause to the color-phi report: what do the words say, what would the clause's purpose suggest, what would its authors most plausibly have said if the case had been put to them, and which of these should govern here? Second, explain what the difficulty reveals about the deeper assumption the clause appears to encode — the 'Cartesian theater' assumption that there is a definite moment at which a definite conscious content enters awareness. Third, briefly say whether the clause could be revised to handle the case without abandoning its original function of letting researchers say when a stimulus was consciously seen, or whether that function is part of what needs to be rethought.
