Consider an asymmetry in how the two components of a personal name are treated. In the modern English-speaking world and in much of Europe, a person acquires a surname by inheritance from one parental line, and it is the element the bearer never chose; the given name is selected for them and is the name by which those who know them address them in everyday life. Yet in formal contexts—legal records, indexes, bibliographies, the attribution of written work—the surname is the canonical name. We cite Kant rather than Immanuel, and the standard polite-but-not-yet-intimate address is Ms. Surname, while use of the given name alone is precisely what marks familiarity. The practice thus treats the deliberately attached, individually addressed component as secondary and the inherited, widely shared component as the privileged instrument for fixing reference. That sits badly with the elementary picture of a proper name as a tag for an individual: a surname can be borne by strangers and often functions more like a predicate over a lineage, while a given name looks like a direct attempt to name this particular person. Is this asymmetry justified by anything in the theory of proper names and reference, or is it a convention bred by record-keeping and kinship that we have mistaken for semantics? Give me the strongest justification you can find and stress it against nearby cases—patronymic and matronymic systems, a surname that happens to be unique to one person, someone who has chosen a new surname, and contexts in which the given name alone serves as the public name. Then take a definite position on whether the practice should be kept, reversed, or given up. I want a committed verdict and its defense, not a survey of possible views.
