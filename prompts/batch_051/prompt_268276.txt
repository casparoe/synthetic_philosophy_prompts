A friend of mine is translating a long poem by a contemporary poet whose work is composed in tightly rhymed, metred forms. She has made two complete drafts. The first preserves the rhyme scheme and metre of the original, but at several points it is forced to change images, reorder lines, and replace a central metaphor. The second follows the wording and imagery closely but abandons the metre and rhyme altogether. Her publisher wants to issue the second draft, describing it as the more faithful translation. My friend insists that this judgement is too narrow: she argues that in this poet’s work the experience of constraint is part of the meaning, that the form exerts pressure on the language and partly constitutes what the poem does, and therefore that a free-verse version translates only what the poem says. She has asked me whether she is being principled or merely stubborn. What should I tell her? Do not answer with practical publishing advice; address the underlying philosophical question about what fidelity in translation requires when the source text is itself governed by a demanding form.
