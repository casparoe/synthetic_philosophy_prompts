I have a running dispute about whether joint action contains anything beyond a suitably related set of individual attitudes. My view is deflationary: when two people carry a table, sing a duet, or walk somewhere together, what exists is only (a) each person's intention to do their own contribution, (b) each person's expectation that the other will do theirs, (c) common knowledge of this interdependence, and (d) the ordinary individual beliefs and desires that support those attitudes. I cannot see what a shared intention adds. My colleague says the list leaves out exactly what makes the action joint: an irreducible we-intention, whose content is not decomposable into my part and your part, and that the difference is supposed to show up in how joint agents behave — their commitment when a partner defects, their persistence when the plan is blocked, their entitlement to rebuke a shirker, and their reaction when the outcome is achieved by accident. I want to know whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal. Design the strongest empirical test you can of the claim that joint action involves something over and above the individual attitudes in (a)–(d); specify the manipulation, the predicted outcomes, and which result would favor each side. Then say exactly what a favorable result would and would not settle — in particular, whether any observation could distinguish an irreducible shared state from a structured set of individual states, or whether the dispute collapses into a choice about how to describe a single set of facts. Finally, tell me what evidence or argument would change your mind about your verdict, and where in your reasoning you are least confident.
