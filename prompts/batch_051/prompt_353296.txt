I have been trying to think systematically about the idea of national character, and my reactions are more tangled than I would like. I am suspicious of the notion because attributing a character to a people has so often served to essentialize, rank, or exclude them; yet I also resist the claim that there are no broad and durable behavioral differences among societies, since that seems either empirically false or merely a stipulation about words. To force myself to be precise, I wrote down a series of cases in which someone attributes a trait to a nation, a people, or a region, with my own honest verdict on each.

A guidebook warns tourists that drivers in a particular country tend to treat red lights as advisory and to drive accordingly; I find this entirely unobjectionable, assuming it is accurate. A landlord refuses to show an apartment to an applicant, saying that people from the applicant's region are lazy and unreliable tenants; I regard this as a textbook case of prejudice. After two unpleasant incidents in Paris, a friend announces that the French are rude; this makes me wince, and it feels altogether different from the landlord, yet both are trait attributions resting on thin evidence, and I cannot say what principle separates them. A comparative political scientist reports, with survey evidence and an institutional-historical explanation, that generalized interpersonal trust is substantially higher in the Nordic countries than in parts of Southern Europe; I find this credible and worth knowing. A novelist portrays a character as formed by a certain English emotional reserve and treats this trait as real and deeply ingrained; I accept this as legitimate novelistic portraiture. A politician defends an economic program by proclaiming that our people are industrious by nature; even though the attribution is flattering, I find it chauvinist and faintly dangerous. Finally, a cross-cultural psychologist publishes country-level scores on individualism as against collectivism and on uncertainty avoidance; I think these abstractions are coarse but scientifically useful.

My tentative principle is something like this: generalizing about a group is acceptable when it is statistical and defeasible, and when it characterizes norms or institutions rather than a fixed inner essence. But the principle already strains. The novelist's reserve is neither statistical nor institutional, yet I want to keep it; the guidebook is not statistical either, yet it seems harmless. Conversely, the politician could simply recast his chauvinism in statistical language and my principle would let him off too easily. So I would like your help finding a principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that best captures my verdicts. State each candidate precisely, say which of my seven verdicts it preserves and which it must overturn, and tell me what it predicts about cases I have not listed: an insurer using nationality-based actuarial tables; a satirical comedy trading in exaggerated national stereotypes; a historian who explains a region's economic dynamism partly by the ethical habits its dominant religion encouraged; and an immigration officer who assumes an applicant will be industrious because migration from their country is highly selective. If no single simple principle captures all seven verdicts, please say plainly which verdicts I should give up and which are the commitments worth keeping.
