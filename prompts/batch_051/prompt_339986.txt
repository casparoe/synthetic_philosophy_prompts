Let C be the prospect of receiving £1,000,000 with probability 0.11 and £0 with probability 0.89. Let D be the prospect of receiving £5,000,000 with probability 0.10 and £0 with probability 0.90. Let A* be the certain prospect of £1,000,000, let B* be the prospect of £5,000,000 with probability 10/11 and £0 with probability 1/11, and let Z be the certain prospect of £0. Write X ≻ Y for X is strictly preferred to Y.

The argument:

(1) Independence. For any prospects X, Y, and Z and any probability p strictly between 0 and 1, X ≻ Y if and only if the compound prospect that yields X with probability p and Z with probability 1−p is strictly preferred to the compound prospect that yields Y with probability p and Z with probability 1−p.

(2) Reduction. A compound prospect is ranked exactly as the one-stage prospect that assigns the same total probability to each final outcome.

(3) By reduction, B is preference-equivalent to the compound prospect that yields A with probability 0.89 and B* with probability 0.11; likewise, A is preference-equivalent to the compound prospect that yields A with probability 0.89 and A* with probability 0.11. Since A and B therefore share a common 0.89 branch yielding A, independence requires that A ≻ B if and only if A* ≻ B*.

(4) By reduction, C is preference-equivalent to the compound prospect that yields Z with probability 0.89 and A* with probability 0.11; likewise, D is preference-equivalent to the compound prospect that yields Z with probability 0.89 and B* with probability 0.11. Since C and D therefore share a common 0.89 branch yielding Z, independence requires that C ≻ D if and only if A* ≻ B*.

(5) From (3) and (4), A ≻ B if and only if C ≻ D.

(6) The classic Allais pattern is A ≻ B together with D ≻ C.

(7) Therefore, the classic Allais pattern is inconsistent with the conjunction of independence and reduction: it requires ranking A* against B* one way in the first problem and the opposite way in the second, even though the two problems differ only in their common 0.89 branch.
