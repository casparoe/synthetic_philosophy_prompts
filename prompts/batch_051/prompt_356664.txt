I lead the model-governance group at a consumer lender that underwrites small unsecured loans using a machine-learned default-risk score. Every quarter our model-risk committee meets for one hour to approve, modify, or reject new model versions; the committee includes legal, compliance, and at least one non-technical director, and my group must hand them, in advance, a fairness review they can actually follow without a statistician in the room. The stakes run both ways: sending a model back costs months of work, while approving one shapes the offers made to several hundred thousand applicants a year and sits in a regulated audit file for years. The committee keeps asking for what it treats as the complete fairness package: a score that means the same level of risk for every protected group (calibration), a decision rule whose false approvals and false rejections fall equally on every group (equalized odds), and approval rates that do not diverge too far across groups (demographic parity, or something close). From what I understand of the algorithmic fairness literature, that package cannot in general be had: once a score is calibrated across two groups whose underlying default rates genuinely differ, it cannot also satisfy equalized odds unless the score is essentially a perfect predictor of default, which none of our models are. The conflict among these criteria is partly mathematical, not something better data or more features will dissolve.

I want you to turn this impossibility result — and the broader point that group-fairness criteria can conflict rather than simply form a hierarchy of more or less demanding standards — into a reusable procedure for our quarterly reviews. Give me a sequence of checks to run in a fixed order on every model version, using only quantities we can compute from validation data: base default rates, calibration diagnostics, false approval and false rejection rates by group, and approval rates by group. For each step, say whether it is mechanical or whether it quietly encodes a value judgment that the committee, not the data, must make. The procedure should tell us, for a given model and dataset, which fairness criteria to treat as hard requirements, which to treat as diagnostics to be reviewed and documented, and how to decide what to do when one criterion can be satisfied only by sacrificing another. It must survive translation for a partly non-technical audience and fit inside the hour.

Then apply the procedure to at least two cases, showing exactly where it leads: (a) a model that is well calibrated and performs well on our standard accuracy metrics, but approves applicants from two groups at noticeably different rates because their base default rates truly differ; and (b) a model that is well calibrated and strongly profitable, but produces more false approvals for one group — the case the committee is inclined to wave through on commercial grounds. In each case I want to see what the procedure would require us to do, change, or document, and whether it would block a model we would otherwise ship. Finally, state the limits of the procedure plainly: the cases it cannot settle, the inputs that are irreducibly policy choices, and the points at which the impossibility results fall silent.
