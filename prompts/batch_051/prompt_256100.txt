I'm giving a paper at an ecumenical theology conference defending classical impassibility: God cannot literally suffer, grieve, be disappointed, or be changed by what creatures do. My angle is that this isn't a bullet to bite but the ground of God's reliability — exactly because nothing outside God can force a change in God, God's love can't be derailed. The audience is mostly working pastors and hospital chaplains doing continuing ed, plus a couple of process theologians and one scholar who works on Cyril of Alexandria. My argument, roughly: (1) a God who can be changed by creatures is one more actor inside the causal order, not its source; (2) the Bible's emotional language about God is anthropomorphic but does the work of anchoring trust, not of reporting fluctuations in God; (3) Christ's suffering is real because the human nature suffers even if the divine nature doesn't. Put together my Q&A prep sheet: the objections this crowd will actually raise (not the ones a philosophy-of-religion audience would), ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way a pastor or a process theologian would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply would get me in trouble. And tell me the one question I'm dreading. Commit to the list and the ranking instead of hedging.
