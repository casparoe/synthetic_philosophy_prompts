I am a journalist who covers artificial intelligence and the ideas behind it, and before I moved into reporting I spent several years writing production software. When I read the "low-impact AI" debate, my old engineering vocabulary keeps insisting that the problem is already solved by something I used every day: the boundary between what a function returns and everything else it does. I want you to test that instinct rigorously rather than flatter it — I am deciding whether to lean on this analogy in an article, and an overgenerous translation would be an editorial failure, not a helpful one.

Here is the debate as I understand it. The aim is to build an agent that pursues an assigned task without otherwise rearranging the world, and to do so without a full specification of value, since the whole point of "low impact" is to get safety from minimal assumptions. The difficulty is that "side effect" seems to be definable only relative to a specification of what you wanted to happen. Two workarounds feature most prominently in the discussion. One compares the world the agent produces against a default trajectory in which it effectively did nothing, and penalizes the difference, which makes it a measure of how far the agent moved the world. The other penalizes any action that reduces the agent's ability to achieve a wide range of future goals, which makes it a measure of lost capability rather than of altered state.

Restate this debate in the vocabulary of functional programming and database transactions: function contracts and specifications, return values, side effects, purity, referential transparency, the IO monad as a device for marking impurity, transactions and rollback, write-sets, and invariants. Assume I am fully fluent in that vocabulary; it is my native conceptual language, and you do not need to explain its terms.

Organize your answer into the following sections, in this order:

1. "The translation" (roughly 300 words). Restate the original problem and the two workaround approaches as claims about functions, specifications, and transactions. Map each element to its closest engineering counterpart, and state where the project of avoiding side effects without a full value specification lands once it has been translated.

2. "The residue report" (roughly 350 words). Split into three labeled parts:
   a. "Maps cleanly": which parts of the debate carry over without distortion, and why.
   b. "Maps with distortion": which parts can be forced into the vocabulary only by stretching terms, and what is lost in the stretch.
   c. "No counterpart": which parts of the debate have no real analogue in how programs work. Pay particular attention to three points: that a programmer authors the specification; whether the default-trajectory comparison has a genuine engineering analogue; and whether the lost-capability approach corresponds to any idea a working engineer would recognize.

3. "Verdict" (roughly 150 words). Say whether my instinct — that the low-impact AI problem is just the pure-versus-impure distinction wearing a different costume — is right, mostly right with an important exception, or misleading. Then say whether the residue you have identified is a limitation of my engineering vocabulary or evidence that the original debate is less well-defined than its participants assume.
