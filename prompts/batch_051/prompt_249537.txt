I sit on a university ethics panel that rules on requests for cognitive and mood enhancement procedures, and we're trying to keep our decisions consistent with each other. Our past rulings:

- We approved a trial of a mild, reversible attention-enhancing drug for healthy adults, on condition of informed consent and psychiatric screening.
- We rejected a proposal for germline editing of embryos to raise predicted cognitive ability, mostly because the future person couldn't consent and the change would be permanent.
- We approved, with conditions, an implanted device that keeps a person's mood within a normal range, as long as it can be switched off and doesn't blunt ordinary sadness or fear.
- We rejected a moral-enhancement drug that dampens aggressive impulses by acting directly on the brain, even though it was reversible, because it seemed to bypass the person's own reasoning rather than support it.
- We approved a memory-consolidation aid for older adults, with regular medical review.

The new application is for a voluntary procedure that substantially raises general intelligence in adults, but it reliably makes people much more analytical, less attached to old relationships and hobbies, and shifts their values toward impersonal, efficiency-oriented ones. These effects are well documented and fully disclosed. Participants would be competent adults, there'd be staged re-consent each month, and the procedure is fully reversible if stopped within the first six months, but after about a year it becomes difficult to undo.

What should we do if we're trying to be consistent with our own track record? Tell me which of our earlier rulings this is most like, and if the track record doesn't force a single answer, what rule we'd be committing ourselves to either way. It would also help if you could describe one further case we could rule on that would cleanly seperate the competing rules. Please write the answer in British English.
