I spent most of my working life as a natural scientist, and I have carried one habit into retirement: whenever I meet a theory, I ask what would count against it — what observation, result, or internal difficulty would lead its defenders to revise or abandon it. I have recently been reading the work of David Lewis, and his modal realism strikes me as a particularly hard case for this habit. As I understand the position, possible worlds are not abstract representations, linguistic artefacts, or useful fictions; they are full-blooded concrete universes of exactly the same kind as the one we inhabit. The word actual is an indexical: it merely marks the world in which a speaker is located, just as here marks a place. There is, on this view, a world for every way a world could be, and these worlds are causally and spatiotemporally isolated from one another, so that nothing in one can act on anything in another.

I would like a disciplined assessment of how exposed this position really is. If modal realism were false, what would have to be true instead? Which commitments are load-bearing — concreteness, the plenitude of worlds, the indexical account of actuality, causal and spatiotemporal isolation — and which, if surrendered, would bring down the whole view? I am especially interested in whether anything external could rationally pressure a Lewisian: a result in mathematics, linguistics, or physics, for instance, or a well-described case of modal thought that the view handles awkwardly. And I want you to distinguish considerations that would be decisive from those that would be merely awkward, to say which escape routes are live rather than merely logically possible, and to indicate where defenders of the view would themselves concede vulnerability, not only where critics attack it. You should be willing to conclude that the view is well insulated, and to say plainly whether that insulation is a virtue or a defect.

From my old habits, I suspect I would find it most persuasive if it were shown that the theoretical work modal realism is praised for doing — a uniform semantics for modal language, a reductive account of possibility and necessity, and so on — can be had without the ontological cost of concrete worlds. But I want to know whether that is the right place to look, or whether the view's real exposure lies elsewhere. Please keep the discussion to the arguments themselves rather than a catalogue of literature; I can pursue the texts separately.
