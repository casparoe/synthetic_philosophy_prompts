I don't have any academic background, but I have been reading online arguments about whose values advanced AI systems should serve, and one analogy appears constantly. The claim is that building an aligned AI is like designing a liberal state. A well-run liberal state does not write one religion or one vision of the good life into law as the official one; it stays neutral among the different ways its citizens want to live and gives them fair procedures — courts, elections, rights — through which people with incompatible values can still share a society. The conclusion people draw is that an aligned AI should do the same: it should not hard-code any one group's substantive values, and should instead act as a neutral referee or fair aggregator of everyone's preferences. If we build in specific values, so the argument goes, we are imposing them the way an illiberal state imposes a religion.

I want this analogy audited properly. Please respond as a political philosopher who works on the ethics of AI, and think carefully rather than giving a stock answer. Before you commit to a verdict, brainstorm the features of a liberal state on one side and the features of an AI system that makes decisions for many people on the other, and only then sort them into matches and mismatches.

Then give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the citizens, the constitution, the legislature, the courts, the police, the territory, the state's monopoly on legitimate force, and so on? After that, identify the places where the mapping breaks down. Watch especially for features of a liberal state that the analogy quietly imports into AI: that the state has citizens who can vote or exit, that its neutrality is backed by a monopoly on legitimate coercion, that it governs people who share a territory and a legal system, and similar. For each disanalogy, tell me whether it matters for the intended conclusion — that AI should be a neutral aggregator rather than a carrier of substantive values — or whether it is only incidental.

Finally, say whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Does the analogy actually support the neutral-aggregator view, or was it doing more work than it can carry? If some modified version of the conclusion survives, state what has to change. And if there is a better analogy that keeps what this one gets right while avoiding its failures, offer it and explain why it is better.
