I find the Ramsey-style framework for social discounting in climate economics about as close to a consensus decision procedure as the field has: discount the consumption of someone born later at r = δ + ηg, where δ is the pure rate of time preference and η the elasticity of marginal utility. I can see why a strictly positive δ is attractive: with δ = 0 and an unbounded horizon, welfare sums refuse to converge, cost-benefit analysis comes to be dominated by speculative far-future scenarios, and the present generation appears bound to save at absurd rates. Yet I cannot bring myself to believe that the date on which someone is born is itself a reason for her welfare to count less—not a fraction of a percent per year less, not any amount less—and I count that conviction among the firmest I have. I am regularly told that this intuition is just scope insensitivity, or a failure to appreciate how much richer the future will be, and that I should set δ > 0 as a modelling convention and stop moralizing it. I want to know what it would take for each side to give way: for the temporal-impartiality intuition to be a debunkable artefact rather than a fixed point, for a positive pure rate of time preference to be abandoned rather than reinterpreted into harmlessness, and whether there is a reading on which both survive—say, δ as a parameter about risk or about what we can responsibly optimize, rather than a normative claim that later lives matter less. Give me the strongest case for each of the three outcomes, including the mistaken step each would require.
