I've been going back and forth with a friend about how social media platforms moderate speech, and I keep tripping over what looks like a real inconsistency in the practice itself. The big platforms will remove a post that calls a particular person a slur, suspend an account for repeatedly telling someone to kill themselves, and take down things like doxxing, but they'll leave up — and sometimes even algorithmically promote — a video claiming vaccines are a plot or that an election was stolen, stuff thats flatly false and that plausibly gets real people hurt over time. Their stated rationale is always something in the neighborhood of harm: harassment and targeted cruelty cause real damage, so removing them is compatible with a free-speech principle. But at the same time they treat dangerous falsehoods as protected expression. I can't figure out what account of harm would make that distinction principled. It can't be harm to an individual, because the vaccine lie harms individuals too, just slowly and statistically. It can't be offense, because they insist offense isn't enough. It can't be falsity, because they leave up enormous amounts of false stuff that isn't dangerous. So I want to know what theory of speech, or of harm, the pattern of enforcement actually presupposes.

Reconstruct the implicit theory the platforms are committed to, and be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with — maybe several rival theories would license exactly the same takedowns. Say whether the parts are internally coherent (the rules on slurs versus the rules on misinformation, or an appeals process that claims to care about impact rather than intent), and then tell me what the moderation system would have to look like if you swapped in a strict Millian harm principle instead: only restrict speech that directly harms others, with offense and merely probable or diffuse danger not counting. Would that actually fix the inconsistency, or just relocate it? I'm less interested in a verdict on the platforms than in having the implicit philosophy laid out cleanly, because right now I can't tell whether my friend and I are even disagreeing about the same thing.
