In several distinct corners of the law, courts and legislatures openly treat claims they know to be false as true for legal purposes. A corporation is said to be a person that can own property, suffer injury, form intentions, and be held responsible, even though no one in the courtroom believes there is a flesh-and-blood person answering to that description. An adopted child is treated as if born to the adoptive parents, with the official record of birth itself rewritten accordingly, though no one is confused about the underlying biology. Conduct is measured against the "reasonable person," a standard that no actual human being fully instantiates. Courts sometimes speak as though they are discovering a common-law rule that was always already there rather than making one, and a defendant who has never opened a statute book is nonetheless said to have known the law all along. None of this is fraud, incompetence, or loose talk; these are standard, self-conscious instruments of legal reasoning, often grouped under the heading of legal fictions.

What is puzzling is that the legal system also presents itself as an institution devoted to finding out what actually happened and what the law actually is. A court is not allowed deliberately to make a false factual finding, yet it is allowed deliberately to adopt a fiction. What picture of legal reality does this practice presuppose? In particular, does the smooth functioning of legal fictions reveal that legal entities and legal truth—rights, duties, corporate personality, legal status—are themselves creations of the legal system rather than preexisting facts that the law merely reports? Reconstruct the implicit ontology as precisely as you can, and separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Then say what would have to change in legal practice if the implicit picture were replaced with a straightforwardly factualist one, on which a legal proposition is true only if it corresponds to a mind-independent legal fact.
