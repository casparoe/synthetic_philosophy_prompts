I am taking a course on the ethics of the far future, and I have been defending a position I want to stress-test rather than protect. The position is that longtermism, translated into a guide for political action, does license substantial control over present people: once one grants that actions shaping the long-run trajectory have astronomically high expected value, ordinary constraints such as democratic consent, individual rights, and procedural legitimacy start to look like manageable sources of risk rather than side-constraints, and it becomes at least pro tanto reasonable to override them to prevent existential or trajectory-level losses. I do not want a debate about whether that conclusion is appealing; I want a map of what would have to be true for the position to be false, and what would merely put pressure on it. Which arguments, cases, or commitments internal to longtermism would its defenders themselves concede as genuine defeaters, and which alleged defeaters would they dismiss as resting on a confusion between axiology and a theory of legitimate coercion? Which considerations would be fatal rather than merely awkward, and would the view turn out to be maintainable only by adding qualifications — if so, is that insulation a theoretical defect rather than a strength? I would personally be most moved by a defeater that shows the inference from astronomical expected value to political control depends on a premise longtermists are already committed to rejecting. Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
