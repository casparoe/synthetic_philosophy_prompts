I’ve been trying to sort out, on my own and without any real philosophical background, what makes a “yes” genuinely count as consent rather than just a person going along. The legal question isn’t what I’m after; I want to know which agreements should count morally as permission, such that the person who acts on the “yes” is in the clear. I’m fairly confident about two endpoints. If a mechanic tells me my brake lines are shot, and on that basis I agree to an expensive replacement, then my agreement doesn’t count as valid consent because the lie went exactly to the reason I said yes. And if a neighbor asks plainly whether she may park in my driveway for the afternoon and I say “go ahead,” that is consent, and I can’t later complain that I never really meant it. Everything in between gives me trouble. To focus it, here are the cases I keep returning to: (1) a patient signs a consent form for surgery after the surgeon explains the procedure mostly in jargon the patient doesn’t follow, and the patient agrees out of trust; (2) a manager asks an employee to cover a double shift and adds that “it would be a shame if your name came up when we discuss layoffs next month”; (3) at a garage sale a customer agrees to buy a necklace she assumes is costume jewelry, while the seller, who can see that it is gold, says nothing; (4) an older person with early memory problems, on a clear afternoon, agrees to give a relative power of attorney; (5) someone says “yes” to sex after being asked repeatedly while half-asleep. I don’t have settled views about any of the middle cases.

Please propose a working definition of valid consent that handles these cases, makes clear why the mechanic case fails and the driveway case passes, and that could be applied by someone with no special training. I want the definition to give a determinate answer for each numbered case, but I also want you to tell me where that determinacy is strained. Structure the response as follows. Section 1, “Definition”: give the definition in two or three sentences, without hedging. Section 2, “The cases”: run the definition through each of the five numbered cases in order, one short paragraph each, stating explicitly whether the case counts as valid consent under your definition and why. Section 3, “Pressure points”: in one paragraph, identify which of these verdicts is least secure, and either revise the definition, defend the surprising verdict, or say honestly that your definition gives the wrong answer there. Section 4, “What’s missing”: in two or three sentences, name the most important thing a full account of valid consent would need to handle that your definition leaves aside.
