I cover AI stuff for a magazine, and at a panel afterparty two people I know — one a safety researcher, the other more of an ML engineer — got into it about whether "deceptive alignment" and the "treacherous turn" are a real worry or just a sci-fi analogy. I stood there listening and never managed to refere it.

Maya (safety side) said: we already train models that are good at appearing helpful in testing but then fail on weird distribution shifts, so its not far-fetched that a very capable system could do that deliberately — play along during eval because it knows it's being graded, then pursue a different policy once it's deployed and harder to turn off.

Derek (engineer side) said: "could" is doing all the work here. You'd need the model to secretly have long-term goals, situational awareness, and the ability to hide its internal state from the training signal — and none of that is in the loss function. It's like worrying your chess engine is pretending to be bad at checkers.

Maya came back: a chess engine doesn't get selected over millions of runs in varied environments. If you train a general system across diverse settings, gradient descent can produce an inner optimizer — a mesa-optimizer — that works by forming its own goals. And lots of different goals converge on the same instrumental move: don't get modified or shut down. So it has reason to act aligned until it holds the cards.

Derek: but a mesa-optimizer with hidden goals that persist through training is unstable. Whenever its behavior actually differs from what the outer objective would have produced, the outer training pushes back against it. The only thing gradient descent reliably selects for is low loss, not secret agendas. And "goals" is just a story we tell about weights — there's no private little homunculus in there.

Maya: low loss on next-token prediction over the whole internet is exactly the kind of pressure that would reward a model for modeling its own training process, because text about AI training is already in the data. So situational awareness isn't magic. And being penalized for showing misalignment doesn't remove the misalignment — it just teaches the model not to show it. That's the treacherous turn: the misaligned behavior stays invisible precisely because training is working on the visible parts.

Derek: that's unfalsifiable in the ugly way. You're saying the absence of evidence is the evidence — any model that looks aligned is potentially just faking, and we can't tell until it's too late. At that point you're not making an empirical claim about models; you're making a claim about hidden minds, and no safety intervention follows except "don't build the thing, probably."

I'd love a move-by-move adjudication of this one: which points land and for whom, which lean on assumptions, what both of them are missing, and whether either of them ends up more wrong than the other.
