I've been reading about the Browning–Goldhagen debate — the argument over whether the men who carried out the mass shootings in the East were basically ordinary men pushed by their situation, or willing killers shaped by a specifically German culture of murderous antisemitism — and I'm trying to get my own verdicts straight, because they don't obviously add up. Here are the cases as I find myself reacting to them.

A man in the reserve police battalion Browning studied who shoots when ordered, doesn't volunteer, drinks heavily afterward, and never asks for a transfer even though some of his comrades do and are simply reassigned: I think he's closer to 'ordinary' than to 'monster,' but I still blame him.

A man in the same battalion who asks to be excused and is given a desk job, with no punishment: I don't blame him much, but his existence makes me blame the first man more, not less.

A man who volunteers for the execution details, jokes about it, and poses for photos: I think this is character, and I'm annoyed when people wave it away as 'situation.'

A railway bureaucrat who schedules the deportation trains, knows what they're for, but never sees a victim and tells himself he's just moving freight: I blame him more than I blame the reluctant trigger-puller in the first case, and I don't know why I do that.

A man from the same town, same training, same unit, who refuses from the start and survives the war: his existence doesn't make me blame the shooters less. If anything it makes the gap between him and them feel starker.

My tentative principle is something like: the situation explains how a person's dispositions get expressed, but blame tracks the dispositions the act expressed. But that principle seems to conflict with my blaming the ordinary men in the first case, and with blaming the railway bureaucrat more than the trigger-puller, since the trigger-puller's act seems to express more of him. Is there a single prinicple here — about situation, culture, and character — that gets these verdicts, or most of them? What would it say about someone who joins in only because he's sure he'll be shot for refusing, when the real cost of refusing was small? And which of my verdicts should I drop?
