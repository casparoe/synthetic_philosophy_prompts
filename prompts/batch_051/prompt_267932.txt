Lately I have been relying on a distinction I invented while thinking about typefaces, and I would like it examined rather than simply accepted. I call it the skeleton/style split. My idea is that every letterform is really two separable things at once. The skeleton is a set of relations among strokes: an 'a' has, roughly, an enclosed or nearly enclosed counter, a stem rising on the right, and an arch closing the top; an 'e' is a closed loop crossed by a horizontal bar; an 'n' is two vertical stems joined by an arch. The style field is a small bundle of global parameters—weight, stroke contrast, slant, aperture, serifs, x-height—and it acts on every skeleton uniformly, the way a single transformation acts on every vector it is applied to. My claim is that letter identity lives entirely in the skeleton, that style lives entirely in the field, and that recognizing a letter is just matching skeleton to skeleton while discounting the style field. I once assumed letter categories were clean, but typefaces convinced me otherwise, and this split felt like the way to make the mess tractable.

It has not survived contact with actual alphabets without strain, which is exactly what I want assessed. A single-storey 'a' (the rounded bowl with a tail, common in geometric sans-serifs and handwriting) and a double-storey 'a' (the two-level form of most book text) do not seem to share a useful skeleton, yet I unhesitatingly call both 'a'. A blackletter 'a' and a hurried handwritten scrawl push the problem further, and an ultra-bold display face can close the aperture until an 'a' starts to look like something else. I also suspect the style field is doing more structural work than I admit: some styles seem to construct the letter rather than merely decorate a pre-existing skeleton. So I want an honest assessment. Is the skeleton/style split coherent, or is it two metaphors stacked on top of each other? What is the strongest version of the claim, where exactly does it break, and has anyone already worked this territory out under another name?
