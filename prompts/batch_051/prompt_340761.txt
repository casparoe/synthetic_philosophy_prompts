I am in my final year of high school, and for the past several months I have been in a class at my church on the theology of worship. The more I understand the regulative principle, the more I find it has the kind of austere, systematic structure I usually find compelling: as our congregation understands it, the public worship of God is to contain only what Scripture expressly commands or necessarily implies, and whatever is introduced beyond that is not ornamental but forbidden. The principle offers me a single decision procedure in a domain where I would otherwise have to make endless judgments of taste; it insists that the service is instituted by God rather than designed by the congregation; and it protects worship from slowly turning into a concert or a motivational talk. I find all of that persuasive, not just as a matter of loyalty but as an argument.

And yet I have reactions that seem to collide with it. Our congregation begins the service with a short instrumental prelude meant to quiet the room and gather attention, and I cannot convince myself that this is a violation. I do not simply enjoy it; I think its being valuable for the congregation is a real reason to include it. The same thing happens when, a few times a year, someone stands during the service to give a brief account of a prayer answered in their life. Those two minutes feel like worship to me in a way that matters more than the principle's tidiness, and if the principle forbids them, I feel that something in the principle, or in how I am applying it, has gone wrong. I do not want to be told which side ought to win. I want to know what it would cost to give each side up. What would I have to believe about these two reactions in order to debunk them as comfort, habit, or category confusion rather than evidence? What exactly would I have to revise in the regulative principle to accommodate them without allowing anything anyone finds edifying, and would that revision still count as the same principle? And is there a reading on which my intuitions and the principle are not really in conflict, for instance because they concern different aspects of the service? Please answer as a philosopher of religion who is willing to press the regulative principle to its strictest form while also taking my intuitions at their strongest; before committing to a verdict, work through the strongest case for each possible resolution, and then tell me which move you judge the evidence most supports and why the alternatives fail.
