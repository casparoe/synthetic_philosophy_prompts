I am a novelist, and in the book I am currently writing there are two characters. One has just finished a long manuscript and believes each sentence in it, while also believing, without embarrassment, that at least one sentence in it is false. The other stands at a fair where the draw is known to be fair, one ticket in a million will win, and he says of each ticket in turn that it will lose while also saying that one of them will win. On the page these have seemed like different people to me: the first is an author confessing his fallibility, the second is a gambler caught in a transparent confusion. I have stopped trusting that difference.

I would like your actual credence in the following claim: the preface paradox and the lottery paradox are a single problem, in the sense that whoever has found the correct solution to one already has the correct solution to the other, needing only to substitute the different subject matter. Please decompose the number rather than merely stating it. Give a rough probability for each of the following, with one sentence of justification for each: first, that both paradoxes are driven by the same rule linking sufficiently high probability to full belief; second, that the preface case is genuinely different because it involves a first-person judgment about one's own total output rather than a statistical judgment about a known setup; third, that a contextualist or stakes-based treatment can be made to cover both symmetrically; fourth, that the apparent difference is presentational and narrative rather than structural. Add any further sub-question whose answer would materially change your result. Then show how the pieces combine into your overall credence, and identify which sub-question is doing the most work, which one you are least confident in, and what evidence would move it most.

If any part of this request is misconceived — if a single credence here distorts the debate, or if the claim as I have stated it cannot bear the weight I am putting on it — I would rather you say so plainly than produce a number simply because one was asked for.
