Two research groups who study failures of collective rationality — the phenomena sometimes summarised by the image of Moloch, and elsewhere described as multipolar traps or races to the bottom — have spent a year arguing past each other and have now agreed to produce a joint document instead of another round of rebuttals. The first group holds a structural view: in a multipolar trap, each agent is responding sensibly to an incentive landscape, and the collectively harmful outcome is an equilibrium from which no unilateral departure is effective or prudent; the defect lies in the game rather than in the players, and the remedy is to change the game through coordination devices, regulation, or binding commitment. The second group holds an agential view: the trap is sustained by the players' own preferences, beliefs, and norms, and what presents itself as a hard incentive is often a revisable interpretation of the situation — the conviction that one must not fall behind a rival, or that defecting first is mandatory because others would otherwise defect. On this view, changing values and expectations can dissolve a trap even before any formal rule changes, and formal rules themselves stick only when enough players have already internalised new norms.

Write the document that such a collaboration should have produced. It should open with a numbered list of claims both camps can sign, phrased so that neither side is merely tolerating a slanted formulation; the agreed section should include a working definition of a multipolar trap, an account of how individually rational choices aggregate into an outcome nobody prefers, and agreement on the diagnosis of at least two concrete cases — an arms race between states, an academic metrics treadmill, and a beauty contest are available as anchoring examples, though the document may choose others. It should then set out the precise residual disagreements in a table, with each side's position stated in wording that side itself would accept, so that neither position can be mistaken for a caricature. The next section should identify the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — and, for each crux, describe a test, natural experiment, or historical case that both camps accept bears on it, recording each side's prediction in advance.

Finally, the document should say what each side would need to observe in order to abandon its own view, and should state explicitly any portion of the dispute that turns out to be verbal rather than substantive.
