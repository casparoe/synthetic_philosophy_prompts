I'm a retired botanist, though lately I've been reading philosophy for fun, and I keep turning over a disagreement at home that I'd like your help with. An old colleague of mine, Dan, has spent the last four years assembling an exhaustive flora of our county — every vascular plant species, when and where it blooms, pollinator visits, the whole thing. It's not filling any scientific gap; it's mostly a careful synthesis of records already scattered through the literature. The county natural history society folded years back, nobody will publish it, and Dan says flat out that he dosn't care whether anyone ever reads it. By his own account he doesn't particularly enjoy the work; a lot of it is tedious cross-checking against old herbarium sheets. He just says it matters to get it right. My wife thinks Dan is wasting his time. Her view is that doing something excellently is only as valuable as what it does for somebody: knowledge that sits in a drawer helps no one, and if Dan isn't even enjoying himself, then the whole project is pointless. She compares it to a beautifully made tool that no one will ever pick up. I want to resist that conclusion, but I can't quite say what's wrong with it. She's not criticizing the quality of Dan's botany; she's saying the quality has no value unless it lands in a life somewhere. Is she right? If not, what's the best case for thinking Dan's getting the flora right is good even if it never benefits anyone, including Dan? I'd like the strongest version of each side spelled out, because I keep going in circles.
