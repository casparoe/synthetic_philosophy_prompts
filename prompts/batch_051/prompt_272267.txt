I'm helping to run an upper-level seminar on cognitive science, and the students keep going back and forth over one fault line: whether clever behavior requires the brain to construct and manipulate internal representations of the world, or whether a great deal of what looks like inner computation is better attributed to the coupled system of brain, body, and environment. I'd like you to write a dialogue between two characters, Miriam, a representationalist, and Alex, an enactivist, who are watching a slow-motion clip of a baseball outfielder making a difficult catch. The outfielder runs a curved path, speeds up and slows down, and catches the ball over his shoulder without ever seeming to identify the landing point in advance.

Miriam should argue that this performance is best explained by an internal model of the ball's trajectory, which the visual system builds, continuously updates, and uses to predict the ball's future location and to generate motor commands. Alex should argue that the same performance can be explained without any rich inner model: the outfielder could be maintaining a stable visual relationship with the ball—say, moving so that its image moves along a straight line at a constant rate—so that the catch is a skill of the organism-environment loop rather than the output of a represented trajectory. Make both positions sophisticated. Miriam should grant that the representational model need not be explicit, propositional, or available to consciousness; Alex should grant that the outfielder is not simply reacting to a stimulus and that enactivism must still explain how the loop is organized. They should respond directly to one another's strongest points instead of trading slogans.

After the dialogue, add a short concluding paragraph in which each speaker states what specifically would change their mind—a concrete experiment, a neural finding, or a conceptual argument—and where each takes their own position to be least secure.
