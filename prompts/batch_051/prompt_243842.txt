You're a philosopher who specializes in decision theory and ethics, especially the question of what someone should do when they're genuinely unsure which moral theory is right. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming maybe twelve or fifteen candidate questions before you commit to a final set.

I've been trying to work out on my own what a person should do when they're torn between moral views — say, a roughly utilitarian way of thinking and a rights-based way of thinking, where they give different answers to the same concrete choice. I don't have the official vocabulary for this stuff, and a lot of what I see online just talks past the thing I'm actually confused about. For my reading group I want to make a "which view of acting under moral uncertainty do you actually hold?" quiz: eight to ten questions, each built around a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions. The point is to sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label sounds good to them, so no question should just ask something like "do you think you should maximize expected choiceworthiness?"

The positions I want it to sort between, in my own words:

- Follow-the-most-likely-theory: when you're torn, just do what the theory you're most confident in says to do, and don't let the others dilute it.
- Weighted-comparison view: every theory you give credence to gets a say in proportion to how likely you think it is, and you add up how strongly each theory recommends each option, which requires some way of comparing strength across theories.
- Weighted-rankings view: every theory gets a say, but you don't think strength across theories can really be compared, so you can only use each theory's ranking of the options.
- Worst-case-stakes view: the main thing when you're unsure is to avoid doing something that would be very wrong by some theory you take seriously, so severe downside under any live theory counts extra.
- Deflationary view: the whole question is misframed, because moral theories aren't the kind of thing you can put probabilities on, or because being unsure about morality just means you haven't finished thinking — and the quiz should be able to sort that out too, not just pretend it doesn't exist.

Now the hard part. Build from the logical space first, not from my list of names: work out which combinations of answers your questions allow, place the actual positions in that space, and flag any unoccupied combinations, with a note on whether each is empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. If two of my positions can't be seperated by any concrete case, tell me that plainly instead of pretending the quiz can sort them. Cases should force the positions apart: for instance, a case where the most-likely theory is fairly mild but a low-probability theory says the same act would be catastrophic; a case where two theories rank the options the same but care with very different intensity; a case where the only thing distinguishing two options is how a theory you're only 10% confident in would score them. Don't just reuse those, and make sure every question earns its place.

I'd rather you tell me plainly if this is set up in a confused way or can't be done well than have you politely hand me something broken. And the scoring key should be honest: leave room for an answer pattern that means "your view is a mess, none of these."
