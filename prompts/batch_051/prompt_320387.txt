Adam Smith, *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations*, Book IV, Chapter VII, "Of Colonies":

"The exclusive trade of the mother countries tends to diminish, or, at least, to keep down below what they would otherwise rise to, both the enjoyments and industry of all those nations in general, and of the American colonies in particular. It is a dead weight upon the action of one of the great springs which puts into motion a great part of the business of mankind."

I have been trying to settle what I actually think about colonial institutions by testing my verdicts against Smith, and my reactions do not obviously cohere. Here are the cases.

1. An ancient Greek city, overcrowded at home, sends out settlers to found Syracuse. The mother city regards the new city as an emancipated child: no direct jurisdiction, the colony enacts its own laws, elects its own magistrates, and makes its own wars. I think this is the most defensible of the cases: no ongoing subordination is created, and both sides can flourish.

2. Rome plants a colony of veterans in a newly conquered Italian province. Land is taken from the conquered inhabitants and assigned to the settlers; the colony is not independent but remains under the jurisdiction and legislative authority of Rome, and serves partly as a garrison. I have a divided reaction: I do not think it is wrong in principle for a state to secure a conquest, yet I also think the arrangement is worse for the settlers' development and does a clear injury to the people whose land was taken.

3. Britain permits and encourages settlers to carry English law, agriculture, and administration into a sparsely inhabited part of North America. But Britain then confines them by the monopoly of the colony trade to a single market: certain colonial goods may be exported only to Britain, and European goods may reach them only through Britain. I think the transplantation of settled law and useful arts was a real benefit, but the monopoly is an unjust clog and a dead weight.

4. A European trading company seizes a tropical island, clears it, imports enslaved laborers, and forces the economy entirely into producing sugar for the metropolitan market under an exclusive trade. I judge this indefensible on every ground that matters.

5. A former British colony has since acquired full internal self-government and open trade with all nations while retaining British legal and political forms. I regard this as, in retrospect, the legitimate terminus: the wrongs were in the intermediate arrangements, not in the original transplantation of institutions.

Is there a single Smithian principle—brought out, for example, by the passage above—that captures all five of my verdicts? Or does the passage force me to revise some of them? If a simple principle cannot fit all five, say which verdicts it cannot accommodate and what the strongest two candidate principles are. Also apply your preferred principle to two further cases Smith would have recognized: (a) a mother country that enforces a mild version of the monopoly while genuinely defending the colony from foreign attack; (b) a colony that declares independence principally to escape the monopoly rather than from political oppression. Present your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Each item should do one job: state the principle or candidates; identify which cases motivate each clause; test against (a) and (b); and state which of my verdicts must go and why.
