I'm in a philosophy of fiction course and we're doing imaginative resistence at the moment. In seminar yesterday the tutor read us a bit from David Hume's "Of the Standard of Taste," and I've been turning it over. This is the passage:

"The great variety of Tastes, as well as of opinions, which prevail in the world, is too obvious not to have fallen under every one's observation. Men of the most confined knowledge are able to remark a difference in the narrow circle of their acquaintance, even where the persons have been educated under the same government, and have early imbibed the same prejudices. But those who can enlarge their view to contemplate distant nations and remote ages, are still more surprised at the great inconsistence and contradiction."

Her point was that this complicates the standard line that imaginative resistance is specific to morality. If an educated person's sense of what's beautiful or fitting gets more unstable as they survey other times and places, why should we think moral resistance in fiction is a different, special phenomenon rather than just one more case of taste getting unsettled? I have an essay due on whether imaginative resistance is best explained as a moral phenomenon or as something broader about taste and evaluation. Please weigh the main positions, actually reach a conclusion, and defend it rather than ending on a both-sides note. Also, keep separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this—don't present a contested view as the consensus.
