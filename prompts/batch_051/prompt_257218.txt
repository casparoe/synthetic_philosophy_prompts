Full disclosure, this stopped being academic for me this year. I live in a country thats right now trying to decide whether to join the push for an international moratorium on outdoor solar geoengineering, and I got asked to help draft a comment. I've gone deep enough to know the standard two worries — moral hazard and termination shock — but honestly every public discussion of them is so slogan-level that I cant actually use it. So could you work through a concrete counterfactual with me?

Suppose it's 2031. After three straight summers of record heat and a stalled UN process, a coalition of twelve mostly rich, mid-latitude countries plus two large crop-battered emitters decides to stop waiting and starts injecting sulfur dioxide into the lower stratosphere without a UN mandate, using a small fleet of high-altitude aircraft. Their stated goal is to hold warming near 1.6 degrees above preindustrial through 2045, which their models say means offsetting roughly 0.4 degrees of warming for at least twenty years. There's no attribution mechanism, no compensation fund, and no agreed exit plan. In 2034 the Sahel has a severe drought and parts of South Asia see an unusually late, weak monsoon; a group of affected countries blames the injections and demands that the coalition do something. In 2036 one of the coalition's biggest members elects a government that campaigned on ending the program, and it says it will stop paying and stop hosting flights within six months.

Give me your best reasoned prediction of how the next fifteen years play out — not a story, the mechanism-level reasoning. Specifically: (1) Does the moral-hazard worry actually show up here, and through which channels? I mean whose emissions behavior changes, how much, and why it might differ between coalition members, non-members, private investors, and negotiators in the next round of climate talks. (2) If the program does stop abruptly in 2036, what does termination shock actually look like? I keep reading people talk about it as if it's one single physical event; I want you to pull apart the physical part (how fast the cooling disappears and what that does to the climate) from the political-economic part (who knew a stop was possible, what hedging would have looked like, and whether it was rational not to hedge). (3) On the who-decides problem: how do the non-consenting countries that think they've been hurt plausibly respond, what levers do they have, and what determines whether the coalition holds or falls apart? For each of these, tell me explicitly which links in your reasoning you'd bet on and which are guesses, and name at least one thing that would flip your answer if it turned out differently.

Oh, and write this in American English please.
