I'm in a historiography seminar and I've built myself an argument that seems to prove way too much, and now I think I'm missing something but I can't locate where the thing snaps. It runs like this:

(1) Any narrative history has to select a tiny bit out of everyhting that happened; a history that includes everything is not a history, it's an archive.
(2) Selecting requires criteria of importance.
(3) The criteria have to come from the present: either they're the historian's own values, or they're values from an earlier tradition that the historian chose, from the present, to keep using.
(4) So every historical narrative is organized around present concerns.
(5) And that, as near as I can tell, is just what "Whig history" means: you pick out and arrange the past by how it matters from where we stand now.
(6) So all narrative history is Whig history.
(7) If all narrative history is Whig, then "Whig" isn't a criticism anymore; it marks no contrast.
(8) But it does mark a contrast. The textbook that treats medieval juries as a clumsy rough draft of modern human rights law is doing something different, and worse, than a study of the same juries that is every bit as selective and every bit as chosen from present interests but doesn't turn them into early drafts of us.

So something has to go. I've tried to get out of it by finding a non-Whiggish history, and I can't; a study of, like, ballast rules in early modern shipping still selects, still uses present criteria, so the argument just eats it too. I would much rather keep (8) than (5), but I can't state cleanly what the bad kind of Whiggishness adds on top of the unavoidable present-shapedness of every history. Where does this argument go wrong, and what does it cost to reject whichever step you'd reject? If it doesn't go wrong, what am I committed to by biting (7)? I want the diagnosis and the prices, not a bibliography.
