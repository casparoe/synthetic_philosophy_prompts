I want an analysis of a real episode in the history of particle physics through the lens of a philosophical theory of scientific explanation, running in both directions: the theory should illuminate the episode, and the episode should test the theory.

The theory is explanation by symmetry: the view that symmetry principles do explanatory work in physics rather than merely summarising regularities. Its strongest exhibit is Noether's first theorem, which derives a conservation law from each continuous symmetry of a system's action; its central claim is that when a theory is form-invariant under a transformation, the invariance can explain why some quantity is conserved or why some object must exist. The theory comes in at least two readings. On the ontic reading, the symmetries are structural features of the world, and the explanation runs through that fact. On the epistemic or deflationary reading, the symmetry argument explains only in the sense that it organises and unifies our descriptions, while the underlying laws do the real explanatory work.

The episode is the prediction and discovery of the omega-minus baryon in the early 1960s. Here are the salient facts, supplied so that no outside research is needed. Gell-Mann and, independently, Ne'eman had proposed the Eightfold Way, a classification of strongly interacting particles using the group SU(3). The spin-3/2 positive-parity baryons were arranged in a decuplet, a ten-member multiplet; by 1962 only nine of its members had been observed. The pattern of the decuplet implied a tenth member: a negatively charged baryon with strangeness minus three and a mass roughly 150 MeV above the next-lighter member of the decuplet, in line with the spacing of the others. The particle was found in 1964 in a Brookhaven bubble chamber photograph, with mass and decay properties matching the prediction. Two features of the case matter for the analysis: the SU(3) symmetry involved is approximate, not exact, because the members of the multiplet have different masses; and the argument was, in effect, the completion of a pattern rather than a derivation from dynamics.

Please analyse this episode through the lens of explanation by symmetry, doing two things at once. First, say what the theory says the physicists faced and what their successful prediction established: did the decuplet structure explain the existence and properties of the omega-minus, on either the ontic or the epistemic reading, or did it merely describe a regularity that something else explains? Second, say what the episode shows about the theory: is a successful prediction from an approximate symmetry a confirmation, a qualification, or an anomaly for explanation by symmetry, and does the distinction between the ontic and epistemic readings do useful work here? Be disciplined about the mapping: say clearly what counts as the symmetry, what counts as the explained item, where the explanation is meant to flow, and where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
