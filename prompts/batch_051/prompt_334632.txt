An association of residents in my district has a written constitution. Article 14 reads: "Every elected delegate shall vote on each resolution according to their own judgment and conscience. No delegate may be bound by any instruction, prescription, pledge, or agreement as to how that delegate shall vote." A recently elected delegate has announced that, for each resolution, she will convene a deliberative poll of resident members chosen by lot, give them briefing materials from both sides, and have them discuss until their positions settle. She will then vote for the option preferred by the polled majority, unless she judges that option gravely wrong, in which case she will vote for the least bad of the remaining options. She argues that she is not bound by anyone: she freely adopted this procedure, can abandon it at any time, and her conscience expresses itself in her choice to defer to a well-run poll and in the grave-wrong threshold. The clause was drafted decades ago to stop political whips, threats, and outright vote-buying; no one then imagined a delegate voluntarily outsourcing her decision to a sampled deliberation of the membership. Is she acting in accordance with Article 14? Show what a textual reading, a purposive reading, and an inquiry into what the drafters would have said each yield for this case, and argue for which approach should decide the matter. Do not assume that the threshold exception automatically saves her: assess whether a vote that simply selects the least bad option is still a vote cast according to her own judgment and conscience, and what this reveals about the difference between being instructed and being influenced.
