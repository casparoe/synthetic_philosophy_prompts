I teach an upper-level undergraduate philosophy course on well-being and the good life, and I'm putting together the final essay prompt. The unit we just finished covered the main theories — hedonism, desire-satisfaction, objective list theories — plus the experience machine thought experiment and the idea that some pleasures are of a higher quality than others. I want one essay question that goes somewhere the intro textbooks don't: on whether novelty, variety, or just plain interestingness is a component of well-being in its own right, or whether it only matters instrumentally because of how habituation and the hedonic treadmill work. I keep going back and forth on this in my own notes, because it looks like two questions get run together — (a) does the felt quality of novelty have positive value for the person experiencing it, and (b) does objective variety in a life (changing projects, relationships, places, routines) make a life go better independently of how the person feels about it. So I want a question that forces students to keep those apart, and that also makes them hook the issue back to at least one of the standard theories we covered — for instance, whether a desire-satisfaction theorist has an obvious answer here, or whether a hedonist can absorb the boring-but-happy life without quietly giving up on hedonism.

Could you write: (1) the essay question itself, pitched at something a student could answer in about 90 minutes; (2) a model answer of roughly 700 words; and (3) a marking scheme out of 25 that seperates a first-class answer from a middling one — concretely naming the moves each has to make, and the two or three specific mistakes students tend to make (like treating a life with less felt novelty as automatically worse by definition, or smuggling in objective-list assumptions while officially arguing for hedonism). The question should be answerable from the course material alone, but hard enough that just reciting the three theories earns basically no marks.
