After thirty years in bench science I have a bad habit of asking what an experiment would look like. So here's one I keep coming back to. I've been reading about AI safety via debate: two models argue opposite sides in front of a human judge, and the hope is that the truth comes out because a false claim can be attacked. The hard case, as I understand it, is what people call obfuscated arguments — an argument where every individual step seems fine but the overall case is wrong, whether because the error lies in how the steps combine, in an unstated assumption, or in a chain of reasoning too long for a judge to hold in mind at once.

I think the idea is to rosey about debate. The conceptual claim I want to stress-test is: debate is specifically robust to obfuscated arguments, not just generally helpful; it makes a human judge less likely to be fooled by a wrong but locally-plausible case than a single model's monologue would. Can that claim be tested at all, or is "obfuscated" just a name for whatever happens to exceed a particular judge's limits? Design the strongest test you can: what tasks with known ground truth you would use, what the two sides would be, how you would tell whether the judge was actually tracking the truth rather than just sounding confident, and what pattern of results would convince you the claim is true or false. Then be honest about what a positive result would not settle — for example, the real disagreements where there is no independent ground truth for the experimenter to score the judge against.
