For the past several months I have been using a language model as a working partner in a field I am still learning, and I have gradually adopted the following rule: I no longer read the model's explanations or intermediate reasoning. I read only its final answer, and I vet that answer with checks I can perform without its help — testing it against facts I already know, applying my own edge cases, and, for important results, obtaining a second independent answer to see whether the two converge. I adopted the rule for three reasons. First, reading the reasoning did not improve my error detection; it seems to have worsened it, because a mistake that arrived wrapped in fluent intermediate steps was more likely to pass my scrutiny. Second, the understanding I felt while reading the reasoning was borrowed rather than durable: I could reproduce the explanation immediately but rarely a week later, so my in-the-moment confidence systematically outran what I retained. Third, I only ever act on output-level checks anyway, and those can be done while completely ignoring what the model says about how it reached the answer. My worry is that I may have built a rule that protects me from being persuaded by superficially legible reasoning while quietly foreclosing the route by which I might have acquired the deeper understanding this field requires. Is this policy defensible, or have I found a way to make epistemic dependence feel like epistemic caution? Under what conditions would deliberately ignoring the stated reasons of a reliable informant be an epistemic improvement rather than a source of comfortable ignorance? If the rule as stated is not defensible, state the soundest restatement of it.
