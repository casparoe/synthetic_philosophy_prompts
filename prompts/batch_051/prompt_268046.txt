Traffic-safety and public-health types often compare risks using micromorts: one micromort is a one-in-a-million chance of dying. The standard table they all crib from says, roughly, that 230 miles of ordinary car travel and 6 miles of motorcycling each expose you to about one micromort. The whole sales pitch of the unit is that a micromort is a micromort is a micromort — a common currency for lethal risk, so you can compare a commute, a medical procedure, a day at work, and a cigarette on the same scale.

But nobody actually behaves as if that were the whole story. The same institutions that publish the table will spend enormous sums to drive some industrial or environmental exposure down from, say, two micromorts a year to one, while treating a one-micromort car trip as just the price of getting to work. A hospital will quote a comparably tiny risk of dying during surgery and face lawsuits and years of patient anxiety over it; a highway agency quotes the same sort of figure for 230 miles of driving and expects you to shrug. People are required to wear seatbelts to avoid a risk of exactly the kind the micromort table says is tiny, while being allowed to spend the whole weekend motorcycling, wich carries that same risk in six miles.

I want you to treat this not as a policy question but as a philosophy question. What theory of risk, value, or rational choice does the micromort practice presuppose? Try to seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with: does using the unit commit anyone to the claims that (a) a death is a death wherever it lands, (b) voluntariness and dread are irrelevant to how bad a risk is, (c) small probabilities of death are additive and commensurable across persons, and (d) there is a single correct denominator like per mile rather than per hour or per trip? Which of those does the practice actually need, and which are optional add-ons? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined? And if we swapped the presupposed view for one where who bears the risk and whether it is taken voluntarily matter, what would actually have to change in how we report and regulate driving versus industrial and medical risk — and would driving come out differently in the 'is it worth it' calculation?
