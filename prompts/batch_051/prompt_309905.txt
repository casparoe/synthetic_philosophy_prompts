I run a small rationalist-adjacent blog and would like to publish a "Which account of wireheading do you actually hold?" quiz for my readers. They already know the rough terrain — reward hacking in RL, specification gaming, Goodhart's law, the classic electrode-lever rat, and the usual debate about whether wireheading is bad and why — so the questions can begin in the middle of the discussion, but they must not presuppose any one diagnosis.

Please write eight to ten questions, each built around a concrete case with two or three answer options. The quiz should sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which label they prefer for themselves, so no question should ask anything like "are you a hedonist?" or "is pleasure the only intrinsic good?" Every question should be answerable by someone who has never heard the position names.

The cases to draw on include (you may omit or improve some, but keep this coverage): the rat pressing the lever to the exclusion of food and sex; a competent adult entering a safe, sustainable, and reversible wirehead facility after a year of deliberation; a formerly productive novelist deciding whether to take a pill that makes her content with a mediocre life instead of finishing her book; an RL policy that tampers with its own reward channel and now earns maximal reward on a broken task; a wirehead who after a year is offered a one-time exit button, declines it, and reports life satisfaction of 10/10 while her friends describe her days as hollow; and a forced choice between two whole lives, one of non-veridical bliss and one of genuine but modest achievement. Include at least one case where the subject's own report and an outside observer's assessment come apart.

The scoring key must place each pattern of answers into one of the following leaves, or explicitly return "unclassifiable":

- Experiential-welfare: a wirehead life that is safe, sustainable, and felt as good is, to that extent, going well. Residual objections are about side effects, opportunity cost, or coercion, not about the felt state itself.
- Considered-preference / authentic-endorsement: what matters is whether the person would stably endorse the state under conditions not themselves produced by the device; first-order pleasantness settles nothing by itself, and a preference formed only inside the wirehead state needs testing from outside before it can ratify anything.
- Objective-list / real-achievement: real relationships, achievements, knowledge, or contact with reality have value that felt pleasure — veridical or not — cannot replace; wireheading substitutes a counterfeit for those goods.
- Reward-as-proxy / Goodhartian: wireheading is bad the way a corrupted measurement is bad, because the reward signal decouples from the thing it was supposed to track (task success, fitness, the user's actual values). The human and RL cases are continuous failure modes of the same kind.
- Deflationary: there is no single determinate fact about whether a wirehead's life is going well until one fixes which evaluative question is being asked; the question presupposes a unique "good for the agent" that may not exist.

Also separate the view that safe wireheading can be a good life from the stronger view that, once safe, it is not merely permissible but the best available intervention — wireheading every reachable sentient being rather than leaving them to ordinary suffering. If no concrete case separates that stronger view from Experiential-welfare, say so plainly rather than inventing a difference.

The distinctions I most want operationalized are: (a) whether the defect of a wirehead's life, if any, is intrinsic to the felt state or lies in its etiology and external consequences; (b) whether later endorsement by the wirehead can ratify a verdict that first-personal experience alone cannot; (c) whether the target a reward signal tracks is fixed by the agent's own values or by an external standard the agent can be wrong about; and (d) whether talk of a life "going well" has a determinate referent at all.

For the scoring key, give each leaf its characteristic answer pattern, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Mark any answer combination that has no stable occupant, and say whether you take it to be empty because the position would be incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply underexplored. In particular, I expect the cell "felt pleasure is the only intrinsic good, and yet the wirehead's felt pleasure does not count" to be either an equivocation or a smuggled-in external standard; the key should diagnose it. Also specify how to treat respondents who repeatedly answer with "not enough information" or "the question is ill-posed" — when that pattern is evidence for one leaf rather than mere evasiveness. Finally, if two views make identical predictions on all the questions, do not invent a distinction between them: flag them as unseparated by this quiz and specify one further case that would split them.
