You're a philosopher of mathematics with an experimentalist streak. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming a bunch of candidate ideas before you commit to the best one or two.

Here's a claim I keep going back and forth on: for the two square roots of -1, there is no fact of the matter which one is i and which one is -i. The labeling is pure convention, and any mathematical statement that seems to single out one root over the other is secretly smuggling in an arbitrary choice. More generally, I suspect that whenever a polynomial has two roots that are perfectly symmetric, any apparent difference between them is merely verbal.

I don't just want arguments for or against that claim. I want you to treat it as a testable hypothesis, even though it smells like a claim about convention. Design the best tests you can. For example, you might try swapping i and -i everywhere and asking which true statements survive; or ask whether any predicate, ordering, or invariant distinguishes the two roots without first fixing a convention; or think about what a computer algebra system does when it is forced to pick a root and whether that choice leaks information that wasn't in the object itself. Those are just hints - brainstorm your own and don't feel boxed in by them.

For each test you propose, spell out: what result would push you toward 'the labels are pure convention', what result would push you toward 'there is a real, non-arbitrary way the roots differ', and what the test could never settle even in principle. When a test comes out one way or the other, say exactly what that result would show and what it would not show.

Also say wheter the claim itself needs sharpening before any test can bite. Does 'no fact of the matter' mean no symmetry-respecting predicate distinguishes them, that no canonical choice exists, or something else? And tell me whether the picture changes when we compare i and -i to the two roots of x^2 - 2, which are both real and can be ordered by the usual < relation.
