I lead a data-science team at a payments company. We deployed a neural network that flags suspicious transactions for review. It was trained on human-labeled cases, but it now catches patterns no one on the team anticipated, and it sometimes overrides experienced analysts.

An outside consultant has been telling our leadership something that bothers me. He says that when the model outputs a high risk score, it is not really detecting fraud. The output is "about" fraud only in the borrowed way a vending machine's coin validator is "about" real quarters: whatever meaning it has is supplied entirely by the people who built it, labeled the training data, and set the thresholds. The model itself, he says, understands nothing, refers to nothing, and has no interest in being right.

I want to object that the model learned structure we never built into it, and that our junior analysts likewise acquired their sense of what counts as fraud through labeled examples and corrections from mentors. But I cannot articulate what the consultant is really claiming, or why it should matter.

Is the consultant right? Is there a principled difference between what the model does and what a human analyst does here, or is he drawing a line where no difference that matters actually exists? And if the model is reliable, does it matter for how we should treat its flags whether its meaning is borrowed or original?
