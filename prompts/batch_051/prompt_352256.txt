I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun reading philosophy and behavioral economics. I am sketching a short essay on whether loss aversion and the endowment effect should be classified as cognitive biases or as legitimate preferences, and I would like your comments on the idea before I commit to a full draft. Below are my working notes; they are intentionally compressed. I am more interested in learning where the argument misfires than in reassurance.

Working title: Against the bias/preference dichotomy: what the endowment effect and loss aversion actually show

1. The standard debate forces a choice: either these phenomena are defects in reasoning, to be corrected, or they are genuine features of what a person values, to be respected. I suspect this is a false alternative. Calling something a bias requires a normative baseline, and the choice of that baseline is partly evaluative rather than purely empirical.

2. In experimental physics, one speaks of measurement error only relative to a model of the true value; a reading that departs from a convenient prediction is not automatically an error. The behavioral-economics analogue is that departures from a parametric model of rational choice are not automatically mistakes unless the model's normative authority has been independently established, which is exactly what is contested.

3. The endowment effect is probably not one phenomenon. At least the following may be mixed together: attachment to what is one's own, anticipated regret, status-quo inertia, genuine learning about a good under uncertainty, transaction costs, and experimenter demand. Some of these look corrigible; others look like defensible values.

4. For loss aversion, the key question is whether the reference point is one the person would endorse reflectively. Framing effects that move the reference point around and produce inconsistent choices look more like bias; durable reference points rooted in a person's life look more like legitimate preference.

5. Suppose, as is often reported, that the endowment effect shrinks with trading experience. Even granting the finding, its interpretation is ambiguous. It could be debiasing, learning to reason more correctly, or it could be preference adaptation. Without an independent standard of the true valuation, the direction of change cannot simply be read as improvement.

6. I therefore want to conclude that the useful question is not the coarse "bias or preference?" but the disaggregated one: which components, under which conditions, survive which tests? And I want to suggest that this disaggregation is both more tractable empirically and less question-begging normatively.
