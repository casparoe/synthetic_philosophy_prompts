I am putting together a seminar discussion on Alan Turing's writings about teaching machines, and I want you to reason with me about how his views would carry over to a technology he never saw. In his 1950 essay Computing Machinery and Intelligence, Turing set aside the project of building a program that directly embodies an adult mind and proposed instead that one start with something more like a child's mind, relatively unstructured but highly teachable, and submit it to something like an education, complete with analogues of reward, punishment, and the production of mistakes; he also stressed that a genuinely intelligent machine would sometimes surprise its designer. Now imagine Turing is shown a contemporary large language model of the kind deployed as a tutor: first pretrained on an enormous corpus of human-written text, then fine-tuned through reinforcement learning from human feedback, and only then set to work explaining algebra, commenting on student essays, and answering conceptual questions. What do you think he would say it is? In particular, would he regard the pretraining stage as giving the machine something like a child's exposure to the world, or would he see it as closer to the adult-mind simulation route he set aside? And would he count fine-tuning on human preference ratings as the kind of teacherly approval and disapproval he imagined, or as a much thinner pressure toward fluent, socially acceptable output?

I am less interested in a firm yes or no than in the reasoning. Anchor your answer in the commitments Turing actually expressed about learning machines, distinguish clearly between those commitments and what later interpreters have read into him, note where the analogy between his picture and today's systems strains or breaks, and conclude by saying which step in your reconstruction you find least secure.
