I am a high school student, and I have been trying to work out my own account of what is going on when we talk about fictional characters such as Sherlock Holmes. Please act as a professional philosopher working in metaphysics and the philosophy of language, especially on empty names and truth in fiction. Think carefully and be candid: I would rather learn that my idea is broken than be flattered. It may help to begin by reconstructing my view in your own words and brainstorming the hardest test cases before you give your assessment. Please write your answer in British English.

Here is the idea, which I call branching story-space realism. I claim that the name "Sherlock Holmes" does not refer to a real person, but it also does not simply fail to refer. Instead it refers to a node in an abstract structure I call a story-space. A story-space is generated by the text of the stories together with ordinary inference rules and the conventions of the genre. Everything the text explicitly says is true in the story-space, and so is whatever those statements, together with the genre conventions, require. I suspect that much of this is close to views that already exist. My distinctive claim concerns contradictions. Real texts contradict themselves: for example, the Holmes stories are famously inconsistent about where Watson's old war wound was. When the text conflicts with itself, I say the story-space splits into branches, one branch for each consistent way of resolving the conflict. A statement is then true in the fiction if it holds in every branch, false in the fiction if it fails in every branch, and fictionally indeterminate if the branches disagree. That yields the result that "Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street" is true in the stories because it survives every branch, while "Watson was wounded in the shoulder" is neither true nor false in the stories because the branches disagree. The name "Sherlock Holmes" refers to the branch-stable Holmes-node: whatever is held fixed across all admissible branches.

I would like an honest assessment. Specifically: (1) Is this view coherent, or do the notions of a branch and a story-space do more work than I have explained? (2) Is branching story-space realism just a version of a view that already exists, and if so, which one, and what does my branching idea add or lose? (3) What does it get right about Holmes, and where does it break down? Please give me the hardest case you can think of for it. (4) Should I accept that statements about an inconsistently described character are neither true nor false in the fiction, or is that a reason to reject the whole approach?
