I'm a linguistics grad student and our typology reading group has spent weeks going in circles on a debate that's been rumbling along in the field for decades, and I'm starting to think the impasse is less about grammar than about what "same category" is even supposed to mean. The surface question is whether grammatical categories—noun, verb, adjective, subject, case, even preposition—are natural kinds: real, language-independent categories out there that languages draw on, the way chemists discover elements, or just labels we impose on messy data, with every language cutting the space a bit differently. The data-minded people keep bringing up languages with no clear adjective class, or case markers that mark topic and focus as much as argument role, or prepositions that look suspiciously like verbs (the old Mandarin coverb debate). The theory-minded people reply that surface messiness doesn't refute an underlying category, any more than a weird chair refutes the category furniture. And then we stall, because nobody disputes the facts; they dispute what the facts are facts about.

My own hypothesis is that the fight is kept alive by a false choice between two things that are both true: there are language-particular descriptive categories, and there are cross-linguistic comparative concepts the linguist picks partly for convenience, and the mistake is demanding that the second be a natural kind that explains the first. But I'm worried I'm just flattening a real disagreement because it makes me feel smart. So I'd like a honest diagnosis of the impasse, not a survey of who said what. Is this a verbal dispute, a methodological dispute, a factual dispute about how much cross-linguistic variation actually exists, or a disagreement about what counts as an explanation in linguistics? And given your diagnosis, what would genuinely count as progress—a distinction that dissolves it, a formal criterion for categoryhood, a better typological database, or a reason to think the question is just ill-posed?
