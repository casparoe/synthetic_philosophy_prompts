I have been reading about Dutch book arguments with my school's philosophy and mathematics club, and a member presented an argument that seems to prove too much: if the usual Dutch book reasoning is sound, then no actual human being has rational degrees of belief. The conclusion seems absurd, but I cannot locate the mistake. Here is the argument as he gave it, with degrees of belief understood as numerical confidences, from 0 to 1, that a proposition is true.

(1) A set of degrees of belief is rational only if it is not open to a Dutch book: a set of bets each of which is fair, or better than fair, by the person's own degrees of belief, but whose combination guarantees a net loss no matter how the world turns out.

(2) It is a theorem that any set of degrees of belief that violates the probability calculus is open to such a Dutch book.

(3) Therefore, a set of degrees of belief is rational only if it satisfies the probability calculus.

(4) The probability calculus requires that every logical truth receive probability 1. So a person who assigns anything less than 1 to a tautology violates the calculus, even when the tautology is too complicated for that person to recognize as one.

(5) No human being is logically omniscient. For any human, there are tautologies so complex that the person cannot tell that they are tautologies; if asked for a degree of belief about one of them, the person will either decline or give a value below 1.

(6) Rationality also requires having a precise degree of belief in every proposition one considers. Declining to give a number is not a way of escaping the requirement; it merely leaves the person's degrees of belief incomplete.

(7) Now take any human being and present them with one of the tautologies described in (5). By (6), the person must assign it some precise value. By (5), the value will be below 1. By (4), their degrees of belief violate the probability calculus. By (3), their degrees of belief are irrational.

(8) Since this procedure works for any human being, no human being has rational degrees of belief.

(9) But that conclusion is too strong. It turns rationality into a demand for logical omniscience, and a Dutch book argument, which works by pointing to avoidable monetary losses, should not be able to impose a requirement of unlimited computational power on finite reasoners.

Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I most want to keep (1) and (2), since they are the core of the Dutch book argument, and I cannot see how to deny (5), but then some other step must give way. Can the Dutch book argument be restated so that it still supports the probability axioms for finite reasoners without demanding logical omniscience?
