I watched my town board do two things in one meeting last month and I still can't tell wether there's a coherent line between them or just an inconsistency. They opened the session with a prayer from a rotating local pastor, and nobody so much as raised an eyebrow. Two hours later a woman argued against the proposed casino by reading a few verses about greed from the Bible and saying the board had a duty to keep out something God calls sin. The chair cut her off and said the board could only consider secular reasons, not religious ones, and a couple of people in the audience muttered that it was inappropriate to drag her religion into a public decision. Same room, same institution, same evening: religion welcomed as ceremony, ruled out as a reason. And it wasn't just rudeness — the chair told me afterwards that he personally shares her view of the casino but that as a board member he has to bracket all of that.

I want you to reconstruct the theory of public reason this practice presupposes. I don't need the names or the history. What I want to know is: what exactly is the line it draws, and is the ceremonial-versus-justificatory distinction actually principled here or is the practice just inconsistent? What does it strictly presuppose versus what it merely fits with — for instance, is it committed to saying religious reasons are worse reasons, or only that they're the wrong kind of thing for this forum? Does the fact that nobody asked her to translate her worry into the secular harms she plainly also had in mind (property values, crime, problem gambling) make the practice stricter than a translate-but-don't-exclude norm? And if you swapped the underlying view for one that says any sincerely held reason is an acceptable basis for a public decision, what exactly about the practice would have to change? Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction and no conclusion.
