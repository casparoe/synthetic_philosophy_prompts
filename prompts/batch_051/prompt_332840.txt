Consider the following exercise in philosophical disagreement resolution. In a longstanding reading group on the philosophy of language, two participants have spent a year arguing past each other about the legacy of Quine's attack on the analytic–synthetic distinction. One participant, Dana, is a Quinean holist: she holds that no principled line can be drawn between truths that hold in virtue of meaning and truths that hold in virtue of fact, and that Quine's paper *Two Dogmas of Empiricism* shows appeals to synonymy, definition, and interchangeability salva veritate to be either circular or inadequate. The other, Aaron, is a neo-Carnapian: he grants that Quine damaged the traditional definitional story but maintains that the distinction can be rebuilt from meaning postulates, inferential role, and empirically contentless linguistic conventions, and that some version of the distinction is needed to explain why certain revisions are linguistically licensed and others are substantive theory change. Their moderator has asked them to stop debating and produce the one document they should have produced all along: a joint adversarial-collaboration statement that both can sign.

You are an expert philosopher of language and epistemology who has run several successful adversarial collaborations. Think carefully before drafting, and begin by brainstorming formulations each side would accept while rejecting any wording that is slanted toward one camp or vacuous. Then write the document as follows. Part one: a numbered list of claims both parties genuinely accept, including claims about the content of Quine's actual criticisms—the circularity worry regarding synonymy, the role of definitions and salva veritate substitution, the bearing of confirmation holism—about what would count as successfully drawing or failing to draw the distinction, and about which historical formulations of analyticity the two parties agree are untenable. Part two: a disagreements table with columns for (i) the residual disagreement stated neutrally, (ii) the formulation the Quinean would sign, (iii) the formulation the neo-Carnapian would sign, (iv) the crux, meaning the sub-question whose resolution would move at least one side, (v) a concrete case, argument, or kind of evidence both agree bears on the crux, and (vi) each side's prediction about it recorded in advance. For at least two cruxes, include a concrete test case both sides have already agreed to treat as probative—for instance, the status of the sentence All bachelors are unmarried under a revision of the underlying logic, and a translation pair whose members share stimulus meaning but differ in inferential commitments—and state what each side expects to find. Where part of the disagreement is merely verbal, say explicitly which part and why, and isolate the substantive remainder. Be especially careful to separate two things the participants have often conflated: the claim that analyticity lacks any behavioral criterion of application, and the claim that analyticity fails to mark a real distinction. Also probe whether revisability under global theory revision actually shows a sentence is synthetic, and whether meaning postulates can avoid the circularity Quine pressed against synonymy.
