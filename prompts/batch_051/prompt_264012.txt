I'm teaching a unit on Sartre and I want to use Groundhog Day to get at bad faith and radical freedom, but I keep worrying the film might muddy the distinction between bad faith and plain old self-deception. Phil's loop means nothing he does has lasting consequences, so it looks like it strips away all the standard excuses (a past, a job, other people's expectations) and forces him to face being radically free. But a lot of what he does early on — convincing himself he has no choice but to kill himself, or deciding he's in love with Rita because it's the only thing that makes the loop bearable — reads more like role-play or just being wrong about himself than like the specific thing Sartre means by bad faith, where you lie to yourself and sort of know you're doing it. Can you walk me through the film with that distinction in mind? Where does it actually show bad faith, where does it just show someone revising their self-story, and does the movie get Sartre's idea that we're condemned to be free right or does it soften it? I don't want to teach students a mushy version.
