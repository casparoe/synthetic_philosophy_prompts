Mill's corn-dealer case, paraphrased: the claim that corn dealers are starvers of the poor should be protected when circulated in the press, but may be punished when spoken to an angry crowd gathered outside a corn dealer's shop. Now vary the case. The same sentence is displayed on a digital billboard across the street from the shop. The billboard has cycled through slogans on a random schedule for months; its operators aim only at general persuasion and know nothing about any particular gathering. On the night of a food riot, the sentence happens to be showing just as a crowd forms outside the shop, and several people later say the sign tipped them into breaking in. How does Mill's principle assess the billboard? Which feature of the original case does the variation isolate — the speaker's intent, the foreseeability of harm, the dangerousness of the circumstances, or something else — and does the distinction between print and direct address to a mob still track that feature? Say what a consistent Millian should do about the operators, the sign, or the displayed sentence, and say where Mill's criterion begins to become indeterminate.
