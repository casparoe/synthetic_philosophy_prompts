I'm working through the Economic and Philosophical Manuscripts of 1844 for a seminar, and a passage in the section usually called "Estranged Labour" is bugging me because my professor's handout seems to summarize it in a way that might be off. Here's the exact passage:

"The worker becomes all the poorer the more wealth he produces, the more his production increases in power and size. The worker becomes an ever cheaper commodity the more commodities he creates. The devaluation of the world of men is in direct proportion to the increasing value of the world of things. Labor produces not only commodities; it produces itself and the worker as a commodity – and this at the same rate at which it produces commodities in general."

Then the handout says: Marx's point is that work under capitalism makes people poor and miserable, but his deeper claim is that labor by its nature objectifies itself in a product, and that very objectification—turning living activity into a dead thing—is the root of alienation. Since any product is an alien object standing over against the worker, alienation isn't really about who owns the factory or whether wages are fair; it's about the fact that life-activity gets objectified at all. So a worker-owned factory would still be alienating, and ending alienation would require abolishing objectification, not just private property.

Is that a fair summary of this passage? I want to know which parts Marx would accept, which parts confuse objectification with its specifically capitalist form, and whether the conclusion about worker-owned factories follows from the passage or is a stronger claim then the text supports.
