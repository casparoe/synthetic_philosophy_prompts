I've got a friend doing a PhD in economic history who keeps leaning on an analogy I can't shake. He says the way most people (and, he claims, most history departments) do history is like doing a weather report: every storm, every heatwave, every bad hurricane season is a narratable 'event.' But what actually decides whether crops fail over a century, where cities get built, or who ends up rich is the climate — the slow stuff: soil, rainfall averages, trade routes, population density, institutions that only change over generations. Tracking kings and battles and elections is, on his telling, like keeping a diary of the weather while ignoring that you live in a desert and its slowly getting hotter. His conclusion is that events and individual actors are close to noise; if you want to understand history you should mostly ignore the newsreel and study the slow variables.

I don't know enough to tell where this works and where it's doing rhetorical work. Can you audit it as an analogy: what maps onto what, where it breaks, what the weather/climate picture quietly imports that history doesn't have (or vice versa)? Does the conclusion that events and individuals are near-noise survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does it only look right if you've already decided the slow variables are what matter? And is there a better analogy for what he's trying to get at?
