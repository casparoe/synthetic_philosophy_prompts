Standard inverse reinforcement learning rests on an argument that goes roughly: you watch an expert's behavior, invert it, and recover the reward function their roughly optimizing, with suboptimal choices treated as noise around a rational core. The development I want to throw at it is that most behavior we'd now want to learn human preferences from doesn't look like that. It's a person scrolling a feed where a recommender system picks the items, orders them, and frames them, all while optimizing for engagement. So when you run IRL on that data, whose reward function are you actually inverting—the user's, the recommender's, or the reward of the user-plus-recommender loop? Does the inference just get noisier in a way the noise model can absorb, or does its target quietly change because the environment is now another active optimizer? Which premise of the standard argument gives out, and can it be patched by modeling the recommender as part of the environment rather than as a neutral menu? I'm interested in what happens to the argument, not in a general essay on recommendation ethics.
