I'm teaching a unit on vagueness and the sorites this term, and I've realized I don't have a stable view; I just have a pile of gut verdicts that pull in different directions. Take a heap of 10,000 grains of sand. I'm sure it's a heap, and I'm sure a single grain isn't one. But if you remove grains one by one, there's no removal where I'd say: before that grain it was a heap and after it wasn't. Yet I also don't think of the midway piles as borderline the way a man with 300 hairs is borderline bald. With baldness I'll happily say "he's neither clearly bald nor clearly not bald" — there's a real gap. For the sand, though, saying every midway pile is neither a heap nor not-a-heap feels wrong; each one is just heap or not, I just couldn't tell you which. Color is different again: if you hand me a continuous red-to-orange ramp and make me sort the chips into red and orange, I'll do it, but I don't think I've found a hidden boundary — I drew one, and I'd draw it slightly differently an hour later. Meanwhile the 18th-birthday cutoff for legal adulthood doesn't bother me at all: one second before midnight you're a minor, one second after you're an adult, and that's fine because the law just stipulates it.

My standing theory has always been something like the gap view: vague predicates have boundry cases where neither the sentence nor its negation is true, and that's just what vagueness is. But I can see now that it handles the bald case pretty well and handles the heap and color-sorting cases not at all, or at least not without being forced.

So: is there one coherent account that captures all these verdicts, and which ones does it force me to drop? Also tell me what it predicts about two cases I haven't made up my mind on: a man getting taller in one-inch steps from five feet to seven feet, and a lake getting one drop of fertilizer runoff added each day until it's plainly polluted. Answer as exactly five numbered items, each no more than two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the principle stated as precisely as you can, with the case of mine that motivates each clause; (2) which of my verdicts it keeps and why; (3) which it overturns and why; (4) its rulings on the tall-man and lake cases; and (5) the one place I should still feel uneasy about the fit.
