I am taking a module on biotechnology and moral philosophy, and next month I am giving a seminar talk in which I defend a gradualist version of the position sometimes called the hedonistic imperative. My thesis, stated plainly, is this: because felt suffering is intrinsically bad, and because a mature biotechnology would plausibly allow us to phase out the capacity for severe suffering while retaining the informational and motivational functions of pain, there is a strong moral obligation to direct research and eventual policy toward the elective, non-coercive elimination of suffering, first in humans and eventually in other sentient animals. My argument runs from the badness of suffering, through a principle that we ought to remove a great intrinsic bad when we can do so without sacrificing anything of comparable value, to the empirical claim that genetic editing and neural modification can eventually deliver that removal.

The audience for the talk is a bioethics research seminar of perhaps a dozen people: mostly secular medical ethicists with Kantian leanings, two disability-studies scholars who work on the value of disabled lives and the social model of disability, one palliative-care physician, and one broadly utilitarian animal ethicist. What I want is a Q&A prep sheet, not a restatement of my argument. Please give me the eight or so objections I am genuinely likely to receive from this audience, ordered by a rough combination of how likely each is and how much damage it would do, each phrased the way a member of that group would actually put it, with a two-minute reply for each and a note on where that reply is softest. Tell me which objections I should concede outright rather than try to rebut, and end with the question I am hoping nobody asks. Please write the answer in British English.
