im in year twelve and we've just done split-brain patients in psychology, and my mate made an argument that i still cant find the hole in. the standard case is this: surgeons cut the corpus callosum, the main bundle of fibres between the two hemispheres, and afterwards you can flash a picture to the side of a patients vision that only the non-speaking hemisphere can see. the hemisphere that controls speech then says it saw nothing, while the other hemisphere uses the opposite hand to point to the object. so it looks like the two halves of the brain have become two seperate knowers that dont share what they see.

here is his argument.

(1) before the surgery the patient has one mind; after it the two hemispheres act like two agents with separate awareness.

(2) the surgery cuts one main thing, the bundle that passes information between the hemispheres. so if the patient really does go from one mind to two, it must be the passing of information that was holding the single mind together.

(3) if passing information is what makes two systems into one mind, then any two systems that pass enough information between them should also count as one mind.

(4) but two people with headsets on, each narrating everything they see and hear to the other with only a half second delay, pass loads of information back and forth and are still obviously two minds, not one.

(5) so passing information cannot be what unifies a mind. but then cutting that bundle cannot have turned one mind into two, which basically says the split-brain case isnt interesting after all.

i want to keep (4), because the headset case just seems obviously two people, but i dont know what that costs me elsewhere. which step is the bad one, and whats the cost of rejecting it? give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion, and write it in British English.
