In my work on the philosophy of creativity, I have been thinking about what distinguishes creative variation from merely mechanical or derivative variation, using the image, associated with Hofstadter, of creativity as knob-twiddling: the thought that much of what we admire as creative consists in taking an existing pattern and turning one or more of its parameters, its knobs, to produce something new. The image is attractive, but my own verdicts on cases are less tidy than it suggests, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Here are the verdicts, reported as honestly as I can give them.

1. A jazz musician plays a well-known standard, keeping the melody intact but giving it an entirely new harmonic setting that changes the tune's emotional arc. Verdict: creative, without hesitation.

2. A producer takes a finished recording and pitch-shifts it up a semitone, leaving everything else unchanged. Verdict: not creative. Yet this, too, keeps the melody intact and turns a single knob; the difference between this and case 1 needs explaining.

3. A painter makes forty studies of the same bowl of fruit, varying only the light and the shadows it casts. Verdict: creative as a series, though no single canvas is a major work.

4. Someone runs a photograph through forty different color filters and exhibits the results as a series. Verdict: not creative, though it also varies light and color across a fixed image.

5. A novelist writes the same short scene six times, each from the point of view of a different character. Verdict: creative.

6. A student replaces every noun in a poem with a dictionary synonym, changing nothing else. Verdict: not creative.

7. A translator renders a sonnet into another language, departing from rhyme and meter where necessary to preserve the argument, tone, and imagery. Verdict: creative, indeed a paradigm of creative variation.

8. A poet rewrites a well-known paragraph under the constraint that it contain no letter e. Verdict: creative, and the externally imposed constraint seems to be doing much of the work.

9. A cover band reproduces a recording as faithfully as it can. Verdict: skillful, but not creative.

10. A mathematician weakens one hypothesis of a known theorem and proves the correspondingly weaker conclusion. Verdict: creative, though in a qualified, technical way.

11. A generative model, prompted to continue a poem in its author's style, produces a plausible next stanza. Verdict: I find myself unwilling to call this creative, though it seems to turn many knobs at once, and I cannot yet say which knob it fails to turn.

My working hypothesis has been this: a variation is creative just in case the knob it turns is semantically or structurally load-bearing for the genre, and the variation is made for reasons internal to the work rather than applied mechanically from outside. But the hypothesis does not survive contact with my own cases. Translation turns the most load-bearing knob of all, the words, and is creative, while synonym substitution turns the same knob and is not. The no-e constraint looks like an external mechanical rule, yet it strikes me as creative; the pitch-shift is likewise an external mechanical rule and does not. The cover band turns no knob at all, which fits the hypothesis, but the generative model turns many knobs and I still resist the word creative.

What I would like from you is the principle, if there is one, that captures these verdicts, or the largest coherent subset of them. Please state it precisely, and tie each clause to the case or pair of cases that motivates it. Then tell me what your principle predicts about cases I have not listed, and say honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to revise or abandon. In particular, I would like to know whether a single principle can respect the pairings of case 1 with case 2, case 3 with case 4, case 6 with case 7, and case 2 with case 8, or whether I should overturn one verdict in some of those pairs.

To test the proposal, please apply it as well to at least two further cases of your own devising, and to these three: a composer who orchestrates a keyboard fugue for full orchestra; a photographer who shoots the same portrait at different focal lengths; and a parody that keeps a poem's form while replacing its subject matter.

Finally, please tell me how confident you are in the principle you propose, and single out the point in your answer you are least sure about.
