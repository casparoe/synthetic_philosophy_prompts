I have been on my hospital's clinical ethics committee for two years, and the advance-directive cases have left me with a set of moral reactions I can report clearly but cannot unify. I am not asking for the law, for clinical practice guidelines, or for references; I want help extracting the normative principle, if there is one, behind my own verdicts about when an earlier self's instructions should govern a later self's body and medical care.

The cases, with my current verdicts, are these. In the music case, a woman wrote at fifty that if she could no longer recognize her children and was no longer aware of herself as a person, she refused antibiotics for pneumonia; she is now in exactly that state, does not recognize her daughter, cannot say who she is, but plainly takes pleasure in music, sweets, and her daughter's voice, and has developed pneumonia. My verdict is to withhold antibiotics, as the directive instructs, even though it feels cruel against present pleasure. In the agreeing-selves case, a directive refuses artificial nutrition and hydration, and the now-demented man pushes the feeding tube away and says no; honoring it seems uncontroversial because both selves agree. In the revoked-directive case, a woman has a written directive refusing mechanical ventilation, but two years later, while still fully competent, she revokes it in front of witnesses, and then after a car accident needs ventilation; I say ventilate her, because the present competent self wins outright. In the stale-card case, a man signed a wallet card at twenty-four refusing blood transfusion on religious grounds, left the faith years ago, never destroyed the card, and is now unconscious after a crash and needs blood; I say transfuse him. In the blanket-request case, a directive says the author wants every possible measure to keep him alive no matter what, and he is now in a persistent vegetative state with no realistic prospect of recovery and a treatable infection; I say the family may decline antibiotics, because a blanket request for treatment does not bind the way a refusal does.

The pressure point is the music case against the revoked-directive case. I treat a demented person's present pleasures as not mattering enough to override her old refusal, yet I would never let an old refusal override a competent person's new choice. That seems to commit me to saying that present experience without capacity has little authority, while present autonomy with capacity has all of it; but I cannot see why losing capacity should deflate the present person so thoroughly. The contrast between the music case and the blanket-request case creates a second strain: refusals are doing all the work for me and requests almost none. Then there are two cases I cannot settle. In the undecided-wish case, a woman under a directive like the music case now repeatedly says she does not want to die, though she cannot understand what treatment is; does her current wish to live carry more force than wordless pleasure did? In the future-involuntary-treatment case, a man with schizophrenia has signed a directive saying that if he becomes floridly psychotic and refuses antipsychotic medication, he is to be given it over his current objection; he is now floridly psychotic, insists the medication is poison, and refuses. Should his earlier request be enforced? Please state the principle or small set of candidate principles precisely, tie each clause to the case that motivates it, give the principle's verdicts on the two unsettled cases, and say honestly which of my stated verdicts it cannot accommodate and would have to be revised. After that prose answer, provide a table covering every case above, including the two unsettled ones, with these columns: Case, Verdict under your preferred principle, Deciding clause of the principle, and Change to my stated view, where the last column says either None or which of my verdicts must go and why.
