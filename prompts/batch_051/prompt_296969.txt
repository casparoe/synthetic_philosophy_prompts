I'm an undergrad in a seminar called The Politics of the Archive, and Ive been chewing on an idea for my term paper. I want someone to tell me honestly if it's any good before I build the whole paper around it.

Here's the idea, which I'm tentatively calling documentary gravity. The archive doesn't just record people once and then sit there. Once someone is in the record, later record-keepers can find them more easily: their name is legible, they're in the parish register, the register points to a census entry, the census points to a school file, and so on. So the already-recorded keep getting recorded, not because anyone thinks they matter more, but because they're the ones the finding tools can actually locate. Meanwhile people who never made it into an early layer stay invisible, and the tools don't even know they're missing. The silence compounds.

So the claim is that archival silences aren't a one-time filter applied at the moment a record is created; they're a path-dependent process, basically a rich-get-richer dynamic like preferential attachment in networks. If that's right, two archives that start with slightly different early inclusions should drift apart sharply, and late efforts to add missing voices should mostly fail unless you also rebuild the finding aids, because the new records just sit in the catalog unlinked to the dense existing web of references. Adding a name to the archive isn't the same as making it findable.

Small example from class: a family that shows up in an 1840 land survey keeps showing up in later records because every later record-keeper could find them; a laborer who missed that survey is basically unfindable after that, even when he's still alive and doing things that ought to have been recorded. I know about the Matthew effect, but my hunch is this isn't just that: it's not the recorded people having more advantages, it's the archive's own internal dynamic rewarding prior recording.
