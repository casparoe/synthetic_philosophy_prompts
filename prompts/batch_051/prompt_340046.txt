I should say at the outset that I am a retired experimental chemist, not a decision theorist, and I have only recently begun reading the philosophical literature on probability and rational choice. Please treat me as a competent outsider: you may use technical apparatus, but ground it in a concrete experimental design.

One phenomenon I have been turning over is the Ellsberg paradox. The standard two-urn version, as I understand it, runs as follows. Urn A contains fifty red and fifty black balls. Urn B also contains one hundred balls, each either red or black, but the proportion is unspecified. A person is asked to choose which urn to draw from for a prize if a named color comes up. Most people, offered the same color, prefer to draw from Urn A; and this is true whether the color named is red or black. Since no single probability distribution over Urn B's composition can make both bets strictly less attractive than their Urn A counterparts, the usual reading is that people are not merely risk-averse: they are ambiguity-averse. They prefer known probabilities over unknown ones as such.

Here is what I want to interrogate. The phrase "ambiguity aversion" seems to name a single, basic attitude, distinct from risk aversion and defined purely by a preference for known over unknown probability. But I can think of several other things that might be doing the work: suspicion that an experimenter with opaque urns is not a neutral source; a failure to reduce a compound lottery to a simple one; a heuristic of avoiding any option for which a probability cannot be computed quickly; or a reasonable caution in the face of asymmetrically missing information. It is not obvious to me that there is one psychological kind underneath the choice pattern at all.

Act as a philosopher of experimental decision theory. Think carefully before committing to a design; start by brainstorming the rival mechanisms and the observations that would separate them. Then propose the strongest single experiment, or a small tightly integrated battery of experiments, that would test whether there is a unified, distinct attitude of ambiguity aversion as opposed to one or more of the rival explanations above. Be concrete about the design, the conditions, the incentive structure, and the measurements. State exactly what result would support the ambiguity-aversion reading and what result would support each rival. Finally, and just as important: tell me what even a clean positive result would not settle. In particular, address whether a positive result would show that ambiguity aversion is a rational attitude, whether it would show that the attitude is one unified kind rather than a family of related effects, and what part of the notion resists operationalization no matter how careful the experiment.
