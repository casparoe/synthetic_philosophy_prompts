I keep running into the following pair of idealizations in formal models of knowledge and belief, and I want to sort out how they are related. The first is familiar enough: an agent is treated as knowing every logical truth, and as knowing every logical consequence of whatever she knows, so she can never fail to combine two premises she already accepts. The second is different, and I want to know whether it has a name of its own. The same models treat an agent as perfectly informed about her own epistemic state. Whenever she knows P, the model automatically counts her as knowing that she knows P; and whenever she does not know P, the model automatically counts her as knowing that she does not know P. Here is a concrete contrast. A system that stores the implication if P then Q and the premise P but has not drawn the inference to Q violates the first idealization; a subject who answers with high confidence but denies knowing, or who is unaware that she has forgotten something, violates something like the second. I can see that the first is about deductive power and the second is about self-transparency, but I want the exact relation. Does the second idealization have an established name of its own, or is it best treated as one face of the more general problem of logical omniscience? And do its two halves, knowing that you know and knowing that you do not know, come apart, or do they stand or fall together? I am not satisfied with a bare label. I want the carving: exactly which assumptions in the framework generate each part, what must be dropped or weakened to give up one without the other, and what the boundary cases are where a real agent approximates one part but not the other.
