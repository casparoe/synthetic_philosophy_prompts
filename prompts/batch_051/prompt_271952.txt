A friend and I spent most of yesterday arguing about whether a universe containing only two exactly similar iron spheres is really possible, and I came away unsure whether the commitments I defended about identity and indiscernibility actually form a consistent set. I have written them out below and would like an audit rather than a summary of the debate.

My commitments, as I defended them:
(1) The identity of indiscernibles is true: no two distinct objects share every property.
(2) Black's two-sphere universe is a genuine possibility: otherwise empty space containing only two iron spheres, exactly alike in size, composition, temperature, and history, two miles apart, is a way the world could have been.
(3) There are no haecceities: "being this sphere and not the other" is not a real feature of the world, only an artifact of how we label things.
(4) Relational properties count as properties, and in the Black universe each sphere has the same relational properties as the other—each is two miles from an iron sphere exactly like itself, and so on.
(5) The spheres are nevertheless weakly discernible by the irreflexive spatial relation "is two miles apart from," which holds between them but not between a sphere and itself, and I take this to be enough of a qualitative difference to satisfy the principle in (1) without haecceities.
(6) I therefore need not posit primitive thisness to say that the spheres are two.

Please audit this package in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. For each point, say which commitments are genuinely in tension (or only appear to be), how severe the strain is, and what the cheapest revision or clarification would be. Order the points from most to least severe. Do not simply tell me which commitment to drop; trace what each tension commits me to.
