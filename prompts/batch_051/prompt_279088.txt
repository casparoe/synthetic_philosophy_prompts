Working on war games and the ethics of play for my PhD, and I keep turning over a design proposal a studio supposedly floated. I'd love it pressed on as an incentive scheme rather than judged as taste or politics.

The setup: a single-player shooter set in a fictional mid-century European war. During missions, killing an enemy soldier gives you nothing mechanically — no points, no XP, no kill feed, no headshot pop-ups, just whatever the gunplay itself needs. Only objectives matter for progression. Under the hood, though, every enemy soldier is generated with a name, an age, and one short civilian detail pulled from a faction-appropriate pool ("worked in a print shop", "has a two-year-old son", that kind of thing). At the end of each mission you sit through an unskippable "roll of the dead": every soldier you killed listed with their name, age, and detail, one row at a time, maybe four seconds per row, in the order you killed them. If you kill a civilian, they appear in the same roll but in a different color, and the game never shows you a civilian casualty count or fails the mission for it. The generated details are fixed per soldier across playthroughs.

The studio's pitch is that war games usually do their seriousness in cutscenes while the mechanics turn killing into a score or spectacle. This is meant to keep the gunplay fun in the moment but make the aftermath feel like a ledger you have to sit with, not a number you run up.

I'm not asking whether this is a good idea in the abstract, or whether its tasteful. I want to know how it would actually behave once players understand the system. What does it incentivize? Do people start minimizing kills, or do they treat the roll as a collection mechanic and hunt for the weirdest or rarest generated details? Does slapping a fake biography on every enemy flatten the moral difference between, say, a conscript and a camp guard, because the game refuses to rank the people it generates? Does the unskippable roll just become a cutscene people tab out of, or does it accidentally make killing more attractive because a longer roll is "more content"? What happens with speedrunners, streamers, mods, repeat playthroughs, and the surrounding community? And are any of these failure modes fixable — capping the roll's length, randomizing the details, only listing named characters, letting players skip after the first playthrough — without gutting the mechanism?
