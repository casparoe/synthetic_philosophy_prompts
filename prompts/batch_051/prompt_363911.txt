"A distinct idea is defined as one which contains nothing which is not clear. This is technical language; by the contents of an idea logicians understand whatever is contained in its definition. So that an idea is distinctly apprehended, according to them, when we can give a precise definition of it, in abstract terms."

Priya said the passage shows Peirce attacking the idea that you've understood a concept just because you can define it in abstract words, so his real target is verbal definitions that float free of practical consequences. Dan said that's right as far as it goes, but the passage is about clearness of ideas, not truth, and Priya is smuggling William James into it. Priya answered that the same anti-abstraction impulse licenses James's claim that truth happens to an idea — that truth isn't a dead property we detect by comparing thought to reality but something made in the course of experience. Dan replied that Peirce would never say truth is made; Peirce's whole point was that truth is what inquiry is fated to agree upon, and that is independent of any individual's current sense that things "work." Priya said Dan is treating Peirce's convergence claim as if it were a version of the old correspondence-to-reality picture, when in fact convergence is itself just a long-run practical consequence, so James is extending Peirce rather than betraying him. Dan came back with the point that if convergence is supposed to settle truth, then "truth happens to an idea" cannot mean verification gradually makes a claim true; it can only mean our grip on pre-existing truth improves, which is very different from what James wanted. Then Priya said maybe James didn't actually want the subjectivist thing Dan is attributing to him, and the whole "betrayal" is a caricature. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't.
