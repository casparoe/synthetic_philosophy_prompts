I'm a third-year PhD student working on social choice, and my supervisor is a little obsessed with Sen's liberal paradox—the point that respecting individuals' protected personal choices can conflict with Pareto efficiency even when everyone is being reasonable. Over the last two years I've been collecting her rulings on disputes in our department housing co-op, because she treats them like miniature social-choice cases instead of roommate drama. The precedents I have: (1) Rana wanted to spend the Saturday in her room reading a romance novel rather than joining the house deep-clean; all four roomates prefered she clean, and my supervisor sided with Rana. (2) Samir wanted to paint his private bedroom matte black; everyone else wanted it to stay off-white for future showings, and he won. (3) Teresa wanted to keep a quiet, caged iguana in her own bedroom; the roommates wanted a no-pets rule for the whole house, and she won. (4) Dev wanted to burn jasmine incense in the shared living room every evening; everyone objected because the smell hung around, and the group won. (5) Ehsan wanted to host his improv troupe in the shared living room on Tuesday nights; everyone objected to the noise, and the group won.

Now there's a live case: Farah wants to keep a parrot in her own bedroom. The parrot is genuinely loud at 6 a.m., the noise carries through the whole upstairs, and all four roommates prefer no bird. I can feel the iguana case pulling one way and the incense/improv cases pulling the other. What should my supervisor rule if she wants to be consistent with her own track record? Which precedent is the closest analog? And if her precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle would she be committing to with either decision—something like a locational rule (your private room is your private sphere) versus a spillover threshold rule (you win until the effects on others become nontrivial)—and what further case would cleanly tell those two apart?
