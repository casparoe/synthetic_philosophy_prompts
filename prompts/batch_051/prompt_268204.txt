I'm a PhD student in economics, and in last week's seminar on market microstructure someone ended a debate about whether an individual investor should ever act on a conviction that runs against the current price by saying, flatly, that the market has already priced that in. The conclusion being drawn was that acting on your own information is at best redundant and at worst a failure to respect the assembled judgment of every other trader. The comment clearly did real work in the room; people nodded as if the discussion were over. I know roughly the theoretical landscape the slogan is gesturing at—no-trade theorems, agreeing-to-disagree results, the efficient-market hypothesis, and the argument that fully informationally efficient prices would destroy the incentive to acquire information in the first place—but the sentence is doing too much at once, and I keep finding that different people mean different things by it.

Please respond as an expert in the philosophy and epistemology of financial markets. I would like you to disassemble the slogan "The market has already priced that in" into a menu of distinct, precise theses it could be used to express. For each candidate reading, tell me (a) whether it is a tautology, an empirical claim about real markets, a theorem that holds only under specific modeling assumptions, a practical heuristic, or something else; (b) how plausible or defensible it is; and (c) whether it would actually support the seminar's conclusion that an individual should not trade on information the market seems not to share. To get you started, here are some readings I can already half-see: the trivial point that any market-clearing price is by definition the price at which supply and demand currently happen to balance; the semi-strong efficient-market claim that prices already incorporate the publicly available information; and the no-trade-theorem claim that, under common priors and common knowledge of rationality, purely informational trade should not occur, so the price already reflects private information as well. I suspect some of these readings are nearly empty, and I suspect at least one strong reading is self-undermining.

Please brainstorm the possible readings before committing to a final list, then present the readings systematically with verdicts, and finish by saying which reading or readings actually license the seminar's dismissal, and which readings license only a much weaker practical conclusion.
