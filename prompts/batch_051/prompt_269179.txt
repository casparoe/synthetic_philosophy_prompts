I spent forty years in a wet lab before I started reading philosophy, so I tend to think about policies the way I think about an apparatus: what does it actually meter, what gets around it, where does it leak. Here's one I've been turning over.

Suppose a midsize city decides it wants neither the German/Dutch legalized-brothel model nor the Nordic buyer-criminalization model, and it's also nervous about going straight to New Zealand-style decriminalization. So it floats a third apparatus. All paid sexual services in the city must be arranged through a licensed, worker-owned cooperative. Clients pay into a city-run escrow account, not to the worker or the cooperative directly. The money is released after a 72-hour window. During that window, either party can rescind the transaction and the client gets the money back minus a fixed fee; the fee goes to the worker as a cancellation payment. Cooperatives must publish quarterly, anonymized data on rescissions, disputes, earnings distribution, and how many workers join or leave. Any advertising or solicitation outside the cooperative system is banned. Board seats at each co-op must be held by current or former workers, and a special tribunal can blacklist a client or a worker from the entire system.

What I want to know is how this thing would actually behave, not whether it's a principled idea. What does it do to who works and who buys, and to what gets sold? How would clients game the 72-hour rescission window, and how would workers or managers game the data publishing? Where does the parallel market reappear, and who gets pushed into it? On which margins does this end up functioning more like the German model, the Nordic model, or the NZ model? And which of the failure modes could be patched without turning the whole apparatus into one of the three systems it's trying to avoid?

I'm especially after the second-order effects a well-meaning city counsel wouldnt see coming.
