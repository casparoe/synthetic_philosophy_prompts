I have been puzzling over a line of thought in existentialism on my own, and it has brought me to a conclusion I find hard to accept. I have no formal training in philosophy, so I will lay the argument out in numbered steps and ask you to tell me where it goes wrong.

The thought begins with the existentialist claim that, for human beings, existence precedes essence. A human being is not born with a ready-made nature that fixes what they are and what they ought to become. One first exists, and then makes oneself into something through one's choices. There is no prior human essence to consult and no independent scale of value that settles how to live.

So far so good. But then:

1. If there is no fixed human nature and no independent scale of value, then no particular way of living is better or worse than any other, considered just as a way of living.

2. If no way of living is better or worse than any other, then the distinction existentialists draw between an authentic life and a life of bad faith is either empty or merely a matter of personal preference.

3. But existentialists clearly do not treat the distinction as empty or merely personal. They use it to commend a way of relating to one's own freedom — owning the fact that one must choose and standing behind one's choices — and to condemn the attempt to hide from that freedom, for example by treating one's role, religion, temperament, or circumstances as if they simply settled what one must do.

4. Commending one stance toward life and condemning another requires some standard by which one is better and the other worse, a standard that binds a person whether or not they choose it. Otherwise the commendation is just an expression of the author's taste.

5. Therefore existentialism turns out to need exactly what it denies at the start: a standard independent of our choices. If it accepts such a standard, it gives up existence preceding essence. If it refuses such a standard, then authenticity and bad faith are only two more preferences, and there is no reason anyone must take the one more seriously than the other.

That conclusion — that existentialism either contradicts itself or collapses into a matter of taste — seems to prove too much, because the distinction between authenticity and bad faith is also the reason the position is interesting in the first place. Where does the argument go wrong? If it does not go wrong, which claim should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I would most like to keep both the starting claim and the idea that authenticity is genuinely to be preferred over bad faith, but the argument seems to say I cannot have both.
