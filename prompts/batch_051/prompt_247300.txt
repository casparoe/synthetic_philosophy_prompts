Suppose a common-law jurisdiction becomes persuaded by a broadly Bayesian theory of legal proof: a finding for the party bearing the burden is justified exactly when the trier of fact's rational credence in the facts to be proven clears the relevant threshold, and the source of the evidence matters only insofar as it moves that credence. On the strength of this view, the jurisdiction amends its evidence law to provide that a verdict may rest entirely on naked statistical evidence—evidence that establishes only that the defendant belongs to a class with a known base rate of liability or guilt. The blue bus plaintiff may recover on the fact that eighty per cent of the buses on the route belonged to the defendant; the gatecrasher may be held liable on the fact that ninety-nine per cent of attendees entered without paying; the prisoner may be convicted on the fact that he was one of a hundred inmates, ninety-nine of whom took part in the attack. The standards of proof themselves are left unchanged: preponderance of the evidence in civil cases, proof beyond a reasonable doubt in criminal cases, and the amendment is silent about jury instructions, admissibility rulings, and sentencing. Predict, with reasoning, how this jurisdiction's legal system looks thirty years later. In particular: which kinds of cases are now brought that previously could not be, and which previously losing cases now succeed? Does the practical distinction between statistical and individualized evidence disappear, or does it migrate into other doctrines such as admissibility, damages, jury instructions, prosecutorial discretion, or the treatment of expert testimony? How do police, insurers, employers, and repeat litigants alter their evidence-gathering, record-keeping, charging, and settlement behavior? Do verdicts become more or less accurate in the long run, and through which mechanisms? Indicate which predictions you regard as robust and which depend on contestable assumptions about jury reasoning, strategic adaptation, or the meaning of proof beyond a reasonable doubt.
