I am a PhD student working on social choice theory and the philosophy of economics, preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on Sen's liberal paradox. I would like you to write a decision-tree taxonomy of the main positions one can take in response to the paradox, for students who know the statement of the impossibility result but not the secondary literature.

Take the framework to be one in which a social evaluation is built from individual ordinal preferences, and in which the social verdict is required to be at least acyclic. Sen's result is then that unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, and minimal liberalism (at least two persons each decisive over some pair of alternatives) cannot all be satisfied. Please build the tree from the logical space of which ingredient gives way: the weak Pareto condition, the decisiveness condition on rights, the unrestricted-domain condition, the informational basis, or the demand for a single acyclic social ranking. Each internal node should be phrased as a question a student can answer without already knowing the resolution literature, and should be labeled only by the claim it tests (for example, whether meddlesome preferences over someone else's protected choice should enter the Pareto calculation). Do not start from a list of named positions and attach questions afterward.

The leaves should be recognizable positions. At minimum include: the conditional-Pareto response that keeps rights but refuses to let meddlesome preferences count (commonly attributed to Sen); restriction of the domain to admissible preference profiles; the game-form reconstruction of rights as entitlements over actions rather than decisiveness over social states; dropping rights from social evaluation in favor of bare Paretian welfarism; treating rights as side constraints that stand outside social evaluation; enriching the informational basis (for instance with meta-rankings or rankings of preferences); and procedural acceptance of social cycles. Add any further leaves the space suggests, including "unnamed" if a coherent combination is empty.

Include at least one concrete diagnostic case in the questions—for instance, a two-person case in which each strongly prefers to do something in private while each also strongly prefers that the other not do the counterpart thing, so that weak Pareto and narrow decisiveness cycle. The questions should sort students by what they say about such cases, not by which label they find appealing.

Two further requirements. First, make explicit the combinations of branch answers that the space permits, locate the positions within that space, and for any unoccupied combination say briefly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Second, flag positions the tree cannot separate. In particular, I suspect that weakening Pareto and restricting the domain can be made formally dual once a preference filter is written in either form; the tree should either ask the additional question that separates them, or say plainly that they cannot be separated without importing a substantive criterion of which preferences are relevant.

Aim for roughly seven to ten leaves, each accompanied by one sentence on the position's most awkward commitment. Make the handout self-contained: no citations and no reading list. Conclude with a short note stating how confident you are in the placement of the leaves and singling out the point you are least sure about.
