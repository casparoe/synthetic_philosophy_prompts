I've been trying to get clear on whether explaining economic outcomes by appeal to a group's culture or values is ever really a causal explanation, or whether it's usually just renaming the outcome after the fact. My gut verdicts keep pulling in different directions. I mostly buy the Protestant ethic thesis in its Calvinist version: predestination plus salvation anxiety gave people a reason to treat ordinary work as a calling, to be methodical, and to keep reinvesting rather than consuming, and I can see a mechanism from the belief to the practice, so I want to say that culture did causal work there. But when someone says the Italian city-states led Mediterranean trade because their culture valued commerce, I find that empty—its just "they traded because they liked trading." The postwar East Asian growth story I half buy: the focus on education and saving did real work, but "Confucian values" were being used to explain stagnation in much the same region a generation earlier, so I worry the value is being fitted to whatever happened. I have the same worry about the claim that Britain industrialized before France because it had a more commercial and less aristocratic culture: its tempting, but lots of commercial cultures didn't industrialize, and the story sounds like the outcome read backward. For high self-employment among certain immigrant groups I mostly reject "their culture values entrepreneurship" unless someone can point to specific networks, skills, or blocked labor markets; the value claim by itself adds nothing. And yet there's one more verdict I can't shake: I think something genuinely causal is going on in the story that a Protestant, universalist ethos made some Northern European countries more willing to build universal welfare states, even though the mechanism there is much vaguer than in the Calvinist case.

Is there a single principle here about when a culture-or-values explanation is a cause and when it's a relabeling? My tentative rule is something like: values are causal only when you can trace a concrete mechanism from a specific belief or norm to the behavior, and the value has to be identifiable independently of the outcome rather than projected back onto it. But I can tell that rule probably wrecks my welfare-state verdict, and I'm not sure it cleanly lets the Calvinist case in while keeping the Italian city-state case out. What principle should I actually hold—or what small set of candidates should I be choosing between? If a clean principle forces me to drop one or two of my verdicts, which ones and why is that better than a complicated principle that saves all of them? And what does the principle you recommend say about the case I keep going back and forth on: that Silicon Valley happened in California partly because of a regional culture of risk-taking and tolerance of failure?
