I keep encountering a compressed version of the following argument in rationalist-adjacent writing, and I want it checked before I repeat it. The target is the idealist's habit of political hope.

(1) To hope for a political outcome is to want that outcome strongly while being disposed to judge it more probable than the evidence warrants. (2) A hopeful political agent therefore routinely deliberates with a hope-inflated probability estimate in play, and often acts on it without noticing that she is doing so. (3) The action chosen under a hope-inflated estimate is, by the lights of the evidence-based estimate, at most as good in expected value as the action chosen under the evidence-based estimate, and typically worse. (4) When an agent's own inflation of the outcome probabilities worsens the expected value of her political action, that expected-value loss is borne by everyone the action affects, not only by the agent. (5) Therefore the hopeful political agent imposes an avoidable expected-value loss on others; political hope is a moral hazard, and the realist is right to treat idealism's optimism as something to suppress.

I half-suspect the argument draws its force from treating hope as a contamination of probability estimates rather than as a wanting that can coexist with a mostly calibrated estimate, but I cannot state the alternative cleanly enough to know whether the argument would collapse under it. Please say exactly which step, if any, fails; if one does, give the corrected version of that step; then say whether the corrected argument is valid and whether any form of the conclusion survives. Also say how confident you are in the diagnosis and single out the specific point you are least sure about.
