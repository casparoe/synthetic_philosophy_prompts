I am a professor of philosophy preparing a graduate seminar on practical reason and self-knowledge, and I have been reading a review essay that frames part of the debate around the slogan that deliberation crowds out prediction. The essay summarizes the position it defends as follows:

"From the deliberative standpoint, the question is not what I am going to do but what I am to do. Assigning a probability to one of my own possible actions is an exercise of theoretical reason that adopts a third-personal, predictive stance toward myself, and that stance is incompatible with the practical, first-personal stance of deliberation. A rational agent who is genuinely deliberating therefore cannot coherently assign probabilities to the options she is considering: to ask, while deciding whether to take a job, 'What is the probability that I will take it?' is already to stop deciding and to start forecasting oneself. The agent's own choice is the one thing that, from her own point of view, is not chancy, and so models of rational choice that assign probabilities to acts rather than to states of the world fail even as idealizations."

The first two sentences strike me as broadly right, but the passage seems to slide from that modest starting point into much stronger modal and normative claims, and I am not sure whether that slide is part of the view the essay is expounding or the essayist's own overstatement. Could you audit the passage sentence by sentence? For each claim, say what a careful philosopher of action or decision theorist would accept, what they would qualify or reject, and why; then assess whether the final sentence about models of rational choice follows from the accurate parts of the summary. I want to know how much of this I can defensibly teach, so a precise audit would be more useful to me than a broad overview of the debate.
