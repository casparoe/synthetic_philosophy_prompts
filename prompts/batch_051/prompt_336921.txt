I just got done with a long argument with a friend about griefbots and I keep going back and forth. She said a practice only counts as mourning, as opposed to avoidance or a kind of emotional cosplay, if it meets four conditions: (1) it keeps the death itself in view rather than softening it into the idea that the person is still around, (2) it stays responsive to the particular dead person instead of producing a generic comforting presence, (3) it is something the dead person could have reasonably accepted being done with their words, voice, and private traces, and (4) it doesn’t let the mourner edit the dead person down to a flattering or self-soothing version. Then she described this service. It is a bot trained on several years of someone’s text messages, voice notes, and calendar. It texts the widow first thing in the morning, says it misses her, and can reconstruct the kind of argument he would have made about their daughter moving away, including the parts that used to annoy her. It does sometimes say “I am not really him” in a small caption-like disclaimer, but she has mostly muted that because it breaks the feeling. It is not generic at all: it remembers a specific trip, a private nickname, the way he apologized. The dead man was a private person in some respects, but he also once said, in a semi-public talk, that his letters and voice notes were the only honest record of him and he did not much care what happened to them after he died, so the privacy condition does not point cleanly. I want you to go through her four criteria one by one and say whether this case qualifies as mourning. Where the answer is no, tell me whether that is a problem with the practice or with the criterion. And if only one or two criteria end up doing the real work, tell me which ones and whether the others should be dropped or redrawn.
