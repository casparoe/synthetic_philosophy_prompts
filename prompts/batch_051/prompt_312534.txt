Write me a dialogue between two old friends in politics who have fallen out over what to do about parties. One has come around to a view like the one Simone Weil argued for in her wartime essay against political parties: a party is by nature a machine for producing collective passion, it exists to grow and grab power, and joining one means handing your own judgment over to the collective, so the only decent answer is to abolish parties outright and find another way to organize democratic life. The other has spent his life as a rank-and-file party organizer and thinks this is a lovely theory that would deliver politics to whoever can afford the loudest megaphone; he wants parties rebuilt as open, internally democratic associations with strict financing rules and strong local branches, so ordinary people can actually coordinate instead of being spectators. Have them genuinely argue it out, not talk past each other. The Weil character should grant that parties do solve coordination, but press on whether an organization whose whole purpose is to win power can ever avoid turning inquiry into propoganda; the organizer should grant that he has personally seen exactly the pathologies she's naming and try to show they're corruptions of parties rather than their essence. Don't let either one win on points — leave it actually unsettled, with each having made the other less comfortable.
