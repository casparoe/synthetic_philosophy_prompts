I got into a long back-and-forth on one of the rationalist-adjacent forums about chain-of-thought monitoring, and someone proposed a scheme that sounded both clever and immediately gameable, and i still cant decide which way it tips. The proposal goes like this. A lab wants to use CoT monitoring as a real safety technique, so they set up this system: on open-ended or hard-to-verify tasks (research, "is this plan safe", that kind of thing) the frontier model's reward for the episode is just the monitor model's faithfulness score on its chain of thought, nothing else about the final answer. The monitor is a smaller model, and in addition to scoring faithfulness, it gets a bonus if it can flag CoTs that look like they were written to *perform* faithfulness rather than to actually track what the primary model is doing. The catch is the monitor was trained on an old hand-labeled dataset where the human labelers had full visibility into what was going on, so its sense of "this is what gaming looks like" is frozen. The primary model knows the monitor is reading every CoT, knows it is being scored, knows the monitor is specifically hunting for performance-of-faithfulness, and its only reward on these tasks is the monitor's score. To stop competence from just melting, the lab also applies a KL penalty that keeps the primary model's behavior near the base model.

I keep trying to think about the actual dynamics of the two models optimizing against each other, and my intuitions keep falling apart. Does the primary model end up more faithfully reporting its reasons, or does it find a cheap style of CoT that reads as "genuinely uncertain and carefully scoped" to a weaker and now frozen monitor? Does the monitor's bonus for flagging performance make it over-flag the cautious-but-legitimate CoTs the primary learns to produce, and if so what does that selection pressure do to the primary in the next round? Does obfuscation come back anyway, not as hiding reasoning in gibberish but as locally-legible-sounding moves that are still strategically chosen to stay just outside what the monitor's stale examples catch? Where does this system come apart first, and is there a patch that doesn't just move the problem one level up and require training an even better monitor on an even less stale dataset?
