The slogan "we only know what we make" keeps appearing in the literature on Hobbes's philosophy of geometry, where it is asked to do several jobs at once: to explain why geometry is demonstrable (its lines and figures are drawn and defined by us), why natural philosophy cannot be demonstrative (nature is not our artefact), and why civil philosophy might nonetheless achieve a geometrical kind of certainty (the commonwealth is an artefact too). But the sentence collapses together a number of distinct theses: that construction is necessary for knowledge, that construction is sufficient for it, that knowledge is of causes, that our concepts are fully determinate only for things we have made, and perhaps merely a methodological point about stipulative definitions. Disambiguate the slogan into the precise claims it could be expressing, say which of them are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false, and then identify which reading or readings Hobbes actually needs to underwrite the asymmetry between geometry and physics and the extension of that certainty to civil philosophy. Where the readings differ in strength, say what Hobbes's overall position would have to be like for each to be the intended one. Please answer in British English.
