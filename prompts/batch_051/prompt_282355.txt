I've been trying to nail down my Dewey-ish democratic experimentalism before a discussion group asks me to defend it. Roughly, I hold: (1) political decisions are a kind of inquiry, and like empirical inquiry they should be revisable in light of new consequences, not treated as final expressions of consent or contract; (2) the main epistemic reason democracy beats expert rule is that ordinary people have scattered local knowledge about how policies affect them, and public deliberation is how that knowledge gets aggregated and pressure-tested; (3) if a policy has run for decades and the consequences genuinely seem good on the metrics we cared about, the fact that it was never democratically ratified shouldn't trouble me much, because what matters is experimental adequacy, not pedigree; (4) I am suspicious of constitutional rights as trumps: they lock in earlier experimental results and insulate them from ongoing consequence-based testing, which seems anti-Deweyan; (5) I also think some democratic outcomes should be able to be overturned by well-designed institutions even when the majority currently supports them, if the institutions show, with data, that the consequences are bad. Are these five consistent? I can feel that (3) and (4) might pull against something, but I also worry (5) just is elite veto and I'm smuggling back the expert rule I rejected in (2). Tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like it, and rank the tensions by severity, then say what the cheapest fix is for each. Please answer in American English.
