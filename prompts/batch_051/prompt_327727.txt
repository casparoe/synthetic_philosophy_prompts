I'm producing an in-depth episode for a philosophy of cognitive science podcast, and the guest is a philosopher who defends an updated, avowedly Fodorian language of thought hypothesis: the view that conceptual thought consists in computations over syntactically structured, amodal mental representations, and that no other format can explain the systematicity and productivity of central cognition. She has recently argued that the success of deep learning leaves this picture intact, because trained networks are doing something different — statistical pattern recognition at the periphery — rather than the domain-general, compositional reasoning that the language of thought is posited to explain.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that push beyond restating the position. I want at least one question on each of the following, and for each, a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give:

(a) Whether the systematicity and productivity considerations should be treated as defeasible empirical constraints rather than as definitional truths about thought, and precisely what a trained network that passed the standard systematicity tests would or would not show.

(b) The challenge from putatively iconic or analog representational formats — mental imagery, cognitive maps, mental rotation, approximate magnitude — and whether she can absorb them into a propositional format or must concede a plurality of formats; if she concedes any pluralism, press her on what makes central cognition the one domain that is discursive.

(c) The relationship between natural language and the language of thought. If she holds that thought is prior to and independent of natural language, press her on developmental and cross-linguistic findings that seem to show language restructuring or enabling certain concepts rather than merely expressing pre-existing ones.

(d) What the performance of large language models shows about the format of human thought. If she classifies them as pattern recognition at the periphery, or as implementing something short of genuine compositional structure, press her on what behavioral benchmark would count as evidence that a network had acquired a compositional representational format, and whether she would revise the view if that benchmark were met.

End with one question about what, over the past two decades of empirical work on representation, should most have worried a defender of this view — and whether that countervailing evidence has actually materialized or merely been absorbed.
