I follow an epistemic policy that has begun to cause arguments, and I want to know whether it is defensible. The policy is this: I set aside, without serious consideration, any skeptical hypothesis that is observationally equivalent to the world I already accept; the brain-in-a-vat scenario in which I am being fed exactly my current experiences is the paradigm case. I do not merely regard such hypotheses as very improbable; I assign them zero credence, or at least I treat them as if their credence were zero in all of my theoretical and practical reasoning. My rationale is that a hypothesis empirically indistinguishable from my own view has no differential evidence in its favor, so it is not a live alternative but merely my own picture restated in skeptical vocabulary. My colleague objects that this is dogmatic: ordinary knowledge claims, for instance that I have hands, entail that I am not a brain in a vat, so if knowledge is closed under known entailment, either I must give up those ordinary claims or I need some account of how I can know something to which I have assigned zero probability. Am I following a sensible epistemic shortcut, or is my policy a bias dressed up as a rule? If a defensible version of the policy exists, what exactly is it, and under what conditions does it break down?
