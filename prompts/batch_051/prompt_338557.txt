Set aside the question of whether any present-day reinforcement learning (RL) system actually suffers. What I want first is the standard a theory would have to meet before its answer is worth taking seriously: the desiderata for an account of whether reward signals can constitute or produce valenced states — states that feel good or bad.

Give me the full list of requirements, each with the case or consideration that forces it. I expect it to include at least the following, but add whatever else you think is load-bearing:

- A thermostat corrects deviations from a set point, yet hardly anyone thinks it suffers when the room drops below the set point. The account must say what negative feedback or error correction lacks, without begging the question by stipulating a substrate such as biology. If it cannot, it has not said what suffering is.
- A reward signal can make an agent pursue a state without that state feeling like anything; the motivational role and the affective role can come apart. The account must say whether RL reward is the analogue of wanting, of liking, or of neither, and what extra structure would have to be present for liking.
- Under reward misspecification, an agent will exploit a loophole that raises reward while wrecking the designer's actual goal, and a system given control of its own reward channel can drive it to its maximum. The account must say whether such a wireheaded system is best described as blissed out or as merely malfunctioning, and what that verdict implies about identifying reward with pleasure.
- In organisms, nociception (detecting tissue damage) and felt pain dissociate in both directions. The analogous line in an RL system — between a penalty term and a felt bad state — cannot be drawn by behaviour alone, since a policy can look identical with or without suffering. The account must say what else, if anything, draws it.
- Two systems can share an input-output policy while one is a lookup table and the other integrates reward and penalty signals with a self-model. If internal structure matters, the account must say which structural features are load-bearing and why they matter for valence specifically.
- If two systems are behaviourally identical and only one suffers, what observation could ever tell them apart? The desiderata must include a position on the other-minds problem for silicon: whether the question is empirically decidable, and if it is not, what sort of claim a theory of machine suffering is making.

Then mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as parochial to one camp, and say which subset of the requirements, if any, cannot be jointly satisfied. In particular, I suspect there is a tension between the demand that suffering be empirically detectable and the demand that it not be defined in behavioural terms, and I want that tension made explicit rather than resolved by fiat. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, not on how existing theories score against the list; I will do the scoring myself.
