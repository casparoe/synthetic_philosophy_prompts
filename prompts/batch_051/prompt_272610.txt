I have a standing epistemic policy in political life, and I want to know whether it is defensible rather than a rationalization for my own impatience with ordinary people. The policy comes loosely from John Dewey's The Public and Its Problems. As I understand that book, Dewey argues that a public only becomes a genuine public when the indirect consequences of associated action are noticed and made the object of deliberate, experimentally framed inquiry; democracy, for him, is not chiefly a procedure for counting votes, but a form of associated living in which institutions are held open to revision in light of consequences. I take this as a license for the following personal rule: I do not treat current majority opinion as settling contested policy questions, even when the majority is stable and well-informed by ordinary standards. Instead, I treat every proposed policy as a hypothesis. I support it only provisionally, and only if I can identify in advance which observable results would count as confirming or disconfirming it, and only if there is some real path to revision or repeal when the test goes badly. My friends object that this rule would make stable legislation impossible, because almost every policy has ambiguous consequences and almost every failed test can be re-described as a success by a motivated advocate. They also say my rule is latently technocratic: in practice, the people who decide what counts as a relevant consequence or a failed test will be specialists rather than the public, which is exactly the displacement of democratic authority I claim to avoid. I want a rigorous assessment of this policy as a policy: under what conditions is it rational for an individual in a democratic public to suspend deference to current majority opinion and require experimental feedback before committing to a position? Where does the policy fail, and what is the strongest version of it that would keep its benefits while avoiding my friends' objections? Please answer in American English.
