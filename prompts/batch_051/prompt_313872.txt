In my epistemology course we have been discussing the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, and I have been developing a criterion that I would like an honest assessment of. I call it the Deliberative Continuity Test. A consideration R is a right-kind reason for an attitude A toward a content p if and only if R could, without loss of rational force, function as a premise in first-person deliberation that moves directly to A-ing that p; if the only deliberative route from R to A-p must pass through the question of whether it would be good or desirable to hold A-p, then R is only a wrong-kind reason. For example, evidence that the barometer has fallen can take me directly to the belief that a storm is coming, but the fact that a millionaire will pay me to believe this cannot: sooner or later I have to deliberate about the benefits of believing, not about the weather. The test seems to capture the intuitive contrast between the two kinds of reasons without directly invoking fittingness or correctness. I suspect it may simply recast the object-given/state-given distinction, and I am unsure how it handles cases in which holding the attitude is virtuous or beneficial in itself. What are the three strongest objections to the Deliberative Continuity Test, does it collapse into an existing account, and is any of the objections fatal rather than merely pointing to a needed refinement?
