I'm in a philosophy of perception course and we just went over Molyneux's problem a few weeks ago, and the standard readings have started to feel a bit same-y. You probably know the setup: a person born blind can tell a cube from a sphere by touch, and you ask whether, if they suddenly gained sight, they could point to the cube before touching either object. The usual split is Locke and Berkeley saying no (the link between the visual look and the tactile feel of a shape has to be learned through correlated experience), and the nativist/rationalist side saying possibly yes, because there's some innate or hardwired cross-modal representation of shape. Fine.

But a classmate brought up a variant that's been bugging me, and I want to think it through. Suppose that before the operation, the blind person isn't just left to handle the two shapes; they receive extensive verbal and symbolic training in geometry while feeling them. They learn that the cube has eight vertices and six square faces meeting at right angles, that the sphere has uniform curvature and a constant distance from its center, and—here's the part I care about—they are taught how these surfaces project onto a plane: that a cube from a particular vantage point casts a certain arrangement of quadrilaterals on a flat image, and a sphere projects as a disc with a shading gradient across it. They can answer exam questions about all of this, reliably, while still blind. When the bandages come off, they know which two objects they're seeing, and they can deliberately reason about the image in front of them.

The question: does this change how the classic empiricist and nativist analyses come out? In particular, if the subject succeeds, should we count that as solving Molyneux's problem, or has the task silently stopped being recognition and become inference from a self-taught theory? And does the Lockean "no" still have teeth here, or does it reduce to the claim that only raw sensory association counts as experience, with propositional geometry somehow not counting? Walk me through what each side should say in this version, and whether the variant exposes a hidden assumption in the original problem.
