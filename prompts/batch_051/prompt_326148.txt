Here is a variation on the familiar modal-sorites argument about transworld identity, and I want you to work through exactly what it does to the standoff between strict transworld identity and counterpart theory.

The base case. A particular wooden table, T, in the actual world was made from a hunk of wood W0. Let W1 be a hunk that overlaps W0 in all but one molecule, W2 overlap W1 in all but one molecule, and so on until Wn overlaps W0 in no molecules at all. The argument runs:

(1) T could have been made from W1.
(2) For each i, if T could have been made from W_i, then T could have been made from W_{i+1}.
(3) Therefore, T could have been made from W_n.

Since W_n has no matter in common with W0, (3) seems to say that this very table could have been made from an entirely different hunk of wood, which most people find false. So we have a sorites: either one of the tolerance conditionals in (2) is false at some sharp point, or the conclusion must be swallowed.

Now vary the case. Suppose we evaluate the argument in a Lewis-style counterpart-theoretic framework, with two amendments. Strict transworld identity says T itself exists in many possible worlds and is the very thing that bears modal properties. Counterpart theory says no individual exists in more than one world, and de re modal claims about T are made true by counterparts of T in other worlds under a counterpart relation. For this variant, let the counterpart relation be (a) intransitive and (b) context-sensitive in a specified way: in contexts where minute differences in material origin are salient, the relation heavily weights overlap of original matter, but as the conversation moves further along the series, the context shifts and the relation weights continuity of construction history and structural similarity more heavily than material overlap.

Your task is to trace the argument step by step through this amended counterpart-theoretic machinery. In particular:

- Identify the first point, if any, at which the argument loses its force, and say whether what gives way is a tolerance conditional, the transitivity of the possibility operator, or something about counterpart selection.
- State whether (3) comes out true, false, or context-dependent, and why.
- Compare that result with what a strict transworld-identity theorist must say about the base version, and explain which of the two views is better off under the amendment.
- Finally, say whether the amended case still separates the two views in a useful way, or whether one side simply takes over once the context-sensitivity is built in. If a strict transworld-identity theorist could run a revenge version of the sorites against the amended counterpart view, describe it and explain what the counterpart theorist would have to do to block it.

Keep the answer at the level of clear reasoning about the semantics, not exegesis of any particular author.
