I have recently been reading about the Korean Four–Seven debate, the long dispute between Yi Hwang and Ki Dae-seung over the relation between the Four Beginnings — compassion, shame, deference, and the sense of right and wrong — and the Seven Emotions — joy, anger, sorrow, fear, love, hatred, and desire. My interest is no longer merely scholarly. My mother has entered hospice care, and I have found myself watching my own emotions with an uncomfortable precision. I notice, for example, that my grief and my compassion do not behave the same way. Grief rises, subsides, spikes again at odd triggers, and does not seem to answer to any standard. Compassion, by contrast, feels as though it can be mistaken: I can feel it toward someone, learn that I misread the situation, and find that the compassion itself changes in character — it corrects. That asymmetry has led me to formulate a distinction, and I want it examined honestly before I build more on it.

I call the distinction one between principle-seeking and state-seeking emotions. A principle-seeking emotion is one that, when it misfires, produces a specifically normative correction: if my compassion is directed at someone who turns out not to be suffering, I revise the judgment, not merely wait for the feeling to pass. The emotion tracks something outside itself, and its validity depends on whether that thing is really there. A state-seeking emotion is one that persists or fades according to the condition of the body and the situation: if I am afraid of something I know to be harmless, the fear may outrun the judgment, but it does not correct the judgment; it simply expires. My further claim is that this distinction, rather than the original li/qi distinction, is what the Four–Seven debate was reaching for: the Four Beginnings are paradigm principle-seeking emotions, and the Seven are paradigm state-seeking emotions. And my strongest claim is that a principle-seeking emotion can ride on a state-seeking one. Anger, for example, can serve as the vehicle for the sense that something is wrong; the wrongness is not identical with the anger, because the wrongness may remain after the anger has cooled. I have begun to use this to understand moments in which my rage at a family member feels both like a mere bodily storm and like an accurate uptake of a real wrong.

Is this a coherent distinction, or have I merely relabeled the difference between moral and non-moral emotions and then pretended to discover it? Where does it break — what would be an emotion that is neither, or both? And am I entitled to the strongest claim, that one can ride on the other, or does that dissolve the very distinction I need?
