Write the opening remarks for a weekend workshop for advanced undergraduates on Stoic ethics, specifically on the question whether the ideal of apatheia — freedom from the passions — can be squared with the Stoic claim that the sage feels natural affection for his children. The audience is mixed: most are philosophy majors who have read Epictetus and Seneca, but a third are psychology students interested in emotion regulation who have not read the primary texts. The speech must be built around the following passage from Epictetus, Discourses I.11, as translated in the edition on Standard Ebooks, and must not try to summarize all of Stoicism:

“That a father should feel affection for his child, and for his child’s good have to endure such things as these, is this not natural? Would you have him then, because he is a father, no longer be a man? Or why else do you say that a philosopher ought not to be anxious about his children? I tell you, if my own child were ill, I should not be anxious; but I should not wish you to think that I am any more heartless than other men, nor that I care less for my child than another man cares for his; but the anxiety in my case would be of a different kind. But come, is your anxiety a natural affection, or is it something else? Let us see. You say that you are following nature: is it nature, or is it merely opinion? Bring the matter to the test of your own feelings. If you are acting in accordance with nature, give me a proof of it: show me that your anxiety is a natural affection. If it is in accordance with nature, then it must be good, and if it is good, it must be reasonable, and if it is reasonable, it must be well for you. But is this what you would have me think? Would you have me think that you care for your child because you are reasonable? Not so: you care for him because he is your child; and if he dies, you will go distracted. But what shall we say then? Are you not anxious about your child? Is he not a part of you? Yes, but in the sense that he is a part of you, not in the sense that he is your own property; and you are not your own property either. For you also are a part of the whole, and the whole is not your own property, but you are a part of it. For this reason, when you are disturbed, you are not following nature; you are not following your own nature only, but you are following the nature of the whole, which is the nature of God.”

The speech should do three things. First, it should give the psychology students enough of a foothold to follow the philosophical problem: what is the difference between the father’s felt concern for his child and the sage’s affection, if the sage will not be anxious or distressed even when the child is ill? Second, it should press on a real difficulty in the passage: Epictetus slides from saying his anxiety would be “of a different kind” to saying that when you are disturbed you are not following nature at all, which seems to leave no room for the very natural affection he began by defending. Is the sage’s affection a felt attachment, or only a disposition to act lovingly without the accompanying tender emotion? Third, it should close by telling the students what is at stake if they cannot answer that question: either the Stoic has to admit the sage is missing part of what makes parental love good, or “freedom from disturbance” starts to look like a kind of emotional poverty. Do not resolve the difficulty; the speech should set up the workshop as an attempt to resolve it. Do not mention texts after Epictetus. The speech should take about six minutes to deliver.
