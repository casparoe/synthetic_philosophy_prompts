Okay so I'm on the safety review board at a company that runs a fleet of autonomous forklifts in big distribution warehouses. Two weeks ago one of the forklifts turned into a rack of inventory because, as the investigators put it, it "thought the aisle was clear": a barcode was half covered and the lighting was weird, and the model went with a bad identification. Now the board has to sign off on the incident report and set a standing policy for how we describe the system's behavior in future reports. I argued with a friend about this for hours. He wants us to ban belief-and-intention talk ("it believed," "it chose," "it expected") from official reports, since the forklifts don't literally have beliefs, and he says that language will confuse regulators and maybe expose us legally by implying intent where there's only a learned policy. I think the fully mechanistic description — probabilities, feature activations, weights — is exact but useless for the board and for the engineers trying to figure out what kind of error this was. The belief talk isn't pretending the machines are conscious; its a predictive summary of the system, and it's the only description at the grain where we actually make decisions. Draft the memo I'd circulate before our meeting: recommend either banning intentional language, allowing it, or allowing it under stated conditions, with a short rubric our investigators can actually apply. And steelman the side you don't recommend so my friend recognizes his view even if we land against him.
