I am a novelist, and I want to think through how I settle disagreements about what a draft of my work means and how it should be revised. I have been reading Peirce's distinction among four ways of fixing belief. By the method of tenacity one keeps a belief because it comforts one and turns away from whatever might disturb it; by the method of authority one adopts the verdict of an institution or a commanding voice; by the a priori method one settles on whatever seems antecedently reasonable or aesthetically fitting to one's own cast of mind; and by the method of science one treats a belief as a fallible hypothesis answerable to public constraints that would eventually force convergence among inquirers. Please argue for the following position: when I must decide among competing interpretations of a draft—my own, an editor's, a trusted early reader's—I should proceed by something like the method of science. That is, I should treat my intended meaning as one fallible hypothesis among others and test it against the surprising, recalcitrant responses the text actually elicits in competent readers, rather than protecting it by tenacity, deferring to authority, or treating as settled whichever interpretation merely feels most elegant to me beforehand. If you think this request rests on a confused premise—for example, if a draft's meaning is not the sort of thing that can be settled by public evidence, or if Peirce's taxonomy misdescribes the interpretation of a literary work—tell me that plainly rather than complying for the sake of the argument.
