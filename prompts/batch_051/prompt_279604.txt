Write a villanelle about the lottery paradox in which the two refrains are the two commitments whose conjunction the paradox turns on: first, that any given ticket in a large fair lottery is almost certain to lose, and second, that exactly one ticket is certain to win. Let the first refrain return like an agent moving down the row of tickets and endorsing each one in turn, so that the repetitions enact the threshold conception of rational acceptance, while the second refrain keeps arriving from outside that sequence, as if it belongs to a different order of judgment. The villanelle's turn should be the aggregation step: closure of rational acceptance under conjunction, when the many separately accepted judgments that this ticket loses and that ticket loses are gathered into the single judgment that every ticket loses. By the final two lines, when the refrains stand side by side, the reader should feel the contradiction as a formal collision rather than being told that it is one. Accuracy matters: a formal epistemologist should recognize the Lockean threshold premise, the closure premise, and the shape of the resulting inconsistent belief set, while a newcomer should meet the paradox as a poem rather than as versified doctrine. Do not let the poem drift into the preface paradox; the two paradoxes are cousins, but this one should pay for its contradiction in probabilities, not in the honest preface of a book.
