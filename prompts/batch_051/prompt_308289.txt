Please argue for the claim that a positive rate of pure time preference can be rationally permissible in an agent's prudential assessment of her own life, even though the same rate is morally indefensible when used to discount the welfare of future generations in social choice. In making the argument, set aside uncertainty about whether the future will arrive, the risk of death, and the diminishing marginal utility of consumption; assume those factors have already been accounted for, so that the only question is whether the bare temporal location of a benefit or cost provides a reason to discount it. The argument should identify a feature of the intrapersonal relation between an agent's present and future self that licenses discounting within a life but has no interpersonal analogue, explain why that feature is not merely a form of bias, and answer the objection that temporal location is no more rationally significant than spatial location and so cannot justify discounting anywhere.
