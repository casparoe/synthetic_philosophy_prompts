I am an undergraduate in a course on property, coercion, and the state, and this week's seminar question is whether taxation is theft. Please write a decision tree for my tutorial group that takes a respondent from a single opening judgment — whether a democratic state's levy of an income tax is, before any further argument, the same kind of taking as a private mugger who forces someone to hand over money under threat — down to one of six named positions in the debate. The seminar mixes philosophy majors with economics students who have not done all the reading, so each branch question must be answerable from a concrete case or a plain statement of principle, without the respondent knowing which position the answer leads to and without appeal to named authors.

The six leaves should be: (1) the natural-rights anarchist, for whom all taxation is theft and even a minimal tax-funded state is illegitimate; (2) the minarchist classical liberal, for whom compulsory payment is legitimate only as a fee for the protection of rights and any redistributive levy is theft; (3) the left-libertarian or land-tax position, for whom self-ownership makes taxes on earned wages theft-like but taxes on the unearned value of land and natural resources rightful; (4) the luck egalitarian, for whom forced transfer is not aptly described as theft, or is at least significantly less theft-like, when it targets holdings traceable to brute luck such as an unearned inheritance; (5) the social-contract liberal, for whom democratically authorized and reciprocal taxation is not theft because the tax system partly constitutes what counts as one's holdings; and (6) the legal conventionalist, for whom the term theft has application only within a legal system's property rules, so the charge of theft cannot be made from outside those rules.

Use at least these four diagnostic cases somewhere in the branch questions: (a) a mugger who takes two hundred dollars at gunpoint and hands it to a homeless person, set beside a tax authority that collects two hundred dollars under a democratically enacted income tax and pays it out as a housing subsidy; (b) a person who inherits a fortune from a relative they never met; (c) a worker who takes extra shifts and loses a large share of the added pay to income tax; and (d) a vacant city lot whose market value has risen only because others built the surrounding city.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of labels. First determine the two or three yes-or-no questions whose combinations generate the positions, then locate each of the six leaves at its combination, and mark any combination of answers that has no occupant. For each empty combination, say in a phrase whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any two leaves that the available questions cannot genuinely separate, rather than inventing a distinction that does not do the work.

Keep the tree usable in a forty-minute tutorial: no more than seven branch questions, with each leaf described in one or two sentences stating the commitments that place a respondent there. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the exercise is diagnostic rather than exegetical.
