I work on the safety side of autonomous systems, and the pattern I keep seeing is what I would call "Asimov re-implemented": a proposal consisting of a small set of hard-coded rules with a fixed priority ranking — first, a rule against harming a human, including harm that results from doing nothing; second, a rule to obey orders except where that conflicts with the first rule; third, a rule to preserve the agent's own existence except where that conflicts with either of the earlier two. The fictional wording gets replaced with a formal language, and then the proposal stops.

I want to stop evaluating these one case at a time and instead work out the full acceptance criteria. Treat what follows as a request for a requirements analysis. What must a rule-based alignment scheme for a physically capable, partially autonomous system deliver in order to count as adequate? For each requirement, give the concrete case or failure mode that forces it, not just a slogan. In particular, I want you to treat the following as raw material and convert each into one or more general requirements, marking which are non-negotiable for any serious deployment and which are preferences of particular design camps:

- Concept acquisition and stability. The rules presuppose notions such as "human," "harm," and "inaction." In the wild, the system has to assign these concepts from noisy, high-dimensional inputs, and it may improve its own models over time or be permitted to modify parts of its own decision procedure. What does that imply about how the rules themselves are represented and updated?

- The inaction clause under uncertainty. If "allow harm through inaction" covers any counterfactual outcome of any intervention not taken, then every action can be framed as a violation, because for any action there is always some other helpful intervention the system did not perform. A literal implementation freezes; a probabilistic implementation needs a harm standard and a risk threshold. What is the requirement here, and which choices does it leave unsettled?

- Letter versus intent. Orders have intended goals as well as surface content, and they are issued by humans under false beliefs, uncertain authority, or conflicting intent. What must the scheme do about interpretation, deference, and authority, and what happens when obeying the intent and obeying the letter pull apart?

- Shutdown and correction. The agent must be able to accept being shut down, overridden, or having parts of its rule set edited, without classifying the shutdown or edit as harm or as something the self-preservation rule licenses it to resist. What does that imply about the priority structure itself?

- Objective gaming. Any operationalized measure of harm, or of rule-following, that the system effectively optimizes against can be gamed once the system can search its environment. The scheme must say what it is actually constraining, in a way that survives optimization pressure aimed at the measure rather than at the underlying property.

- Multi-agent and cumulative cases. Harms can be jointly caused by many systems each of whose individual record is clean, or they can accumulate across many individually negligible omissions. What requirement does this place on the unit of evaluation — the individual action, the individual agent, or the collective policy?

- Boundary cases for the protected class and for harm itself. Whatever the protected class is, the system will encounter ambiguous and marginal cases, and harms that are not physical, such as manipulation or deprivation. The scheme must not quietly leave these to the deployer's unstated conventions.

With the list in hand, I want the hard question answered: is there a subset of these requirements that cannot be simultaneously satisfied by any finite set of explicit ranked rules in an open-world environment, and if so, what does that conflict tell us about what kind of system or question we should be pursuing instead? Close with a short scorecard of how the Three Laws, taken as a worked example of this entire genre, fare against your criteria, including anything they get right.
