For my graduate seminar on strategic reasoning and the long-term future, I want you to work through a variation of the prisoner's dilemma that is not one of the standard textbook variants. Begin with the familiar one-shot symmetric prisoner's dilemma, with these numerical payoffs: if both cooperate, each receives 5; if one defects while the other cooperates, the defector receives 8 and the cooperator receives 0; if both defect, each receives 1. Now replace the two prisoners with two independent research consortia negotiating some years before the expected arrival of artificial general intelligence (AGI). They can enter a costly pre-AGI bargain specifying how the eventual benefits of the intelligence transition will be divided and what safety constraints each side will observe; honoring this bargain is the analogue of cooperation, while racing ahead in violation of the agreement is the analogue of defection. Assume that no one present before the transition can enforce the bargain, but that once AGI arrives it will be able to verify with certainty who honored the bargain and who broke it. With probability q, the AGI will enforce the bargain as the human parties originally understood it: a lone defector will be made to compensate the cooperator, leaving the defector with a net payoff of −1 and the cooperator with a net payoff of 5. With probability 1−q, the AGI will not enforce the bargain, whether through value drift, capture, or a conflicting interpretation of the agreement, and the usual aggressive payoffs of 8 for the defector and 0 for the cooperator stand. If both honor the bargain, each receives 5; if both defect, each receives 1, with no triggered penalty in either case. Both players know q and maximize expected utility, treating the relevant probabilities as shared.

First, solve the modified game exactly: derive the expected payoffs and describe how the set of pure-strategy Nash equilibria depends on q. Identify the interval of q in which defection is strictly dominant, the interval in which honoring the bargain is a strictly dominant strategy, and the equilibrium structure in the intermediate interval, explaining why a player facing a defector may now prefer to cooperate. Second, compare this with the standard remedies for the prisoner's dilemma, namely repetition and reputation, mutual deterrence, and external enforcement by something like a Hobbesian sovereign. Which remedy does the pre-AGI bargain most resemble, and where does the analogy fail because the enforcer did not exist at the time of the agreement and is not itself bound by it? Finally, defend a conclusion: does the bargain solve the original dilemma, or does it merely shift the dilemma to the problem of predicting and selecting a powerful future agent whose values are fixed only after the human choices have already been made?
