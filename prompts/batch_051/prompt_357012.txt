I am a novelist, and I am drafting a novel in which a recurring character — a retired logician living in a care facility — has adopted the sentence “I think, therefore I am” as a personal motto. He invokes it in at least four distinct ways over the course of the manuscript, and I want him to be wrong or right in precise, local ways rather than merely in a vague, hand-waving way. The four uses, as I currently imagine them:

1. Consoling a fellow resident who is losing her memory, he tells her that as long as she is thinking, she can be certain that she exists, and that this certainty is one no disease can touch.
2. In a dispute with a parapsychologist, he claims that the cogito establishes the existence of the mind as a thing in its own right, distinct from any body.
3. In a letter, he denies that the cogito is a deduction from premises and calls it a single, immediately grasped truth.
4. In a scene about fiction itself, he maintains that first-person narration guarantees the existence of a narrator at the moment of narrating in something like the way the cogito guarantees the existence of the thinker.

I need to know exactly what this little sentence can carry, so I can calibrate how much weight each utterance in the manuscript is allowed to bear without my character becoming a mouthpiece for philosophy he has not actually earned.

You are a professional philosopher who specializes in Descartes and early modern epistemology, and you are an unusually careful reader of philosophical slogans. Think carefully before writing. Begin by brainstorming the distinct precise claims that “I think, therefore I am” (or cogito, ergo sum) could be used to assert — distinguishing, for example, among claims about the existence of a thinker, claims about the nature of that thinker, claims about the certainty and indubitability of the assertion, claims about whether it is an argument and what logical form it has, claims about how long the warranted conclusion lasts, and claims about what the conclusion does not yet prove. Then lay out the readings as a menu, marking for each whether it is defensible, trivially true but weak, or something the sentence cannot actually support.

Finally, return to the four scene-level uses above. For each one, identify which menu reading or readings would actually justify it, and identify any uses that no defensible reading supports, so I know which lines the character can deliver with intellectual integrity and which must be written as overreach.

Two subtleties matter most to me for the novel: first, the difference between what is certain only at the moment of thinking and what can be inferred about a persisting self across time; second, the question whether the cogito is an inference relying on a suppressed premise to the effect that whatever thinks exists, or an intellectual intuition that needs no such premise — and what turns on that choice. Please also address the possibility that the grammar of the “I” overreaches: that all the claim strictly licenses is something more like “there is thinking” rather than “I exist.” Keep the menu structure explicit and aim for roughly 900–1,200 words of polished prose.
