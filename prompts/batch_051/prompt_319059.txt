I'm taking a seminar on nineteenth-century political thought, and after a late-night argument with a friend I keep turning over a question about John Stuart Mill. Mill died in 1873, so he never saw the way the word 'liberal' split during the twentieth century: in the United States it settled into meaning the center-left—New Deal, welfare-state, regulation, civil-rights sorts of politics—while in much of Europe it kept meaning something closer to free markets, free trade, and a small state, with centrist and right-of-center parties calling themselves liberal. He even sat in Parliament as a Liberal, so the label wasn't abstract for him, and reading him now I can see why both camps try to claim him; its genuinely ambiguous which side better preserves what he was after. On one page he sounds like he is defending the priority of individual liberty and open discussion; on the next he is sympathetic to worker cooperatives and worried about the condition of the laboring classes in ways that sit awkwardly with laissez-faire.

What would Mill actually have made of this split, if he could have seen it? Would he say the twentieth-century split exposes a tension that was already present in what he meant by liberty, or would he say one side simply changed the subject? And how confident would you be in each inference, given that he wrote before the split existed?

Keep the answer under 400 words, and make the chain of reasoning visible rather than giving me a tidy verdict. If you're guessing at some point, say so.
