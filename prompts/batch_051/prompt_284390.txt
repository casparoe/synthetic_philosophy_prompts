Half of lunch today was eaten with me watching two of my coworkers argue about a probability estimate one of them had made. I got asked to referee and couldn't. Here's the exchange, as close as I remember it, and I'd like you to adjudicate it move by move.

(1) Raj had made a little spreadsheet estimating the chance that two bad things hit our team in the same week. He put the chance of missing a client deadline in a given week at 5%, the chance of our main server going down in a given week at 2%, and multiplied them: 0.1% for both happening in the same week. His line was that there's no reason to think the two events are connected, so multiplying is the natural move.

(2) Lena said that's not how probability works. You can only multiply the two marginal probabilities when you know the events are independent, and "we don't know whether they're connected" isn't the same as "they're independent." Not knowing about a dependence isn't evidence that the dependence is absent.

(3) Raj said you have to take independence as the default, otherwise you can never combine probabilities and every model grinds to a halt. Given the two marginal rates and no information about how they interact, the independent joint distribution is the least arbitrary one.

(4) Lena said "least arbitrary" is doing a lot of work. There are infinitely many joint distributions with those same two marginals, and independence is just one of them. Picking it because its easy doesn't make it less arbitrary than the others; it just looks neutral.

(5) Raj said there's an actual formal argument for it: among all distributions with those two marginal rates, the independent one has maximum entropy, so it's the one that adds no information beyond the marginals. It's epistemically conservative, not just convenient.

(6) Lena said maximum entropy is a rule for choosing a prior, not evidence about the world. It tells you which distribution is least informative given the constraints you chose, and choosing to constrain only the two marginal rates is itself a modeling choice. And here the two events plausibly share a common cause — like an understaffed week making both missed deadlines and outages more likely — so the product isn't neutral, it's actively misleading.

(7) Raj answered that "there's a common cause" is also a claim about the world you'd need evidence for. If you're not entitled to assume dependence either, then his multiplication stands until someone gives a reason to change it.

I couldn't sort out who was right. Please rate each turn from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a line or two justifying each rating. Then say who is right about which specific points, and what both of them are missing. Finally, say how confident you are in those ratings overall, and identify the one rating or judgment you're least sure of.
