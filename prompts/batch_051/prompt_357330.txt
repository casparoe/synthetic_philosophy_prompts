I sit on the board of a small maritime museum, and we've got to sort out a mess before next month's grant deadline. Our flagship exhibit is a late-19th-century fishing schooner, the Mercy B. Over ninety years of upkeep, every one of it's original timbers—keel, frames, planking, decking, mast partners, the lot—has been replaced piece by piece as things rotted or cracked. Nobody threw the old pieces away; they're sitting in our warehouse, labeled and catalogued. Now a donor has offered to fund restoration of "the original vessel," and the board has split into two mutually exclusive proposals. Half wants to use the money to reassemble the ship from the stored original timbers. The other half thinks that's perverse: the schooner in our boathouse is plainly the same ship that's been on display for a century, just repaired. I know this is basically the Ship of Theseus with a donation attached, but we can't just shrug it off—the grant requires us to name the object being funded, and the two proposals can't both go forward.

Could you draft the short written recommendation I'll circulate before next week's meeting? It should recommend one concrete course of action with a time frame, explain the reasoning in a way the losing side would recognize as fair rather than dismissive, and somewhere get clear about what we're actually disagreeing over. My own suspicion is the board is split on whether the stored timbers currently compose anything at all, and whether a thing can stay "the same object" while every one of its parts is slowly replaced—so please address that head-on in the memo. If your honest view is that neither proposal should win, say that and recommend the alternative, but make it something we can actually vote on.
