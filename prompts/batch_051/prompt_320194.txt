I am teaching a philosophy-and-film unit that pairs Habermas's account of communicative rationality and the ideal speech situation with the film 12 Angry Men, and I want a reading of the film through those ideas rather than a summary of either. Treat the jury room as an arena in which twelve speakers raise and contest the kinds of validity claims Habermas distinguishes, roughly: claims to truth about what happened the night of the killing, claims to normative rightness about what the law and a just verdict require, and claims to sincerity about whether a given juror is giving reasons or merely giving cover to a private agenda. Habermas's central thought is that agreement produced by uncoerced reason-giving has an epistemic and moral weight that agreement produced by bargaining, pressure, or simple exhaustion does not, and his ideal speech situation names the counterfactual conditions under which nothing but the better argument prevails: everyone competent may participate, anything may be questioned, and no one is coerced by force or by inequalities of status and power. The film offers an almost theatrical test of how far real deliberation can approach that ideal under heat, sequestration, the unanimity rule, time pressure, class and ethnic prejudice, and the informal authority some jurors hold over others.

Now give me the reading. Work through the film's exchanges and say which function as communicative action in Habermas's sense and which function as strategic action, and note where the film lets you tell the two apart and where it deliberately makes that distinction hard to apply. Attend especially to the moments when a juror changes his vote: does the film present those conversions as the work of reasons, of rhetorical skill, of emotional exhaustion, of social pressure, or of something less clean? Where does the film push against Habermas, for instance by making persuasion depend on theatrical re-enactment, on judgments of individual credibility, or on a juror's private shame rather than on impersonal validity claims? And where does Habermas push against the film: the ideal speech situation is not a courtroom procedure, and a jury is charged with producing a verdict under decision pressure, not with achieving consensus on contested validity claims. End by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate about communicative rationality that Habermas's own distinction between communicative and strategic action overlooks.

Throughout, separate what is settled about Habermas's position and about the film's plot from what is genuinely contested among people who know the material — for example, whether the ideal speech situation is a transcendental presupposition of serious argument or a regulative ideal no real exchange can meet, and whether a jury can even approximate it. Do not present a contested interpretation of Habermas or of the film as though it were the settled consensus.
