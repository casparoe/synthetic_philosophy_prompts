Okay, so I've been trying to get clear about what people mean when they treat something as sacred, and I wrote down five features I think pick out purity/sanctity norms specifically, rather than ordinary moral rules about harm or cheating:

1. The thing is set apart and treated as non-fungible. You can't swap it for something else or put a price on it without changing what it is.
2. Violations feel like contamination. The wrongness doesn't wash off with an apology or money, and even well-meaning contact can pollute.
3. The rules apply regardless of whether anyone is harmed in the narrow sense, and regardless of whether anyone consented.
4. Contact rules mark insiders and outsiders. The disgust is partly about who may touch, by what ritual, and whether they're one of us.
5. The thing anchors identity. Defiling it feels like a threat to the group, not just an individual perference.

Now the case. A well-known novelist dies halfway through the last book in a long series, leaving a messy but unmistakable unfinished manuscript. A publisher, with the estate's approval, trains a model on all the author's previous novels, letters, and drafts, and has it generate the rest of the book in the author's style. The book comes out with the author's name big on the cover and, in small print, a line saying it was completed with the assistance of machine learning. Nobody is flat-out deceived, and the estate is compensated. The fans get an ending instead of nothing. But a large chunk of readers, and a surprising number of critics, react with language that sounds like a purity taboo: defiling a corpse, grave-robbing, an abomination, the series is now tainted, they can't reread the earlier books without it feeling unclean.

My question: is this a genuine sanctity violation on my five criteria, worked through one by one? Where the case doesn't fit, tell me whether that's because the case is really just harm/fairness talk in vivid metaphor, or because my criteria are too narrow or too broad. And then the part I actually care about: is the revulsion doing moral work here — tracking a real wrong, like posthumous autonomy or a right over one's own style and voice — or is it a case where disgust is freeloading on some other legitimate objection and dressing it up as a purity taboo? Be candid about where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and say what argument or consideration would change your verdict.
