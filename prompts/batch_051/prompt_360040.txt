I am a retired biochemist who has spent the past few years reading philosophy of statistics and following the replication debate, and I have been trying to make my own judgments about statistical evidence coherent. My working view used to be something like this: a finding is supported to the extent that the observed data would be unlikely under a no-effect null hypothesis, with support discounted by any flexibility in how the data were collected or analyzed. But my actual verdicts do not consistently fit that principle, and I would like your help either repairing the principle or replacing it. Please act as an expert in the philosophy of statistics and scientific inference, and before settling on an answer, spend some time brainstorming the main candidate accounts—frequentist error control, severity, likelihoodist, and Bayesian approaches—and how each would handle the cases.

Here are my verdicts on nine concrete cases.

1. A large, pre-registered randomized trial tests a single primary endpoint and reports p = 0.049. I take this as genuine but modest evidence for the effect.

2. A colleague runs a survey with forty outcome measures and no pre-registration. Thirty-nine show nothing, but one has p = 0.01, and the colleague then writes up a plausible-sounding post hoc explanation. I regard this as almost worthless as evidence, despite the smaller p-value.

3. A well-powered study with one clearly specified analysis, though not pre-registered, finds p = 0.01 for an effect the field had already expected on independent grounds, with a plausible effect size in line with those expectations. I count this as meaningful evidence, not much weaker than case 1.

4. A small, flexible study claims a highly surprising effect in a domain with very low prior plausibility, reporting p = 0.01 after several analysis choices made with the data in view. I think this provides almost no evidence, and I suspect the p-value is actively misleading.

5. A result with p = 0.049 from a flexible, unregistered study is later probed by a pre-registered, well-powered replication. The replication returns p = 0.35 with a confidence interval that excludes the original point estimate. I conclude that the original effect is probably not real, though I stop short of certainty.

6. A Bayesian reanalysis of a published p < 0.05 result using a skeptical prior yields a Bayes factor near one. I find this reanalysis damaging to the epistemic standing of the result: it suggests the data were never strong evidence, even though the original significance test was passed.

7. A group collects data until a test crosses the 0.05 threshold and then stops, publishing p = 0.03. Although the nominal threshold was passed, I heavily discount this result and put it in the same class as the cherry-picked survey finding.

8. A huge observational dataset yields p = 0.0001 for a correlation, but there is an obvious confound that would be expected to produce an association of roughly the observed size. I treat this as weak evidence for a causal interpretation, tiny p-value notwithstanding.

9. A pre-registered replication of a pre-registered original comes back with p = 0.06, but the point estimate is nearly identical to the original effect and the confidence interval comfortably includes the original estimate. I am inclined to call this a successful, or at least not a failed, replication, despite its missing the conventional threshold.

I would like to know whether there is a single principle behind these verdicts, and if so what it is. Test my old null-surprise principle against the cases: where exactly does it break, and what is the minimal repair? If no one account captures all nine verdicts without becoming ad hoc, say which of my verdicts should be revised, and give a precise statement of the resulting principle, explaining which cases motivate each of its clauses. Finally, apply the principle you settle on to one case I have not listed: a pre-registered study that reports a very small p-value for a hypothesis that was assigned low prior probability before the data arrived, with a large effect estimated in a modest sample. I am less interested in labels than in a clear account of when data count as evidence and how much.
