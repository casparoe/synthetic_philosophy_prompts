Below is a passage from Section I of David Hume's *The Natural History of Religion*:

> The mind rises gradually, from inferior to superior: By abstracting from what is imperfect, it forms an idea of perfection: And slowly distinguishing the nobler parts of its frame from the grosser, it learns to transfer only the former, much elevated and refined, to its divinity.

Hume here defends a psychological thesis about religion's natural history: human beings begin with a powerful but limited deity endowed with human passions, appetites, limbs, and organs, and only later abstract away its imperfections until they arrive at the pure, omniscient, omnipotent, omnipresent deity of theism. In the same section he argues that polytheism or idolatry was therefore the first and most ancient religion of mankind.

A significant later development complicates this picture. The cognitive science of religion has proposed that supernatural concepts are largely by-products of ordinary human cognition—for example, agency detection applied to ambiguous events, or concepts that minimally violate intuitive expectations about persons, animals, and artefacts. On such accounts, god and spirit concepts need not arise through gradual abstraction from human-like models; they can arise wherever normal mental systems generate representations that are attention-grabbing enough to spread. Cross-cultural and ethnographic work also indicates that many small-scale societies organise religious attention around ancestors, spirits, or diffuse powers rather than the tidy anthropomorphic polytheism Hume treats as universal.

Does Hume's natural-history argument survive this development? Specifically, does the cognitive-science programme refute his claim about the psychological order of religious ideas, or does it merely complicate the historical evidence while leaving a reformulated version of the argument intact? Could Hume reply that "polytheism" was meant broadly enough to absorb ancestor cults and animism, and that the deeper claim—that the earliest religious concepts are less abstract than later theological ones—remains true?
