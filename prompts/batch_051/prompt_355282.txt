I have a rule I use in arguments about institutions, and I've started to suspect it's doing too much work. Whenever a policy debate turns on what people would lose if we changed course -- a subsidy, a neighborhood, a pension formula, a language that's dying out -- I treat the loss part of the argument as much less informative than it looks. My reasoning: losses and gains aren't mirror images; the endowment effect makes people overvalue what they already have, so the fact that they'd be miserable under a change isn't evidence the change is bad. An acquaintance who works in housing policy pushed back hard. She said if someone has lived in the same flat for thirty years and fights a rezoning, their attachment to that specific place isn't a processing error, it's a preference with a history, and a planner who only counted preferences about gaining new things would be callous. I didn't have a good response.

I've also noticed I dont apply the discount to my own losses, which is a red flag, and I mostly apply it to other people's loss claims while letting optimistic gain claims through. But the idea still feels right to me: some weight should be subtracted for status quo bias, otherwise every existing arrangement has an unfair head start. What I want to know is whether this habit can be restated in a defensible way. When exactly is loss aversion a bias to be corrected for, and when is it a legitimate (especially reference-dependent) preference that a decent policy should respect? Is there a principled rate at which to discount loss-based arguments, or is the whole project of "discounting losses" confused once you allow that people genuinely prefer not to lose what they have? Please put your bottom line at the very end as \answer{...}, choosing one of: defensible as stated / not defensible / defensible only in a revised form.
