I'm a retired physicist who's recently gotten into philosophy of science, and I've ended up on the grants committee of a small private trust. The trust deed says its income is to support "scientific research aimed at discoveries that will relieve suffering or materially improve the conditions of human life." We've just recieved a proposal from a cosmology group that wants to search for a signature of primordial gravitational waves, as a way of narrowing down what kind of inflationary process, if any, got the universe going. The proposal is explicit that there is no foreseeable practical payoff and perhaps never will be; it says the value is that it would be a real answer about the origin of the universe. The commitee is split. Some say this plainly falls outside the words of the deed; others say the founder must have meant something broader, like enlarging human understanding, and would have been thrilled to fund it. I know the science but I'm new to this kind of reading. Please think through it like a careful legal interpreter, not a science advocate — start by brainstorming the different interpretive considerations before committing — and then give me your considered view. Specifically: what do (a) the plain words, (b) the purpose of the deed, (c) what the founder would likely have said if asked, and (d) what a reasonable member of the public would take the deed to promise each imply about the proposal? If they pull apart, which should govern and why? And since I'll have to defend my vote: tell me what the strongest contrary argument is, and what consideration would most likely change your mind.
