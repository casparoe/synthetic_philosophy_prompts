Here is an argument that Williams's one-thought-too-many objection, pressed hard, proves too much; I cannot find the bad step. (1) Grant for the sake of argument what Williams says about the man who saves his drowning wife: that he would fail in some way if, alongside the thought of who she is, he needed the further thought that partiality toward one's wife is morally permitted in such cases. (2) The failure cannot be the sheer addition of a further thought, because sound practical reasoning constantly adds further thoughts without damage: a referee who sees a trip and then thinks the rule book mandates a stoppage has not become any less a good referee. (3) So the failure must be that the rescuer steps back from the relationship itself to a general moral justification of it. (4) But judges, triage nurses, and teachers likewise step back from their particular responses to general justifications—statutes, triage criteria, marking rubrics—and this is normally the exercise of good judgment, not deficient integrity. (5) So the objection would count ordinary, respectable institutional deliberation as an integrity failure. (6) That cannot be right. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. I most want to keep the thought that Williams has put his finger on a real defect in some moral outlooks, but I cannot see how to keep it from also swallowing the judge. Answer in at most 250 words.
