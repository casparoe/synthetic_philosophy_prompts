I am a novelist, and I am drafting a book that gives a real, long-dead historical figure a fictional inner life; before I go further, I want to think clearly about what I owe the person I am turning into a character. I keep returning to Shakespeare's Richard III as a limit case: a play that turns a real king into a seductive, witty villain who, in the play's account, orders the deaths of his young nephews, and that was long treated by audiences and teachers as record rather than acknowledged invention. Read the play through the philosophical question of whether one can wrong the dead by dramatising them.

In particular, address the following. Does the play treat Richard as a person who could be wronged, or only as raw material for a moral and political story? Where should a philosopher of posthumous wronging look in the play — for instance, the scene in which Richard courts Lady Anne beside the corpse of a man he has killed, the treatment of the princes in the Tower, the ghosts of his victims on the eve of battle, and the contrast with Richmond — and what does each of these show about the difference between harming the dead and wronging them? Does it matter that the play was offered and received as history rather than as admitted fiction, so that its portrait was taken for truth? And where does the play resist this reading: because its theatrical logic makes Richard's truth subordinate to the audience's experience, or because it is finally more concerned with the living than with the dead?

I would like a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should take the play seriously as drama and the philosophy seriously as philosophy, and it should be candid about the places where the play will not cooperate with the theory. End by saying what the play adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone cannot supply — what a dramatist understands about the dead that the literature on posthumous harm may miss. Please write your answer in British English.
