I am a PhD student in political theory, working on early modern accounts of the relation between coercion and right. I have been reading Pascal's Pensées, and I keep getting stuck on the logic of the fragments where he treats justice and force. As I understand them, Pascal maintains roughly that a purely just order without force would be obeyed by no one, because there will always be offenders, while a purely forceful order without justice would be condemned by everyone. The problem is not merely one of political engineering, because Pascal also seems to deny that there is any natural law or universal standard of justice available to us: he insists, if I read him correctly, that what counts as equity is largely the effect of custom, time, and possession. My difficulty is this: if there is no independent standard by which the just is identified, what is the bite of saying that force must be made just and justice must be made forceful? Does the prescription amount to anything more than a recommendation to stabilize whatever customs happen to prevail and then give them an aura of rightness? In particular, I would like to know whether Pascal has a coherent third position between the view that justice is a real natural order that power should serve and the view that justice is simply the name we give to the commands of the strongest. Could you reconstruct his view and explain whether the apparent circularity can be blocked? Please answer in British English.
