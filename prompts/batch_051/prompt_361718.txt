I'm currently taking a seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and I am planning to defend a broadly Frankfurt-style compatibilism in my final paper. Before I build the paper around this view, I would like an audit of whether the commitments I find myself holding actually hang together; I would rather locate the problems now than have them pointed out in comments.

Here is where I have landed:

(1) In the standard Frankfurt case, Jones is morally responsible for deciding on his own to perform the action, even though Black's device guarantees that Jones could not have decided otherwise, since Black would have intervened the moment Jones showed any sign of wavering. I take this to show that the principle of alternative possibilities — the principle that moral responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise — is false.

(2) The positive lesson I draw is that responsibility is a matter of the actual sequence: what matters is the actual causal history of the action as it actually unfolds — the deliberation, judgment, and choice the agent really makes — not facts about what could have happened instead.

(3) I also have a firm intuition about manipulation cases. If a neuroscientist covertly installs in someone a stable set of values and deliberative dispositions, and the person then reasons about those values, endorses them, and acts on them in an otherwise ordinary way, the person is not morally responsible, even though the action flows from her current self in the same way an ordinary action does.

(4) I explain (3) by appeal to sourcehood rather than alternatives: responsibility requires that the will on which one acts be genuinely one's own. Jones retains this even though he lacks alternatives; the manipulated person lacks it, not because she could not have done otherwise, but because her will was planted in her.

(5) But I also believe that ordinary adults are responsible for actions that issue from values formed by parents, schooling, advertising, peers, and genetic luck, none of which they chose. So I do not want sourcehood to require that one be the original or self-created author of one's own character.

(6) Finally, I believe a willing addict who endorses her dominant desire and acts on it can be fully responsible even though she cannot do otherwise, whereas an unwilling addict who acts on a desire she repudiates is not fully responsible. In both addict cases the lack of alternative possibilities is the same, so the difference must be something about whether the will is really hers.

I suspect some of these pull against each other, but I am not sure which ones or how badly. I would like you to go through the list and tell me which pairs are really in tension, which only appear to be, and rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say what would have to be given up and what the cheapest revision would be; then tell me whether there is a single revision that would clean up the most with the least collateral damage.
