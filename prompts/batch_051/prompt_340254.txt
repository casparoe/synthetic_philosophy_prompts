I am a retired natural scientist—most of my working life was spent in the earth and life sciences, thinking about deep time and the history of organisms—and I have only recently begun to read philosophy and theology seriously. I want to set down the position I have been gravitating toward on a problem that followed me out of my scientific career: how to hold together the evolutionary history of animal life, with its very long periods of predation, parasitism, disease, and death, and the theological conviction that the world is the good creation of a good God. I am not asking for a general survey of the problem. I want a structural audit of the specific set of commitments I enumerate below, several of which I know to be in some friction with one another, though I am not confident which frictions are serious and which are merely apparent.

My present commitments:

1. The standard evolutionary and paleontological account is correct, and I regard it as settled. For hundreds of millions of years before human beings existed, animals killed, injured, parasitized, starved, sickened, and died; however far back one dates genuine sentience, it long predates any human moral agent.

2. A theological position that requires denying or distorting well-established empirical findings has already failed. Scripture is authoritative in matters of faith and practice, but it is not a scientific textbook; where a biblical text and a well-established scientific finding appear to conflict, the finding constrains the interpretation rather than the other way around.

3. God is the perfectly good creator of all that exists.

4. The creation that proceeded originally from God was good—not merely instrumentally good as a stage on the way to human beings, but good in its own right. The goodness of the pre-human world is not exhausted by its eventual issue in us.

5. The pain, fear, and death of sentient nonhuman animals are genuine evils. They are not morally neutral features of nature, and a perfectly good creator would not build them into the fabric of the world without a morally sufficient reason.

6. The Fall is best understood as the entry of sin and spiritual death into the human moral world, not as the first biological death among animals. Consequently, the Fall cannot be invoked to explain the animal suffering that preceded human beings; whatever explains that suffering must be sought elsewhere, if it can be found at all.

7. I am drawn to the thought that the goodness of a creation may be a property of the whole order rather than of each event within it, so that a world containing predation might still be a good world overall. But I also worry that this thought will either empty the notion of a good creation of content or fail to take individual animal suffering with the seriousness that the fifth commitment demands.

I ask you to audit this set. In particular:

(i) Identify which pairs or groupings among these commitments are in genuine tension and which are only apparently so; where a tension dissolves on closer inspection, say why.

(ii) Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each explain briefly why it arises.

(iii) For each genuine tension, specify which commitment—or which component of a commitment—would have to be revised to remove it, and state the least costly adequate revision.

(iv) If the entire package cannot be made consistent without abandoning one of the numbered claims, say which single revision would be the cheapest overall and give the revised wording of the position that would result.

Do not spend time introducing the general debate; go straight to the structural analysis.
