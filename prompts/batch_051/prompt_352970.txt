So I'm in this philosophy club at school and we got really into this argument about whether you can rationally choose something when you can't know beforehand what it will be like — having a kid, moving to another countery, going to college, or that vampire thought experiment where you can only know what being a vampire is like after you're turned. Half the club says you literally can't make a rational choice in those cases because you don't have the right kind of information (this is the L.A. Paul-style line), and the other half says you can still weigh the decision fine with your current values, or that it's not about information at all but about which future self you want to become. Everyone keeps talking past each other because we're actually disagreeing about different things underneath: whether hearing other people describe the experience counts, whether your post-change values get a vote now, whether irreversability changes anything.

I want to make a sortable quiz for the club, like the political compass ones, so people can find out which position they actually hold instead of arguing past each other. Please write it as a decision tree where the forks are labeled by the concrete question that actually splits the positions, not by the names people call themselves, and where each question uses one of these cases somewhere: the vampire one, going to college, having a child, and a drug that permanently changes your personality. Each end point should get a short name (coin one if there isn't a standard one) and one sentence on the thing defenders of that position would least want to admit. Include at least the leaves for the no-special-problem view, the testimony-can-close-the-gap view, the you-can't-rationally-choose view, and the decision-is-self-making view. Also note any combinations of answers that nobody seems to occupy and say whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored, and flag any positions your tree can't separate from each other. Try to keep it to about 7 leaves max so we can actually run people through it in a 10 minute club activity.

One more thing: make the very last line a one-sentence summary of the single distinction that ends up doing the most sorting work, and start that line with Summary:.
