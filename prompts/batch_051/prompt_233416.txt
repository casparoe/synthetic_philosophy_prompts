I am assembling a set of teaching notes on Bertrand's paradox and the lessons it is supposed to carry about the principle of indifference. The setup is familiar: take a circle, ask for the probability that a chord chosen at random is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and obtain 1/3 if the chord is generated by two endpoints chosen independently and uniformly on the circumference, 1/2 if it is generated by choosing a radius and then a point uniformly along that radius, and 1/4 if it is generated by choosing a midpoint uniformly inside the disk. I am not looking for the calculations, and I do not need the three generating procedures explained again.

What I am collecting are diagnoses of the paradox: accounts of why the apparent contradiction arises and what the paradox actually shows. The four I keep meeting in textbooks and lecture notes are these. First, the under-specification diagnosis: the phrase a chord chosen at random does not name a stochastic process, and once a concrete process is specified the probability is fixed, so there are three different problems rather than one problem with three right answers. Second, the measure-theoretic diagnosis: the principle of indifference has no canonical application to continuous spaces, because a uniform density over one parameterization transforms non-uniformly under a change of variable, so the paradox only reveals that equal likelihood depends on a choice of parameter. Third, Jaynes's invariance resolution: the problem carries a symmetry group under rotations, translations, and changes of scale, and requiring the chord distribution to respect that full group leaves exactly one distribution, the one generated by the radial method, which gives 1/2. Fourth, the subjectivist moral: the paradox shows that a distribution is always relative to a mechanism, a prior, or background information, and that the principle of indifference cannot supply an objective distribution without an extra rule for choosing a reference measure.

I suspect these four already cover the standard territory, which is why I want you to go further: give me a diagnosis of Bertrand's paradox that is genuinely different in kind from all four, not a restatement, special case, or combination of them. Explain it concretely enough that it could be presented to students, and then assess honestly whether it is as illuminating as the standard diagnoses or only an interesting afterthought. Throughout, please keep the settled and contested matters clearly separated. That the three generating procedures yield three different probabilities is uncontroversial; whether the paradox shows the principle of indifference to be incoherent or merely incomplete is not, and the claim that one of the three probabilities is uniquely correct is itself a partisan position. Do not present a disputed resolution as the consensus.
