For an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, write a strict villanelle spoken by a first-language learner — a child working out how public words come to carry meaning — that states the regress argument behind the language of thought hypothesis without ever naming it. The two refrains should voice the two sides of the argument. The opening refrain should be the stopping-point thesis: one can master a public word only by already having thoughts with which to frame and test hypotheses about its use, so understanding cannot bottom out in another public language and must terminate in an innate, uninterpreted medium of thought whose symbols mean without being read by anything further. The second refrain should be the objector's demand: every meaningful sign seems to require a further sign to interpret it, so the regress threatens to continue inside the head. Let the form carry the dialectic — the alternating refrains enact the regress chasing the learner down from the public sentence toward the inner symbols, and the final quatrain brings the two refrains together with the objection as the poem's last line, the homunculus worry reasserting itself even after the official solution has been stated. The poem should be accurate enough that a Fodorian would recognize the regress argument for Mentalese and its standard objection, but it should show the view rather than name it, avoiding technical vocabulary, name-dropping, and anything resembling a Cartesian theater.
