I work in a research group that studies how people read and reason about programs, and a colleague who works on compiler design has been pressing a specific version of a familiar claim. He does not deny that mutable programs are often longer or require more bookkeeping. His claim is narrower: the characteristic difficulty of reasoning about mutable state comes from the combination of aliasing and object identity — two different names can denote the same mutable cell, and what a given expression evaluates to can change over the history of execution — so a reader cannot assess a fragment locally and instead must reconstruct a hidden timeline of side effects. On his account, mutation without aliasing would be only modestly harder than immutability, and the real tax is paid when aliases let distant parts of a program interfere.

I want to know whether that narrower claim can be tested rather than simply asserted. Design the strongest empirical test you can of the proposition that aliased mutable state, as distinct from alias-free mutation or mere program complexity, is what impairs human readers' ability to predict program behavior. Be concrete about the experimental design: what tasks participants would perform (for instance, predicting the outputs of short programs, judging whether two fragments always compute the same value, or locating the statement responsible for a bug), what conditions you would compare, what you would measure, and how you would control for length, unfamiliarity, and working-memory load. Then say what pattern of results would favour the colleague's specific claim, what pattern would refute the narrower claim while leaving intact the broader claim that mutation is hard, and what even a decisive result could not settle — about whether the difficulty resides in the code itself, in the reader's representation, or in the programming tools and notations we happen to use.
