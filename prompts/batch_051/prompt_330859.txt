Been chewing on a puzzle about frugality that I think proves too much, and I can't locate the bad step. I was raised to think there's something admirable about being frugal — tracking spending, not wasting food, not replacing stuff that still works. But when I try to cash out why frugality matters, I land somewhere that seems to delete exactly that thing.

The argument:

(1) Frugality matters, when it does, because of what it's for: less waste, lower energy use, not being owned by your cravings, having slack for the things you actually care about.

(2) If a trait matters because of what it brings about, then what we should care about is bringing about those outcomes, not the trait itself.

(3) So if I can bring about a lot more of those outcomes by getting a hundred other people to be frugal while my own spending stays roughly where it is than by tightening my own belt further, the promoting move is the better one.

(4) So "promote frugality" beats "be frugal" whenever the marginal yield is higher.

(5) The same logic applies across the board: honesty matters because of trust, courage because of the effects of brave acts, temperance because of health and stability. In each case the practice is just one production channel among others.

(6) But that conclusion seems off. On this picture "be honest" has no grip on you in a case where lying would promote honesty (or the trust that honesty is for) better, and I want to say that misses something. The virtues turn into mere causes — instruments — and the requirement to *be* frugal, honest, etc. evaporates.

So from the innocent thought that frugality's point is its payoffs I've apparently arrived at "no virtue is required, only the payoffs are." I'd rather keep (1) than swallow (6), but I suspect that trade is more expensive than it looks. Which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what do I pay for whichever fix I pick? Please answer in American English.
