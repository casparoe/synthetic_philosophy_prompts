Two of the devs on my team got into a proper argument over lunch about whether “training” rationality actually does anything outside the training. Sam’s been doing a load of forecasting practice and calibration quizzes on some app, and reckons it’s made him a clearer thinker generally. Priya wasnt having it. This is roughly how it went, as best I remember it.

Sam: I’ve been doing daily calibration tests for a few months and I’m clearly better at putting numbers on things, even at work. I catch myself more.

Priya: That just shows you got better at the tests. Being better at putting numbers on things *is* the test. What you havent shown is that your actual decisions improved.

Sam: But that’s how any skill works. Karate people drill katas over and over so the movements become automatic. You don’t say katas are pointless just because they’re not a real fight.

Priya: Actually, that’s exactly the debate in martial arts. Lots of coaches say kata without live resistance gives you a false sense of being able to fight — it looks like the skill but doesn’t transfer to someone actually punching back. If your app is the rationality version of a kata, you might just be getting good at a choreography.

Sam: Okay, but pilots train in simulators and nobody says simulators are useless. It’s all transferable.

Priya: A simulator is high-fidelity. It recreates the relevant stresses and feedback. A calibration quiz on your phone recreates almost nothing about an actual decision — no stakes, no time pressure, no social consequences.

Sam: Right, but flying is a specific skill. Rationality isn’t. Updating on base rates, noticing when I’m being defensive — that’s meant to apply everywhere, so the practice should generalise precisely because it’s abstract.

Priya: That’s the thing you were meant to be proving. You can’t just assert the skill is general to show the training transfers. It’s a known pattern that people train on abstract stuff and it stays stuck. Learning Latin doesn’t automatically make you better at reasoning, and chess masters aren’t better at stock trading. The general-skill claim keeps failing.

Sam: Sure, but those are claims about content. Rationality is metacognitive — it’s about how you process whatever content you’re dealing with. That’s a different kind of thing, closer to a habit than a body of knowledge.

Priya: Fine, but if it’s a habit, habits are learned in context with real feedback. You can’t practise the habit of not being defensive in a quiz about whether it’ll rain. The quiz won’t punish you for the actual failure mode, so the habit won’t fire when the failure mode shows up.

Sam: That feels too pessimistic. If I drill the pattern enough, the idea is that the cue “I’m about to argue for what I want to be true” triggers the pause. The cue isn’t the weather — the cue is a feeling, and the app can drill that feeling.

Priya: Can it? Because when it actually matters, the feeling comes with a spike of stress and ego. A calm little quiz never produces that spike. So the cue you’ve trained on is the calm version, which isn’t the one that appears in real cases.

They basically had to get back to standup at that point, so it’s unresolved. Can you adjudicate it move by move? I’m after who’s right about which specific points, not just who wins overall, and whether there’s something both of them are missing.
