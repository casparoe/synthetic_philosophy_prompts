I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the past several weeks I have been following a metaphysician as she prepares for a departmental colloquium. Her thesis is this: Bradley's regress should be read not as proof that relations or structured propositions are incoherent, but as showing that the unity of a proposition—the fact that the apple is red says one thing rather than merely listing an apple and the property of being red—is a primitive, non-relational feature that no further relation or constituent can explain. On her view the regress is benign, and every attempt to stop it by adding an instantiation relation, an unsaturated concept, or a special non-relational tie merely relocates the unity it was meant to produce. The audience will be a mixed department: a truthmaker theorist who holds that every true proposition needs a worldly truthmaker and suspects her view quietly gives up on truthmaking; a neo-Fregean philosopher of language who thinks the saturated/unsaturated distinction already dissolves the problem; a structuralist about relations who thinks she has reified unity without placing any constraint on which constituents get tied into a proposition; a nominalist who rejects propositions and states of affairs as needless posits; and a historian of British idealism who believes contemporary readings of Bradley are far too tame.
