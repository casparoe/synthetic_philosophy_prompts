I'm taking a philosophy of biology course in which the instructor refuses to state her theory of biological function and instead gives verdicts on cases, one per week. So far she has said: a normal human heart has the function of pumping blood; a kidney has the function of filtering nitrogenous waste from the blood; a peacock's tail has the function of attracting mates. She also said that the human appendix — stipulating for the exercise that it currently does neither digestion nor immune maintenance — has no function; it is a vestige. And she said that a new coat-color mutation that neither helps nor harms its bearer has no function; it is a neutral variant. I can tell she is somewhere in the neighborhood of the familiar split between causal-role and etiological accounts of function, but her record seems to me to underdetermine which kind of account she holds.

The next case is ours to predict. A mutation has arisen in a single fish in a lake; the mutation lets the fish break down a recently introduced environmental toxin and gives it a clear survival advantage in the current lake. But the mutation is only a few months old, the fish has not yet reproduced, and there has been no selection on the trait. Does it have a biological function? On a current causal-role account, the trait has a function now, since it contributes to the fish's survival. On an etiological account that makes function depend on selection history — especially one that requires the trait to have been maintained by selection in the lineage — the trait has no function yet, since it is a novelty in a single individual. Which principle is her record better evidence for, and which earlier verdict is doing the most work in your judgment? Give your best prediction of what she will say, and specify the further case you would want to see in order to settle whether she is running a current-role view or a selection-history view.
