I write about science and ideas, and I recently read a long-form piece on the psychology of choice that summarized Newcomb's problem, and I would like a careful accuracy audit before I lean on it. The piece says: You are shown two boxes. Box A transparently contains $1,000; Box B is opaque, and a highly accurate predictor has already either placed $1,000,000 in Box B or left it empty after predicting which option you will take. You may take both boxes or only Box B. The piece presents the puzzle as a contest between two intuitions: taking both boxes seems rational because the prediction is already made and your current choice cannot causally change the contents of Box B, while taking only Box B seems rational because nearly all one-boxers walk away with a million dollars and nearly all two-boxers end up with only a thousand. It then resolves the contest in favor of two-boxing, on the grounds that rational choice must follow dominance reasoning, and it says that anyone who chooses only Box B is implicitly committed to backward causation—the idea that a later decision can reach back and alter the predictor's earlier setup. It concludes that Newcomb's problem establishes that the widespread intuition for one-boxing, though common, is a cognitive illusion. I can tell the setup is conveyed cleanly, but the backward-causation charge and the word "establishes" make me uneasy. Which parts of this summary would a decision theorist accept as accurate, which are defensible simplifications, and which are misrepresentations or overstatements? Does the conclusion follow from the accurate parts? Please answer in American English.
