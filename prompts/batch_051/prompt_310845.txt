Bit of an odd request, but I'm running a small reading group at my local library on philosophy and the good life, and we've spent the last two sessions on the paradox of hedonism — the idea that the more directly you aim at happiness or pleasure, the more it seems to recede, and that happiness mostly turns up as a byproduct of doing other things for their own sake. It's become weirdly personal for me becuase I've spent the past year trying to optimise my life (mood tracking, scheduling 'enjoyable activities', the lot) and have basically found that the harder I try to be happy, the more self-conscious and flat everything feels. So it's stopped being abstract, and I'd like to close the final session by reading the group a poem that does the paradox rather than explaining it at them.

Could you write a villanelle — the 19-line form with two refrains that alternate through the tercets and then meet in the closing couplet — where the two refrains are the two pulls of the paradox: one about chasing happiness directly and finding it thin or absent, the other about turning away into some absorbing task or relationship and finding that happiness has crept in anyway. The form should do the philosophical work: the refrains should keep returning and passing each other the way the paradox keeps reasserting itself, and the final couplet should leave the tension standing rather than resolve it into a tidy moral like 'just stop trying.' I want it accurate enough that someone who knows the standard statement of the paradox (the Mill and Sidgwick line) would recognise it as getting the idea right, but please keep jargon like 'axiological' or 'instrumental value' out, and make it something that actually works read aloud to a room of non-specialists. The metre can be loose, but the refrain scheme should be exact.
