I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophical training, but I've been reading behavioral economics because it bears directly on the product decisions I work on: defaults, cancellation flows, feature deprecation, and how much weight to give users' resistance to losing something they already have. A dispute runs through that literature — and through our internal debates — about whether loss aversion and the endowment effect are cognitive biases we should design around or legitimate preferences we should respect. The debate has been live for decades, and positions barely move, so before I pick a side I want to understand what kind of disagreement it actually is.

The bias camp, as I understand it, points to the willingness-to-accept versus willingness-to-pay gap: people who would pay only a little to acquire an object will demand substantially more to give it up once it's theirs, even for a trivial, interchangeable item like a mug. That looks like the reference point warping value — a loss is weighed more heavily than an equivalent gain — so choices become internally inconsistent and a poor guide to what would actually benefit someone. The preference camp replies that ownership changes the real situation: once something is part of a person's baseline, giving it up is a genuine loss, not a framing artifact, and for many goods there is no neutral "unowned" valuation to serve as the standard. Calling the gap a bias therefore presupposes a benchmark that is exactly what's contested. A third camp is more purely empirical: the gap shrinks or disappears with careful instructions, repeated choices, and market experience, which suggests the finding itself may be an artifact of one-shot laboratory settings.

What confuses me is that these camps largely accept the same experimental demonstrations and still don't converge. My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement isn't primarily empirical and isn't just about the word "bias." It's about what gets to count as a person's true preferences when different methods yield different answers: a cool one-shot judgment, a seasoned market choice, a later report of satisfaction, or logical consistency across framings. But I want that hypothesis tested, not assumed.

What kind of disagreement is this, then? Which of these is doing the real work — a hidden factual premise, a difference about which cases count as data, a difference about what "preference" means, a disagreement about what should be respected as a person's real interest, or a combination that makes the question ill-posed? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a specific experimental design, a conceptual distinction, a reframing — or is the stalemate the expected outcome? I want the structure of the impasse more than a verdict on who is right.
