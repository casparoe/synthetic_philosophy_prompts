Title: Hinges and the Problem of Changing Your Mind

My friend kept returning to the idea that our doubts and questions only make sense because some things are not doubted: every critical practice runs on a stock of unquestioned commitments, the way a door turns only because its hinges stay fixed. I grant that. What I don't grant is the step hinge epistemologists then make: that these commitments are somehow exempt from rational criticism, or that they can't be known or justified because they are the precondition of justification itself.

My main complaint is that the view runs together three different phenomena. First, there are hinges we can't drop without ceasing to be recognizably human — things like the stability of mid-sized physical objects across time. Second, there are hinges belonging to a particular inquiry or institution, which are revisable the moment you step outside that inquiry: a lab's background assumptions, a court's rules of evidence, a market's assumption that contracts will be honored. Third, there are what I'd call cultural hinges — that private property is legitimate, that a person's worth tracks their productivity, that grades measure ability. These are acquired, contested, and sometimes abandoned; they aren't preconditions for thought. By calling all three "hinges," the view makes the third sort look as unassailable as the first.

This isn't just a taxonomy point. It matters for disagreement. If you and I disagree about a cultural hinge, hinge epistemology seems to say that reasons run out and we just have different pictures — different forms of life. But that can't be the whole story, because people do move between such hinges. Maybe rarely in one argument, I'll grant, but over years: through exposure, through experience, through noticing that a commitment doesn't deliver on its promises, through seeing that it was propping up a bad arrangement. If hinges are beyond rational evaluation, then this movement can only be described as conversion, never as learning. But some of it plainly is learning. People don't merely swap one groundless picture for another; they come to see that a commitment was doing real work, or blocking evidence, or serving someone's interests while masquerading as common sense.

So here's the dilemma I want to press. Either hinges can be rationally evaluated — piecemeal, by later lights, from a new perspective — or they cannot. If they can, then the sharp contrast between hinges and ordinary empirical claims softens: a hinge is just a claim we are not currently evaluating, not a claim that is in principle beyond evaluation. If they cannot, then hinge epistemology has no resources to say why some hinge-shifts are cognitive achievements and others are just falling for propaganda. The second horn also makes the view weirdly conservative, because the framework you're in always gets a veto over any criticism of it — any criticism will itself rely on some hinge or other, and the framework can always fall back on the idea that this is simply the way we do things.

There is also a reflexive problem I'm not sure hinge people have fully gotten out of. The claim "every rational criticism depends on embedded hinges" looks like a general claim about rationality, supported by examples and argument — the product of ordinary critical inquiry. But if the view is right, that very claim is made from inside a particular hinge scheme. So either it's just the expression of one hinge scheme among others, or it's an insight about hinges from a standpoint outside all schemes. The view seems to want both: it wants to be a neutral description of our epistemic condition, and it also wants to explain why there can't be a neutral description.

My positive suggestion is modest. Maybe "hinge" is a functional term, not a kind term. A proposition functions as a hinge within a practice when it isn't up for grabs in that practice; the same proposition can be up for grabs in a different practice, at a different stage of a life, or in a different conversation. Something like "the earth is billions of years old" functions as a hinge in most ordinary geology, but not in a philosophy seminar or a young-earth church group, though it's pinned in different directions in each. If that's right, the interesting questions aren't which propositions are hinges, but which hinges a practice is allowed to keep, and what would count as a reason to loosen one. Those are questions about the distribution of trust and authority — about who gets to decide what's not up for grabs. They're ethical and political questions, not just epistemological ones. And hinge epistemology's quietism, the this-is-just-how-we-act move, tends to shut those questions down before they get asked.

That's what I couldn't manage to say cleanly to my friend at 1 a.m. I'm pretty sure I'm onto something, but I'm also aware I might be attacking a strawman or running together two different versions of the view. If you think I'm wrong, say so plainly.
