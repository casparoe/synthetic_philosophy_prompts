Okay so I got into it with my cousin who's in a neuroscience-adjacent program, and he kept saying that beliefs and desires are a bit like the humors or caloric fluid — a rough way of talking that happens to make us very good at predicting each other, but nothing in the brain will ever line up with them, so a mature science will just stop using those words. I said fine, but what would you actually need to see to change your mind? He didn't have a answer, which bugged me, but maybe my question was the hard one.

Here's what I want. Suppose you had a full recording of someone's neural activity while they're trying to decide whether to take a job, plus everything they say and do. Design the strongest test you can of whether they literally have something like a belief and a desire — say, the belief that the pay is too low and the desire for a short commute — or whether those are just handy labels for patterns that could be described just as well without them. For example, would it settle anything if you found a stable pattern of activity that shows up across very different ways of expressing that belief, and if you interfered with it their choice shifted the way a changed belief would? What result would favor the 'nothing really there' view, what result would favor the 'they're real inner causes' view, and what would a positive result still leave undecided? In particular, be honest about whether the leftover gap is just a measurement problem neuroscience could one day close, or whether it's baked into the very idea of having a belief at all.
