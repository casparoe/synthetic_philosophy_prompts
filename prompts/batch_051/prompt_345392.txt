In epistemic game theory, the standard motivations for rationalizability and Nash equilibrium suppose that each player is rational, that each knows the others are rational, that each knows that each knows, and so on without end. A classical result in distributed computing, the coordinated attack problem, appears to show that common knowledge cannot be achieved by agents whose messages can be lost, because every acknowledgment introduces a further level of uncertainty about whether the previous acknowledgment arrived, so no finite exchange closes the regress. Does this impossibility result undermine the game-theoretic assumption of common knowledge of rationality, or does it bear only on a different sense of common knowledge? I want to know which premise or use of the epistemic game-theoretic assumption, if any, the distributed-systems result actually touches, and what the best repair is: treating common knowledge of rationality as a purely normative idealization, as the limit of finite-order reasoning, or as a fixed-point condition that never needs to be realized in time.
