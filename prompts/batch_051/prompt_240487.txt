I run a small, informal reading group on the ethics of bringing people into existence, and I set the following short written exercise:

Explain what the procreation asymmetry is meant to claim, and how it is supposed to support the conclusion that coming into existence is always a harm.

Below is one participant's answer. Please assign a grade out of 10 and give the specific, point-by-point comments that justify that grade, distinguishing what the answer gets right from any places where it goes subtly wrong.

The participant's answer:

The procreation asymmetry says that pain and pleasure play different roles when we compare an actually lived life with the scenario in which that person never exists. If the person exists, pain in her life is bad for her, and pleasure in her life is good for her. If she never exists, the absence of pain is good for the would-be person, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad, because there would be nobody to be deprived of that pleasure. We can see that this is right by looking at ordinary thought: people commonly treat the prospect of a child's suffering as a strong reason not to bring that child into existence, but they do not treat the loss of a happy life as a reason to regret failing to bring a child into existence. That feature of ordinary thought shows that the asymmetry reflects a genuine difference in value. Once we accept the asymmetry, never existing looks better than existing. Never existing contains a good, namely all the pain that is avoided, and no corresponding bad; existing, by contrast, always includes at least some pain. Therefore, coming into existence is always a harm relative to never existing.
