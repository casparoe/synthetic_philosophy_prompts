I'm in a distributive justice seminar and we're doing a unit on inheritance. I've been trying to use Mill's *Utilitarianism*, ch. 5 to think through an estate tax case, but I keep hitting a problem with the passage where he says:

"In the first place, it is mostly considered unjust to deprive any one of his personal liberty, his property, or any other thing which belongs to him by law. Here, therefore, is one instance of the application of the terms just and unjust in a perfectly definite sense, namely, that it is just to respect, unjust to violate, the legal rights of any one."

The case: a country passes a law that taxes estates above a threshold at 100%, with the revenue going into a fund that pays each citizen an equal lump sum at 18, specifically to stop dynastic wealth and equalize opportunity. Before the law, parents had a legal right to leave their entire business to their children and children had a legal right to receive it; the new law extinguishes those rights. So on Mill's own stated criterion, is the tax unjust? It doesn't violate any legal right in force because the law has already removed the right. But that makes it look like any legal change can make itself just by first extinguishing the right it violates. A few lines later Mill says the legal rights in question might be rights that ought not to have belonged to the person, because the law conferring them may be a bad law. But here the right was considered valid before the tax, and it's being extinguished by a democratic choice. Does the child or parent have a claim of justice that survives the change in the law, or does justice just track whatever legal rights exist plus utilitarian considerations? And does the equal-opportunity purpose of the tax matter for whether it's just, or is that a separate question?

I'd like a close reading of that passage and then an application to this tax case. Please seperate what's settled in the philosophical literature from what's genuinely contested among people who work on justice and inheritance, and don't present a contested reading of Mill as the consensus. If the tax case exposes a gap or ambiguity in Mill's criterion, just say so.
