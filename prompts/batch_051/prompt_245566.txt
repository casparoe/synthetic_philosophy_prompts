After a career in the physical sciences, I have recently been working through philosophical logic, and a derivation I was shown in a reading group still bothers me. It appears to prove, from premises that look almost impossible to deny, that everything that exists exists necessarily. I reproduce the steps as they were given to me:

(1) Necessarily, everything is self-identical: □∀x(x = x).
(2) The converse Barcan formula: for any predicate F, □∀xFx → ∀x□Fx. (Roughly: if necessarily everything is F, then each thing is necessarily F.)
(3) From (1) and (2): every object is necessarily self-identical, i.e. ∀x□(x = x).
(4) In ordinary first-order logic with identity, x = x entails ∃y(y = x): there is something identical to x. Since necessity is closed under logical consequence, from □(x = x) we may infer □∃y(y = x), and hence, generalizing, ∀x□∃y(y = x).
(5) But ∀x□∃y(y = x) says that, for every object, necessarily something identical to it exists. That is exactly the claim that everything exists necessarily.

That conclusion strikes me as wildly false: surely this table, and I myself, might have failed to exist. Yet I cannot locate the bad step. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it commit one to? I would most like to preserve ordinary quantifier logic, but I am not sure that is the least costly option; I would like the menu of possible responses and what each gives up.
