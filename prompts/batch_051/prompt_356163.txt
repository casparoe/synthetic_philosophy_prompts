I'm a novelist. I keep trying to turn a half-remembered philosophical idea into something I can use at my desk. The idea, as I've got hold of it: people owe each other a kind of legibility. Not full disclosure, not constant self-explanation—but a default against being needlessly opaque, against making yourself unreadable and then letting someone else carry the cost of not being able to read you. I want you to turn that into a decision procedure I can run on my fiction.

Because that's where it bites for me. Every scene, I choose how much of a character's inside the reader gets. First-person narrators especially: when they're evasive, am I doing it because the book has earned it, or because evasiveness flaters the prose and lets me dodge the hard work of knowing why someone did the thing? I don't need a theory lecture; I need a checklist. Something reusable across projects, not tuned to one manuscript.

So: give me a set of questions to run, in order, on a scene or a draft, that operationalises that duty to be legible. For each question, say what counts as a good enough answer. Say where the procedure says "withhold—this is fine," where it says "you're being opaque because it's easier or prettier, not because the reader is owed it," and where it should stop and hand the call back to me because it's a matter of values rather than procedure. Keep the presumption a presumption: don't turn it into "never withhold" or "always spell everything out." Be honest about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure.

In practice: I write a novel every couple of years, short stories in between, and these decisions come up most weeks while drafting and constantly in revision. I haven't read the philosophy and I won't learn the jargon. I'm usually under deadline. My editor signs off on the book but doesn't run my process, so the checklist has to be explainable to her in plain English.

Then run it on three cases, at least one of which should come out saying I shouldn't withhold even though I'll hate that answer: (1) a first-person narrator who waits until the last chapter to reveal he lied to the person the whole book is about—the twist is the point; (2) a third-person novel about two sisters who've become opaque to each other after an inheritance fight, where the narration itself withholds their motives to mirror the estrangement; (3) a short story with a deliberately evasive narrator that readers called masterful, but I suspect it's just fog and I got away with it.

Finally, tell me what this procedure can't do—where "is the book good" simply isn't reducible to legibility. And please write the answer in British English; my house style guide is sat on my desk glowering at me.
