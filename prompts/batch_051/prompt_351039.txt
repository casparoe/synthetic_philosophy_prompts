Ok, here's what I need. In my upper-level metaethics seminar I'm about to spend a week on the internal/external reasons debate, and I want a handout that gets students to stop throwing around one phrase like it settles everything. The phrase is: a reason has to be able to motivate you. (They also say it's "obvious," which is doing a lot of work by itself.)

The scene: last Tuesday a very confident student used that sentence to dismiss the idea that someone can have a reason to give to charity even if they don't currently care about it. The argument was roughly: reasons must be able to motivate you; a person who doesn't care about charity can't be motivated by charity; so they have no reason to give. And the room split on what the first premise even meant. Some students heard it as the boring point that reasons are the sort of thing that can move an agent at all. Others heard it as a Williams-style internalist claim: a reason for you has to be reachable by a sound deliberative route from your actual subjective motivational set. A few heard it as a much stronger rationalist claim, that a genuine reason must be one that any fully rational agent would be moved by — which, notice, doesn't obviously help the student's conclusion at all and might actually give the external reasons crowd what they want.

You are an expert metaethicist who works on reasons and motivation. Think carefully, and before you write anything polished, brainstorm a list of candidate precisifications first. Then produce roughly a 700-word handout in my voice: dry, a little exasperated, but philosophically rigorous. For the slogan "a reason has to be able to motivate you," lay out four or five distinct precise claims it could be making, and keep them seperate. For each one, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or just false, and whether that reading can actually do the work it was being made to do in the student's argument against external reasons. I want the students to see that the readings which are trivially true don't get him to his conclusion, while the reading that would get him there is controversial and needs an argument of its own. No citations or bibliography, please; I care about the arguments, not the name-dropping.
