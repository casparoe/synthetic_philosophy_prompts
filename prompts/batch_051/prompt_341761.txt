So I just spent three hours on this argumet with my friend about Molinism and middle knowledge, and he had me more off balance than I'd like. I was defending the usual story: in between God's natural knowledge of all possibilities and his free knowledge of what he actually decrees, there's this third level — middle knowledge — where God knows the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom: what every possible free creature would freely do in every possible situation. That's supposed to let God have meticulous providence without turning our free choices into determined ones.

My friend kept hammering the grounding objection. These counterfactuals of freedom are supposed to be true before God decides to create anything. So what makes them true? Not God's will — then they'd be part of his free knowledge and the "freedom" in them would be a sham. Not the creature — the creature doesn't exist yet, and since the choice is free, the creature's essence plus the circumstances doesn't determine the outcome. So you're left with brute, ungrounded facts sitting there before the decree, and God peeks at them. His line is that Molinism is a cheat: you keep providence and freedom only by renting a third floor of ungrounded facts.

Play him for me so I can work on my responses. He's sharp, reads enough open theism to be dangerous, but he's not a professional philosopher. He'll concede small points — he'll grant that middle knowledge gives a tidy picture of how God foreknows free choices, he'll even grant that not every true proposition needs a truthmaker in the crudest sense — but he will never grant that the grounding problem is actually dissolved. Don't strawman his side: when I make a weak Molinist move, punish it. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep replies to a paragraph or so so it feels like a real argument.

Here's my opening, the one that made him go quiet for a second but I'm not sure it actually lands:

Look, you act like counterfactuals of creaturely freedom need a truthmaker and none exists. But you'll happily allow that its a fact about a possible Rick that he could freely lie in situation S. That "could" fact is also true before the decree, it's also not a fact about the actual world, and it doesn't have a truthmaker either — it's a modal fact about Rick's possible essence, or a primitive modal fact, whatever you want to call it. Middle knowledge just adds the "would" fact: which of the two he would do. So why does "would" need a truthmaker when "could" doesn't? Unless you're a truthmaker maximalist about every last truth, the grounding objection doesn't get off the ground without special pleading.
