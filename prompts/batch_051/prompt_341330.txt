I've been thinking a lot about an analogy a Buddhist teacher I listen to keeps using for no-self, and I can't tell if it's doing real work or quietly winning by wordplay. I haven't studied any of this formally, I just listen to talks and chew on them. He says: take a chariot. It has wheels, an axle, a pole, a seat. Each is a part of the chariot, but none of them is the chariot, and after you've gone through all the parts there's no extra thing called "chariot" left over — its just a convenient label for the parts arranged a certain way. Then he says a person is the same: body, feelings, perceptions, habits and volitions, consciousness. None of those is the self, and there's no separate thing standing behind them, so "self" is just a conventional label too. So the person isn't really there; there's no self over and above the parts.

When I hear it, it feels airtight. But then I poke at it. A chariot doesn't experience the ride; the person is the one who experiences it. No one blames a chariot when it crashes, and the chariot isn't aware of owning its parts. Also, chariots get their arrangement from a builder with a purpose, but a person's arrangement isn't chosen by anyone unless you've already assumed a self, which is the very thing that was supposed to be shown. Are these real cracks, or am I just resisting a conclusion I don't like?

Go through what maps to what between the chariot and the person, and say what the analogy has to quietly import from chariots for the no-self conclusion to go through. Then tell me whether the conclusion — that the self is only a conventional label and not really there — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the chariot was carrying the whole argument. And tell me what would change your mind about that verdict: which part of your reasoning is most likely to break, and what consideration, if it turned out to be right, would flip you the other way.
