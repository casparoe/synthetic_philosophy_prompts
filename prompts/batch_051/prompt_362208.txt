I've been re-reading Robert Bolt's A Man for All Seasons (going back and forth between the play and the Paul Scofield film) because I'm teaching a unit on conscience and compromise, and I'm worried I'm going to end up using it as a flat illustration of the man of principle and then just move on. The play wont quite let me do that. More is praised, and also resented, for being the person who can't be bought or moved: he won't take money, won't pin his conscience to whatever the king wants, won't even save himself by keeping quiet when a little strategic silence would do. But Bolt is honestly clear about what that costs Alice and Meg, and about More getting colder and more alone the more unbending he becomes, so the play isn't simply cheering him on.

What I'd like the essay assignment to work toward is a distinction between being incorruptible in the narrow sense — not for sale, not bribable, not the sort of person who does wrong out of fear or advancement — and a broader sense where people use incorruptible to mean someone who will not bend or compromise on any point of conscience whatsoever. Read the play through that distinction. Does it keep the two senses apart, or does More's refusal to be bought quietly get turned into a justification for his refusal to bend at all? And does the play actually treat incorruptibility as an unqualified good, or does it leave room for the thought that a person this rigid becomes a kind of tyrant over the people who depend on him, or that his purity is partly self-regard? I'd rather have a reading that goes both ways than one that hands students a verdict — a reading where the play genuinely complicates the praise it also wants to give him.
