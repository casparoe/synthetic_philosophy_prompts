In my philosophy of language seminar we've been going in circles on empty names for weeks and I'm starting to think the positions aren't actually answering each other. The standoff is this: one camp says "Sherlock Holmes" has no referent, so a sentence like "Holmes smokes a pipe" is literally false or truth-valueless; another camp says Holmes is an abstract artifact created by Conan Doyle, so the sentence is just plain true; and a third keeps insisting it's true in the stories but not true simpliciter, at which point the first camp retorts that the distinction does no work or is only restating their view. Everyone agrees on what's written in the stories and that no flesh-and-blood Holmes exists, and yet nobody's moved in a month. My hunch is the real fault line lies in whether "true in the fiction" is a genuine truth predicate relativized to a story-world or just an operator that reports what the story prescribes, with the rest downstream of that—but I'm not confident. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict: is this a verbal dispute about the word "true," a substantive dispute about whether abstract artifacts exist, a dispute about which bits of ordinary talk the semantics has to respect, or are the sides answering different questions under one label? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—a distinction, a formal result, attention to how people actually talk, or narrowing the question until each side is just right about its own question?
