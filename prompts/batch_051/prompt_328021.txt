Design a 60-minute classroom game for 16 undergrads in a social epistemology seminar. The takeaway they should leave with: a karma/reputation system can drift from rewarding good contributions to rewarding optimized popularity, even when nobody sets out to game it. Don't let the facilitator name that upfront — build it into the rules so participants run into it themselves. Here's the dynamic I want them to collide with: once karma buys credibility, there's pressure to farm it with safe, crowd-pleasing contributions, and people who spend earned karma on risky or unpopular takes end up with less influence than people who just play the token game well. Use physical tokens for karma, with rules for earning, spending, and cashing out into small real stakes (say, deciding how a shared snack gets split). Specify the roles, rules, a 10-minute setup, the timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the idea. Then flag what predictably goes wrong when it's actually run, and be honest about what the game can't reproduce about real reputation economies — long histories, durable identities, stakes that actually hurt.
