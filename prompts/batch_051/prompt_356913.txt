I've spent years reading rationalist-blog discussions of Newcomb's problem and formal epistemology, and I want help with a variant I haven't seen treated carefully. Take the standard setup: Box A is transparent and contains $1,000; Box B is opaque and contains $1,000,000 iff a Predictor, who filled it in advance, predicted that you would take only Box B. You may take either both boxes or only Box B. In the familiar precise version, suppose you have a single credence r in the Predictor being correct about your act—so P(predicted one-box | you one-box) = r and P(predicted two-box | you two-box) = r, with the opposite outcomes receiving 1 − r. Then evidential decision theory one-boxes when r is above its payoff threshold and two-boxes when r is below it, while causal decision theory two-boxes at every value of r.

Now vary the setup by making your evidence about the Predictor's reliability imprecise. Suppose you know only that r lies in the interval [0.49, 0.52], and that you cannot defensibly narrow this interval or put a precise distribution over it: your credal state is the full set of probability functions with r in that range. The expected utilities under evidential decision theory then become intervals, and the standard criteria for choice under imprecise probabilities—Γ-maximin (maximize worst-case expected utility), maximality (avoid acts whose expected utility under every permissible r is strictly below that of some alternative), E-admissibility (choose acts that are expected-utility optimal under at least one permissible precise distribution), and interval dominance—appear to point in different directions.

Please answer as a professional decision theorist with expertise in imprecise probability. Work through, step by step, what evidential decision theory and causal decision theory each say about this imprecise-Newcomb problem, and where exactly the interval-valued analysis departs from the precise version. Then I want you to commit: in this variant, should a rational agent one-box or two-box, and which rule for choice under imprecise probabilities should a decision theorist adopt to justify that answer? Don't just list the options or say that it depends on the choice of rule; take a definite position and defend it. Before your final answer, start with a short brainstorming paragraph about how one ought to handle overlapping interval-valued expected utilities in a Newcomb-like decision.
