I'm a machine learning engineer with a working command of tokenization, byte-pair encoding, sequence modeling, entropy and coding length, grapheme-to-phoneme conversion, and the vocab-size-versus-sequence-length tradeoff. I have no formal training in linguistics. I keep hearing that a writing system is not a neutral transcription of speech but something more like a theory of the language: an alphabet treats the phoneme as the unit that matters, a syllabary treats the syllable as the unit, a logography treats the morpheme or word as the unit, and a featural script such as Hangul treats articulatory features as the unit. I also keep hearing that alphabets can be more or less phonemic—that Finnish is shallow, with graphemes mapping to phonemes almost one-to-one, while English is deep, preserving morphemes and etymology at the cost of regular sound-to-symbol correspondence.

Restate this position in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can. Translate the choice of script type into the choice of tokenization granularity; translate orthographic depth into properties of the text-to-sound mapping—how regular the function is, how much context a decoder needs, how much information is left to morphology or the lexicon rather than the symbol inventory; and translate the phonemic ideal of a one-to-one grapheme-to-phoneme correspondence into an engineering claim about the right representation. Do not settle for analogy: give the systematic translation, and if my vocabulary makes some distinctions invisible, say so flatly rather than papering over them.

Then give me the residue report. What maps cleanly? What did you have to distort? What has no expression at all in my vocabulary? In particular, tell me whether the residue points to limitations of my vocabulary or to confusions in the original claims—for example, whether phonemic is one dimension or several masquerading as one, and whether the idea that a script reveals the real units of the language survives the translation at all. Finish with a verdict: is my vocabulary a better theory of what a script is doing than the linguistics one, is it worse, or are they answering different questions?
