I'm a novelist. I'm working on a book whose whole plot runs on a chain of coincidences, and the more I look at my source material the less sure I am about when a coincidence should make a character — or me — conclude that something beyond chance is going on. I keep a notebook of real-life cases that I've collected for the book, along with my gut verdicts, and I'd like you to reverse-engineer whatever rule I'm secretly using, if there is one. Here's the current list:

- A room of twenty-three people, and two of them share a birthday. Verdict: mildly surprising the first time, but not meaningful; I know the math says shared birthdays are more likely than not at that number.
- I'm thinking about a friend I havent spoken to in years, and at that exact moment she texts me. Verdict: it feels spooky while it's happening, but I remind myself how many idle thoughts I have and how many texts I get. I land on "not meaningful," but with low confidence.
- A woman I interviewed dreamt her brother had a car crash on a particular winding road, and that same night he had a near-miss on exactly that road. Verdict: this one gets to me. I'd call it meaningful, or at least too much to shrug off.
- A man dreams a plane crashes, and a plane crashes somewhere in the world the next day. Verdict: not meaningful. With that many dreamers and that many crashes, somebody was bound to draw the lucky match.
- Same man, but the dream names the city, the airline, and one distinctive detail — a yellow bird painted on the tail — and two days later a crash in that city matches all of it. Verdict: I cant write this one off like the generic dream. It feels too specific to be chance, and I'd treat it as meaningful.
- A woman wins the lottery and says she dreamt those exact numbers the night before. Verdict: not meaningful at all. Somebody has to win, and the winner will always have some kind of story. But I notice this has the same shape as the specific plane dream, and I don't like that I feel so differently about them.
- A friend claimed after a market crash that he'd seen it coming. Looking back at what he actually wrote beforehand, it's all stuff like "a correction is due eventually." Verdict: not meaningful; the specifics seem to arrive only after the event.

Is there one principle — something about base rates, the number of opportunities, how specific a prediction is, whether it was fixed in advance, and whether the matching outcome is one we'd have noticed anyway — that captures all or most of these? Which of my verdicts does it force me to abandon, and what would it predict for the case I keep going back and forth on: I think about my sister many times a week, and one afternoon a thought about her is interrupted by a call saying she's been in an accident that hour.
