I have been thinking about what it means to understand a mathematical proof, as opposed to merely being able to check or recreate the steps. I have sketched a short essay that approaches the question by analogy with software, and I would like comments on whether the argument holds up. I do not have a background in philosophy, so I would particularly value comments on whether I am missing an obvious objection or mischaracterizing the distinction.

The essay draft is below.

Mathematicians often distinguish between being able to follow a proof and being able to understand it. A student can verify that each line follows from the previous one, and can even reproduce the proof on an exam, while still feeling — and being told — that they have not understood it. What is the missing ingredient?

I suspect it is the same ingredient that separates knowing that a program passes its tests from understanding the program. A developer can confirm that every function satisfies its specification and still have no real grip on the codebase: they cannot predict what a small change will do, explain why a given data structure was chosen over an alternative, or say which component would break under a new requirement. Passing tests are evidence that the program currently works, but they do not by themselves constitute an understanding of how it works.

My suggestion is that understanding a proof works in the same way. To understand a proof is not to hold all of its steps in mind at once, and it is not merely to be able to certify each step. It is to be able to modify the proof and anticipate the consequences: to relax a hypothesis and see where the argument first fails, to tighten a bound and know which inequality takes the hit, or to carry the method over to a neighboring theorem and know in advance which lemma needs rework. That is the same competence an engineer shows by refactoring a module, extending it under conditions the original author did not anticipate, and debugging it when it breaks.

I can see at least three objections. First, this may make understanding sound too behavioral: someone might learn to make the right modifications without any felt sense of seeing why the proof works. Second, the view may leave out the aesthetic or explanatory side of understanding, the sense in which a good proof shows not just that something is true but why it must be true. Third, the analogy may break down because a proof is not executed the way a program is. When a program goes wrong, something happens at runtime; when a proof goes wrong, a step simply fails to follow. The corresponding what-if tests may therefore be importantly different in kind.

I would welcome comments on the overall analogy, on whether the proposed criterion of understanding is too weak or too strong, and on which of the three objections seems most damaging — or whether a different objection matters more.
