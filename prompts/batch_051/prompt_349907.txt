I'm putting together an intro linguistics unit and I want a handout that works like a "which position do you actually hold?" quiz, but on a question that keeps triping up my students: what it actually takes for something to count as a language, and whether sign languages are languages in exactly the same sense as spoken ones. Students usually arrive with a half-formed idea that language just means speech, then they run into ASL and homesign and they don't know how to update. So I'd like you to build me a branching decision tree, or a short questionnaire with a scoring key, whichever comes out cleaner, that sorts people into positions on this. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of labels: each branch should be a yes/no question about a concrete claim or case that someone can answer without already knowing the terms of art. Work these cases in somewhere as the diagnostic items: a deaf child raised without access to an established sign language who develops a home sign system; Nicaraguan Sign Language emerging among schoolchildren; a fluent ASL signer producing a sentence with verb agreement and classifier constructions; a written English text; and a parrot that can produce and recombine word-like sounds. The angle I care about is how much work modality does versus how much work structure does: is the obstacle to counting sign languages just the channel, or do they fail some structural test that spoken languages pass. The leaves I'd like to see are roughly the old "language = speech" view, the view that sign languages are full languages but writing is a derivative representation, the view that the crucial thing is a shared grammar/convention transmitted across a community, a nativist view that requires hierarchical syntax or recursion, and a loose communicative view on which the parrot might count for something. But don't force leaves if the questions don't produce them: mark unoccupied or incoherent combinations and say briefly why nobody lives there. And flag any two positions your tree genuinely can't separate. Keep it to seven or eight leaves max, since it's for one class session.
