I have recently been seconded from our national audit office to the cabinet of a small state that is weighing whether to join a regional customs union, and I have been asked to produce the framing paper on what membership would mean for our sovereignty. Every working document I read uses the vocabulary differently: one ministry writes that sovereignty would be pooled in the common external tariff; another insists it would be merely delegated and reclaimable at will; a third says the distinction is empty, because what matters is whether we have a seat at the table when the union negotiates trade agreements with third countries. I do not want to read another theory of sovereignty until I know what I am asking of one.

Please write the desiderata that an adequate account of sovereignty under membership arrangements must meet. The purpose is specific and I would like you to hold the list to it: the account must be good enough to guide a real accession decision, and to underwrite truthful statements afterwards about what the state has retained, what it has transferred, and what it has made costly or conditional. I want the full specification: the cases it must handle (a simple free trade area with rules-of-origin certificates; a customs union with a common external tariff and joint trade-negotiating machinery; a confederation with common defence commitments and a court whose rulings bind member states; an association agreement that copies most of a union's rules without giving the state a vote in making them), the distinctions it cannot collapse (for example between holding a competence and being able to exercise it unilaterally; between revocable delegation and genuinely pooled decision-making; between a legal right to withdraw and a realistic ability to do so), and the operational facts it cannot contradict (border checks that move outward; tariff schedules that become non-negotiable at home; dispute rulings implemented without a domestic vote). For each entry tell me the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of one camp, since a defender of parliamentary supremacy will demand something different from a defender of popular sovereignty. Tell me which single requirement knocks out the most common ways of talking about membership, and flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Please be committed: give me your definite list and your reasons, not a survey of possible lists.
