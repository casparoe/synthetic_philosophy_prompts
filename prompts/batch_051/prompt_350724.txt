Consider a jurisdiction that replaces its ordinary permitting, nuisance, and environmental-tort rules with a market in transferable risk credits. Each resident receives an annual endowment of 1,000 credits; one credit represents an entitlement not to be subjected, during that year, to an activity that a regulator's risk model estimates will raise the holder's probability of dying by one in a million. Any operator—trucking, industrial emissions, building materials, mass-distributed food—whose modeled activity would impose more than one credit of mortality risk on a resident in a year must buy that many credits from that resident, or from an earlier buyer. Exposures below one credit are unregulated; buying credits immunizes the operator for the modeled ex ante risk, while grossly negligent modeling remains tortious; credits expire at year's end; and risks imposed on minors and future persons are charged against credits held by a public trustee.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral position. Work through what it does to who bears risk and at what price; where the de minimis threshold is gamed first by many small emitters each staying just below it; how the divergence between modeled and actual risk creates secondary markets in modeling, monitoring, and insurance; how the trustee behaves; and what failure modes cannot be fixed by adjusting the number of credits or adding safeguards. End with a claim about whether the scheme, once its loopholes are visible, still tracks anything like consent.
