My philosophy class has been reading Cicero’s *Cato Maior de Senectute* (*On Old Age*), and I keep getting stuck on two passages that seem to pull in opposite directions. Early in the dialogue, Cato lays out the standard case against growing old. He says that when he reflects on why old age appears unhappy, he finds four reasons: “first, that it withdraws us from active pursuits; second, that it makes the body weaker; third, that it deprives us of almost all physical pleasures; and, fourth, that it is not far removed from death.” So the first criticism is that old age takes us out of genuine activity. But later he attacks people who say old age is devoid of useful activity. He compares an old man to a pilot who sits quietly in the stern holding the tiller while the younger crew members climb masts and work the pumps, and then he says: “It is not by muscle, speed, or physical dexterity that great things are achieved, but by reflection, force of character, and judgement; in these qualities old age is usually not only not poorer, but is even richer.”

These two passages strike me as at least superficially incompatible. Is Cato answering the worry that old age withdraws us from active pursuits, or is he quietly redefining “active” so that the worry no longer applies? Could the first passage mean only that old age withdraws us from the particular activities that depend on youth and bodily vigor, while the second passage is meant to show that the most important activities remain? Or is there a stronger reading on which he is really admitting a loss and then arguing that the loss is less important than we think? I’d like you to explain what reconciliation, if any, makes the best sense of the text, and to say which readings would be honest to the argument and which would be special pleading. Please answer in American English.
