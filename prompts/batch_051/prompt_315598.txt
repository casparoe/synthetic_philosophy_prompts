In The Future of an Illusion, Freud appears to hold two positions that sit uneasily together. In one strand of argument, he insists that the central claims of religion are not straightforwardly refutable, because they are illusions: they derive their power from the most persistent and powerful wishes of humanity rather than from evidence, and reasoned argument therefore has little purchase on them. In another strand, he nonetheless presses the case that these religious ideas ought to be relinquished, and that civilisation would be healthier if people faced reality without them.

Are these two strands actually in tension? If a body of ideas cannot be refuted by evidence in the ordinary way, on what grounds can Freud legitimately call for its abandonment? Distinguish the candidate reconciliations—for example, an epistemic criticism versus a practical one, an argument from the motives for belief rather than from the content of belief, or a claim about intellectual honesty rather than about falsity—and say which reading makes the book most defensible, and which readings amount to special pleading.
