I'm a PhD student in information studies, working on a chapter about whether personal data can be treated as property, and I've realized I keep using the phrase "your data" without being able to say what makes a piece of information yours. People seem to mean several different things at once: information you generated, information about you, information you control, information whose use should require your permission. I'm not sure these pick out the same set of things, and I'd like to know whether the phrase has one defensible core or is doing two jobs that need to be pulled apart.

Give me a working definition of when a piece of information belongs to a person, in the sense that would make it apt to call it "their data" and to think others normally need their permission to use it. Test it against these cases, with my rough intuitions in parentheses:

1. A diary entry I wrote on my own laptop and never shared. (clearly mine)
2. Security-camera footage of me entering a store at 4:12 pm, recorded and stored by the store. (it's of me, but not obviously mine)
3. A bank's transaction record showing my card purchases for the month. (I generated the activity; the bank generated the record)
4. The fact that I carry a particular rare genetic variant, considered apart from any test anyone has run. (a fact about my body—can facts like that be owned?)
5. An inference made by a retailer's model that I am probably pregnant, based on my purchases and evryone else's. (the retailer created it, but it's about me)
6. My legal name and current address as they appear in a public county database. (clearly personal data under a privacy law, but not something I control)
7. An anonymized aggregate statistic, "81% of respondents own a car," from a survey I answered. (nothing about me remains identifiable)
8. A 300-dimensional embedding an ad network computed from my browsing history. (derived from my behavior, but I don't know it exists)

I'm confident about 1 and 7; the rest I keep flipping on. Run the definition against each case, say where it gives an answer that feels wrong or no answer at all, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdict. If you think the term splits into two—say, a descriptive sense and an entitlement sense—define each strand and note which cases move between them.
