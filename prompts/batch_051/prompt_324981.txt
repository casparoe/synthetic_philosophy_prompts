I am a graduate student sitting on my university's graduate admissions review committee, and after this year's cycle we were sent aggregate data showing noticeably different offer rates for male and female applicants. Before we had even seen the breakdowns, a colleague waved the gap away as an artifact, invoking the Berkeley graduate admissions case of 1973 without actually explaining how the analogy is supposed to apply to us. I know the rough contours of that case: the aggregate figures seemed to show bias against women, with something like 44 percent of male applicants admitted against 35 percent of female applicants, but when the numbers were disaggregated by department the disparity mostly vanished, because women were applying disproportionately to departments with lower admit rates across the board. What I would like is a careful analysis of the Berkeley episode through the lens of Simpson's paradox.

Specifically, I want to know where in that data the reversal or attenuation actually comes from, what structural features of aggregation make Simpson's paradox possible here, and what rigorous conclusions the Berkeley case does and does not license about whether the apparent bias was real. Then I want the analysis turned around: what does this episode show about Simpson's paradox itself? Does it teach that aggregate statistics are routinely misleading and should always be mistrusted, or does it also teach that disaggregation can be used too quickly to explain away genuine discrimination, especially when the variable one disaggregates by, like department choice, may itself be shaped by the very bias one is investigating? Finally, since our committee has not yet disaggregated this year's data, I want to know what the Simpson's paradox lens would force me to concede about the aggregate gap, what it would leave open, and how I should argue about the numbers before and after we see the department-level breakdowns.
