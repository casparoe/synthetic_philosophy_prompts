I write about science and ideas for a living, and I'm working on a long piece about how much of our bad luck we should have to carry ourselves. I've been reading around in luck egalitarianism, and before I settle on a throughline I want a consistency check on my own working beliefs, because I keep defending each one separately and I'm starting to worry they don't all cohere. Here's the set:

1. When two people make the same choices and one ends up far worse off for reasons neither chose — a congenital illness, a random accident, a factory closing in one town and not the next — that gap is exactly what justice is for, and society should transfer resources to close it.

2. When someone knowingly takes a gamble and loses, the outcome is not the same kind of injustice. A lifelong smoker who gets lung cancer is in a different position from a nonsmoker who gets the same cancer, even if the suffering is the same.

3. But I also don't think the imprudent should be left to bare the full costs of their choices. A health system that quietly lets people die of emphysema because "they smoked" is cruel in a way that no theory of justice should be able to endorse.

4. I'm committed to the chosen/unchosen disadvantage distinction being a real feature of the world, not a projection of our politics: in principle we could sort cases into the two piles without first deciding how much we like the people involved or how risk-averse we happen to be.

5. I also believe our choices are themselves made from traits and circumstances we never chose — temperament, willpower, all of it — so any choice, looked at closely, bottoms out in unchosen material.

I don't need a survey of the field; I need an audit of this specific set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look that way, and can you rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them flat? For each tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest coherent revision of my view would be, and what that revision costs. Tell me also where your verdict is least secure: which argument or case detail, if it broke a certain way, would change the ranking or erase a tension you found.
