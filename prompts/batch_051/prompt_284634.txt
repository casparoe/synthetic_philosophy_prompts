An old friend and I keep returning to a disagreement about whether a quiet, routine-bound life — same work, same town, same close friends — can be among the best lives open to a person. She consistently reaches for the proverb that 'variety is the spice of life' and then moves, as though the matter were settled, to the conclusion that a life without novelty is bland and therefore defective as a life. I am convinced the proverb bundles several different claims and that she is sliding among them, but I want the possible precisifications on the table before I answer her. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct claims that 'variety is the spice of life' could be making about the place of novelty in well-being. For each reading, say whether it is a trivial claim of descriptive psychology or aggregate preference, a substantive and defensible claim about happiness or flourishing, or an overstatement; then say whether that reading actually supports her conclusion that a quiet life cannot be fully good. In particular, I want to separate at least these candidates: novelty as a source of passing stimulation or relief from boredom, novelty as an instrument for slowing hedonic adaptation, and novelty as a constituent of well-being in its own right — but please supply any further readings you can distinguish, including more radical ones such as a claim that a good life requires variety or that the radically unadventurous are making an error, rather than restricting yourself to those three.
