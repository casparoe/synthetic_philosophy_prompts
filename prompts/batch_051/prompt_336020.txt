I am a senior risk analyst at a regional electric utility. Twice a week I issue probabilistic forecasts for high-impact events: whether a heat wave will force rolling load-shedding within 72 hours, whether a substation will flood during a storm, whether a supply disruption will force interruptible customers to be cut off. The operations director uses my number the same day to decide whether to buy emergency power, pre-position repair crews, or issue public conservation alerts. The stakes are usually in the mid six figures and sometimes much larger. I have about an hour to produce each forecast, I am numerate but have no philosophical training, and my number has to be defensible to the operations director and, occasionally, to regulators.

The recurring difficulty is this. By tonight or tomorrow morning, after the overnight model suite and weather runs, my future self will assign a different probability to the same event. Formal epistemology has a principle — I gather it is called the reflection principle — which says that a rational agent's present probability for an event, conditional on the supposition that her future self will assign probability r to that event, should be r. In idealized conditions, my later self is an expert I should defer to, and my current number should already reflect whatever I predict my later number will be. But real later selves are messier. A later self may be better informed, or merely later. It may be sleep-deprived, working from a model that overreacts to recent events, missing an on-the-ground detail I know now but will have forgotten, or under pressure from the control-room narrative. Sometimes I can already predict the direction my later self will move, and not because of new evidence.

I want a reusable decision procedure for deciding, on any given forecast day, how much my present probability should be constrained by the probability I expect my future self to hold. The deliverable should be an ordered set of tests or a checklist in words that I can run top to bottom. Before the tests, it should say what deferring to the future self would mean operationally — adopt the expected future number, average with it, bound it, or something else — and under what conditions each is appropriate. The procedure should specify what counts as a good enough answer to each test, where it stops with "use today's number," where it stops with "adopt the future number," and where it stops with "no rule here, this is a judgment call." It should also say what to do when I cannot get a definite answer to one of the tests, and it should mark honestly which steps are genuine judgment calls dressed up as tests. It should be usable in about thirty minutes beyond the hour I already spend on the forecast, using only materials I already have: my own past calibration records, the model outputs, my contemporaneous notes, and the current control-room narrative.

Then run the procedure on these three cases.

Case 1. I currently assign 40% to load-shedding by Friday. I expect tomorrow morning, after a model suite whose outputs I have historically found worth waiting for, I will assign about 70%. My calibration records show that my tomorrow-morning updates after this suite have been better calibrated than my evening-before numbers, and I have no reason to think tomorrow's conditions will be unusual.

Case 2. I currently assign 30% to a particular substation flooding in an incoming storm. I am about to hand over to a version of myself who will have slept four hours, is under internal scrutiny after a previous flooding event, and tends under fatigue to raise probabilities defensively. I also have a note in front of me — a drainage inspection detail I expect my later self to forget, and which points against flooding by roughly ten points.

Case 3. I currently assign 55% to a gas-supply disruption next month. I expect my evening self to assign 45% after the evening operations brief. My records show that my evening updates after that brief are neither better nor worse calibrated than my afternoon numbers. I would like to adopt the evening number because it would make the next morning's review easier for me, but I cannot point to any way in which the evening self is more expert.

Finally, tell me explicitly what the procedure cannot do — in particular, whether it carries over to preferences and values and not just probabilities — and where you expect it to misfire or be gamed.
