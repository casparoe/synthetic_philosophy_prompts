I am currently taking an upper-level aesthetics course, and the topic my study group has found most difficult to get straight is aesthetic testimony: roughly, whether one person's aesthetic judgments can justify belief or confer knowledge on another person, or whether achieving either requires first-hand experience of the work. I would like to make a sorting quiz for the group, so that we locate our actual positions by how we answer concrete cases rather than by which label sounds best to us.

Please write a quiz of eight to ten questions with the title "Which position on aesthetic testimony do you actually hold?" Each question should present a concrete case involving someone forming, reporting, or acting on an aesthetic judgment on the basis of what another person says, and should offer two or three answer options. Each option should be something a person in that case would naturally say, do, or infer. No question stem and no answer option may mention a position label or ask respondents to classify themselves.

The quiz should be designed to sort respondents into at least these five positions:

1. Strong pessimism: testimony about a work's aesthetic merit can justify only second-order beliefs, such as the belief that the speaker judged the work good; no first-order aesthetic judgment about the work can be justified or known without first-hand acquaintance with the work.

2. Belief without assertion: testimony can justify coarse first-order aesthetic beliefs when those beliefs are needed for practical decisions about what to experience, but it cannot supply aesthetic knowledge or entitle the recipient to assert the aesthetic judgment in their own voice.

3. Moderate optimism: reliable testimony can justify coarse first-order aesthetic beliefs, but first-hand experience is still required for fine-grained appreciation and for the kind of explanatory understanding that lets someone defend the judgment.

4. Robust optimism: aesthetic testimony is not epistemically special, so a reliable judge's say-so can, under the same conditions that make ordinary factual testimony trustworthy, yield knowledge and, provided the recipient attributes the judgment honestly, a license to assert it.

5. Non-cognitivism: aesthetic utterances express taste or attitude rather than report taste-independent aesthetic facts, so the question of transmitting aesthetic knowledge is partly miscast and should be reframed in terms of influence, recommendation, and convergence.

The diagnostic questions are the important part. The cases should vary along these fault lines: whether testimony can support a first-order judgment or only a second-order one; whether justified belief, knowledge, and first-person assertion set progressively higher bars; whether the source matters only for its reliability or in a way that depends on the relationship to the respondent; whether coarse judgments and fine-grained appreciative judgments can come apart; and whether a conflict between one's own direct experience and strong critical consensus should be resolved by deference, by retaining one's own judgment, or by treating the disagreement as non-factual. At least one question should use a case in which the respondent's own experience conflicts with a large critical consensus, and at least one should use a case in which someone must choose what to experience under time pressure with only second-hand information available. The remaining cases are yours to design, but no two questions should probe the same distinction in the same way.

Please also provide a full scoring key. It should explain which questions discriminate which fault lines, how to score mixed response patterns, and how much weight each answer carries toward each position. For any stable combination of answers that suggests a coherent position but is not among the five above, say briefly whether you take that combination to be incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored. Finally, identify any pair of positions the quiz cannot reliably separate, and explain what further question would be needed to distinguish them.
