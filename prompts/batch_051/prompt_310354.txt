I retired a few years ago after a long career in zoology, mostly doing systematics, so I'm used to treating "is an X a Y?" as a question with a real answer — set membership, shared ancestry, that kind of thing. Since retiring ive been reading philosophy, and right now I'm stuck on the old School of Names line from the white horse dialogue: "a white horse is not a horse." A friend of mine has adopted it as a conversation-ender about classification. Last month he used it to close down an argument about whether viruses are alive; before that it was whether Pluto is a planet. His point, as far as I can tell, is that categories are just verbal conventions and membership isnt a feature of the world, so these debates are all just words.

Heres my problem. Taken as a claim about horses the line looks flatly false — a white horse is a horse, any seven-year-old could tell you that. Taken as a claim about language it looks boringly true — obviously the phrase "white horse" is not the phrase "horse." Neither reading explains why the line earned a whole dialogue. A friend who reads some Chinese adds that the word translated "is not" could also mean something like "is not identical with," which only deepens my confusion about what exactly is being asserted. I'd like you to lay out the distinct theses the slogan could be expressing — about qualifiers and nouns, about the extension of a phrase versus its meaning, about identity conditions, about whether "horse" picks out a kind or a stuff or a shape — and for each say whether its trivial, substantive and defensible, or flatly false as a claim about actual horses. And then: which of these readings, if any, would actually do the work my friend wants done in the virus and Pluto cases? I want a menu to bring back to him, not a single interpretation.
