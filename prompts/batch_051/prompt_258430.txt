My ethics professor has been going on about Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason, and when I reconstruct the argument underneath it, it seems to prove too much, but I cant find the step that's doing the damage. I ran it by my study group and nobody could agree on where it slips. Here's the version I wrote down:

(1) If an act maximizes my own happiness, then it's what I have most reason to do. Call this the egoist axiom. (2) If an act maximizes everyone's happiness considered impartially, then it's what I have most reason to do. Call this the utilitarian axiom. (3) Each of these looks like a fundamental, self-evident requirement of practical reason — neither seems derivable from the other, and neither is a mere rule of thumb. (4) "What I have most reason to do" cannot be two incompatible actions in the very same situation; if A is the thing to do, then a rival B isn't also the thing to do. (5) But the axioms can pull apart: suppose I can choose A, which leaves me with a moderate benefit and leaves everyone else exactly as they were, or B, which sacrifices that benefit but gives a slightly larger combined benefit to two other people. My own happiness is higher under A, while total happiness is higher under B, so (1) says A and (2) says B. (6) Therefore, in that situation, I have most reason to do A and I have most reason to do B — which contradicts (4).

The professor takes this to show that (4) has to go: practical reason is "dual," with two independent sources that can issue rival verdicts, so there's no guaranteed all-things-considered answer to "what ought I to do." But that sounds to me like admitting that in an ordinary choice the true answer could be both "you ought to do A" and "you ought to do B," where A and B can't both be done. That's not a dualism, it's just an inconsistent picture of reasoning. Where does the argument actually go wrong — if it does? If it doesn't go wrong, which bullet are we biting, and is there a way to keep something like (4) while still doing justice to both axioms? For what it's worth, I'd rather give up (1) than (2) — maybe my own good only makes a claim on me from a perspective I don't have to take up — but I don't see how to say that without making self-interest rationally irrelevant.
