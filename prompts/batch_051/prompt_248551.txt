The city I live in is finally dealing with something it buried a century ago, and I've been put on the commission that has to decide what to do about it. When the municipal archive was digitized a few years back, it became public and uncontested that in 1911 the city council evicted the Black families of a neighborhood called the Flats, condemned the houses as unsanitary, and sold the land to a rail company, keeping the proceeds. Nobody involved is alive, and the current council knew nothing until the archive was released. The evicted families' descendants have petitioned for a formal city apology plus a housing-assistance fund. The commission has spent four meetings going back and forth. Some of us think a present-day apology would be incoherent: the council today is just a different set of people, and you can't apologize for deeds you had no part in. Others think the city is the same continuing legal entity that did it, the way a corporation can apologize for conduct predating every current employee, and that what's being offered is not personal guilt but institutional repudiation. A third group insists the apology only makes sense paired with the fund, because by itself it would just be cheap words. And a couple of us keep returning to who the apology would even be addressed to, given the actual victims are gone and the descendants are not the same persons. I need to circulate a short written opinion before this Thursdays vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action — apologize or not, on what terms, with or without the fund — and it should take the strongest argument on the losing side seriously rather than wave it away.
