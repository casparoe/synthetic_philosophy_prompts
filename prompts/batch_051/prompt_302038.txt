i just spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether discounting the future is rational, and i walked away with a pile of verdicts that dont obviously hang together. his move was roughly: if i'd take $100 today over $103 next year with no inflation and no chance of not being paid, then i'm already discounting; and once you discount at even a couple percent a year, a life in 2124 might as well be worthless. so either the far future matters or it doesn't. i said it obviously matters, but i couldn't say why my impatience doesn't leak over into that.

here's what i actually feel, case by case. (1) i'd take the $100 now over the $103 next year, guaranteed; it isn't about investing it, i just want it sooner. (2) but a life saved today versus a life saved in a hundred years, same certainty, feels like a tie — time shouldn't make the life smaller. (3) i don't think a government should count future climate deaths as less important just because they're future. (4) i do count a benefit two hundred years off as worth less in expectation, but only because i'm not sure civilization will still be around to receive it; stipulate that we'll be there and the discount disappears, so that's about probability, not about later people mattering less. (5) if i could spend a million now to save ten lives now, or invest it and save twenty lives in thirty years, i'd pick the twenty. later wins because twenty is more than ten, not because later matters less. (6) but i'll also put off the dentist when i'm told it'll hurt more later, and take the cake tonight while telling myself i'll eat clean tomorrow. those feel like akrasia, not like a principle i'd sign.

so is there one coherent account that saves all six, maybe by splitting pure time preference off from uncertainty and opportunity cost, and treating my private impatience as a self-control failure with no claim to being a principled discount rate? or do i have to give one of them up, and if so which goes first? and whatever view comes out, how should i think about the case i'm actually stuck on: spending a lot now to prevent a statistical death eighty years out, when i'm confident the policy won't miss if i fund it.
