Okay, so I've been arguing with my philosophy-reading group about Harry Frankfurt's 1969 paper "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility." There's the passage early on where he describes Jones, who is going to do something bad, and there's another guy standing by with a device who would intervene and make Jones do it if Jones showed any sign of deciding not to. But since Jones decides on his own, the guy never acts. I keep coming back to that passage. My policy is this: whenever someone presents me with a thought experiment like that — a would-be intervener who never actually does anything — I don't treat it as evidence against the principle that you can only be responsible if you could of done otherwise. I just sort of bracket it. My rule is: a counterexample to a principle about actual responsibility has to work by describing what actually happens in the agent's head, not by adding a fail-safe that never fires. Otherwise it's too easy to cook up a story that feels like a counterexample but doesn't tell me what the agent's real-world abilities were. Is this a defensible epistemic policy, or am I just immunizing the principle against counterexamples? Under what conditions should a merely counterfactual intervener change my mind about what actual agents are responsible for? And is there a way to restate my rule that keeps the good part without the motivated-reasoning part?
