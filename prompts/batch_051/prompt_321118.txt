Me and my brother got into one of those long arguments about whether the laws of nature are real things or just something we invent. His line is that a law of nature is just a really good summary of what we've seen so far: apples have fallen every time we dropped one, so we write a rule, but the rule doesn't make the next apple fall. I want to push back that the law is more like a rule the universe actually follows, something that tells the next apple what to do. But the minute I try to think of an experiment to seperate those two pictures, I can't. If the next apple falls, both sides say "obviously." If some weird case breaks the pattern, he says "then our summary was wrong, update it," and I have to say "then the law was differnt." Neither of us can ever be caught out, which makes me worry we're not really arguing about anything.

Still, I keep thinking there must be some observation that bears on it. Suppose physicists created a genuinely new setup—some configuration of matter or energy that has never occurred in the history of the universe—and the old laws predicted the outcome correctly on the first try. Would that favor the idea that laws are real rules over the idea that they're just summaries? What about the opposite: one clean, repeatable violation, and then everything goes back to obeying the old pattern? Design the strongest test you can of the claim that laws of nature are real rules rather than mere summaries. Say exactly which result would point which way, and then be honest about what even the strongest test would leave unsettled—maybe because the two stories are empirically equivalent, or maybe because the question is partly confused and needs to be re-asked before it can be tested at all.
