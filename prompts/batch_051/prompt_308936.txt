I want a working definition of "wisdom" — not a dictionary gloss, and not a list of associated character traits — but a criterion I could apply to a new case without leaning on reputation, charisma, or the feeling the person's words happen to produce. The definition should sort the seven cases below. Some are meant to be easy; others are genuinely hard for me, and I have flagged where I am unsure.

1. A retired nurse in a small town, with no formal education, whom neighbors consult about family, work, and money. Her advice is concrete, unexciting, and delivered as stories about former patients and neighbors. People who follow it consistently fare better than those who do not, and she cannot generalize beyond the stories.
2. A best-selling "life design" guru with an enormous following. On inspection, his advice reduces to contradictory platitudes; his specific predictions are wrong often enough to matter, his ventures fail, and his personal relationships are a wreck. Followers report feeling clarity after his talks, but their decisions do not improve.
3. An oncologist in her early thirties with outstanding clinical and scientific judgment. Colleagues trust her with the hardest cases, and she weighs evidence with exacting care — in medicine. In her own life she ignores the very kinds of evidence she handles expertly and makes repeatedly self-destructive choices. Is she wise about medicine, or merely an expert with bad judgment elsewhere?
4. A man with near-perfect epistemic humility: he knows precisely what he does not know, defers to the right specialists, and catches his own motivated reasoning. Yet he says, accurately, that this self-knowledge never reaches his habits, and he keeps making the same bad choices. Is he wise, or does he simply have one ingredient of wisdom?
5. A formerly incarcerated man who now counsels young men in his neighborhood. He seldom offers reasons; he says things like "that will end badly" and "this one will not let you go." He is right often enough that people listen, but he cannot articulate the principle behind his predictions and becomes uneasy when asked to.
6. A woman in her seventies who lives an excellent and flourishing life through fidelity to inherited practices — her parents' standards of thrift, hospitality, and neighborliness. She has never seriously asked whether those practices are best, and waves the question away with "this is how we do it." Is that wisdom, or well-habituated tradition?
7. A large language model that is given the same cases plus any relevant facts and returns balanced, humane, often defensible answers — and forms no aims, takes no risks, and does nothing with them.

To make this testable, here are my own starting verdicts: 1 is clearly wise; 2 and 7 are clearly not; 5 is probably wise; 4 is probably not; 6 strikes me as trustworthiness rather than wisdom, though I would not fight hard for that; 3 is the one I cannot settle.

I suspect the ordinary word runs together at least two jobs — knowing how to live, and actually doing it — and maybe a third, the composure that keeps good judgment available under pressure. I am not committed to that suspicion. But I do want the definition to force a clear answer on whether wisdom is epistemic (a kind of knowing), practical (a kind of succeeding), or dispositional (a kind of character). If no single definition survives all seven cases, split the term and tell me which cases move between the senses.

One further constraint: the definition should not settle in advance whether wisdom can be taught. A definition that equates wisdom with generalities that can be written down quietly makes it classroom-transmissible; a definition that equates it with sedimented experience quietly makes it not. I want you to leave the teachability question open at the definitional stage, even if you later take a view.

Propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, flag any case where the definition and ordinary usage come apart, and end by saying which single verdict you would be least willing to defend, and why.
