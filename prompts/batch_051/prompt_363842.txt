Consider the following argument, which is meant to show that a well-known apparent counterexample to the principle of the identity of indiscernibles is not a counterexample after all.

The principle of the identity of indiscernibles says that if two things share all of their properties, then they are identical. In the thought experiment associated with Max Black, a universe contains exactly two spheres, made of the same material and exactly alike in every qualitative respect, located some fixed distance apart in otherwise empty space. Because the situation is perfectly symmetric, the spheres seem to share every pure qualitative property, intrinsic and relational alike, while nevertheless being two distinct objects. Call the spheres A and B.

Now consider this response:

(1) A and B are two spheres, so A is not identical with B.
(2) By the contrapositive of the principle of the identity of indiscernibles, if A is not identical with B, then A and B differ in at least one property.
(3) A and B do differ: A has the property of being non-identical with B.
(4) B does not have the property of being non-identical with B, since B is identical with B.
(5) Therefore A and B differ in at least one property.
(6) Therefore A and B are discernible, not indiscernible.
(7) Therefore the imagined universe is not a case of two distinct but indiscernible spheres, and it does not threaten the principle of the identity of indiscernibles.

Is this argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails? If the steps are all correct as written, is there nonetheless something wrong with the argument's treatment of Black's example, and what is it?
