I’ve been going back and forth with a friend about tipping, queueing, and jaywalking—basically, whether any of these little everyday conventions have actual moral force, or whether they’re just habits people get weirdly judgmental about. I ended up reading a bit of David Hume’s A Treatise of Human Nature, Book III, Part II, Section II, and this passage stuck with me:

“Society provides a remedy for these three inconveniences. By the conjunction of forces, our power is augmented: By the partition of employments, our ability encreases: And by mutual succour we are less expos’d to fortune and accidents. ’Tis by this additional force, ability, and security, that society becomes advantageous.”

I know he’s mainly explaining why we form society, but the section around it is about justice being something humans invent, not something written into nature. That’s what got me thinking. If queueing, tipping, and not jaywalking are also artificial conventions that help society run, does that make them moral obligations in the same way as not stealing? Or are they a different, weaker sort of thing? And what should a visitor do—like, if I’m in a place where everyone queues strictly, or where tipping is basically a understood part of someone’s wages, am I morally required to follow the local convention even if I think it’s foolish or fake?

I want you to write an essay that uses the passage as a starting point to work through this. My strong guess is that some of these conventions do become real obligations and some don’t, but I want you to test that and not just wave at “when in Rome.” Be specific about what makes the difference—e.g., the harms, expectations, or background social contracts involved—and then defend a clear verdict about whether visitors must follow local tipping, queueing, and jaywalking norms.
