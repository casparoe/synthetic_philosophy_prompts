i'm teaching a philosophy of film unit and keep coming back to arrival, the villeneuve film based on ted chiang's story of your life, because it reads like a feature-length version of quine's radical translation setup: louise walks into the shell with two heptapods, no shared language, and has to build a vocabulary from co-occuring stimuli — 'gavagai' except the rabbits are inky squiggles. But the film pretty clearly assumes that the question she's translating around — is the sign a tool or a weapon, a gift or a threat — has a determinate right answer that more evidence and more fluency will settle. That's exactly the assumption quine's indeterminacy of translation denies: two rival translation manuals can fit all the behavioral evidence, and there's no fact of the matter about which one is right. Then the movie takes a hard sapir-whorf turn, where learning the heptapod writing (a whole thought appears at once, no word order) rewires her experience of time. I want a two-way reading: where does arrival genuinely dramatize quine — underdetermination, holism, the interpreter imposing the manual — and where does it quietly smuggle back determinate meanings, like the heptapods' 'actual' message or one true grammar of their physics? Does the whorfian turn answer quine, or just switch the topic from translation to cognition? And what does the film give a philosophy of language class that a discussion of undetached rabbit parts can't? I'm trying to teach the quine without the movie running off with a theory he'd reject, so be honest about where the reading breaks down.
