William James argued in his 1884 article "What is an Emotion?" that the perception of an exciting fact directly triggers bodily changes, and that our feeling of those bodily changes as they occur is the emotion itself. On his view, a disembodied or bodily-muted response would be a pale intellectual state, not a full emotion. Suppose a colleague insists that this is a conceptual claim about what emotions are, not an empirical hypothesis about mechanisms, and therefore cannot be meaningfully tested. You think that is only partly right. Design the strongest experimental test of the core Jamesian claim that you can, specify which outcomes would count against it and which would merely leave it underdetermined, and then be precise about what the test could and could not settle. In particular, separate two things James might be claiming: that bodily feedback is causally necessary for normal emotional intensity, and that an emotion simply is the feeling of bodily changes. Can any observation or experiment adjudicate the identity claim, or can it at best bear on the necessity and intensity claims? Be explicit about the operationalizations you choose, the confounds a positive result would not rule out, and the residue that remains a matter of philosophical interpretation even after the data are in.
