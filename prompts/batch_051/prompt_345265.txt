I have been trying to work out whether an analogy I noticed between cross-cultural psychology and statistical learning theory is substantive or merely verbal. In the tightness–looseness literature, societies are ordered by how many strong norms they impose and how much deviance they tolerate: tight societies regulate behavior heavily and punish deviation, while loose societies leave room for improvisation. The usual functional explanation is that tightness is adaptive under ecological threat—such as high population density, resource scarcity, or recurrent external conflict—because strict norms coordinate people and suppress dangerous variation in behavior. Structurally, this resembles the bias–variance tradeoff and regularization in machine learning: a system of strict norms is like a strong regularizer that keeps individual behavior near the culturally prescribed template, giving predictable behavior across a society (low variance) at the cost of fitting novel situations poorly when the old norms no longer suit the environment (high bias). Loose cultures look like the under-regularized regime: high flexibility and good fit to new circumstances, but less coordinated and more variable behavior. Even the threat-to-noise mapping seems natural: noisy or dangerous environments are exactly where one would strengthen regularization. If this mapping is more than a metaphor, then the normative question of how much rule-following a society should demand would collapse into an empirical question about the structure and noisiness of its environment. Is this a genuine structural isomorphism, a loose analogy, or a confusion? If it is genuine, what can be transferred from learning theory to the study of social norms, and what false prediction does the parallel generate?
