So I've been teaching Russell on definite descriptions this week, and something keeps nagging me. The way Russell handles a definite description — not as a referring expression but as an incomplete symbol that disappears under analysis, so that the present King of France is bald comes out as an existence claim plus a uniqueness claim plus a predication — looks to me a lot like a desugaring pass in a compiler. You write a suface expression that looks as if it denotes a particular object, and then the analysis expands it away into a conjunction with no denoting component left. In a programming language, sugar is definitionally equivalent to its expansion: the expansion just is what the notation means. But Russell is making a substantial claim about the semantics of natural language, not stipulating a notation. So here's what I can't decide: is this a deep structural identity or am I just pattern-matching? What would have to be true of an ordinary language for the compiler analogy to really go through, and does the analogy predict something false — about paraphrase preserving speaker intuitions, or about presuppositions factoring out cleanly? I honestly can't tell whether the compiler story explains Russell or just flatters my students' programmer instincts.
