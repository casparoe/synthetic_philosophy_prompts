I'm a retired pharmacist, not an academic, and I've been reading popular accounts of AI safety on my own. Something about the discussion keeps bothering me. In my old profession, a drug does not have side effects written into its chemistry; an effect is a side effect only relative to the indication we chose. The same drop in blood pressure is the therapy when we treat hypertension and the side effect when we give the drug for something else. Minimizing side effects therefore presupposes that someone has already decided which effects matter and why, which is really a judgment about what health consists in. Now I read that AI researchers want an agent that carries out an assigned task while keeping its impact on the rest of the world low, avoiding side effects. That sounds like the same move: avoid all changes except the ones the assigned task permits. But then low impact seems to inherit the same problem, since you cannot tell which changes are side effects without already knowing what it is for the world to be going well. There may be one important difference: in medicine we do not have a counterfactual baseline of the patient-without-the-drug, whereas for an AI there is, at least in principle, the world as it would have gone had the agent done nothing. Is that difference enough to make low-impact AI solvable in a way that the medical version is not, or does the counterfactual simply smuggle in a value judgment — that whatever would have happened anyway is acceptable? I am trying to work out whether this resemblance is deep, loose, or merely an accident of shared vocabulary.
