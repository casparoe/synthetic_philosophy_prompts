I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been asked to write up a decision framework for my team. Over the last few months I have run small structured votes on hiring, project, and architecture decisions. The pattern is: break a decision into separate yes/no questions, have each person vote on each question individually, and treat the majority answer on each line as the group's official position.

I've kept notes on how I reacted to several such votes. Alice, Bob, and Carla are three engineers, and in every case below each of them is individually consistent.

Case 1 (hiring). We asked whether (P) the candidate has strong distributed-systems experience, whether (Q) they would handle our on-call rotation well, and whether (R) we should make an offer. We had explicitly agreed that R just means "P and Q." Alice voted P yes, Q no, R no. Bob voted P no, Q yes, R no. Carla voted P yes, Q yes, R yes. The majority positions were therefore P yes, Q yes, and R no. Our official stance said the candidate had the right experience and would handle the on-call rotation well, and that we should not make an offer. That felt broken to me: the problem seemed to be in the aggregation, not in any individual voter.

Case 2 (deprecating a service). Same vote split, different subject. We asked whether (P) the new service covers all uses of the old one, whether (Q) the downstream teams have had enough migration time, and whether (R) we should deprecate the old service by Q3. Again Alice voted P yes, Q no, R no; Bob voted P no, Q yes, R no; Carla voted P yes, Q yes, R yes. The majority positions were P yes, Q yes, R no. This did not bother me at all, because everyone clearly treated R as also involving risk tolerance and opportunity cost that P and Q did not capture.

Case 3 (shipping a feature, premise-based). Trying to avoid what happened in Case 1, we never asked R at all. We asked only whether (P) users actually have the problem and whether (Q) we can ship within our compliance budget, and we agreed in advance that if both passed, R ("ship it") followed automatically. P passed 2–1 and Q passed 2–1, so we shipped. But the underlying views were the same as in Case 1: Alice and Bob would have voted no on R and only Carla would have voted yes, so a direct vote on R would have failed 2–1. This feels more defensible to me than Case 1, but I cannot shake the feeling that we took an action a majority of the group would have voted down if asked the question directly.

Case 4 (conclusion-only vote). Whenever we have just put the final yes/no question itself to the whole group and gone with the majority, the process has felt clean, even though nobody had to articulate the reasons behind their vote.

Case 5 (advisory input to a single owner). When our director polls each of us on the premise questions but treats our votes as advisory and makes the final call herself, I see no aggregation problem at all. Whatever she decides is her decision; if she is inconsistent, that is a flaw in her reasoning, not in the group's.

My provisional rule is that a decision procedure is broken only when the group's official positions are logically inconsistent with each other — when the official majorities say P and Q but also not-R, while we had agreed that R follows from P and Q. Case 1 violates that rule; Cases 2, 3, 4, and 5 do not. But the rule leaves me uneasy. It says nothing about Case 3's conflict with what the majority would have said about R, and I cannot tell whether that uneasiness is a bug in me or a real signal that the rule is missing something.

Please test, refine, or replace that principle. I would also like your principle's verdict on a sixth case I have deliberately left undecided: three voters, three premises P, Q, and S, all required for R; each voter rejects exactly one different premise, so each premise passes 2–1, but if all three were asked about R directly, every one of them would vote no.

Please structure your answer as: (1) the principle (or small set of candidate principles) you would defend, stated in one or two sentences each; (2) how it handles each of my six cases; (3) its prediction for one further case of your own construction; and (4) which of my verdicts, if any, I would have to abandon. Keep the entire response under 750 words.
