A friend summarized the sunk cost fallacy for me as follows:

"A sunk cost is any time, money, or effort you have already spent and cannot get back. The sunk cost fallacy is treating that unrecoverable cost as a reason to keep going. A rational choice should be based only on the future benefits and future costs of the options ahead, because whatever you have already spent is gone no matter what you choose. So a past cost can never be a reason to choose one course of action over another. If you buy a nonrefundable ticket, wake up sick on the day of the event, and drag yourself there just because you paid, you are committing the fallacy. The same goes for finishing a book you dislike because you are halfway through, sticking with a degree you hate because you are two years in, or continuing a failing project at work because the firm has already sunk millions into it. In every case the correct move is to ignore what you have already spent and compare only the remaining paths from here on. It follows that honoring sunk costs is always irrational, and any story about why continuing might make sense is just a rationalization."

Something about this seems too clean to me. I am inclined to accept the ticket example and the general forward-looking principle, but I have also heard that past expenditures can matter for forward-looking reasons — by signaling something to other people, by teaching you something about yourself, or by affecting what you are committed to — and the summary dismisses all of that as "just a rationalization" without argument. I want to know whether that dismissal is fair.
