I'm considering a forecasting platform whose selling point is that it lets forecasters express how unsure they are, not just what they guess. The proposed rule is this. For every yes/no event, a forecaster submits a closed interval [l, u] of probabilities, with 0 ≤ l ≤ u ≤ 1. When the event resolves, the platform scores the interval by the best Brier score it could have received if, after the fact, it had been allowed to commit to any single probability inside the interval: if the event happens, the forecaster is treated as having reported u; if it does not happen, as having reported l. From this base score the platform then subtracts a width penalty λ(u − l), where λ is a fixed positive rate. Thus a forecaster who posts [0.3, 0.7] is scored as if they had said 0.7 in the event of a yes and 0.3 in the event of a no, and pays λ · 0.4 either way. The founders' hope is that precise forecasters will post tight intervals, genuinely ambiguous questions will attract wide ones, and the width penalty will stop everyone from posting [0, 1] as a costless perfect score.

Walk through how this mechanism would actually behave. First take a forecaster with a precise probability p who maximizes expected score under p: what interval do they post, how does it depend on p and λ, and at what penalty rate do they finally collapse to a point? Then take a forecaster who genuinely holds a range of probabilities — an imprecise credal set — and evaluates any interval by its worst expected score over that set: do they report their range, shrink it, or widen it? Now add the platform's real-world features: a public leaderboard, bonuses tied to score, and the ability to revise intervals up to the deadline. What do these do to the interval widths people post, to who rises to the top, and to whether the platform ends up measuring imprecision, ambiguity-seeking, or something else? Finally, identify where the scheme gets gamed first and whether changing the penalty from linear to convex — say a fee on (u − l)² — fixes the failure without destroying whatever honest signal the interval carries.

Throughout, please keep two kinds of claims apart: results that follow from the arithmetic of the scoring rule, and results that depend on a contested model of how rational agents handle imprecise probabilities (maximin, Hurwicz optimism–pessimism, second-order expected utility, and the like). Where you rely on one of those models, say that you are doing so and note that it is not the consensus.
