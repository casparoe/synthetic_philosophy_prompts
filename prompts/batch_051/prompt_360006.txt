I'm on the governance committee of a co-op that has to pick a single proposal from a slate of five or six every month — capital projects, fee changes, that sort of thing — and we keep going in circles about process before we even argue about the proposals. One member has proposed a rule he calls "last two standing." It works like this: every member submits a secret complete ranking of all the proposals (no ties). You count first choices and eliminate the proposal with the fewest. Ties for elimination are broken by lottery. Then there's a short open discussion, and members may submit fresh rankings over the survivors — so rankings aren't locked in, and everyone can react to who just got knocked out. Repeat until exactly two proposals are left; then, instead of a head-to-head vote, the final decision is approval voting: each member may approve one or both of the two survivors, and the survivor with more approvals wins, with a lottery tiebreak.

I'd like you to act as a social choice theorist who also has a realistic feel for how committees behave. Before you commit to any claims, actually walk through two or three small concrete electorates (say, four proposals and four or five factions) under this rule, so your analysis is grounded in a simulation rather than slogans. Tell me how it plays out: what it does to sincere voters, where its gamed first, whether the re-ranking between rounds makes things better or worse, and whether the final approval stage patches the pathologies of the elimination stage or just adds new ones. In particular, compare the winner the rule produces to the proposal that would beat each rival head-to-head (the Condorcet winner, if one exists), and tell me which of the standard Arrow-style conditions it trips over first — the usual ones about unrestricted domain, Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship — and be honest about whether the Arrow framing even applies cleanly to a single-winner rule rather than a social ranking, or whether the real instability shows up as manipulability.

And keep clear the line between what's forced by the definitions (settled) and what's genuinely contested or depends on empirical assumptions about how real voters strategize. I don't want empirical guesses dressed up as theorems. Where does all this leave the co-op, and is the rule fixable without just replacing it with something standard?
