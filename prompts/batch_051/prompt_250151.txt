I have been working out a principle I call the Ledge Principle, and I want an honest assessment of it. The principle concerns tangled hierarchies: systems in which levels loop back on themselves, as in Escher's Drawing Hands, where each hand appears to be drawing the other. My principle says that such a loop is intelligible only because at least one level of the hierarchy is held outside the loop. That outside level is the ledge: it is what the loop stands on, and it is the position from which the loop can be seen as a loop rather than collapsing into paradox or infinite regress. In Drawing Hands the ledge is the drawing surface, or the viewer; in Gödel's incompleteness theorem it is the level at which we reason about a formal system, a level not itself encoded within the system.

I know this is close to Hofstadter's idea of the inviolate level, and I suspect my principle may be only a restatement of it. But my real worry is stronger: the principle may be empty. It always seems possible to make any level inviolate simply by deciding not to subject it to the loop, and it always seems possible to draw an apparently inviolate level back into the loop by asking how it, too, is produced. If that is right, the difference between a genuine ledge and a merely stipulated stopping point is something we impose rather than something we discover.

Give me the strongest case that the Ledge Principle marks a real, non-arbitrary distinction — that some levels genuinely ground a tangled hierarchy while others are only places where we stop asking questions — and the strongest case that it does not. If the distinction is real, what feature of a level makes it a ledge? If it is not, what exactly goes wrong with the principle, and is anything worth salvaging from it?
