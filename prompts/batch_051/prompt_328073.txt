You are an expert in behavioral genetics and the philosophy of science, someone who is really careful about what words like "heritable" can and cannot support. Think slowly, brainstorm the relevant concepts before answering, and then give me a move-by-move verdict.

At lunch the other day I watched two of my labmates go in circles about a press report on a big twin study. The study reportedly found that educational attainment is about 40% heritable in the population sampled. The argument went like this.

Dana said: "This is going to get used to say school doesn't matter. But heritability only tells you about variation in a population. It says nothing about how much of any one person's educational outcome came from their genes."

Marcus said: "Sure, it's about variance. But if genes account for a big slice of the differences between people, then giving everyone the same great school won't equalize outcomes, because the remaining differences will be mostly genetic. So it does imply a kind of ceiling on what environmental interventions can do."

Dana said: "That's too fast. You can raise the whole distribution with an intervention—average attainment can go up even while heritability stays high. In fact if you equalized environment for everyone, heritability could go up, but that wouldn't show environment doesn't matter. Heritability just doesn't tell you how the system responds to a change in the environment."

Marcus said: "Okay, but it still predicts something. If a trait is strongly heritable, then siblings raised in the same home differ more for genetic reasons than for whatever the shared home does, and adopted kids resemble their biological parents. So specific parenting and schooling decisions matter less than people think."

Dana said: "Those adoption findings are themselves only about the range of environments in the sample. And heritability estimates can be inflated by gene–environment correlation: kids with certain genetic tendencies also tend to get certain environments—parents read to them more, teachers track them—and the whole package gets counted as 'genetic.'"

Marcus said: "But part of gene–environment correlation is genetic in origin. If a kid's temperament makes adults hand them more books, then just changing the environment might do less unless you break that correlation. So the ceiling point has some teeth."

Neither would budge and they asked me to settle it, which I couldn't. Please adjudicate the whole thing move by move. For each move, say whether it's clearly mistaken, clearly right, or partly right, and why. Then tell me what both of them are missing, and who is more right overall.
