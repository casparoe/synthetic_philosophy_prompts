In my English class we read this short story where people can get a "grief vaccine" right after a funeral — you keep all the memories but the actual pain of grief gets deleted. At lunch two people in my class got into a real arguement about whether that would be a good thing, and I couldn't tell who was winning because both sides kept landing points that sounded right.

Javi said taking it would obviously be the rational move: grief feels terrible, the memories stay either way, and refusing it is like refusing painkillers for a broken leg because the pain is "natural." Maya said no, a broken leg is different because that pain isn't about anything; grief is about the person who died, and the pain is part of actually getting it — that they aren't coming back. Javi shot back that you can get it without hurting; people deep in grief often understand things worse because they can barely think, so if grief is information, it's a garbage messenger. Maya said the "information" isn't just the fact that she's dead; it includes stuff like "I'm now the kind of person who loses people," and your sense of yourself has to be rebuilt. Grief is that rebuilding, so skipping it leaves a blank spot where part of you used to be. Javi said that's romanticizing: people skip the rebuilding all the time by burying themselves in work or moving away, and we say they're avoiding it, not that their identity is broken; a pill plus grief counseling could do the rebuilding without the suffering — and if suffering did the rebuilding, then worse, longer grief would build a better self, but complicated grief just traps people. Then Maya said she was never saying more pain equals more growth; she's saying the capacity for grief is load-bearing for love. In the story the vaccinated stop visiting graves, stop telling stories, and treat the dead like deleted contacts. If you love someone with a "until it hurts, then I switch it off" clause, that lowers the stakes of every bond you have, and grief is what keeps those stakes real.

Act as a philosopher who works on the emotions. Start by steelmanning each move before you judge it, then go through the exchange move by move and rate each point from clearly wrong to clearly right, with a short justification. Also say what, if anything, both of them are missing. Don't just crown a winner — I want to know exactly which arguments hold up and which ones don't.
