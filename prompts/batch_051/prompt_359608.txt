I keep coming back to a tension between two things I find almost equally hard to give up, and I want someone to help me think through what it would actually cost to resolve it one way or the other.

On the one hand, the hyperbolic discounting account of what people call weakness of will seems to me to be one of the more successful pieces of psychological theory around. It gives a clean mechanism: rewards are discounted by delay, but the discount curve is steep up close and shallow far away, so your preferences can flip depending on how near a temptation is. That explains why someone can sincerely plan on Sunday to go to the gym on Monday and then stay in bed: on Sunday the delayed benefit outweighs the pain, but on Monday morning the bed is right there and it's comfort looms larger than the same calculation made from a distance. The account has real virtues: it's formal, it makes predictions about preference reversals that seem to come out true, and it unifies procrastination, overeating, failing to save, and lots of other stuff under one mechanism. On this picture akrasia is not a special kind of action at all. What feels like acting against your own better judgment is just acting on the preferences you have at the time, which are not the preferences you had before; the old judgment didn't get overruled, it got replaced.

On the other hand, I can't shake the intuition that there is a real phenomenon this story doesn't reach. Sometimes it really does feel like I am acting against a judgment I still hold at the moment I act: I know, right then, that getting up is better all things considered, and I stay in bed anyway. There's no sense of having changed my mind; the better judgment stays fully endorsed while I do the worse thing. That's the thing the Greeks meant by akrasia, and it seems like a distinct failure of self-governance, not just a property of a discount function. If the theory says that can't happen, or that it's an illusion, I'm not sure the theory should get to dictate that.

So that's the clash: a theory with systematic and predictive virtues that says the phenomenon is really something else, against a firm first-person intuition that the something else doesn't capture what's most important about the cases. I don't want a verdict that just declares a winner. I want to know, concretely, what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that gets debunked or re-described, what it would take for the theory to be amended or restricted in its reach, and whether there's a plausible reading on which both are true of different aspects of the same situation. And I'd want an honest assessment of whether the compatibilist-looking options are genuinely compatible or just a way of letting the theory keep the word and the intuition keep the music.
