For the past two years I have been part of a small group that teaches a course on improving everyday reasoning, and we have become increasingly dissatisfied with the standard format — lectures on biases, taxonomies of fallacies, and worked examples — because participants grasp the material in the classroom and then mostly fail to use it in their actual decisions months later. One of our instructors has been pushing a different model: train rationality the way martial arts train perception and response, through repeated practice of standardized forms, or katas, where a kata is a deliberately simplified, repeatable exercise that isolates a single epistemic move — noticing a base rate before answering, generating a third option before accepting a forced choice, checking whether a belief would survive a change in source, or separating a forecast from the feeling of confidence that arrives with it. The hope is that drilling these forms until they become automatic might transfer to real contexts the way drilled footwork transfers to sparring. Some of us are skeptical: a kata is artificial, and the long history of transfer-of-training worries suggests that what is learned in one setting often stays there. Others reply that martial artists plainly do bring trained patterns into sparring, and that the transfer problem is exactly what a well-designed curriculum should target rather than assume away.

We now have a small grant that will fund one coherent line of work for about three years, and we have to choose where to concentrate. We keep circling a set of questions, and we cannot pursue them all. Candidate questions include: What exactly has to be true of a kata for it to train rationality, rather than merely training someone to solve a familiar puzzle? Is transfer after drilled practice best understood as a kind of habit, a kind of judgment, or a kind of concept-application — and why does that distinction matter for how exercises should be designed? Which aspects of rationality, such as updating on evidence, allocating attention, noticing one's own motivated reasoning, or seeking disconfirmation, are most plausibly trainable by this method, and which are not? Is there a principled reason to expect far transfer at all, or should we restrict the project's ambitions to near transfer and modestly better domain-specific habits? And what would count as success for such a program in the first place: better scores on new but structurally similar problems, better real-world decisions, or a durable change in how people respond under stress?

Please offer a defended agenda. Which two or three of these questions, or other questions we have not listed, do you think we should prioritize, and why? Make your criteria explicit — tractability, neglectedness, how many other questions depend on the answer, or whatever else you use — and say what argument or evidence would most change your ranking.
