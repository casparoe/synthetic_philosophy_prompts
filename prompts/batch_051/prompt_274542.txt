In a graduate seminar on the epistemology of artificial intelligence I have been developing an argument about the proposal to use adversarial debate between AI systems as a method of scalable oversight. The background thought is this: if we cannot check a piece of reasoning ourselves, perhaps two systems of comparable ability could be set to argue for opposite conclusions, with a human judge awarding victory to whichever side better withstands the other’s challenges. The following argument seems to show that such a procedure would be reliable even when the judge lacks the expertise to assess the underlying claims. I cannot satisfy myself that the argument goes through, and I would value a step-by-step diagnosis.

(1) In a debate between two systems of comparable competence, where victory is awarded by a human overseer according to which side better answers the challenges put to it, each system has a strong incentive to identify and publicly expose errors in the other’s case.

(2) If a system has both the incentive and the competence to expose an error in the other’s reasoning, then, given sufficiently many rounds of challenge and reply, that error will tend to be exposed.

(3) An error that has been exposed in debate—by which I mean a challenge to which one side conspicuously fails to give a satisfactory answer—is the sort of thing a non-expert judge can recognise without herself being able to construct or evaluate the underlying technical argument.

(4) Therefore, the side whose case conspicuously fails in this way will tend to appear defeated to the judge, and the judge can reasonably favour the other side.

(5) Therefore, adversarial debate between systems of comparable competence is a sound method for selecting the more defensible position on questions that no human judge can independently evaluate.

Please assess the argument as follows. First, state whether the argument, as written, is valid; if it is not, identify the exact step at which it fails and explain what would have to be added to that step for the inference to go through. If it is valid but unsound, identify the premises you would reject and the weakest revision under which the argument would go through while remaining interesting. In either case, make explicit the conditions the argument tacitly places on the judge and on the two systems. I would be grateful if the entire answer were written in British English.
