I keep going back and forth on whether death is bad for the person who dies, and I've realized my gut reactions to a bunch of cases don't obviously fit together. I want to know if there's a single principle behind them, or whether some of my reactions have to go.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. You're killed painlessly in your sleep at 30. You never wake up, never feel anything. This seems clearly bad for you to me.

2. You spend a year in a dreamless, medically induced coma with no awareness, then wake up exactly as you were, no side effects. This seems only mildly bad — you missed a year, but it's nothing like dying at 30.

3. You fall into a permanent dreamless coma at 30; your body lives to 80 but you never regain consciousness. I want to say this is about as bad as death itself, maybe worse because your body keeps going. But I can't explain why the permanent coma is so much worse than the one-year coma, if in both cases you just miss time without experiencing the loss.

4. You die painlessly the night before your daugher's wedding, versus the night after. The first seems worse to me, even though in both cases you don't experience anything after the moment of death.

5. After you die, someone publishes a book of lies that destroys your reputation, and everyone believes it. This feels like a harm to you, not just to the people who loved you. But if you can be harmed by something that happens after you're gone, the Epicurean point starts to look wrong.

6. Symmetrically, I don't feel that your nonexistence before you were born was bad for you. If you'd been born twenty years earlier, you'd have had twenty more years of life, but I don't think the fact that you didn't exist then was a harm.

7. A two-year-old who dies painlessly and a ninety-year-old who dies painlessly: the childs death seems much worse for the child.

The Epicurean argument says none of these deaths can be bad for the one who dies, because there's no subject around to undergo the badness. My verdicts in 1, 4, 5 and 7 seem to reject that. But verdict 6 seems to accept the symmetry argument: if the time after death can be bad because you miss goods you'd have had, why isn't the time before birth just as bad? And case 3 vs case 2 bugs me: if what's bad is missing future goods, the one-year coma and the permanent coma are the same kind of thing at different scales, so why do they feel qualitatively different?

Is there a principle that captures all seven verdicts, and what does it say about cases I haven't considered — for example, posthumous fame, is that good for you in the same way posthumous slander is bad? If no principle captures all of them, which verdicts should I give up?
