The position I want you to interrogate is the version of moral particularism I have defended in my teaching and, at times perhaps too confidently, in print: roughly, the thesis that the moral relevance of a feature—whether it counts in favor of an action, against it, and with what weight—is not fixed by the feature itself but depends on the wider context, and that this dependence cannot be captured by any finite, exceptionless set of moral principles. I want to keep this distinct from the weaker thesis that reasons are merely context-sensitive, which many generalists now grant; my particularism is the stronger claim that the context-sensitivity resists codification, so that familiar principles such as keep promises or do not harm are best understood as heuristic summaries that neither state sufficient conditions nor pick out exceptionless contributions.

What I am asking you to supply is a map of the view's exposure. Tell me what would count against it, sorted into three grades: evidence or arguments that should be fatal, evidence that should force me to retreat to some weaker and explicitly hedged particularism, and evidence that is merely awkward or calls for clarification. Include what a committed particularist should honestly concede counts against the view, not only what critics say—for instance, a well-confirmed, exceptionless normative principle in an unexpected domain, or a demonstrated failure of particularist moral education. Also address the standard charges that particularism collapses into generalism once the context is described with enough specificity, or that it is committed to an implausible epistemology in which each case is known by an unanalyzable perception or intuition. Which of these, if any, should move a careful particularist, and which can safely be dismissed?

Finally, I want your honest assessment of whether the position I have stated has been qualified into unfalsifiability by moving the goalposts whenever a principle seems to hold, and whether that kind of insulation is a vice in a view of this sort or a defensible consequence of treating moral competence as a skill rather than a theory. To keep the analysis from floating, I would like you to test the view's exposure in at least three registers: moral epistemology, moral education, and the law. In each, say what development—a psychological result, a pedagogical failure, a legal case that seems to require codification—would genuinely count against particularism, and how decisive it would be.
