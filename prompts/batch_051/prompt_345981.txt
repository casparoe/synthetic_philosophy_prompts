Over the past few years I have been thinking on my own about a pattern in the way ordinary categories drift, and I have given it a name I want you to test: the exception ratchet. The claim is that categories do not usually change because someone redefines them; they change when a borderline case is admitted as a one-off exception because it shares one salient feature with the category's core. Chess, for example, was let into sport on the strength of competition and skill, even though it involves no physical exertion. Each admission is defensible on its own, so nobody objects. But once admitted, the exception stops being an exception and becomes a new reference point: the next borderline case gets compared to chess rather than to running, so the center of the category travels without anyone deciding to move it. The ratchet part is my stronger claim: the movement is one-directional. Expansion is easy, contraction is very hard, because every case that got in now has a reason to be in and people who would feel wronged if it were expelled. By now sport comfortably includes poker and esports, where the original anchor of physical exertion has been quietly retired, and almost no one proposes shrinking the category back, even though shrinking would be the mirror image of the same steps. The same drift seems to have happened with cooking, where heating a prepared meal is now unremarkably counted as cooking.

I know this may just be ordinary vagueness, or the familiar idea that categories hang together by overlapping similarities rather than by a single definition. My reason for thinking there is something more is the asymmetry: vague boundaries should blur in both directions, but I am claiming they reliably blur only one way. So my questions are these. Is this a real mechanism, or only a re-description of those older ideas? Does someone already have it under another name? And where does it break — what would count against it rather than merely complicate it? I would like your honest assessment as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
