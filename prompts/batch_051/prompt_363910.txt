Consider the puzzle behind the old question of when a legend becomes history. I want you to work with a specific construct — the minimal historicity threshold — and run three test cases through it: the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus. The deliverable is not a survey of the scholarship. It is a committed case analysis that runs in both directions: the threshold should discipline the cases, and the cases should reveal where the threshold is wrong, missing something, or smuggling in its own conclusion.

Here is the threshold. A later story may be called minimally historical when it preserves a real event, person, or setting as at least one ingredient of an otherwise transformed tradition. I propose four conditions. (1) Anchorage: a datable source or artifact from roughly the claimed period, produced independently of the tradition that later tells the story, contains at least one of the names, places, or conflicts the narrative centers on. (2) Fit: the material or documentary remains of the candidate place and time are broadly consistent with the narrative's central non-supernatural elements, not merely with some abstract "there was a war" or "there was a migration." (3) Chain of transmission: there is a traceable route from the claimed events to the earliest surviving account, through oral tradition, scribal revision, or political reuse, with identifiable compression, duplication, and amplification. (4) Invention pressure: if the existence and shape of the narrative are better explained by the needs of its transmitters — legitimation, identity, entertainment, propaganda — than by the persistence of a memory, the story fails the bar. Treat these as a lens rather than a checklist; part of the job is saying where they are silent or forced.

For the factual starting points, take these as given so the exercise does not become an archaeology quiz:

- Troy. There is a Bronze Age fortified settlement at Hisarlik in northwest Anatolia, destroyed by fire in a horizon usually dated around 1180 BC. Hittite diplomatic letters from roughly the preceding centuries mention a western Anatolian kingdom called Wilusa and a western power called Ahhiyawa, names often equated with (W)ilios/Troy and the Achaeans. The Homeric epics were written down only centuries later, after a long oral phase, and their material details come from several different periods; nothing in the destruction layer proves the city fell to a Greek coalition rather than to fire, earthquake, internal conflict, or another enemy.

- Arthur. The sixth-century British writer Gildas, in his polemic about the ruin and conquest of Roman Britain, describes a British victory at Mount Badon but never names Arthur. The name Arthur first appears in a ninth-century Latin historical miscellany, where he is a battle-leader credited with twelve victories including Badon, not a king. A set of annals compiled in the tenth century gives dates for Badon and records Arthur's death at Camlann alongside someone called Medraut. Only in the twelfth century does Geoffrey of Monmouth build the full story of King Arthur, his court, and his conquests. No contemporary fifth- or sixth-century source or inscription names Arthur at all.

- Exodus. An Egyptian victory stele from the late thirteenth century BC uses the name Israel for a people already present in Canaan, which is the earliest extrabiblical occurrence of the name; no Egyptian text mentions a large enslaved Israelite population, the plagues, or a departure. Archaeological survey of Sinai and the Negev has produced no material trace of a decades-long movement of anything like the numbers given in Exodus, and the written biblical account was set down centuries after the events it narrates.

Now do the work. For each of the three cases, assign a definite verdict — over the threshold or under it — and defend it. Give the strongest considerations first; where you are torn, say what specific evidence would flip you rather than retreating into "on the other hand." Say explicitly how confident you are in each verdict and whether the four conditions agree among themselves or point in different directions within a case.

Then reverse direction. For each case, identify which of the four conditions does the least work or is hardest to apply honestly, and where the threshold would mislead someone who used it mechanically. State the least piece of additional evidence that would change each verdict. Finally, say what the three cases jointly show about the construct itself: should "minimally historical" be a single uniform standard across persons, events, and peoples, or does the threshold secretly split into several different standards? Does the Exodus case expose a principled limit to the framework, and if so, what does that limit show about the difference between a legend that has absorbed history and a history that has been written in the idiom of legend?
