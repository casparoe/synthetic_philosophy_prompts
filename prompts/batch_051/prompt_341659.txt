Here's the thing I keep coming back to when I teach the Chinese-room debate. Forget, for now, whether the room as a whole understands. What I want is a checklist for what it would take for a machine-translation system to understand the sentences it translates, rather than just producing acceptable output. In my undergrad course I give three marks of understanding a sentence: (i) it can flag a source sentence that is syntactically fine but semantically broken, rather than quietly 'translating' it into something sane; (ii) it can render the same content in several genuinely different target sentences, not just surface variants; (iii) it is sensitive to what follows from the sentence, so its choice doesn't contradict the surrounding passage. Now consider a system trained end-to-end on a few billion aligned sentence pairs: no grammar rules, no dictionary, no explicit semantic representation — just a very large statistical model with an attention mechanism and a beam search over its outputs. It translates ordinary newspaper prose beautifuly. But when the source sentence is a garden path, it usually gives the plausible first reading and misses the corrective reading. When the source is a tautology with no close parallel in the training data, it sometimes outputs a substantive claim the source never made. When the source is ambiguous, it picks the more frequent reading without indicating that the other reading is even possible. Does this system understand the sentences it translates, on my three marks? Go through the marks one by one and say where the evidence is genuinely borderline rather than a disguised clear no. Then tell me whether the case puts pressure on the criteria themselves rather than just on the system. Finally, tell me what would change your mind — what behaviour, or what argument, would tip you from no to yes or yes to no — and where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail.
