I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and over the past few months my reading has split between old debates in the philosophy of spacetime and, for a piece I'm writing on reproducibility in statistics, the quieter literature on Bayesian mixture models. That collision produced a parallel I can't shake. The hole argument, as I understand it, runs like this. Take a solution of general relativity and a region of spacetime — the 'hole' — lying to the future of some spacelike surface; apply a diffeomorphism that fixes everything outside the hole and on the surface but redistributes the metric field inside it. You get two mathematically distinct solutions that agree on all the initial data and are observationally indistinguishable, yet differ inside the hole. If you think the manifold's points are real things with their own identities, you have to call these two different possible worlds, and determinism then fails: the past plus the laws don't pick out a unique interior. The standard way out is to treat the two solutions as gauge-equivalent descriptions of one situation, and to stop treating spacetime points as having primitive identity.

Bayesian mixture modelling has a notorious annoyance called label switching, and it seems to me to have the same shape. Fit a three-component Gaussian mixture, and the likelihood doesn't care which component gets called 'component one': permute the labels on the mixing weights and component parameters and the model's predictions are unchanged. With exchangeable priors the posterior comes in K! symmetric copies — six of them for three components — and a statistican who is a naive realist about component labels, as if 'component one' named a persistent hidden thing, will look at the MCMC output and conclude the parameters are hopelessly multimodal. The usual fixes are to quotient the labels out in post-processing, or to impose a symmetry-breaking identifiability constraint such as ordering the means. To me that maps onto the hole argument nearly exactly: naive substantivalism about points corresponds to naive realism about labels; quotienting by diffeomorphisms corresponds to relabelling the samples; gauge-fixing corresponds to the identifiability constraint; and the structuralist moral — that the objects are artifacts of a symmetric representation rather than features of the world — looks transferable.

My question is whether this is a genuine identity of structure or just pattern-matching on the word 'symmetry'. If it is real, what does the transfer actually buy? Should statisticians be structuralists about mixture components, and should philosophers of physics borrow the statisticians' relabelling algorithms as a way of making gauge-fixing precise? And here's the worry that keeps me up: the hole argument depends on a symmetry you can apply nontrivially inside a region while doing nothing outside it, whereas label switching is a global finite permutation with no local 'hole' version. Does that local/global difference break the analogy — or is there a localised form of label switching that would reproduce the indeterminism, and if so, what would it look like?
