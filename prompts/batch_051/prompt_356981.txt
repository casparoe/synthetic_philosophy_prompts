You are a philosopher who works in population ethics and on the formal properties of welfare aggregation, and you are especially good at telling apart diagnoses that are usually run together. Think through the problem carefully; before settling on any term, work through what the phenomenon includes and what it excludes.

I keep noticing a configuration in arguments about obligations to future generations, and I do not know what to call it. Suppose a universe contains infinitely many generations. In one outcome, the first generation flourishes at 100 and every later generation lives at 1. In a second outcome, the first generation lives at 0 and every later generation again lives at 1. Comparing only the lives that differ, the second outcome is plainly worse: one person is worse off and no one is better off. But if I compare by the total amount of good in the two histories, both totals are infinite, and the two histories also have the same limiting average over time, so the summing-style comparison refuses to rank them. The same pattern appears in the opposite direction: raise every single generation above a baseline by a shrinking amount that halves each time, and every generation is strictly better off, yet the total and the limiting average again see no difference.

I am not asking about the general fact that infinite sums misbehave. I am asking about this specific tension between generation-by-generation comparisons, which remain sensitive in exactly these cases, and whole-history aggregative comparisons, which go silent. Does this configuration have a name? If nothing standard fits, what would you call it, and how would you bound it against the adjacent ideas? More importantly: what exactly has to be weakened in ordinary finite-population aggregation to keep the first-generation comparison from being drowned out, and which other judgments about impartiality or the long run break as a result?
