The Hart–Devlin debate of the mid-twentieth century turned on an argument of Patrick Devlin's about why a legal system may enforce morality. His argument ran, in outline, as follows. A society is not just a collection of individuals under a single government; it is constituted in part by a shared moral code, and that code is part of what holds the society together as a distinct society. Since a society is entitled to defend the conditions of its own existence, it may use the criminal law against conduct that undermines that shared morality, even when the conduct harms only the participants themselves. The justification is therefore not that the conduct is intrinsically immoral, but that enforcing the shared code is like punishing treason: it protects the society's continuing existence. H. L. A. Hart replied, in effect, that the criminal law is properly limited to preventing harm to others, and that Devlin had not shown that tolerating private immorality actually causes the social order to collapse.

Now consider a development that Devlin, and to a large extent Hart, did not face. In contemporary digital life, the moral communities in which people actually live are no longer simply the territorial publics whose criminal law applies to them. Much moral rule-making and rule-enforcement now takes place within online communities that possess their own norms, reputation systems, and mechanisms of exclusion; the same person can belong to several such communities at once, or move from one to another, at negligible cost. At the same time, much of the day-to-day enforcement of these norms is carried out not by the state but by platforms, through moderation, deplatforming, and algorithmically amplified public condemnation.

Does Devlin's argument survive this development? Identify which premise in the reconstructed argument is under the greatest pressure—for example, that a given society has a single shared moral code, that abandoning that code dissolves the society, that the criminal law is the relevant enforcement mechanism, or that the analogy between moral enforcement and treason does the justificatory work. If the argument survives in its original form, show how it can accommodate the fragmentation and privatization of moral enforcement. If it does not survive, say whether it is best treated as false under the new conditions, inapplicable to them, or repairable; if repairable, state the strongest repaired version you can. I am interested in what happens to the argument itself, not in a defense of any particular platform policy. Please answer in American English.
