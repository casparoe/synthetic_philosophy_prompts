I organize a philosophy of science seminar for roughly fourteen advanced undergraduates, and I have a single 75-minute session in which I want them to feel the pull of the doomsday argument before anyone says its name. Could you design a runnable in-class game for that setting? It should involve drawing "birth ranks" or an equivalent device, with hidden urns representing hypotheses about how long a civilization or species lasts, and a point-based payoff scheme that needs no real money and rewards correct bets on which urn they were drawn from. The rules should be arranged so that participants discover through their own betting that, conditional on their relatively early rank, the rational bet is one most of them will initially find unreasonable — so they meet the self-sampling assumption as something their own behavior commits them to, rather than as a term introduced by the facilitator.

After that first round, I would like a second phase that complicates the result instead of merely reinforcing it. Perhaps participants choose or negotiate their reference class — all observers, only observers with their own cognitive makeup, only members of their assigned species — and watch the optimal bet shift; or perhaps you run a variant that lets them compare the self-sampling assumption with the self-indication assumption and see how the same indexical evidence points in different directions depending on how heavily possible observers are weighted. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the Bayesian core of the doomsday argument, the role of the prior over long-lived versus short-lived hypotheses, and the reference-class problem, and it should be explicit about what the game cannot reproduce — above all, that real history is a single unrepeatable draw and that participants' priors about humanity's future cannot be manufactured by the rules. By the end, I want them to see the doomsday argument not as doom-mongering but as a constraint on how to combine a mundane indexical fact about themselves with a prior they already hold. Finally, tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it — I suspect players will confuse the doomsday move with the gambler's fallacy or with a "why me?" specialness intuition — and how to handle those confusions in the moment.
