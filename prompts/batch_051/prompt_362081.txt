I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm drafting a novel set in a fictional country just coming out of a decades-long internal war. The whole plot turns on an amnesty clause in the peace settlement, and I've realized I can't actually say what "amnesty" means, or how it differs from a pardon, a reduced sentence, or just plain impunity. I need a working definition that gets the following cases right. Some of them I'm confident about; the last few are exactly why I'm stuck.

1. Before any prosecutions begin, the legislature passes a law extinguishing criminal liability for a whole class of politically defined offenses. No application, no admission of guilt, no conditions. (I take this to be a clear case.)
2. After a particular person is tried and convicted, the president commutes the rest of his sentance as a one-off act of mercy. (This feels like a pardon, not an amnesty, but I want the definition to say why.)
3. A truth commission procedure: a perpetrator may apply, and if he fully discloses his crimes in public, the state agrees not to prosecute him for those acts. (Sort of the South African design, but I'm asking about the concept, not the history.)
4. A commander signs a ceasefire on the promise that he won't be prosecuted. Six months later a court annuls the promise and indicts him. Was he amnestied for six months, or was that never really an amnesty?
5. A law extinguishes criminal liability for political crimes but explicitly keeps civil claims alive, so victims can still sue. Is that still an amnesty, or only half of one?
6. A state cancels penalties for people who repatriate undeclared offshore money. Nobody gets hurt; it's pure tax revenue. Is "tax amnesty" the same concept, or is the word being borrowed metaphorically here?
7. The outgoing regime writes itself a blanket immunity into the constitution on its way out the door. No disclosures, no process, no one else included. Is a self-amnesty a genuine amnesty that happens to be illegitimate, or does the self-dealing push it out of the category entirely?

I have a hunch the word is doing two jobs at once: a legal job, extinguishing liability before or without a conviction, and a broader social job, collective forgetting, a line drawn under the past. I'd like the definition to either unify those or cleanly separate them.

So: give me the definition, run it through all seven cases, tell me which verdicts it forces you to give that surprise you, or that you think I should resist, and if the definition breaks on one of these, say so and revise it rather than pretending it works. And please answer in American English, my copyeditor will thank you.
