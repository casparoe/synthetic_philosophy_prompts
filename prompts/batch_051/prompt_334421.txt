I've been teaching myself Bayesian confirmation theory and I've got into a knot about something that feels back-to-front. The rule, as I understand it, is that a piece of evidence confirms a hypothesis only if the evidence is more to be expected under the hypothesis than it would be otherwise — the hypothesis has to make the evidence more likely. But in actual science, a lot of the support a theory gets comes from facts everyone knew before the theory was ever thought of. The odd behaviour of Mercury's orbit was known for decades before general relativity came along; nobody was sitting around unsure where Mercury was. So when you feed that evidence into the rule, the evidence already has probability 1, and no hypothesis can make it more probable than certain — the maths says it should count for nothing. Yet everyone treats general relativity's success with Mercury as a big point in its favour. I keep seeing the same shape elsewhere: a doctor's diagnosis seems supported by symptoms she knew before she'd even considered the disease. Is there a name for this problem? And is it the same thing as the claim that predictions count for more than explanations fitted after the fact? They feel connected but I can't quite seperate them, and I'd rather know where the boundary is than just get a label.
