I am drafting a position paper on the ethics of radical self-modification — cognitive enhancement, mood enhancement, substantial lifespan extension, and changes to the substrate of the mind — and I want a consistency check on the view I have been assembling before I finalize the argument. I am committed to all of the following, but I have not yet worked out how they interact, and I do not want to discover in the Q&A that they cannot be held together.

(a) The only thing that ultimately matters, when we ask whether a self-directed change is worth undergoing or permitting, is the person's own well-being, understood as the quality of their experience together with the satisfaction of suitably informed desires across the whole of their life.

(b) The distinction between restoring someone to a species-typical baseline and enhancing them beyond it has no independent moral weight; an intervention is not made more objectionable merely by being an enhancement rather than a treatment.

(c) There is a strong, though not absolute, reason to pursue — or at least to permit — any self-modification that can reasonably be expected to improve the person's whole-life well-being and that does not wrong others.

(d) The modified individual is literally the same person as before just in case there is sufficient psychological continuity — overlapping chains of memories, values, goals, and dispositions — whether or not the biological substrate changes.

(e) Some modifications that would raise well-being would nonetheless destroy or attenuate goods whose value is not exhausted by their contribution to well-being: effortful achievement, vulnerability to others, the lived acceptance of limits, and forms of intimacy tied to our present embodiment.

(f) Some modifications are transformative in the strict sense that one cannot know in advance what it would be like to be the modified person. For those modifications, the decision cannot be governed by expected well-being; it must be grounded in whether the change expresses or preserves values, attachments, and commitments one already endorses in one's present condition.

I am not asking for a general survey of enhancement ethics. I want an audit of this specific set. Which commitments are genuinely in tension with one another, and which pairs only seem to be? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious. For each, say what has to give and what the least costly revision to the set as a whole would be, including what I would have to give up or reinterpret if the set is to be made consistent.
