Consider the scorekeeping picture of conversation, on which talk is governed by a common ground that is adjusted by the conversational scoreboard. The picture includes a rule of accommodation: when an utterance presupposes something not yet in the common ground, hearers often quietly add the missing proposition, provided it is not contradictory or conspicuous enough to object to. A friend has argued that this commits us to something absurd: the common ground can be edited at will by means of unobtrusive presuppositions. The argument runs as follows.

(1) An utterance of a sentence is appropriate relative to a common ground only if the sentence's presuppositions are already entailed by the common ground.
(2) But when a presupposition is missing, hearers do not generally challenge the utterance; they accommodate. If someone says that even John passed, the audience simply updates the context so that John was among the least likely to pass, without demanding that this have been established.
(3) Such accommodation does not require assertion, argument, or explicit acceptance; it occurs automatically as part of updating the score.
(4) Therefore, any proposition P that is consistent with the current common ground and unobtrusive enough to figure as a presupposition can be added to the common ground by uttering a sentence whose presupposition is P.
(5) A sequence of individually innocuous utterances could thus load the common ground with a substantial body of propositions that nobody ever asserted, argued for, or endorsed.
(6) But a common ground that can be filled by stealth is not a record of what the participants mutually accept; it is merely a record of what nobody happened to block. So the scorekeeping model seems to undermine the very notion of common ground it was introduced to explain.

Every premise looks plausible to me, and the conclusion does not. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?
