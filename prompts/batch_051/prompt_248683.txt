I'm a retired physicist who's been reading Schopenhauer for the last year or two, and I've been playing with an idea I call "second-order striving." I'd like you to take on the role of a professional philosopher who works on Schopenhauer and the philosophy of action, and to think carefully about it before answering.

Here's the claim. Schopenhauer's will is blind striving that's never finally satisfied, and the usual way people put that is that each achieved aim just gives rise to a new one. But I want to say something a bit stronger: the will doesn't primarily aim at its objects at all — it aims at the continuation of willing itself. So for every first-order striving (finish the book, get the job, fix the boat), there's a second-order striving that the first-order striving not be permanently extinguished. That makes actually getting what you want a small structural threat to the system, and I think it explains a lot: hedonic adaptation, the empty feeling after finishing a long project, the way people immediately raise the bar, self-sabotage right before the finish line, the urge to reopen solved questions. I also think it gives a cleaner reading of Schopenhauer's denial of the will: the will can only stably turn on itself because taking itself as its own object is the one case where first-order and second-order striving coincide.

I know it sounds like Frankfurt's second-order desires, and I'm aware its probably just Schopenhauer re-heated with a physicist's habit of looking for feedback loops everywhere. Still, I want an honest assessment. First steelman it, then tell me what it gets right, whether it's genuinely distinct from Frankfurtian second-order volitions and from Schopenhauer's own pain/boredom alternation, and give me the two or three places where it breaks or says something false.
