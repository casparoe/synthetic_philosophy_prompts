You are an expert on inverse reinforcement learning (IRL), on the formal difficulties of inferring preferences from observed behavior, and on philosophical debates about AI value alignment. Think carefully and first brainstorm at least two candidate game mechanics before committing to the strongest one; the design should make the idea arise from what participants do, not from what I tell them afterward.

I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of AI and value alignment. There are fourteen students, and I have one 90-minute session. Design a classroom game with the working title What Did She Want? that makes participants feel the force of a central IRL problem: observed behavior underdetermines the preferences we attribute to a person, so inferring human values from behavior is never a neutral read-off. It always smuggles in assumptions about the agent's rationality, risk attitude, discounting, planning horizon, or the space of possible rewards. No lecture up front, and the technical vocabulary should not appear until the debrief.

The activity should run as follows. In a first phase, a few participants act as humans making choices in small, concrete decision scenarios: a short route through a simple grid, selecting tasks in a lab, choosing snacks at a counter, or allocating an hour among activities. The rest of the class works in teams as reward engineers who observe only the choices and must infer each chooser's underlying preferences. Make the first scenario feel mechanical and solvable, then let it break down: two or more genuinely different preference profiles should fit the same observed choices equally well, and the teams should be forced to defend rival inferences against one another. In a second phase, change exactly one modeling assumption while keeping the observed behavior the same, for example the assumed choice rule, such as a soft-optimality rule with a temperature parameter versus risk-sensitive or worst-case reasoning, the agent's discount rate, or the allowed hypothesis space for rewards. Structure the phase so teams experience their earlier inference flipping or becoming unrecoverable. Use invented scenarios rather than real datasets, paraphrase rather than quoting anything from the literature, and include no citations, bibliographies, or readings.

Specify the full design: group sizes and roles, the exact instructions you would give participants aloud, all materials needed, a minute-by-minute timeline filling 90 minutes, and a scoring rule that produces real engagement without using real money. Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto specific IRL concepts: soft-optimality or maximum-entropy inference, the temperature or rationality parameter, discounting, reward degeneracy or unidentifiability, and the choice of reward hypothesis space. The debrief should clearly separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among researchers. For example, it is settled that a single observed policy is compatible with many reward functions, and that the inferred reward changes when the assumed rationality model changes; it is contested whether we should infer a person's actual preferences, expressed values, or idealized preferences, and whether soft-optimality assumptions are a good default for human behavior. Do not present contested positions as consensus.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: at least three failure modes with concrete recovery moves, for instance teams refusing to commit to an inference, scenarios being so slack that everything fits, or teams copying one another's answers. End with an honest note about what the game cannot reproduce about real-world preference inference and what I should supply in framing to compensate. I suspect the limitation concerns the tidiness of small discrete scenarios versus the high-dimensional, temporally extended, partly non-consequentialist character of actual human behavior, but tell me whatever the real limitation is.
