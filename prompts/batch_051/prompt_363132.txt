I am giving a department colloquium defending the following thesis: when a person exercises power over someone else, or knowingly lets others rely on his future conduct, he incurs a pro tanto moral duty to be legible to them — to state, in terms they could evaluate, what values and decision procedure guide his choices — and not merely to be honest about the local facts. I argue that legibility is distinct from candor and from predictability: a person can avoid lying and still wrong those who must coordinate around him by keeping his principles, intentions, and weighting of reasons opaque. The argument has three parts. From respect, opacity about the rule one is applying treats the other as a system to be managed rather than as an agent able to contest the rule. From reliance, if I let others build their plans around my conduct, I owe them the interpretive resources they need, much as a host owes guests enough understanding of the household to move safely through it. From justification, a moral community is one in which principles are offered rather than merely enforced, and a standing refusal to offer them ranks people into those worth persuading and those merely administered. I concede that the duty is defeasible, can be satisfied in degrees, and does not require total self-disclosure or perfect self-knowledge; it can be outweighed by privacy, self-protection, bad-faith audiences, and unreasonable cost. I am not defending a legal duty or an enforcement mechanism.

The audience is a philosophy department whose center of gravity is Kantian constructivism of the Rawls/Korsgaard sort; there are also two political liberals worried about reasonable pluralism and whether a duty of self-articulation unfairly burdens those whose moral lives are not arranged around explicit principles, plus one virtue ethicist suspicious of all purely procedural duties. There is no applied ethics and no technology person in the room.

I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis: the seven objections I should actually expect from this audience rather than from a generic discussion of transparency, ordered by how likely and how damaging each would be, phrased the way a Rawlsian or a Korsgaardian would phrase it. For each, give me a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. End with the question I should be most afraid someone will ask.
