Okay, here's one I'd like you to actually work through, not just gesture at. Take the Challenger launch decision in January 1986 and analyze it through Good's theorem on the value of information. I'm assuming you know the rough story: the night before the launch, engineers at Morton Thiokol recommended against launching because they were worried the O-rings would lose resilience in the cold, and they had data suggesting a correlation between low temperature and O-ring erosion; Thiokol management reversed that recommendation under pressure from NASA, and the shuttle launched in unusually cold weather and broke apart. I don't need a retelling of the engineering.

Here's what I want. Good's theorem says, roughly, that for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer, the expected value of additional information is never negative: if the information is free, you should be willing to at least look at it before acting, and in expectation it can't hurt you and might change your action for the better. Spell out what the theorem says the actors should have done the night before launch. Who exactly is the "decision-maker" in the theorem's sense — Thiokol? NASA? the launch director? — and does it matter that the information and the decision were split across organizations? Map the options, the relevant evidence, and the payoffs the way the theorem would have us do it, and say what the theorem predicts about whether the decision would have changed if the engineers' information had actually been treated as information.

Then I want the honest part. The theorem rests on assumptions that look almost free but aren't here: the information wasn't free (a delay had schedule and political costs), the decision-makers payoffs weren't the same as the crew's or the program's, and there's a difference between having information and having it accepted by the person who acts. So tell me where Good's theorem is doing real work in this case and where it just talks past it. In particular: does the case show the theorem is trivial or misapplied, or does it show that the decision procedure violated the conditions under which the theorem is actually an argument — and which diagnosis do you think is right?

End by saying what the episode teaches us about the theorem itself. Is this a case where the value of information was positive but ignored, or a case where the theorem's assumptions were violated so badly that it doesn't even get a grip? Could a version of the theorem handle agency problems — where the person who holds the information has different incentives than the person who decides — or does it have to stay silent there?
