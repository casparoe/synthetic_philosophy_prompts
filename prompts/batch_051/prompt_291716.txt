I want a statement of the standard by which proposed treatments of two neighboring epistemological problems should be judged, set down before we commit to any particular theory. Please prepare the desiderata for an adequate account of the bootstrapping problem and the problem of easy knowledge: a theory of how first-order perceptual knowledge and second-order knowledge about the reliability of one's sources are related. The account need not be a complete epistemology, but it must speak to the following phenomena and must not resolve them by fiat.

First, the phenomena. (1) Basic perceptual knowledge. A subject in good light sees a red wall and forms the belief that the wall is red. Nothing is abnormal, and we are not willing on merely skeptical grounds to deny that she knows it, even if she has no independent evidence that her color vision is reliable. (2) Easy knowledge by deduction. From her belief that the animal in the enclosure is a zebra she can deduce that it is not a cleverly disguised mule; in a related move, from her belief that the wall is red, together with her knowledge that she formed that belief on the strength of how things looked, she can infer that her color experience did not mislead her on this occasion. If knowledge is closed under known entailment, deductions of this kind seem to convert an ordinary deliverance of the senses into second-order reassurance about the senses themselves, with no check performed along the way. (3) Bootstrapping by track record. A subject forms beliefs by a process—a driver reading her fuel gauge, say—and then infers from the pattern of those very readings that the process is reliable. The reasoning is epistemically circular: the credentials of each data point already presuppose the reliability of the process whose reliability the track record is supposed to establish, and the inference would feel equally compelling to the subject whether or not the process was in fact reliable.

The obvious exits are all costly: deny the basic perceptual knowledge; deny closure without a principled account of why deduction from known premises transmits warrant in some cases but not these; allow the second-order conclusions and thereby make knowledge of the reliability of one's sources available for free; or allow bootstrapping and thereby let a source ratify itself. I want the criteria before I consider any exit.

Then set out the following. (a) The desiderata, ordered roughly by importance. For each, state the case or example that motivates it and say whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as a default that might be surrendered for a large enough gain. Pay attention to distinctions any adequate theory must respect—at least between first-order and second-order justification and between inferential and non-inferential justification—and say what goes wrong with a view that collapses them. (b) Flag any pairs of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied by one theory; articulate the source of each tension precisely, since the content may lie as much in the inconsistent list as in the entries taken one by one. (c) Identify which desideratum does the most work excluding otherwise attractive views, which demands in the literature are merely local preferences of one research program, and which constraints concern the extension of the term "knowledge" rather than sound epistemic practice. (d) Briefly run three kinds of candidate through the list: straightforward process reliabilism (knowledge as true belief produced by a reliable process, with no independent evidence requirement); an experience-first view on which a perceptual experience with content p confers immediate prima facie justification for p absent defeaters; and a view on which knowledge requires the elimination of relevant alternatives or the satisfaction of a modal condition such as sensitivity or safety. For each, note the desiderata it meets and, more importantly, the ones that appear to force its rejection.
