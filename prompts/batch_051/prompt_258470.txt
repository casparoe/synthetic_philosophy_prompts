I spent my career in the natural sciences, where I was trained to regard a classification as worth keeping only if it tracks distinctions that are there in the world rather than merely convenient for the classifier. Since retiring, I have been reading philosophy and the history of science, and I have found the same instinct pulling against much received historical periodisation.

The claim I have in mind is that era labels such as 'the Middle Ages', 'the Renaissance', and 'the Scientific Revolution' are retrospective inventions rather than discoveries. On this view, they were imposed by later historians and polemicists for pedagogical or polemical purposes; they do not track any especially sharp or robust discontinuity in the historical record; and they would not have been recognised by the people supposedly living through them as the natural divisions of their own time. If the claim is right, asking when the Scientific Revolution began is less like asking when a mountain range was formed and more like asking where a county border falls.

I should like to know how far this claim can be brought under empirical discipline, and I ask that your answer be written in British English.

Please take whichever of the three labels you judge most tractable and design the best test you can of the proposition that it is a retrospective construction rather than the detection of a real historical rupture. I do not expect a decisive test, since the word 'real' is carrying philosophical weight here; the interesting question is where the testable content runs out. In your answer, do the following.

First, state exactly what evidence you would gather, and specify which observations would count as confirming the retrospective-construction claim, which would count against it, and which pattern would leave you unwilling to draw either conclusion.

Second, say how you would guard against a test that merely rediscovers what it was designed to find—for example, by choosing indicators already known to change at the conventional boundary, or by treating that boundary as the null hypothesis.

Third, mark the residue clearly: explain precisely what a favourable result would and would not establish about whether the period is real in any deeper sense, and identify the part of the question that no empirical test could settle even in principle.

To fix ideas, let me offer the crude test I would have proposed as an outsider, which you may correct or replace. In the sciences, one often distinguishes a genuine regime from a classificatory artefact by looking for synchronised, abrupt change across many independent measures—a phase transition. By analogy, one could take a candidate boundary, assemble a large number of independent time series of change (in institutions, vocabulary, techniques, styles of argument, publication practices, and the like), and compare how strongly change clusters at the conventional dates with how strongly it clusters at 'control' dates chosen without reference to the conventional scheme. If the conventional boundary does no better than the controls, that would seem to count against the label's being a discovery; if it does markedly better, that would seem to count in its favour. I can already see that the choice of indicators, and the weighting among them, presupposes some prior view of what the period was supposed to be. Please improve on this sketch rather than simply executing it, and tell me exactly what the improved test would and would not settle.
