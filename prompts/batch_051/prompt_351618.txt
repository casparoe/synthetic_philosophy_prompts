A friend of mine in philosophy of physics keeps insisting that the way quantum mechanics handles identical particles — the 1/N! in the partition function, the symmetrization of the wavefunction, the way two identical photons always bunch at a beam splitter while two identical electrons always split apart, the fact that a box of identical gas gains no mixing entropy when you pull out an imaginary internal partition — is not just a mathmatical fix for our inability to tell particles apart. He says it shows something stronger: that two helium atoms or two electrons are not really two things, at least not in the way two coins are two things, because there is genuinely no fact of the matter about which is which. A hard-nosed chemist I know says that's overheated: we just have two things that happen to be exactly alike in every observable property, so quantum mechanics wisely throws away labels that would only produce false predictions.

I want to know whether an experiment could actually pull these two readings apart, or whether I've got a purely verbal dispute on my hands. Give me the strongest single test you can design (a real measurement or observation, not an armchair argument), say what each possible outcome would favor, and be very clear about what even your best test leaves untouched.

Format the response as four sections, in this order and roughly these lengths:

- **Two readings** (100–150 words): state each position precisely enough that it's clear what would have to be true for them to come apart.
- **The strongest test** (200–250 words): describe the setup, the measurement, the predicted result under each reading, and what a positive or negative result would count as.
- **What the test would and would not settle** (150–200 words).
- **The residue** (100–150 words): the part of the disagreement that no observation can reach, and why.
