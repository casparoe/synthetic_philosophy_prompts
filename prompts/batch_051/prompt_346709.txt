Two postdocs in my lab group, Maya and Julian, have spent the past week arguing about a preregistered replication I helped collect data for, and I couldn't referee them. The original study, from a small exploratory sample, reported d = 0.45, p = 0.028, 95% CI [0.05, 0.85]. The replication, with roughly twice the sample, found d = 0.20, p = 0.16, 95% CI [–0.08, 0.48]. Maya says the effect failed to replicate: the point estimate shrank by more than half, the interval now crosses zero, and the original was probably a small-sample false positive inflated by p-hacking. Julian calls this the oldest mistake in the intro statistics sequence: treating non-significance as evidence of absence. He points out that the replication interval still includes the original estimate, so the two studies are statistically compatible in the standard interval-overlap sense, and p = .16 only means the new data are not surprising under the null.

Maya came back that compatibility is too weak a standard. Once she updates from a skeptical prior that puts most of its mass on small effects, she says, the replication data move her posterior strongly toward negligible effect; and once she adds in the base rate—how likely a barely significant exploratory result is to be a false positive in a file-drawer-heavy literature—she assigns low credence to a real effect of the original size. Julian replied that the skeptical prior is doing all the work: with an imprecise prior, the posterior mean would sit near .20 and most posterior mass would stay above zero, so the data do not force a no-effect conclusion. He added that "probably zero" is exactly what p = .16 cannot license. Maya said she never claimed the null should be accepted, only that confidence in the original effect should drop sharply; that is a posterior claim and does not need significance testing's permission. Julian answered that this more nuanced conclusion may be right, but it is not what "the replication failed" means, so Maya is overstating what the replication alone shows. They also never agreed on what should count as a successful replication in the first place.

Please give a move-by-move adjudication. For each specific claim—that the effect failed to replicate, that non-significance is not evidence of absence, that the two studies are compatible, that the skeptical prior versus an imprecise prior does most of the work, that base rates should lower confidence, and that Maya is overstating—say who is right, who is right only partially, and who is overstating. Then say what both of them are missing and give an overall verdict on whose description of what the replication shows is closer to honest.
