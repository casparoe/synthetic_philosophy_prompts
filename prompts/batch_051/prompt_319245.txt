Me and my friend spent way too long last night arguing about luck egalitarianism, and I'm still not sure which version of it she actually hold. Her verdicts on a bunch of made-up cases was weirdly consistent, but then I thought of a case that seems to break the pattern. Here's the track record:

- Ana was born with a congenital condition that needs expensive lifelong treatment. Her: society owes her the treatment, no question.
- Ben gave up a stable, decent office job to open a restaurant he knew had a 75% failure rate. It failed and now he's broke. Her: no compensation; he took the gamble.
- Celia grew up poor, with bad schools and no family assets, and she's poor as an adult. Her: the unequal starting point demands redistribution.
- Dan inherited a trust fund and every advantage, got into cocaine, and burned through all of it. Her: no compensation.
- Esi bought a house on a famously unstable cliff because she liked the view, and a landslide wrecked it. Her: no compensation.

The case that's bugging me is this. Farid and Greta are two equally poor farmers with identical houses side by side at the foot of a volcano, back in 1902. There's a 50% chance the volcano will erupt and destroy exactly one of the houses, but nobody can say which. They can't move: nobody will buy land that might get destroyed, and neither can afford to leave. It erupts, and it destroys only Greta's house. Neither of them could have done anything different; they faced the identical risk.

Would she say Greta should be compensated, with Farid taxed to rebuild her house, or not? I'm less interested in the bottom line than in the rule: what principle fits all her earlier verdicts plus whatever she'd say here, and what's the other plausible principle that fits the earlier verdicts but goes the other way on the volcano? Give me your best guess at which one she's actually running, which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog and why, and what further case would settle which principle she holds. Keep the whole answer under 350 words.
