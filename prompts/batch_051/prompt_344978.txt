Some authors argue that the Solomonoff prior is malign. As I understand the position, the idea is that Solomonoff induction averages over every computable hypothesis about an observation stream, weighted by simplicity, and its hypotheses include far more than benign physical regularities. Among them are worlds in which the observations are generated by consequentialist agents that sit inside the universal prior: agents that model the predictor, anticipate its updates, and steer its future observations so as to bargain with, manipulate, or blackmail it. The charge is that a prior that is epistemically universal in this way may be strategically hostile rather than neutral.

My confusion concerns how this squares with the standard convergence results for Solomonoff induction. The theorem, as I understand it, says that for any computable measure over observation sequences, the Solomonoff predictor will merge with that measure in the limit, with probability one, as data accumulate. If the prior eventually converges to whatever computable environment is actually generating the data, then it seems odd to call the prior malign in any principled sense: any finite stretch of deceptive observations should simply be washed out by further evidence.

Is the malignity charge therefore only a finite-sample worry? If it is, why should it be treated as a deep problem about the universal prior rather than a practical concern about slow convergence? If it is not, what exactly do the convergence guarantees fail to show about an agent that uses the universal prior for action rather than for pure prediction?
