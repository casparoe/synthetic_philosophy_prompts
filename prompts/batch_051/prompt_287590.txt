We treat curiosity as a virtue and spend public money on knowledge for its own sake, yet in private life we often treat the same disposition as nosiness, prurience, or a harmless failure. A planetary scientist who reconstructs the geology of a world nobody will ever visit is admired and funded; a person who quietly learns everything about the lives of the people on her street is viewed with suspicion; a colleague who wants to know what everyone says about him behind his back is called morbid or insecure; an uncle who memorizes the complete results of a defunct football league is indulged but faintly pitied. Each is pursuing true beliefs with no obvious practical payoff. Is there a principled difference among them, or is calling curiosity a virtue mostly a way of honoring the curiosities we already find respectable? Give the strongest version of the candidate distinctions — significance of the object, structure (understanding versus isolated facts), method, motive — and test them against awkward cases: curiosity about serial killers with no theoretical interest, systematic and careful research into something flatly trivial, a field of pure mathematics whose own practitioners expect no application, a child's insistent why-questions. If the distinctions collapse into "we simply value some objects more than others," say whether you would keep the asymmetry, drop the praise of curiosity, or give up the condemnation of the snoop — and whether a smaller, less self-congratulatory idea of curiosity as a virtue is left standing.
