I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I've been chewing on a line from Thucydides, from the History of the Peloponnesian War, Book 1, chapter 20, in the Crawley translation: "The way that most men deal with traditions, even traditions of their own country, is to receive them all alike as they are delivered, without applying any critical test whatever."

I keep coming back to it because I think there's a homemade concept hiding in there that I've been calling "default credence." The idea is that when we encounter a claim about the distant past, the baseline is to provisionally accept it unless something specific interrupts us. Testing takes energy and a motive, so most inherited claims never get tested. They pile up as an unreviewed background that laypeople and journalists treat as settled, while historians only actively debate a small top layer. For instance, I can list a dozen things I "know" about the Roman Empire or the American founding that I have never actually checked, and I doubt most of them are held because of evidence — they're held because of delivery.

Is "default credence" a real mechanism, or am I just relabeling something that already has a name in historiography or cognitive psychology? Is it even true that the default is credence rather than distrust — or does the default flip depending on who the source is? And where does the idea break: are there cases where something got tested precisely because it was delivered widely, not despite it? Give me the three strongest objections and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than just uncomfortable.
