In my risk-ethics class we've been working with micromorts (one micromort is just a one-in-a-million chance of dying). The public-health people use them to turn everything into a single death-risk number: a few hundred miles of driving is about one micromort, a single scuba dive is a handful, and then we ask whether ordinary stuff like driving is "worth it." I can't shake the feeling that this practice is presupposing way more than it says. It seems to assume the only thing that matters about a risk is the probability of dying, that an involuntary background risk and a risk you volunteer for count the same, and that a quick death in a crash and a slow death from a disease are interchangable for the purposes of advice. What does the whole micromort-comparison practice actually presuppose about what death and risk are? Is that presupposition coherent, and what would have to change in how agencies compare risks and advise people if we rejected it?
