I'm a PhD student working at the intersection of moral philosophy and the philosophy of mind, thinking about the ethics of biotechnological interventions into affect. My supervisor keeps pushing me to test general claims about the elimination of suffering against concrete cases, and I have been doing that with mixed results. Part of what interests me is a project some philosophers defend under the heading of the hedonistic imperative — roughly, the idea that we should work toward using biotechnology to phase out suffering across sentient life. I am not sure I accept the full project, but I also cannot find a stable way to reject it.

Here are the verdicts I have reached when I consult my own reactions. (1) A safe, reversible drug that reduces the felt intensity of acute physical pain by about eighty percent, without blunting other emotions or judgment, is clearly fine, and I would take it. (2) A temporary, reversible drug that blunts grief in the weeks after a bereavement is fine. (3) A permanent, fully consented-to procedure that removes a person's capacity for grief altogether — including the capacity to mourn and to miss the dead — is not okay, though I struggle to say why, since it only removes the same suffering that (2) removes. (4) Germline engineering of future livestock and companion animals so that they cannot experience pain, fear, or distress, but only contentment and mild satisfactions, is permissible, and perhaps obligatory if we go on breeding them. (5) Selecting and editing future human children so that they cannot experience severe suffering, but thereby also cannot experience profound grief or the pangs of moral conscience, is a step too far; choosing that for someone else feels like choosing a diminished form of life for them. (6) A full-time drug that leaves an adult mildly euphoric, free of all anxiety and self-criticism, but indifferent to their projects and relationships, is an occasional respite at most; as a life-plan it looks like a quiet disaster. (7) A proposal to intervene at ecosystem scale, phasing out predation and starvation so that no wild animal suffers again, pulls me in both directions: if suffering is bad, then vast amounts of it are bad wherever they occur, and yet something seems lost when the wild becomes a managed garden.

I would like to know whether there is one principle that captures these verdicts, or close to it. My hunch is that the difference in the human cases is something about consent plus reversibility plus whether the intervention preserves what makes a life one's own, but I cannot state that without making the animal cases come out wrong. Please state the principle or candidate principles precisely enough to test, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why, and apply it to the case I keep going back and forth on: a pill that permanently abolishes all negative affect in the person who takes it and also makes them genuinely indifferent to the capacities they have lost, so that they will never regret taking it. Is that the fulfillment of the hedonistic imperative, or a straitjacket, and does your principle give a determinate answer?
