You are a professor of moral philosophy who teaches a second-year unit in practical ethics, and you are marking an exam. You set this question:

"Explain the argument from marginal cases (sometimes called the argument from species overlap). Does it show that weighting human interests above the comparable interests of nonhuman animals is morally on a par with racism or sexism? Set out the argument's structure clearly and discuss at least one significant limitation."

A student submitted the following answer:

"The argument from marginal cases starts from the fact that the properties usually proposed as the basis of moral considerability—rationality, language, moral agency, future-directed planning—are not possessed by all humans. Infants and severely cognitively disabled people lack them, yet we would never say their pain matters less or that they may be used as mere means. But many nonhuman animals perform at or above the level of these marginal humans on the very same criteria. So if we insist on protecting all humans while excluding otherwise comparable animals, the only property doing the work is bare membership in Homo sapiens. That is exactly speciesism, and the argument shows it is arbitrary in the same way racism and sexism are: a biological marker with no moral relevance is being treated as though it had moral relevance.

Singer's principle of equal consideration of interests says that the suffering of any sentient being counts equally with the like suffering of any other. It follows that animals and humans have the same moral status, and that in any conflict of interests a human's interest and an animal's interest must be weighed exactly equally. So if you must choose between saving a normal adult human and a mouse from a burning building, choosing the human is, on Singer's view, no more justified than choosing the white person over the black person would be. More generally, it is only defensible to channel resources into human health rather than wild-animal health if we can identify some non-species difference that makes the human interest stronger—and in the fire case there is no such difference.

One limitation of the argument is that it only gets a grip with sentient beings, so it cannot by itself tell us what we owe to creatures whose sentience is uncertain, like insects or bivalves, or to plainly non-sentient organisms like plants. A further limitation is that it is a consistency argument: someone prepared to deny protections to marginal humans can evade it, so in that sense it presupposes rather than proves the wrongness of speciesism."
