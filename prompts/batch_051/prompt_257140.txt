Give me a working definition of the etymological fallacy—roughly, the move from a word's origin or older sense to a conclusion about what it really means now—and test it against these cases. An editor says 'decimate' can only mean reduce by one tenth because the Romans killed every tenth man. A party guest says 'nice' really means foolish, since it comes from Latin nescius, so calling someone nice is secretly an insult. A historian notes that 'hysteria' is from the Greek for uterus and that this etymology shaped nineteenth-century medical views. A poet plays on 'companion' as someone you break bread with. A lawyer argues that a statute's 'domicile' must mean a physical house because of Latin domus, even though courts have long used it for a legal residence. Someone says the true meaning of 'democracy' is rule by the people because demos plus kratos, so countries with low turnout arent real democracies. A toastmaster says 'sincere' comes from sine cera, without wax, so sincerity literally means having no cracks to hide. Run the definition against each, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdict. Then tell me which classification is least secure, and keep seperate what's settled among people who actually study this from what's genuinely contested—don't present a contested view as the consensus.
