I am a retired microbiologist who has recently turned to philosophy of biology, and I have been puzzling over biological individuality, especially the growing talk of holobionts, colonies, and superorganisms. I have constructed the following argument, and I cannot locate the fault in it, although I am unwilling to accept its conclusion. I would like a written diagnosis.

(1) An organism is a functionally integrated whole whose parts causally regulate one another so as to maintain the structure and functioning of the whole.

(2) The Portuguese man o' war is a colony of zooids, each zooid developmentally separate and homologous to a free-living animal in related species; it is not a single developmental individual.

(3) Yet its zooids are so specialized and mutually dependent — one kind feeding, one stinging, one reproducing, one forming the float — that none can survive or function alone; by (1), the colony is an organism.

(4) A honeybee colony likewise shows specialized castes, reproductive division of labour, and coordinated colony-level thermoregulation; by (1), it too is an organism, a superorganism.

(5) A human being together with its resident microbiome is integrated in the same way: gut microbes digest plant polysaccharides, synthesize vitamins, and calibrate the immune system, while the host supplies them a stable habitat and nutrients; neither functions normally without the other.

(6) Therefore, by (1), the human is not the organism we took it to be; the organism is the human-microbe holobiont.

(7) But if a honeybee colony and a human with its microbiome count as organisms, there is no principled stopping point: a lichen, a coral with its zooxanthellae, and eventually a human village kept alive by distributed labour would all qualify.

(8) So the term "organism" does not mark a real boundary in nature; it is an honorific we apply after the fact, and there are no organisms in nature.

I am not willing to accept (8), and I would most like to keep the view that there is a deep biological difference between a honeybee and its colony, and between a human and a village. Please tell me which premise or inference step to reject, explain why that step looked so compelling, and say what the main positions in the philosophy of biology would each have to sacrifice in rejecting it.

End with a final line of the form \answer{...} giving the number of the premise or inference step that should be rejected.
