I am a high school student, and I help produce my school's philosophy and ethics club end-of-term zine. This term our topic has been advance directives: living wills, do-not-resuscitate orders, and the broader question of whether a person's earlier, competent self should have authority over a later self who no longer shares those wishes. We have been stuck on a hypothetical in which a woman in her fifties signs an advance directive saying that if she develops advanced dementia and can no longer recognize her family, she refuses life-sustaining treatment. Twenty years later she has advanced dementia, cannot remember the document, and seems content: she responds warmly to the staff and protests, in the small ways available to her, when someone interferes with the things she likes. Some club members say the signed directive must govern because it expresses her considered, lasting will; others say the later self is the person actually in front of us, with her own interests, and that enforcing the old directive would be cruelty to someone who may no longer be the same person. I have volunteered to write a satirical piece for the zine.

I would like the piece to be a modest-proposal parody: a deadpan, earnestly argued policy essay of about 600 words, written in the voice of a reformer addressing a serious audience, who believes he has found the answer and does not realize that his answer is monstrous. The piece should follow the logic of the strongest version of the earlier-self view — roughly, that the competent person's settled values are her real will, and that the later self's resistance and contentment are symptoms of the disease rather than a refutation of the directive — and push that logic to a conclusion that makes the reader recoil. The humor should come from getting the philosophy right, not from mocking the patient or the condition. At some point the reformer should explain why the later self's small pleasures and protests carry no moral weight next to the earlier self's settled values; he should then have to explain, with complete consistency, what happens when the same reasoning is applied to non-pathological change, such as a much younger adult self whose values he now repudiates, a convert's former self, or the person someone was before falling in love; and he should gesture at the thought that the earlier self may in effect be a stranger legislating for a later person without ever recognizing why that is a problem. It should be readable by someone who has never encountered the debate, but someone who has argued about advance directives should recognize that the standard objections are being landed rather than evaded, and the piece should end on a note that leaves the reader feeling that the original case is genuinely hard.
