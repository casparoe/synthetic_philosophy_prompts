I teach an upper-year seminar called Markets, Prices, and Justice, and we just finished a unit on price controls. The midterm included a short essay question that a lot of the class found slippery, and I want a second set of eyes on one answer before I sign off on a grade. I'll paste the question and the answer below.

Question (I asked for roughly 500 words):
"Rent control and minimum wage laws are both price controls, but one is a ceiling and the other a floor, and they operate in very different markets. First, lay out the standard static efficiency analysis of each. Second, say who each policy is intended to protect and who actually tends to gain and lose. Third, make a judgement of your own: do the standard objections to rent control transfer to the minimum wage, or do the two cases differ in kind rather than only in degree?"

Student answer:
A binding rent ceiling of the classic "hard" sort is predicted to produce a persistent shortage: at the controlled price, the quantity of housing demanded exceeds the quantity landlords are willing to supply. Landlords respond by letting units decay, converting them to condos, exiting the rental market, or charging side payments like key money. Existing tenants hang onto apartments that no longer fit them while newcomers cannot find anything. The transfer from landlords to sitting tenants is not itself the efficiency problem, since that money is not lost, just moved. The real efficiency problem is the misallocation of a fixed stock of housing—a couple keeping a three-bedroom while a family of five doubles up—plus the mutually beneficial deals that get blocked by the ceiling.

The minimum wage is a price floor. In the competitive textbook model it prices some low-productivity workers out of the market: employers cut hours, substitute capital, or automate, and the least skilled workers cannot find a first job. The gainers are the workers who keep their jobs and earn more; the losers are the unemployed and first-time entrants. Here too the transfer from employers to retained workers is not what the efficiency argument complains about. The deadweight loss consists in the jobs that stop being worth creating.

The interesting question is whether the two policies differ in kind. I do not think they do. At first rent control looks more damning because its shortage is visible, while minimum-wage unemployment is scattered and easy to miss. But the empirical literature on the minimum wage has found that modest increases often have no detectable effect on employment, which is why most economists now treat the competitive model as wrong rather than merely as having small effects. Since rent control is just the mirror image of a minimum wage, the same methodological lesson should carry over: if labor markets are frequently monopsonistic, we should expect rental housing markets to be frequently monopolistic, and under monopoly a rent ceiling can actually expand the quantity of housing supplied. So the standard hostility toward rent control probably reflects the same overconfidence as the old hostility toward the minimum wage. Both policies are more defensible than the textbook picture lets on.
