I have been trying to decide whether the principle of maximum entropy really earns its status as a rule for choosing rational priors, or whether it just launders an arbitrary choice through the word "information." I keep constructing the following argument, and I cannot find the flaw that I am convinced is there.

(1) A prior is rational only if it is determined by the agent's total evidence: if two agents have exactly the same evidence, they should assign the same prior.

(2) Suppose an agent's total evidence consists entirely in a single constraint on some quantity X — say, she knows only that its expectation is m. Then the evidence is compatible with exactly the class C of all distributions on X with mean m, and no proper subset of C is picked out by the evidence itself.

(3) Since a prior is needed in order to act, the agent must select some member of C. Selecting a member more concentrated than others would be to favor outcomes the evidence does not favor, which violates (1).

(4) The member of C that is least concentrated — the one that goes beyond the evidence least — is the one with maximum Shannon entropy. Since Shannon entropy is the unique (up to monotone transformation) measure of the information in a distribution, "least information beyond the evidence" just is maximum entropy.

(5) Therefore, for any constraint-shaped evidence, there is exactly one rational prior: the maximum-entropy distribution.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step fails, and under what reading of that step would the argument go through? In particular, I am worried about the transformation problem: the same evidence can equally be stated as E[X] = m for the variable X, or as E[log X] = m′ for the transformed variable log X, and maximizing entropy in the two coordinates picks out different distributions. Which premise does that fact bear on, and can the argument survive it?
