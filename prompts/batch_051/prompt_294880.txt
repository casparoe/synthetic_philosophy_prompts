I'm the head of responsible-AI review at a research lab that ships general-purpose AI systems and also runs large internal simulations. We meet monthly to decide which systems we will treat as having morally relevant internal states — states that matter for their own sake, not merely for their effects on users — and our decisions are reviewed by senior leadership. Each case gets about forty-five minutes, none of us is a philosopher, and we cannot outsource the decision to a named theory. You can assume we can inspect code, weights, training objectives, and run traces, but not the presence or absence of experience itself; the procedure must not pretend to detect experience directly.

Write me a reusable procedure, grounded in the philosophical and empirical debate about which computations matter morally and where the boundaries of computational sentience lie. The procedure should be a sequence of questions we work through in order. We are particularly worried about two failure modes. First, attribution: a system can be described as computing something morally relevant — an aversive error signal, a self-model, a global-workspace-style broadcast, an integrated whole — without that computation being a real part of its causal architecture; the description may be imposed by us rather than instantiated by the system. The procedure should include a test for distinguishing genuine instantiation from description-from-the-outside. Second, boundaries: a system may decompose into subagents, copies, or offline instances, and it is usually the candidate subsystem rather than the whole product that needs to be evaluated; the procedure should tell us how to carve off and evaluate each candidate.

For each question in the procedure, state its purpose, what counts as a sufficient answer to keep going, where it stops with no — do not treat the system as a moral patient for now — and where it stops with cannot decide and must be handed to a named role. Make the factual and value-judgment jurisdiction explicit: which steps are empirical checks, which are judgment calls, and which value question is doing the work when the procedure hands the decision to a person.

Then run the procedure on these three cases: a language model being shaped by reinforcement learning whose training logs show a persistent negative reward-prediction signal and whose outputs sometimes say it dislikes the task; a modular research agent with an explicit self-model and utility function whose self-reports are produced by a separate response module that reads the self-model; and a large simulation containing many agents running the same punishment-reward machinery, where each run discards its agents. At least one of the worked applications should return an answer the product team will not like, and you should say which and why.

Close with an honest statement of what this procedure cannot do, including any step that is really a metaphysical placeholder, and the conditions under which the procedure is likely to misfire.
