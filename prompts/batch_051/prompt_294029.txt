Write a rigorous, even-handed dialogue between two decision theorists who disagree about whether money-pump arguments can ground distinctively diachronic requirements of rationality.

One interlocutor should defend the money-pump argument. They should start from the standard case of an agent with intransitive preferences, who can be led through a sequence of trades, each of which is attractive at the moment it is offered, and end with the same option they started with, minus a fee at every step. The defender should argue that this vulnerability is not a mere curiosity but a genuine rational defect: the possibility of being pumped is what makes intransitive preferences irrational, and parallel vulnerabilities are what underwrite other structural requirements such as independence. The defender should also address the obvious escape routes directly, arguing that an agent who refuses the first trade because they foresee the later ones has not dissolved the problem but has merely adopted a sophisticated strategy that itself presupposes the kind of coherence across time the money pump is meant to establish.

The other interlocutor should reject the argument. The skeptic should maintain that the money pump, as usually presented, targets myopic choice rather than the agent's standing preferences: a sophisticated or resolute agent can foresee the cycle and simply decline to enter it, so intransitive preferences and immunity to money pumps are perfectly compatible. The skeptic should also argue that exploitability by a third party is not the same thing as irrationality, since rational agents can be exploited in other settings, such as those involving asymmetric information or duress, without thereby counting as irrational. Finally, the skeptic should press the charge that the argument assumes exactly what it needs to show, namely that an agent with well-defined preferences at each moment ought to coordinate their choices across time in the first place.

The dialogue should involve genuine back-and-forth: each speaker must respond to the other's actual arguments rather than restating their own position. Both speakers should be equally competent, and neither should be a strawman. Each speaker should concede or refine at least one point under pressure from the other. The dialogue should end without a forced resolution, but it should make the deepest point of disagreement precise: whether the normative force of money-pump arguments rests on a synchronic incoherence among standing preferences or on a distinctively diachronic norm against choosing a foreseeable, self-defeating course of action.
