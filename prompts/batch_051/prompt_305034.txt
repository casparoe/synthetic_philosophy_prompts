I'm a retired chemist who's been reading philosophy for a few years, and a passage in Alan Turing's "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (Mind, 1950) has been bothering me. He writes:

"The new problem has the advantage of drawing a fairly sharp line between the physical and the intellectual capacities of a man. No engineer or chemist claims to be able to produce a material which is indistinguishable from the human skin. It is possible that at some time this might be done, but even supposing this invention available we should feel there was little point in trying to make a 'thinking machine' more human by dressing it up in such artificial flesh. The form in which we have set the problem reflects this fact in the condition which prevents the interrogator from seeing or touching the other competitors, or hearing their voices."

The part about chemists stings a little, but fine. What I want to understand is what commitments are built into current AI evaluation practice. Frontier models are routinely called "intelligent" or credited with "reasoning" after being tested through text-only chats or text-based benchmarks, with no one seeing, touching, or hearing the system. That looks like a direct institutional descendant of the imitation game's condition, but now it is often just the cheapest way to test. My question: what theory of mind, or of the relation between bodily capacities and thinking, does that practice presuppose? Does it genuinely presuppose Turing's sharp line between physical and intellectual capacities, or can someone run the practice while rejecting that line? And if we did reject the line, what specifically about current AI evaluation practices would have to change?
