I teach an upper-level seminar on the moral psychology of inequality, and one argument has recurred every year without ever moving. When we reach Rawls on envy, the same split appears. On his account, envy proper is not a moral sentiment — it is a vice, aimed at the greater good of others and corrosive of the person who feels it — while resentment is a moral feeling directed at wrongs; yet Rawls also allows that envy may be excusable when institutions permit inequalities that wound self-respect. That last clause reliably divides the room. One side takes it as a limited concession: envy remains a vice even where excusable, so the right response is to acknowledge the vice while also repairing the institutions that predictably provoke it. The other side takes the excusability to be doing the real philosophical work: if the attitude is a predictable response to unjust arrangements, then redescribing these complaints as envy already depoliticizes them, and many of the cases in question should instead be read as inchoate resentment, or as an institutional failure making itself felt in an individual mood. Everyone in the room agrees about the empirical facts concerning the inequality; nobody changes position.

My standing hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions under one word: one is evaluating an emotion as a feature of character, the other is reading it as information about the social order, and the same term, envy, is being asked to do both jobs at once. But I am unsure whether that diagnosis is right, and I suspect it may merely relocate the disagreement rather than resolve it. What kind of disagreement is this, actually — verbal, a hidden empirical question about the psychology of the emotion, a difference about standards of evaluation, or a bedrock intuition about whether an attitude can be both a vice and a signal of injustice? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a change of question, or a reason to stop expecting the two sides to converge?
