I work as a novelist, and over years of drafting I have begun to suspect that the relation between a reader and a novel is structurally the same as the relation between a player and a game in the account of play associated with Huizinga. In such an account, a game opens a temporary sphere in which actions have meanings they would not carry outside it. Capturing a piece in chess is not robbery; a goal is not vandalism. A player who breaks the rules is a cheater, not a lawbreaker, and someone who knocks over the board ceases to play rather than plays badly. The rules do not merely regulate behavior that could exist anyway; they constitute the moves, and the player takes the game seriously within the circle without confusing it with the world beyond it.

Fictional practice seems to me to have exactly this shape. The conventions of a genre and the established facts of a narrative set up a frame within which a twist can count as fair or cheap, an event can count as impossible for this story but not for another, and a line of dialogue can be taken as tragic without anyone being harmed. A reader does not telephone the police when a person is murdered on the page, yet the grief can be genuine; the practical stakes are suspended while a second-order seriousness remains. Drafting a scene feels eerily like making moves inside a circle of constraints I have partly inherited and partly built myself.

But I cannot tell whether this is a deep identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion. In a game, the rules are shared and binding on all players in the same way, whereas a novel's constitutive choices are made largely by a single writer and are revised across drafts. Rereading, skipping ahead, or reading the ending early are not treated as cheating the way moving an opponent's pieces in secret would be. If those differences matter, what does the play-parallel really license us to say about fiction? If the parallel is deep, what would have to be true on both sides for claims about play — for instance, that it is done for its own sake and is cut off from ordinary consequences — to transfer to the writing and reading of novels? And what is one thing the parallel would predict about fiction that turns out false? I want the mapping stated precisely and tested at its weak points, not simply judged attractive or strained.
