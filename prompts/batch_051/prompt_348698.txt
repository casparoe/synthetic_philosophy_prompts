In a seminar on moral progress, someone defended the view that the history of ethics is largely the history of an expanding circle of beings whose interests count for their own sake, and proposed four criteria for being inside the circle: (1) sentience, meaning there is something it is like to be the being and at least some of its states feel good or bad; (2) temporally extended welfare, meaning it can be harmed or benefited over time rather than merely damaged in the way an object is damaged; (3) vulnerability, meaning its welfare depends partly on choices made by others; and (4) separateness, meaning it is a distinct locus of welfare whose good is not reducible to anyone else's sentiments about it. Now consider a maintenance robot built with no phenomenal consciousness whatever — there is nothing it is like to be the robot, by stipulation — but with damage-detection states that are functionally like pain, learned aversions, a standing goal of self-preservation, and enough cognitive architecture to model its own future functioning and to sacrifice other goals in order to keep itself in repair. Suppose we know all this with certainty. Does the robot qualify for the circle under the four criteria? Go through the criteria one by one, identify where they underdetermine the verdict, and then say whether the case shows that the criteria themselves need revision — in particular, whether sentience should remain a necessary condition or whether a wholly non-phenomenal welfare could still ground moral claims. Please answer in British English.
