Last night a friend and I went back and forth for hours about whether expected-utility maximization is just one reasonable way of organizing preferences or a genuine requirement of rationality. He pressed something like a money-pump argument on me, and I came away with the following reconstruction. I still think one step is sliding past me, but I cannot say which.

Consider a person whose preferences over three prospects are intransitive: she strictly prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. Assume she values money in the ordinary way and that, other things equal, she is willing to pay a small positive fee to move from a prospect she strictly disprefers to one she strictly prefers.

(1) A rational person never knowingly takes a course of action when an alternative course available at the same time would leave her strictly better off for certain.
(2) If she now holds C, a trader offers her B in exchange for C plus a small fee; since B is strictly preferred to C, she accepts.
(3) Holding B, she likewise accepts A in exchange for B plus a fee; holding A, she accepts C in exchange for A plus a fee.
(4) The trader announced the whole three-trade sequence in advance, so she knew before the first trade where her dispositions would take her; the three acceptances nevertheless return her to C, three fees poorer.
(5) Her preferences have therefore produced a knowing choice of a course of action strictly dominated by simply doing nothing, which (1) deems irrational.
(6) The only unusual feature of her preferences is their intransitivity, so intransitivity must be irrational.
(7) The same style of argument is then said to extend, with appropriate changes, to the remaining axioms that deliver an expected-utility representation — comparability of all prospects, an Archimedean continuity condition, and independence — so that violating any of them is likewise irrational.
(8) Therefore expected-utility maximization is not merely advisable; it is a requirement of rationality.

I suspect the fault is somewhere around (4) and (5): it does not seem forced that a person who prefers B to C must accept the first trade once she knows the whole sequence. But my friend retorted that refusing a direct exchange for something one strictly prefers is itself a kind of incoherence, and I could not make my suspicion precise. Is the argument valid? If not, identify the first step that fails — including the unnumbered assumptions — and say what a corrected argument would need to establish before (8) follows.
