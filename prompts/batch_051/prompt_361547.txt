Having retired from a career in the natural sciences, I have recently been working through the philosophical and formal literature on decision under ignorance: the situation in which a single agent knows the possible states of the world and the payoff of every option in every state, but has no probabilities to assign to those states. I have noticed that the standard rules — maximin, minimax regret, the principle of indifference, and the various optimism–pessimism criteria — tend to be attacked or defended one example at a time, with each side producing a case in which the other's rule appears irrational. Before I read much further, I want the specification that seems to be missing. What must an adequate decision rule for this situation actually get right? I should like the desiderata set out in full: the cases it must handle, the distinctions it must not collapse (for example, between ignorance and risk, or between utilities that are merely ordinal and those that carry cardinal information), and the coherence conditions it must respect — including, I suspect, conditions on how the rule behaves when the state space is redescribed, partitioned more finely, or has states merged. Please give the reason each item earns its place, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of one camp. Tell me which single requirement rules out the most familiar candidates. Above all, I should like to know whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — whether the list itself shows that no rule can have everything that has been asked of it, much as Bell's theorem showed for local hidden-variable theories. For the avoidance of doubt, I am interested in a rule for an individual choosing under ignorance, not a principle for aggregating the preferences of several people. I shall do the scoring of the standard rules myself once the criteria are clear, so please keep the weight on the desiderata and their motivation. Kindly write the answer in British English.
