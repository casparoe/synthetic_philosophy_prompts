I have been trying to work out a principle about interpretability, and I want an honest assessment of it: is it true, false, or confused?

Let M be the target system and I the interpreter, where I can be a human, a team, another model, or an algorithm. I have been calling my principle the interpretive-overhead principle, or IOP. Its central claim is this. A faithful interpretation of M must track the internal distinctions that M actually makes when it computes, so the interpretation must have a representational structure at least as complex as the target subsystem it describes. But an interpretation is only usable if I can hold it, inspect it, and act on it, and holding and manipulating a structure requires representational capacity beyond the structure itself. Therefore I must be strictly more complex than the subsystem of M it interprets. Interpretability, on this view, is asymmetrical: a system cannot completely interpret itself, and a system cannot completely interpret an equal-capacity peer, except by one of two degenerate modes. The first mode is exact simulation, which just instantiates the target again and explains nothing. The second is lossy compression, which gives something usable but no longer faithful. Successful interpretation is always forced to sit between a miniature map that omits real distinctions and a duplicate machine with all the old mysteries intact, and what we call understanding is just the useful midpoint.

My favorite example is this. Suppose an AI at roughly capability level N builds a faithful interpretability map of another AI at level N. For the map to be faithful, it must have at least the representational complexity of the target circuits it describes. For the interpreting AI to wield that map, it must be able to manipulate it, which, by my principle, puts the interpreter strictly above level N. Recursive interpretability then becomes a hierarchy: interpreting an interpretation requires a still more capable interpreter, and the hierarchy either bottoms out in an uninterpreted ground or runs away upward.

I know this has the flavor of things people have said before: Goodhart-type limits on measurement, the point that a map containing everything is no shorter than the territory, the engineering distinction between explaining a system and rebuilding it. What I cannot tell is whether there is a real argument in here or just a vivid restatement of those older ideas. Is the IOP coherent as stated? Does it already exist under some name I should know? Where exactly does it break, and what does it get right and wrong about the actual practice of mechanistic interpretability, where humans do seem to make genuine progress on models that are, by many ordinary metrics, more capable than they are? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on interpretability, and do not present a contested view as the field's consensus.
