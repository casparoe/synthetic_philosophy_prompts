I was recently appointed to my city's climate-adaptation review board, and the consultants keep applying a 3 percent annual discount rate to damages that will not arrive for a century or more. One of them defended the practice with the following argument, which I cannot find the flaw in.

(1) To compare present costs with far-future benefits, some rate of discount is unavoidable; otherwise a cost now and a benefit any number of years out would be treated as equivalent. (2) At a constant 3 percent annual rate, one statistical life saved 250 years from now counts as roughly 1/1,600 of one saved today. (3) A human life cannot count as 1/1,600 of a life merely because the person will be born later. (4) So a defensible 3 percent rate cannot be discounting well-being itself; it must be tracking something else, most plausibly the fact that future people will be richer, so that a dollar of future consumption is worth less than a dollar of present consumption. (5) Yet in the actual analysis this distinction seems to make no difference: the analyst takes the value of a prevented death, compounds the 3 percent factor, and reports the future death as worth 1/1,600 of a present one. (6) So whatever the rate is said to measure, the practice commits us to treating later lives as nearly valueless relative to earlier ones. (7) Either we accept that commitment, or we must abandon discounting altogether. (8) But abandoning discounting altogether is untenable, since a benefit an arbitrary number of years out would then have to be paid for at any finite present cost, and no agency can budget that way. (9) Therefore long-run cost–benefit analysis is built on an evaluative claim almost no one would endorse.

Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it commit one to? If I had to choose, I would rather give up the move from (5) to (6) than give up (3), but I do not yet see what that move costs.
