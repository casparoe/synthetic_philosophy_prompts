Here's something that's been bugging me, and I want you to dig into it properly. Different institutions that are all in the business of making a collective decision treat a tie in completely different ways, and I suspect each one is quietly committing to a view about Buridan's ass and whether a rational chooser can ever find itself with no preference between two options.

In a criminal jury, if the vote is tied the defendant doesn't walk free; it's a mistrial and the state can usually retry them. The tie is treated as a failure of the jury to reach a verdict, not as a verdict of innocence or even a finding that the evidence was equal. In a civil trial, if the evidence comes out exactly balanced the plaintiff loses, because they had the burden. In a corporate board, a tied vote usually means the motion fails, full stop (assuming the chair has no casting vote). In many legislatures, a tie is broken by a presiding officer's extra vote, as if the tie is a problem that has to be dissolved. And some organizations resolve a deadlock by flipping a coin.

These cannot all be presupposing the same theory of what it is to have no preference. Reconstruct the implicit view of ties, and of choice without preference, behind each kind of rule. In particular: which ones presuppose that a genuine tie is a real possible state of the reasons or evidence, and that a rational body can just sit there in equipoise; and which ones presuppose that a tie is always a defect, a sign the body hasnt deliberated enough, so something has to give? Say which pairs of practices are inconsistent with each other, if you think their rules are making claims and not merely allocating burdens of proof. Then tell me what would have to change in, say, a criminal court if we took the Buridan's-ass-friendly view that a genuine tie plus no further preference is not a pathology at all, it just means you pick by some arbitrary tie-breaker. Would that commit us to letting tied juries acquit? To flipping coins at sentencing? Be specific about where this view would force a change and where the choice is really a value call that no amount of philosophy will settle.
