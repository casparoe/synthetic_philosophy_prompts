I am a retired natural scientist — I spent my working life in experimental biology — and I have only recently begun reading philosophy of science in earnest, particularly the literature on Aristotle's four causes and on whether teleological explanation can be made respectable in the sciences. The question that now preoccupies me is narrower than Aristotle's: when do the words 'for', 'in order to', and 'the function of' state a fact, and when are they only a manner of speaking? I have collected a set of cases with my own verdicts, and I should like to know what principle, if any, captures them.

Here are the cases and what I find myself wanting to say about each.

1. A healthy heart in a living vertebrate. A physiologist says the heart is for pumping blood. I assent without hesitation.

2. The same heart is removed and sustained on a perfusion apparatus; in these circumstances it cannot pump blood. I still say it is for pumping blood, and that it now has a function it cannot perform.

3. An organism exactly resembling a human being, but with no evolutionary ancestry and no maker, comes into being by sheer accident. Its heart pumps blood just as ours do. I resist saying the heart is for pumping blood; it pumps, but somehow it is not for pumping.

4. A physician detects a valvular defect from the sound a heart makes. I say the sound is not a function of the heart; it is a by-product that the physician happens to exploit.

5. Feathers existed before powered flight, and their earliest functions are thought to have included thermoregulation. In modern birds they are, at least in part, maintained by selection for flight. I judge that flight is a function of feathers, as genuinely as thermoregulation is, even though it was not the original one.

6. A pigmentation variant spreads through a small population by drift rather than selection. Later the environment changes so that the variant now confers camouflage, and selection henceforth maintains it. I judge that camouflage became a function at the point at which selection began to maintain it, despite its accidental origin.

7. The small bones of the mammalian middle ear are derived, so comparative anatomy teaches, from bones of the ancestral jaw articulation. I say their function is now hearing, not chewing; chewing is at most a past function.

8. A stopped mantel clock is still for telling time. A rock whose shadow happens to mark the hours is not for telling time, however useful it is to hikers. I regard these two cases as quite different.

9. The stratospheric ozone layer screens the Earth's surface from much ultraviolet radiation. I find I cannot call this a function in a literal sense, yet it seems to play just the sort of stabilising role that makes functional language attractive. This is the case I keep going back and forth on.

10. The immune system is for defending the body against pathogens. When it attacks the body's own tissues, I say that is a malfunction or a disorder, not a second function of the immune system.

My tentative principle, if I try to make it explicit, is historical: a trait or artefact has F as a function just in case F is what selected it, or what now maintains it, in the relevant population — with the maker's intention taking the place of selection for artefacts. That principle gives me most of my verdicts at a stroke, which makes me suspicious of it. In particular, it entails that a novel mutation that is beneficial from its first appearance has no function until selection has actually operated; and I cannot settle whether that is right. Worse, I cannot say what separates that case from the chance heart of case 3, since both lack a selection history and both presently do something useful, yet I am drawn to treat them differently.

What I should like, then, is this. State precisely the principle, or small set of candidate principles, that best captures these verdicts. Show how it handles each case, indicating which clause does the work. Say what it predicts for cases I have not listed — a beneficial mutation before selection has acted, a formerly adaptive trait now maintained as a harmless by-product, an ecosystem service such as a forest moderating its local climate. And tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and which, if any, I ought to overturn.
