I am a protocol engineer by training, and the vocabulary I actually think in involves packet framing and preambles, headers that describe a payload (content-type, length, encoding), magic numbers that let a parser recognize a file format, codec negotiation, side channels, channel coding, and the distinction between in-band and out-of-band signaling. Over dinner, a friend tried to persuade me that Hofstadter's distinction among the frame message, the outer message, and the inner message — from Gödel, Escher, Bach — is the key to locating meaning, and in particular to seeing why the question of where meaning resides, in the message itself, in the decoder, or in shared conventions, is badly posed. As my friend explained it, and I may be compressing the point: the inner message is the content carried by a physical object; the outer message is the implicit information about how to decode that content, the conventions or mechanisms by which marks on the carrier become content; and the frame message is the often-unspoken signal that the thing is a message at all and ought to be decoded. The example my friend kept returning to was a phonograph record: the music is the inner message, the structural fact that groove wiggles correspond to pressure changes is part of the outer message, and the frame message is whatever disposes us to treat the record as a message-bearing object rather than a lump of vinyl. The punchline my friend drew was that you cannot simply encode the outer message as more data without creating another payload that requires its own outer message, and so on.

I would like you to carry out a systematic translation rather than a chain of analogies. Restate Hofstadter's three-way distinction as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary above: what corresponds cleanly to headers, magic numbers, parser expectations, codec negotiation, out-of-band signaling, or to side information in channel coding, and where do those correspondences start to strain? Then give me the residue report. Specifically: what in Hofstadter's distinction does not survive the translation, and is that because my vocabulary has already absorbed the idea, because it lacks something important (about self-reference, about shared history between encoder and decoder, about ambient culture, about an interpreter that must somehow be primed before any decoding can begin), or because the original distinction is less crisp than my friend takes it to be? I am fluent on the engineering side, so be precise with technical terms; I am much shakier on the philosophical side, so flag every place where the translation is doing the philosophical work rather than merely re-labeling.
