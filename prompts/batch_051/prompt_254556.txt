My two best friends got into it at dinner last weekend and I've been turning it over ever since. I just started a job at a company that makes an AI customer-service bot, and a big part of my role is tuning the bot's empathetic replies — "I hear you," "that does sound frustrating," that kind of thing — so when Dana asked whether the bot actually means any of it, I was sort of the person being cross-examined.

Dana said it obviously doesn't: the bot's sentences have only derived intentionality. The meaning was put there by the engineers and the people who wrote the traning transcripts; the bot just shuffles tokens around. Original intentionality is the only kind worth calling meaning, and a chatbot has none, however fluent it is.

Marcus said that line collapses once you push on it. He brought up Dennett's two-bitser: a vending machine that takes only quarters and gives you your soda. From the outside we say it "wants" the quarter and "knows" the difference between a quarter and a slug, but everyone agrees that's just an as-if stance we take toward a mechanism. The point, Marcus said, is that the stance we take toward each other is the same kind of thing — there's no sharp line where as-if turns into real. Our own intentionality is just as derived, from evolution and social training, so Dana's raw original meaning is special pleading.

Dana shot back that Marcus had erased the inside/outside distinction. Her wanting is something she undergoes from the inside; the vending machine's "wanting" is something we project onto it from the outside. You can describe the machine fully without any talk of wanting, but you can't subtract the wanting from her own description of what it's like to be her. So the two-bitser stays on the derived side, and the customer-service bot, no matter how polished, stays their with it.

Marcus said "from the inside" is doing no work in an argument, because you can't export it into a test. Suppose someone loses the inner pang but keeps the apologetic behavior — nobody would reclassify him as a meaning-free mechanism; we'd go on treating him as a full meaning-bearer. So the line isn't between inner and outer. It's between stances that earn their keep and stances that don't. The bot is just a two-bitser with a bigger vocabulary, and the only real difference is how much explanatory mileage we get from treating it as a meaning-user.

Dana answered that Marcus's own example gave the game away: a more sophisticated projection is still a projection. A bank that plays "we value your call" on hold doesn't value anything, and a trillion-parameter model being harder to catch out doesn't make its "I'm sorry" refer to anything like remorse. If all Marcus can say is that as-if is all there is, then she'd rather keep a word for the difference between her and the bot.

We went around like this for another hour, and I honestly couldn't referee it — I only realized later they'd basically reinvented the Searle/Dennett fight over original vs derived intentionality.
