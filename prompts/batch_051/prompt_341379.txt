Ok, so here's a question I'd like to road-test for my philosophy of history seminar. I've been trying to give students a working test for when a revisionist claim about the ancient past stops being normal historiography and starts being pseudohistory. The criteria I've settled on, roughly, are:

1. Defeasability: the author says what evidence would count against the thesis.
2. No gap-hugging: the thesis doesn't get most of its support from the absence of records.
3. Respect for the evidential pecking order: contemporary inscriptions, direct material remains, and established chronological anchor points count for more than much later traditions or isolated stylistic parallels.
4. Limited revisionism: the thesis revises a specific inference, not the whole accepted chronology or record as a standing conspiracy of error.

Now here's the case that's bugging me. A colleague has a book manuscript arguing for what he calls a dispersed maritime confraternity in the centuries around 1200 BC, connecting the Baltic, the Black Sea, and the western Mediterranean. The evidence is a recurring spiral-and-wave motif on small bronze objects, a few place-names, and isotope results from a handful of burials. He doesn't claim a hidden conspiracy; he says the group built in wood, cremated or exposed their dead, and kept a deliberately light material footprint, so we'd expect almost no settlements. He also says the thesis would be in trouble if the motif turned out to appear in the Baltic a century or two before it shows up in the Aegean. But his main argument at the crucial step is that we have no records of a state doing this, so it must have been something non-state and non-literate, and he repeatedly treats the thinness of the direct evidence as exactly what the hypothesis predicts.

So: is this pseudohistory, or is it just a risky archaeological hypothesis? Apply my four criteria, and be committed about it — I want a definite verdict, not a tour of both sides. Also tell me whether any of my criteria is doing no work or misfiring on this case. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro and no conclusion.
