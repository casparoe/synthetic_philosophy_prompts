I keep coming back to a very stripped-down definition of democracy — the sort where a country counts as democratic if the top offices are filled through free, fair, repeated multiparty elections, and that's the whole test. I like it because it's checkable, it doesn't smuggle in my policy preferences, and it stops me from calling every election I lose a crisis. But I can't shake the intuition that something importantly different has been happening in Hungary since around 2010, and in a fuzzier way in Poland after 2015 and Turkey: elections still happen, opposition parties still run and sometimes win cities, yet by the time the media is captured, courts are packed, the rules for voting and districting get rewritten by the incumbents, and civil society is squeezed, the competition itself feels hollowed out — the election happens but the democracy seems to have already left. Part of it is about reversibility: in a normal loss the pendulum can swing back, but when the rules of the game have been relocked by the current winners, I'm not sure the next election can undo it.

Then I get worried that the concept is doing no work beyond 'the side I dislike is good at winning.' My housemate, whos more hard-nosed about it, says backsliding is just a partisan label for incumbents doing what incumbents everywhere try to do, and that the minimal definition is what keeps me honest. I want to know who should give way and on what grounds: should I debunk the intuition as sour grapes, amend or abandon the minimal definition for a thicker one, or are they actually compatible if backsliding is defined as manipulation of the electoral playing field rather than any particular policy outcome? Don't just pick a side — I want the moves. What would justify each of those three verdicts, and which is most defensible overall? Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
