Working title: "What coherence arguments for expected utility theory actually show."

1. The phrase "coherence arguments for expected utility theory" covers at least two different enterprises. Representation theorems of the von Neumann–Morgenstern or Savage sort show that if an agent's preferences satisfy certain axioms, then the agent can be represented as maximizing expected utility relative to a utility function (and, in the subjective framework, a probability function). Exploitability arguments — Dutch book arguments and money-pump arguments — show that if an agent violates certain axioms, there is a sequence of bets or trades, each acceptable to the agent by her own lights, whose combined effect is a sure loss or leaves her worse off by her own lights.

2. These two families are often run together, but they have different targets and different normative force. A representation theorem is an existence result; it does not by itself say that preferences ought to satisfy the axioms. To get a normative conclusion one needs a bridge premise such as "a rational agent's preferences must be representable in the relevant way," and that premise is seldom stated, let alone defended.

3. Exploitability arguments have conditional force. They show that an agent is exploitable if she evaluates each transaction in isolation, cannot or will not see the whole sequence coming, and cannot commit to refusing later trades. A sophisticated agent who sees the entire sequence can refuse the first trade and avoid the pump. But the evasion has a cost: her pairwise choices still reveal the cyclic preferences, while her choice from the full sequence or full menu departs from her pairwise choices, so she violates a choice-theoretic condition such as menu independence or the weak axiom of revealed preference. The money pump therefore presents a dilemma — be exploited, or relocate the incoherence from the preference relation to the choice function — rather than a direct proof that the original preference axiom is rationally mandatory.

4. This suggests that exploitability arguments are best read not as proofs that expected utility theory is rationally mandatory, but as diagnostics: they reveal that certain combinations of commitments are jointly untenable — for example, cyclic preferences together with myopic pairwise evaluation, or cyclic preferences together with a menu-independent choice function. The conclusion that one particular member of the inconsistent set must be abandoned requires a further argument that the other members are non-negotiable.

5. There is a further gap. Even a successful exploitability argument shows only that an agent is vulnerable to being made worse off by her own lights; it does not by itself show that her preferences are irrational. Closing that gap requires something like a principle that a rational agent must be immune to foreseeable self-exploitation, together with an agglomeration principle to the effect that if each trade in a sequence is acceptable, then the package is acceptable. The agglomeration principle is not trivial; there are familiar cases in which a sequence of individually permissible acts yields an impermissible or unacceptable outcome. I want to press on whether this agglomeration step can be defended in the decision-theoretic setting without already assuming the coherence notion that is at issue.
