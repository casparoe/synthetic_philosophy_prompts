Consider a practice that has become common among large internet platforms. When user-generated content would be unlawful in any single country where the platform has users or legal exposure, the platform often removes the content everywhere, rather than geoblocking it only in that country. A video that is entirely lawful in the United States and most other countries will be taken down globally because it violates one country's hate-speech, insult, or defamation law; the effect is that the most restrictive jurisdiction becomes the de facto global legislator for speech. Yet the reverse is rare: platforms do not generally refuse to remove content in a restrictive country on the ground that the content is protected speech in a more permissive one. Speech restrictions are exported; speech protections are not. What theory of free speech, or of territorial sovereignty, does this practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that speech has no legitimate existence unless it is lawful in every jurisdiction at once? That every state's jurisdiction over online speech extends to the whole world, while no state's free-speech protections do? Or is the pattern philosophically neutral — a purely pragmatic response to legal risk, with no deeper commitments? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what a platform would have to do differently if it instead presupposed that online speech is governed by the law of the speaker's country, or by the most speech-protective jurisdiction with a plausible claim.
