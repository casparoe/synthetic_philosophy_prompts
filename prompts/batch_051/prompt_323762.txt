I spent my career in a neuroscience laboratory, and the issue that most troubles me in the philosophical literature on free will is not the existence of unconscious determinants of action but the way one of them is treated as uniquely catastrophic. People will accept without much alarm that a judge's parole rulings shift with hunger and fatigue, that a chess master cannot report the processes that deliver her candidate moves, and that much of what we decide is prepared by temperament and training acquired long before the moment of choice; they continue to hold the judge responsible and to call the chess player skilled. Yet many of these same people, on learning that in Benjamin Libet's experiments a readiness potential in the brain begins roughly a third of a second before subjects report the conscious intention to move, conclude that the conscious will is a late-arriving report and that free will is in trouble. I cannot identify the principle that makes the last three hundred milliseconds before the feeling of deciding a deeper threat to freedom than the thirty years of prior causes that made the judge and the chess master who they are. Set out the strongest justification you can construct for singling out the readiness potential as specially threatening to free will, test that justification against the other antecedents I have mentioned, and give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept or abandoned.
