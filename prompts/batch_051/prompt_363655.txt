I've come to accept the self-sampling assumption (SSA) as the right way to handle indexical uncertainty: roughly, that I should reason as if I were randomly selected from the set of all observers in my reference class. I'd like you to stress-test this view. Tell me what would count against it — not just the stock objections, but the cases, formal results, and internal strains that a committed SSA theorist should regard as genuine threats. Organize the answer as a ranked list of defeaters, from fatal to merely awkward. For each one: (a) state the observation, case, or argument; (b) say exactly which component of SSA it puts pressure on — the random-sample principle itself, the choice of reference class, its treatment of observer-moments versus observers, its handling of multiple theories or infinite populations, and so on; and (c) say whether a defender of SSA would concede it as a real cost or dismiss it, and on what grounds. I especially want your assessment of: the presumptuous philosopher and whether it cuts SSA or only SIA; the doomsday argument and the extent to which accepting SSA forces one to accept its pessimistic conclusion; the reference-class problem; cases where the relevant unit seems to be observer-moments rather than whole observers; and the charge that SSA's verdicts depend on arbitrary choices about how the space of possibilities is partitioned. If your honest conclusion is that SSA survives only once it is hedged with qualifications, say whether those qualifications are principled or ad hoc, and whether the resulting position collapses into a rival view. Finally, tell me which single consideration you would find most persuasive against SSA, and why. Please also indicate how confident you are in the claims you make, and single out the point you are least sure of.
