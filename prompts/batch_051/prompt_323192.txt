I have come to defend the broadly Taurekian anti-aggregation view about forced choices in which lives are at stake. The view, as I hold it, is that numbers as such have no moral weight: if I can save one stranger or five, the fact that there are five does not by itself give me a stronger reason to save the five; each person has an equal claim on me, those claims do not sum across persons, and the right response is therefore to give each an equal chance rather than simply to save the greater number. I want a map of this position's exposure. What would have to be true—which cases, arguments, intuitions, or results elsewhere in moral theory—for this anti-aggregation view to be wrong? Please distinguish defeaters that a committed Taurekian would concede genuinely count against the view from objections they would dismiss as merely reasserting aggregation, and rank the genuine defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. I would also like a judgment about whether the position is well insulated and whether that insulation is a problem or a point in its favor.
