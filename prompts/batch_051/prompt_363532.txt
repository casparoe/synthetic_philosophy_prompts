Here's the rough-and-ready account of a theory I give my students: a theory posits stuff you can't directly observe, it explains a spread of different phenomena, it makes predictions about cases you haven't seen yet, it's internally consistent enough to hang together, and it gets revised when its predictions keep failing. Now here's a case that's been bugging me. I keep a folder of old intro-bio short answers, and there's one student — call her Maya — who explains evolutionary change the exact same way across plants, animals, even bacteria: organisms feel a need, the need causes the relevant body part to change during the organism's lifetime, and the change gets passed to offspring. She predicts that cave fish lost their eyes because they stopped needing them, and that if you stopped using your arm your descendants would end up with shrunken arms. Its systematic, and it makes real, testable predictions. But when she's shown counterexamples — exercise doesn't make your children stronger, and tail-docking in dogs never produced tailless puppies — she doesn't revise the core idea; she patches with "that's different, the organism didn't need it in the right way." And every so often her explanations conflict: acquired changes are inherited one minute, and then offspring resemble parents because of "blood." So: does Maya's account count as a theory by my criteria? Go criterion by criterion. If it's borderline or a no, tell me whether that's because the criteria are doing real work or because I've quietly built in something like "has to be produced by scientists." Does her case show any of my criteria is doing no work? And does whether we call it a theory change how we should explain the fact that her errors are so systematic?
