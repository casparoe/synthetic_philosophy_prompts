I keep hitting a wall on a claim a colleague made last term. His line is that when a thick community dissolves—a working-class neighborhood cleared for redevelopment, a parish that folds, a union local that goes under—the loss is more than the sum of what each member individually loses. There's a leftover, he says, a genuinely shared good that was never any one person's seperate property, and so it never shows up in how satisfied the former members say they are. I can't tell whether that's a factual claim or just a moral one wearing empirical clothes. If it is factual, I'd like the strongest possible test of it. The candidate I keep coming back to is a natural experiment: find cases where pretty similar communities dissolved, but in some the members were individually compensated for their losses (a plant closes, but everyone lands a comparable job with decent severance) and in others they weren't. Then come back a few years later and measure not self-reported happiness but less gameable things—whether former members still treat one another differently from strangers in one-shot interactions, how quickly they build new collective ventures, how much they trust outsiders. If the compensated group still looks a bit bereaved in these behavioral ways, is that evidence for the shared good? If the two groups are indistinguishable, what precisely has been ruled out? Set out the strongest version of this test (or replace it with something better), say what result would push you one way or the other, and then be explicit about what even a positive result wouldn't settle—for instance, whether the leftover is valuable in itself or just an attachment we happen to like.
