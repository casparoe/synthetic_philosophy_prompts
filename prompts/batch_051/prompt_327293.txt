Consider the classic case of Buridan's ass: an ass is placed exactly halfway between two bales of hay that are identical in every property relevant to its choice. The standard moral drawn from the puzzle is that, because the options are tied, the ass has no reason to choose one over the other, but this does not mean rationality requires it to starve; it may break the tie arbitrarily.

Now change one feature of the case. The bales are no longer equal. One is larger but coarser; the other is smaller but finer. For the ass, there is no common scale on which size and fineness can be combined into an overall score that determines the choice. Neither bale is better overall, and they are not equally good; yet the alternatives are not simply incomparable. A slight improvement to either bale—making the large coarse bale a bit larger, or the small fine bale a bit finer—still would not make the improved bale determinately better than the other. Philosophers sometimes call alternatives like these on a par.

How, if at all, does this variation change the standard analysis of the puzzle? Address the following. (1) On a maximizing conception of rational choice, is the ass permitted to choose either bale? In answering, consider the difference between an option's being optimal—at least as good as every alternative—and its being merely maximal—no alternative better than it—and say what, if anything, is being maximized in the absence of a common scale. (2) Does the original diagnosis, that equality licenses arbitrary tie-breaking, carry over to alternatives on a par, or does parity require a distinct account of what makes the choice rational? (3) If the ass later feels the pull of the value it passed up, is that regret evidence that the original choice was arbitrary, or can it be a fully rational response even to a choice that was well defended?
