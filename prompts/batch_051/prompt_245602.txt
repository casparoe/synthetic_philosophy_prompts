A research institute is convening an adversarial collaboration between two camps in philosophy of science on a single question: is theoretical elegance evidence of a theory's truth? The first camp, which calls itself "elegance as evidence," holds that elegance—construed as a cluster of simplicity (few adjustable parameters, short description length), symmetry and unification (one mechanism covering seemingly unrelated phenomena), and unexpected fit (independently motivated constraints turning out to cohere)—counts as a defeasible epistemic reason to believe a theory or mathematical hypothesis, over and above its fit with the data collected so far. The second camp, "elegance as aesthetic bias," holds that elegance is a subjective, historically unstable taste; that its apparent track record is an artifact of sampling from a record in which failures are forgotten; and that the only legitimate evidential role for elegance runs through tractability—elegant theories are easier to state, test, and falsify, which is a practical advantage, not a reason to think them true.

Draft the joint statement the two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document will be read by a mixed audience of working scientists and philosophers of science, and should be comprehensible to someone new to the debate. Structure it as: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims; (2) a disagreements table; (3) a short candid section on what is settled and what is not. Each row of the disagreements table should contain: the exact residual claim at issue, phrased so that the camp holding the claim accepts it as its own wording and the opposing camp accepts it as a fair statement of the claim being attributed to them; the crux—the narrower sub-question such that a settled answer would genuinely move at least one side; a concrete case, historical episode, or argument both camps agree bears on the crux; and each camp's recorded prediction of what an honest examination of that case or argument will show.

The document should earn its keep by separating four things that popular discussion runs together: elegance as a prior over theories; elegance as a likelihood or a reason to trust the theory's next predictions; elegance as a proxy for tractability and bold testability; and elegance as a virtue of exposition rather than of the theory itself. Any appeal to the historical record—for example, a famous successful prediction made on the basis of theoretical beauty, or a long-developed, widely admired research program that has produced no successful novel predictions—should be treated as a claim about a possibly biased sample, and the camps should state what would need to be shown about the base rate of failures before the historical record becomes evidence for either side. The statement should also consider the hypothesis that the felt elegance of a theory is partly a processing-fluency or familiarity effect rather than a perception of an objective feature of the theory, and say which side's position such a finding would support. Where a row in the table cannot be settled by any evidence the two sides can imagine, flag it as such rather than implying the table resolves it.

In the third section, separate the claims that the collaboration treats as settled from the claims that remain genuinely contested among informed people in the field, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal—for example, the two camps meaning different things by "evidence" or "elegance"—say so explicitly and isolate the non-verbal remainder.
