I cover the science and ideas beat and I keep tripping over the same line: "existence is not a predicate." Last week it came up at a panel on whether the ontological argument has any life left in it—someone dropped the line, everyone nodded, and the moderator moved on like the whole topic had been settled. But I've started to suspect its doing a lot of unstated work whenever it gets used that way. Could you lay out the different precise claims that slogan might be making? Say, about grammar, about logical form, about whether existence adds something to a concept, about what it even means to be a property or predicate in the first place. For each candidate reading, tell me whether it's trivially true, straightforwardly false, or a substantive claim that needs defending. And then the part I actually need: which of those readings, if true, genuinely damage the ontological argument, and which ones leave it untouched—especially the modal version that runs through Gödel and Plantinga. I want to be able to tell which interpretation someone is leaning on when they say it to end an argument.
