I am the superior of a small enclosed religious community whose rule of life contains the following discipline, composed long before photography or live video existed:

'No one who has looked upon the face of a dead person shall enter the shrine-room or touch the vessels, the cloths, or the images until sundown of that same day, and only after washing their face and hands in running water.'

The rule has never been amended, and interpreting it falls to me. This morning the funeral of Sister M.'s father took place abroad. She could not travel, so she attended it by live video link; the camera showed his face closely, in real time, for several minutes. She has not come near the body, and she has not washed in the prescribed way, because she does not believe the rule's condition applies to her. This evening she must light the lamps and prepare the vessels for our rite, and she is the only member presently able to enter the shrine.

I need a ruling. Does the rule bar her from entering the shrine and touching the vessels this evening? Must she first wash, or may she simply proceed? Work the case through four approaches: the words of the rule in their ordinary sense; its purpose; what its drafters would likely have said had they been asked whether a live video image counts; and what a reasonable member of the community today would understand by it. Say where these approaches diverge, and state which should govern here and why. In explaining your ruling, speak to what this case shows about whether a purity norm of this kind is best understood as tracking physical contamination, an inner disposition such as disgust or reverence, or a social boundary — and whether purity and sanctity, so understood, remain genuinely moral demands. Please write the answer in British English.
