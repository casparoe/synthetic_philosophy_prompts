After a long career in the natural sciences, I have lately been reading through the philosophy of law, and I keep returning to the distinction American defamation doctrine draws between statements of fact and expressions of opinion. As I understand the prevailing view, a false factual assertion about a person or firm can be defamatory and actionable, while a pure expression of opinion is constitutionally protected. Yet much of what actually circulates in public life sits awkwardly on that line: a credit-rating agency calls a bond speculative, an analyst writes that a company will likely run out of cash within the year, a critic awards a restaurant one star, a commentator predicts that a proposed rule will shrink an industry by a third. My scientific habits make me want to treat forecasts as testable hypotheses and methodological ratings as claims about a body of evidence rather than mere matters of taste. At the same time, there plainly is a difference between reporting that the fish arrived overcooked and judging that the kitchen is mediocre, and between a measurable misstatement about the past and a prediction that fails to come true.

Write an essay on how defamation law ought to treat evaluative ratings and predictive forecasts. Weigh the considerations for treating them as protected opinion—their evaluative character, their forward-looking nature, the social value of blunt assessment—against the considerations for treating at least some of them as actionable factual claims: the possibility of demonstrable error, the reliance others place on them, the undisclosed factual premises they often imply. Do not end on a non-committal note. Propose a criterion or test for when a rating or forecast loses protection, apply it to a few of the examples above, and defend it against the strongest objection you can formulate. Please write your answer in American English.
