Here is an argument I have been unable to shake, and I want to know where it breaks. It seems to prove that no procedure for turning individual votes into a group decision can be both cleanly democratic and logically consistent. Since that cannot be right, some step must be bad, but I cannot see which.

(1) A group has no mind of its own; whatever the group decides must be constructed out of the judgments of its members.

(2) On any question the group takes up, the democratic answer is to adopt the answer a majority of members give.

(3) Take a three-member panel deciding a single legal claim. The governing rule says the defendant is liable exactly when two conditions both hold: a binding contract existed, and the defendant breached it.

(4) The members vote on each of the three questions separately, and each member applies the rule correctly:

*   Contract existed: A and C say yes; B says no; majority says yes.
*   Breach occurred: B and C say yes; A says no; majority says yes.
*   Liability: A and B say no, since each denies one of the two elements; C says yes; majority says no.

(5) By (2), the group adopts all three majority answers: contract yes, breach yes, liability no.

(6) But that combination contradicts the very rule the panel is applying, under which contract-plus-breach entails liability.

(7) So issue-by-issue majority rule produces an incoherent group position, even though every member is individually consistent and applying one and the same rule.

(8) The example is not a curiosity about courts. The same structure appears whenever a body must decide several points that interact: budget items that must jointly balance, a platform whose planks imply one another, a constitution ratified clause by clause. The legal rule merely makes the logical link explicit.

(9) The only exits appear to be: override a majority on some question (but which one, chosen by whom, and by what right?), tolerate an incoherent collective output (but then the group's decision cannot express a determinate view), or deny that groups can genuinely decide multi-part questions (which would exclude almost every real decision).

(10) Therefore, majority rule on each question is self-undermining for any body whose decisions have more than one moving part. That conclusion is far too strong, so one of the earlier steps must go.

Tell me which step to reject, why it looked forced, and what rejecting it costs. I would most like to keep (2), because every alternative seems to license overriding a majority without a principled basis; but if keeping it forces me to give something else up, I want to know exactly what.
