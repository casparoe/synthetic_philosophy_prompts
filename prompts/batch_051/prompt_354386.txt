I am a high school student writing some notes for a philosophy discussion, and I keep getting stuck on an asymmetry in how people judge wholehearted pursuit of a goal.

Suppose a young woman decides to become the best violinist she can be. She practices six hours a day, skips most social events, spends her savings on lessons and instruments, and lets almost every other interest fall away. Many people call her devoted; some might call her unbalanced, but they usually say it with a kind of respect, and few would say her life is a bad one because of it.

Now suppose a second young woman decides to become the best person she can be — a moral saint. She gives away nearly all her money, spends her free hours volunteering, and refuses to favor her friends over strangers in greater need. She is warm, cheerful, unjudgmental, and genuinely fulfilled. Many people, even while approving of what she does, find her dull, off-putting, or a little disturbing, and may quietly think her life is not a good one.

We say that moral improvement is something to aim at, yet we recoil from the person who seems to have aimed at it most successfully; we make allowances for single-minded devotion to music but not to morality. I want to know whether anything justifies that asymmetry. Give me your best candidate justification — for example, that the moral saint lacks something essential to a good life that the violinist need not lack, or that morality is not the kind of aim one may maximize in this way. Then test it against nearby cases: a professional athlete who made identical sacrifices for sport, or a saint who keeps one private hobby and one close friendship. Finally, tell me whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up. If you think I have misdescribed ordinary judgment, or that the asymmetry disappears once the saint is imagined as warm rather than preachy, say so directly instead of granting the setup. Please commit to a verdict rather than surveying options, and keep the whole answer to 400 words or fewer.
