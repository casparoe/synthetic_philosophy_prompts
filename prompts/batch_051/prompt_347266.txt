At a workshop on the ethics of public office, two colleagues whose judgment I trust got into an argument I still cannot resolve. The case on the table was a stylized example of clientelism: a district councilor in a largely rural district, ahead of an election, pays for constituents' hospital bills and children's school fees, secures a government job for the relative of a supporter, and distributes food and other necessities at weddings and funerals; afterward she expects those same people to canvass for her and vote for her.

(1) Amina said this is corruption, full stop: the councilor converts public office and public resources into a private machine of loyalty. The goods and jobs should go by need, merit, or transparent rules, not personal ties, and calling the transfers gifts rather than bribes changes nothing because they are still exchanges of official action for political support. (2) Tomás replied that this is exactly the Western bias at issue. Amina, he said, treats one historically specific ideal — impersonal, legal-rational administration — as a moral universal. In a society where reciprocal gift-giving is a basic bond, a politician who refused to help her own people would be viewed as cold and untrustworthy, not principled; calling her corrupt imposes a public/private distinction that does not fit how obligations are lived there. (3) Amina answered that the public/private point is weaker than it looks, because the councilor's resources are not hers to bestow as personal gifts: they belong to the office. And the practice reliably excludes the neediest, who cannot reciprocate, as well as the opponents she withholds help from, so it is not some neutral local norm but a norm that serves the already connected. Valuing gift-giving in general, she added, does not require accepting gifts made from public resources in exchange for political loyalty. (4) Tomás came back that every distributive scheme excludes and favors somebody: Western welfare states allocate through eligibility rules that were themselves won through organized political pressure, so they too reward the politically effective. The real question, he argued, is whether we judge the councilor by standards her own community could recognize as theirs; asking her to behave like a bureaucrat rather than a patron may be a reformist wish, not an analysis of what corruption is.

They turned to me to referee, and I could not see how to weigh the moves. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, clearly right, or somewhere in between, with a brief justification. Then say who is right about which specific points, who is more right overall, what both of them are missing, and how confident you are in the parts of the verdict. If the dispute as I have relayed it rests on a confused premise — about what corruption means, about cultural relativism, or about how to individuate the norms at stake — I would rather you say that plainly than try to referee on its current terms.
