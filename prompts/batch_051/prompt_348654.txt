So I'm an ML PhD student (interpretability-adjacent, spend most of my time thinking about objectives, training dynamics, and what a trained model can be said to want), and I keep getting pulled back to a philosophy-of-mind idea I half remember as free-floating rationales. As I understand it, the idea is that there can be real explanatory reasons in nature that nobody represents: it's right to say a bird's hollow bones are there because they reduce weight for flight, or a wasp's digging routine is for finding prey, even though no bird and no wasp ever entertained that rationale, and maybe evolution didn't either. The rationale does causal-explanatory work but has no reasoner attached. This feels eerily close to how I already talk about trained networks: the loss and the credit assignment from stochastic gradient descent are, in some sense, the reason the model has the circuits it has, but nobody — not me, not the model, not the training run in any straightforward sense — holds that reason as a thought. The network is very good at the task without anything like a reason for its own good performance.

I'd like you to restate the free-floating-rationale position as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: objectives, loss landscapes, gradients, credit assignment, mesa-optimizers, whether a model has a goal as opposed to just a loss, instrumental convergence maybe. And then report what did not survive the translation. Which bits of the philosophical view have no counterpart in my vocabulary? Is the leftover a place where ML talk is genuinely impoverished, e.g. no notion of a reason as something that could be correctly or incorrectly applied rather than just a gradient signal, or is it a sign that the philosophical view was leaning on some intentional-sounding words that dissolve once you try to cash them out? Don't let my vocabulary win by default; call out the places where translating into objectives and gradients quietly changes the subject.
