Since I retired from the lab I've been working through philosophy, and one thing my reading group keeps doing is describing Franklin's moral-perfection project as scientific method applied to character: he wrote a little ledger with a virtue at the head, tracked himself once a day, marked a black spot for each failure, changed one virtue at a time like you would adjust one parameter at a time, and iterated. The line is that hes treating himself like an instrument to be calibrated, and that this shows character can be improved by systematic self-measurement — and that we'd all be better off if we ran ourselves more like a maintained instrument. I spent forty years calibrating instruments, and I want to know how far this analogy actually goes. Walk me through the correspondence table first: what maps onto the quantity being measured, the instrument, the calibration standard, the daily logbook, the drift correction, the noise source. Then the places it breaks down. Then which of those disanalogies actually sink the conclusion that systematic self-tracking makes you better, and which are just metaphor doing cosmetic work. And if the conclusion survives, tell me whether it survives because of the analogy or despite it, and what a better lab analogy would be if you have one.
