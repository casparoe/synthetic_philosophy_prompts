Here is a pair of judgments that most people are comfortable holding at once. A person who asks their romantic partner not to have close, confiding friendships with anyone else is widely regarded as controlling, pathologically jealous, or worse; the demand is treated as a warning sign rather than a legitimate condition of a relationship. A person who asks their partner not to have sex with anyone else is not usually regarded as making an additional demand at all, but as stating what a romantic relationship already includes; violating it is treated as a paradigmatic betrayal. Both requests stake an exclusive claim to a form of intimacy, and both appear driven by the same underlying fear of being displaced. Is there a principle that generates both judgments, or is the difference only a convention about the special status of sex? Give the strongest justification you can find for treating sexual exclusivity as a default entitlement of romantic relationships while treating exclusivity over close friendship as presumptively illegitimate. Then test that justification against nearby cases — a partner who demands exclusivity over a shared intellectual collaboration, over a hobby, or over the disclosure of one's inner life — and state whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned. Please write your answer in American English.
