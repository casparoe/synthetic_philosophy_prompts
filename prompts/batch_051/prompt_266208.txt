My friend and I spent most of a long car ride arguing about whether shame gets an unfairly bad name, and he ended up laying out a version of the anti-shame case I can't get out of my head. I'm reconstructing it from memory, so I may have tightened it a little. He went something like this:

(1) An emotion deserves to be called a moral emotion only if it tracks what a person can properly be held answerable for.
(2) What a person can be held answerable for is their acts — things they did or left undone — not their self, their traits, or how they appear in other people's eyes.
(3) Guilt tracks acts: you feel it about what you did, and it pushes you toward repair.
(4) Shame tracks the self: you feel it about what you are, or about being seen as defective, low, or exposed.
(5) So guilt is a moral emotion, and shame is, at best, a social one.
(6) And if that's right, a culture built around shame is built around an emotion aimed at the wrong object, so "guilt culture vs. shame culture" isn't a real choice between two moral styles — one of them is just mis-calibrated.
(7) As for whether a culture could change: no one can choose to feel guilt instead of shame by an act of will, so asking a shame culture to become a guilt culture is asking people to change their feelings on command, which is a badly posed question.

I keep wanting to say the conclusion is too strong, but I can't locate the step where it goes wrong. I have this firm intuition that shame can be moral — the shame of having been cruel to someone feels like it's about the cruelty, not about being seen. Is the argument equivocating between two senses of "shame"? Is (2) too strict about what we're answerable for? Or is the argument valid and it's my intuition that has to go? And even if steps (1)–(5) hold, does (7) follow? Go through step by step, tell me which one breaks (if any), and what the corrected version would say. I keep trying to deny one of the premises, but every time I try, I end up granting him another premise untill I'm not sure which one I'd even deny.
