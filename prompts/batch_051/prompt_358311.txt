Suppose that in 1990 the leading Anglophone journals in psychology and medicine had adopted two publication rules. First, no study could claim an effect unless its hypotheses and primary analysis had been pre-registered and time-stamped before data collection, with any unregistered analyses reported separately as exploratory. Second, editors stopped accepting p-value thresholds as sufficient evidence for a finding, and required every confirmatory claim to be accompanied by a Bayesian analysis with a declared prior or an explicit likelihood statement. Career incentives for publishing remain exactly as they actually were. Give me your best argued prediction of how the empirical literature develops over the next twenty-five years, and in particular whether the replication crisis of the 2010s would have been milder, worse, or roughly the same. I want the chain of reasoning visible: which pressures these rules actually remove, which pressures they leave untouched or worsen, and how researchers would have gamed the new rules. Distinguish the links you consider robust from the links that are guesses, and say what would have to be true for your prediction to fail. I want a committed prediction, not a survey of possible worlds. Please write in British English.
