I recently retired from a career in the physical sciences and have started reading philosophy of computation. The thing I keep getting stuck on is the Church–Turing thesis. As I understand it, the claim is that the informal notion of a function computable by an effective procedure lines up exactly with the class of functions computable by a Turing machine, and that every attempt to make effective computability precise has landed on that same class. But it is called a thesis rather than a theorem, and I cannot tell whether it is an empirical hypothesis, a stipulative definition, a heuristic, or something else.

I want you to treat it as an experimentally minded person would. Suppose I proposed to test the claim. Design the best concrete test or battery of tests one could bring to bear on it, and specify what result would count in favour and what result would count against. In particular, work through what the historical convergence of explicit formalisms on the same class of functions actually establishes, and what a proposed counterexample would have to look like to count as evidence against the claim—for instance, a physical device alleged to compute something no Turing machine can compute. Where it matters, please distinguish the claim about what an idealized human mathematician can compute from the stronger claim about what any physical process can compute.

Then say exactly what a positive or negative result would and would not settle: which parts of the thesis are open to evidence, which parts are matters of convention or stipulation, and which parts could be true yet beyond proof in principle. Finally, tell me what would change your mind. Which argument, formal result, or empirical report would most plausibly force you to revise your verdict, and where is your reasoning least secure?
