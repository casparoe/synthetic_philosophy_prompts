I'm board chair of a 183-unit housing co-op, and I need a written recommendation to take to next month's meeting. We came into a one-time settlement of about $390k from a construction-defects suit, and the board is down to two options. Option A is to put it all in the capital reserve, invested in a plain index fund, which mostly means smaller special assessments over the next decade. Option B is to spend it on a retrofit package — replacing the original windows, upgrading the boiler, rooftop solar — that would cut utility bills and make the units noticeably less drafty, with a payback we caclulated at roughly 14 years and a year of construction noise.

The finance committee is full of people who have been reading futarchy posts forever, and they want to pilot a decision market on this instead of the usual board vote. The proposal is a four-week market before the meeting. Any member can trade, or gift their trading allowance to someone they trust; the market is subsidized from a $1,500 operating-budget line with an automated market maker; and it resolves on our scheduled 24-month member-satisfaction survey rather than on actual heating bills or property values. Specifically two submarkets: one for predicted median satisfaction (1–10) if we take the retrofit, and one for predicted median satisfaction if we take the reserve option. Whichever side's market-implied expected score is higher at close becomes the board's decision, with a two-thirds override for extenuating circumstances.

Half the board thinks this is the first actually interesting governance idea in a decade, because the maintenance staff, the old-timers who have lived through three boiler failures, and the quiet members who never speak at meetings can all put their information into a price without having to win an argument. The other half thinks its a casino-ification of a decision about other people's homes, and their objections are not just vibes: maybe ten people actually trade, so a couple of opinionated residents set the price; the metric is a self-reported satisfaction score that can be nudged by survey wording and timing; once people know the market decides, buying retrofit-good shares is no longer a disinterested prediction, it's a way to make your preferred outcome happen; and richer members can buy more shares than poorer ones, so the whole thing risks operating like a regressive fee for voting.

My own view leans toward trying it with conditions, but I want the memo to land a clean yes or no. Draft the board's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with the reasoning set out well enough that the losing side recognizes its strongest point in your memo, and if you recommend conditions, give the exact terms (thresholds, vetoes, fallback process, survey-design safeguards, whatever is needed). The board will be voting on your text, so make it actionable.
