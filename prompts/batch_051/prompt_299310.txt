You are a professional philosopher of decision theory and probability who has spent way too long thinking about gambles with badly behaved expectations. Before you write anything, silently brainstorm the strongest version of each side, including the objections each side would raise against the other; then use that to write the dialogue.

Write a dialogue between two equally sharp philosophers, Mara and Jules, who disagree about the Pasadena game described below. Neither of them is a strawman: they have to respond to each other's actual arguments, grant the small points, and let the real disagreement get sharper as the exchange goes on.

Here is the game (sometimes called the Pasadena game). A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads. If the first heads comes on the nth toss, you recieve (-1)^(n-1) * (2^n / n) dollars. So the first few possible outcomes are +2, -2, +8/3, -4, +32/5, and so on. If you naively compute the expected value, you get 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., and that series is only conditionally convergent: the positive terms by themselves diverge, the negative terms by themselves diverge, and by rearranging the terms you can make the sum come out to any real number you want, or make it diverge. So the game's expectation is not defined independently of how you sum the series.

Mara's view: the Pasadena game is a real problem for expected-utility maximization, not just a toy curiosity. A perfectly concrete gamble has no fair price, and a theory that answers "undefined" to the question of whether you should pay $1 to play has stopped being a guide to action. She wants to modify or replace the framework—maybe with bounded utility, dominance reasoning, lexicographic utilities, or some other way of handling infinite gambles.

Jules's view: the game is exactly what a careful expected-utility framework predicts, and treating it as a refutation is like treating 0/0 as a refutation of arithmetic. The rational response is to say the gamble has no well-defined expectation and no determinate fair price, and that is a feature, not a failure: not every act-plus-payoff function is entitled to a single real-valued utility. The theory correctly flags the question as ill-posed.

Write the conversation in alternatign turns. Make each of them push on the other's weakest move—for example, can "there is no fair price" really be an answer to an actual offer, is bounded utility just refusing to engage with the mathematical structure, would a casino be able to make the game concrete enough to put pressure on Jules, and does the rearrangement problem undermine only the arithmetic or something deeper about expected utility. Let the dialogue end exactly where the disagreement genuinely stands.
