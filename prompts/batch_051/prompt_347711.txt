I am a novelist, currently drafting a political novel whose central character is meant to be incorruptible: a provincial election clerk in a failing state who, over forty years, has never once been bought. The trouble is that I keep discovering I do not actually know what "incorruptible" means. In one scene my clerk refuses an envelope of cash; in another he refuses, on principle, to correct an obvious administrative error that will disenfranchise elderly voters, because correcting it would mean bending a rule. A beta reader told me the second scene makes him look merely rigid or scrupulous rather than incorruptible. I want the character to possess the trait itself, not some adjacent trait like rigidity, so I need to pin the concept down before I revise.

Act as a professional philosopher who specializes in moral psychology and the analysis of character concepts. Think carefully, and do not settle for the first dictionary-like answer; if your first definition fails on any of the cases, say so and revise it. Then give me a working definition of "incorruptible" that correctly classifies the following seven cases:

1. A municipal clerk who refuses a $10,000 bribe, offered anonymously with no chance of detection, even though the money would pay her mother's cancer treatment.
2. A judge who has never taken a bribe but who gives noticeably harsher sentences to defendants who have irritated him.
3. A senior accountant for a drug cartel who is famous for keeping immaculate books and never stealing, though he knowingly launders the cartel's money.
4. A compliance officer who cannot be bribed but who will not grant an exception even when one is plainly warranted, and blocks an emergency shipment of medicine because a form lacks a stamp.
5. A wealthy technology executive who has never accepted a bribe or traded on inside information, but who has also never faced an offer strong enough to matter to her, so that one cannot tell whether she is incorruptible or merely untested.
6. A dissident who is tortured for the names of her comrades and gives none.
7. A general who is unmoved by money, flattery, and fear, but whose unshakeable cause is monstrous.

I am fairly confident that case 1 is a clear instance of incorruptibility and case 2 is a clear non-instance; my intuitions reverse themselves on cases 4, 6, and 7. I suspect the ordinary word fuses two jobs, one descriptive (unswayable by certain temptations) and one evaluative (holding fast to something worth holding fast to). If you agree, give me the two definitions and say which cases move between them; if you disagree, explain why a single definition handles the cases better. After you have run your definition through all seven cases, tell me which of your verdicts is least secure and why, and say whether your definition entails that incorruptibility is always good. Please write your answer in American English.
