I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about panpsychism, and the exchange kept collapsing into a single frustrating juncture: neither of us could say which version of the combination problem was actually carrying the weight. We agreed that the problem is not one objection but a family of them, yet we kept mixing up five distinguishable difficulties. The first is the subject-summing problem, the worry that numerically distinct centers of experience cannot intelligibly compose a single further center of experience, any more than a crowd of sentient beings obviously adds up to a crowd-level subject. The second is what I will call the inventory problem: the qualities found in micro-experience may be too sparse, or of the wrong kind, for their mere conjunction to yield the novel macro-qualities we actually undergo, such as olfactory or chromatic experience. The third is a structural mismatch problem: the mereological structure of a human experience does not seem to mirror the particle-by-particle structure of the brain, so macro-experience lacks the kind of parts a literal composition story would require. The fourth is the decombination or redundancy problem: even supposing micro-subjects compose a macro-subject, it is unclear why that macro-subject is anything over and above the micro-subjects, or what causal and explanatory work it does. The fifth is the brute emergence problem: the more the panpsychist says unified experience simply arises when micro-subjects are arranged in the right way, the more the view seems to reintroduce the kind of unexplained strong emergence panpsychism was meant to avoid. My friend maintained that the subject-summing problem is the only genuinely hard one and that the others are variations on it; I suspected the redundancy problem is more fundamental, since it seems to arise even after composition is granted.

I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, with a background in metaphysics and some formal training but almost no neuroscience, and I need to choose a dissertation problem. I would like your defended ordering of these five sub-problems rather than a survey of the literature. Which two or three are most worth working on over roughly three years, and why? Please be explicit about the criteria you are using—tractability, how many other positions depend on the answer, whether solving it would actually weigh for or against panpsychism rather than merely sharpen a formulation—and about the trade-offs between them. I would also like to know which of the five you suspect are downstream of the others or would dissolve once one of them is resolved, which are likely to be pseudo-problems, and what evidence or argument would most change your ranking.
