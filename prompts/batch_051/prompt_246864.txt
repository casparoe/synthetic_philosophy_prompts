I’ve been rereading Mill’s Utilitarianism and keep tripping over two claims that look like they ought to fit together but don’t. In chapter 4, Mill argues that virtue is not originally part of the ultimate end but becomes desired for its own sake through association, and that once this happens a person treats virtue as part of their own happiness, so they would not be satisfied without it even when it does not produce the usual beneficial results. In chapter 2 he says the rightness of an action depends solely on its consequences, and the agent’s motive matters only for judging the agent, not the act. Here’s the problem. If a person has come to want virtue for its own sake, and acts from that motive, is virtue now an independent source of value in Mill’s system, or is it still merely instrumental because it is simply a component of that person’s pleasure? And if chapter 4 is just a psychological account of how people learn to value virtue, why does he put it inside his argument about what is desirable in itself? I want candidate reconciliations: different senses of virtue as an end, a distinction between criteria of right action and objects of value, a change of mind, or something else. Then give a verdict on which reconciliation takes Mill at his word and which one is special pleading.
