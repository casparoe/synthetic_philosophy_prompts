I have been trying to systematize my reactions to a set of life-and-death cases, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, best captures them. I doubt that a single familiar distinction such as doing versus allowing or killing versus letting die will do all the work, but I am not sure what else is doing it.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. Switch. A runaway rail car is heading toward five track workers; if nothing is done, all five die. I can throw a switch that diverts it onto a siding, where it will hit one different worker. I think throwing the switch is permissible.

2. Footbridge. The same rail car is heading toward the five, but the only way to stop it is to push a large stranger from an overhead bridge into its path. The stranger dies and the five are saved. I think pushing is wrong.

3. Transplant. Five hospital patients will die unless each receives an organ now. A healthy courier in the building happens to be a suitable donor. A surgeon could give him a drug that painlessly stops his heart, remove his organs, and save the five. I think doing so would be murder.

4. Ventilator. A patient with end-stage organ failure is on a ventilator, unconscious, with no realistic prospect of recovery. With the family's consent, the physician removes the tube so that the underlying disease runs its course; the patient dies. I think removal is permissible.

5. Injection. Same patient and same prognosis, but the physician instead gives a respiratory depressant that directly stops the patient's breathing, because extubation would be difficult to arrange and distressing for the family. I think this is importantly worse than the removal in Case 4, even if it is not always impermissible.

6. Overflow valve. A ruptured pipe is flooding a control room where five workers are trapped. The only way to stop the flooding is to close a valve that seals that room, but this will divert the flood into an adjacent room where one worker is trapped. I think closing the valve is permissible, and that it is closer to Case 1 than to Case 2.

The two pairs I cannot quite reconcile are 1 versus 2 and 4 versus 5. In each pair, one person dies either way and the outcome is the same, yet my verdicts come apart. The cases also do not sort cleanly by the doing/allowing distinction: Cases 1, 2, 3 and 5 are all acts, and I judge them very differently. Nor do they sort by whether the one who dies would have died anyway, since in Cases 1 and 6 the single victim is killed by a threat I redirect while in Case 2 the victim would not otherwise have died, and I still judge 1 and 6 differently from 2.

My provisional hunch is that my verdicts track the conjunction of two things: whether a person's body or death is used as a means to save others, and whether I am redirecting an existing lethal object or process rather than creating a new one. But that hunch mishandles Case 4, which is neither a case of using someone as a means nor a case of redirecting a threat, and I worry the two clauses are themselves groping at a single deeper idea.

Please do the following. First, state as precisely as you can the best principle or small set of candidate principles that captures my verdicts, and identify, for each clause, which case or pair motivates it. Second, tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and would have me overturn or classify as an exception. Third, apply the principle to this case, which I have not been able to settle:

7. Barrier. The rail car is again heading toward five workers. I can lower an automatic barrier that will stop the car before it reaches them; but a worker is trapped on the barrier's track, and lowering it will crush him. My hunch says this is worse than diverting, because I am introducing a new lethal movement of the barrier rather than merely redirecting the rail car's existing movement, but the difference strikes me as suspiciously thin. What does the principle say, and should I revise the hunch?

I would like the answer to be useful to someone trying to reach reflective equilibrium, so please be explicit about the costs of each candidate principle, including any that saves all my verdicts only by becoming complicated. Do not give me a bibliography or a list of citations; I want the reasoning itself.

At the very end, after your discussion, write a final line of exactly the form

\answer{<short label of the principle you recommend>}

If your considered view is that no single principle captures the cases, write \answer{no single principle: <label for the best combination or the verdict that should be overturned>}.
