In Leviathan, Thomas Hobbes introduces the office of the sovereign as a trust for the people’s safety, and then immediately adds that safety must be understood broadly: it is not just keeping people alive but also securing the conditions in which they can lawfully obtain the ordinary goods of life through their own efforts, without harming the commonwealth. As a PhD student in political theory working on risk and the normative boundaries of state responsibility, I keep returning to this passage because it looks neither plainly empirical nor purely stipulative: it makes a claim about what the concept of safety includes. If one wanted to test the claim that the working concept of safety in actual political judgment has this expansive structure—that people treat threats to the conditions of lawful flourishing as failures of safety, and not merely threats to bodily survival—what would the strongest test be? Could one, for example, compare how citizens in different institutional settings respond to equivalent trade-offs between mortality risk and broader life-chances, or code how high courts distinguish safety from mere survival in emergency-powers cases? I would like the experimental or observational design specified concretely. And then I want an honest statement of what a positive or negative finding would and would not settle: would it tell us only about current usage, or could it bear on whether Hobbes’s definition is the right one?
