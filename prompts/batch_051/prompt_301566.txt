I've been arguing with a friend about why a recording of a piece can feel like a different work from a live performance of it, and I've ended up with a theory I would like pressure-tested before I bring it back to them. I call it the priority-weighting view of musical works. The core idea is that a musical work is not a fixed abstract sound structure but a weighted set of constraints — pitch and rhythmic content, instrumentation, timbral character, performance practice, even the acoustics of the room — where the weights mark how central each constraint is to keeping a performance recognizable as the same work. A performance counts as of the work when it honors the high-priority constraints and departs mainly in low-priority ones. A recording, on my view, is a derivative constraint-set rather than just another instance: once a microphone or an edit becomes part of what we listen through, it enters the weighting, so a heavily compressed lo-fi file is importantly not the same object as a studio master.

This, I think, explains a few things at once: why we can accept a jazz trio's take on a Bach fugue as the same work while treating a smooth-jazz arrangement that demotes the fugal subject to background texture as a new piece; why two recordings of one performance can sound like different works; and why debates about historically informed performance are really debates about which constraints should count as high-priority. I realize the view sounds adjacent to type-token Platonism and perhaps to indicated-structure views in aesthetics, but I want to know whether the weights do genuine work or just relabel what they explain. Please first reconstruct my view in its strongest form, then give the three strongest objections, and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
