I am a PhD student in comparative literature, writing a chapter on hunger, asceticism, and the body in early twentieth-century fiction, and I keep returning to the idea of reading Kafka's 'A Hunger Artist' through Nietzsche's late remarks on diet and health. I cannot decide whether the story confirms those remarks or quietly resists them. Nietzsche is usually invoked for his critique of ascetic ideals, the claim that self-denial can be a disguised form of will to power and a symptom of life turning against itself. But he also makes a set of more concrete, physiological-sounding claims: that what and how one eats conditions the quality of one's thinking, that heavy or badly digested food produces heavy or muddy thought, and that a thinker's diet is not a trivial biographical detail but a part of his philosophy. The hunger artist looks like a limit case for this: a figure whose entire identity consists in refusing food, who turns that refusal into a public spectacle, and who ends by confessing that he fasted because he could never find food he liked, not because of any ideal.

I am torn between two readings. On one, Kafka gives us exactly the life-denying ascetic Nietzsche diagnoses, and the story's irony is that the hunger artist's art is finickiness dressed up as spiritual achievement. On the other, the story resists the diagnosis: the fasting is less ascetic self-mastery than an aesthetic compulsion the artist cannot give up, and the vigorous panther that replaces him in the cage at the end reads less like a celebration of healthy life than a comment on the crowd's shallow appetite for vitality. I would like a careful reading of the story through Nietzsche's ideas about diet, digestion, and asceticism: where does the lens genuinely illuminate the hunger artist, where does the story push back on or complicate it, and what would a reading that takes both seriously conclude about whether Kafka is criticizing asceticism or criticizing an audience that needs asceticism to be legible as art?
