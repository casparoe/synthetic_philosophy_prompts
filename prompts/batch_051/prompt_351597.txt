I am a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate course on the governance of advanced artificial intelligence, and I am calibrating my grading for a short-essay midterm question. The course uses standard letter grades, and I would like you to assign a grade to one student's response and then provide point-by-point comments that justify the grade, rather than a general overall impression.

Question: Distinguish the alignment tax from the competitiveness constraint, and then evaluate the following argument: because firms developing advanced AI face a competitiveness constraint, any unilateral effort to make a system safer will impose an alignment tax on the firm that undertakes it, so we should not expect rationally self-interested firms to adopt costly safety measures.

Student answer: The alignment tax is the extra compute, engineering effort, or performance penalty that results from using alignment techniques such as reinforcement learning from human feedback, Constitutional AI, or red-teaming, as compared with a baseline model optimized purely for capability. The competitiveness constraint is the pressure on a lab not to spend resources on anything other than staying ahead, because a rival that skimps on safety can ship faster, release earlier, and capture the market. The two notions are closely related, and in effect the alignment tax is just the firm-level manifestation of the competitiveness constraint: an extra dollar spent on safety is a dollar not spent keeping up. The argument in the question is therefore sound for any normally functioning competitive market. Whenever a firm unilaterally pays the alignment tax, it falls behind competitors who do not pay it, so the rational strategy is not to adopt costly safety measures. The only way this conclusion could fail would be if alignment work carried no tax at all, but by definition alignment work imposes costs, so firms locked in a competitive race cannot be expected to make their systems safer.
