I’m in a medieval ethics course and we just read Abelard’s Ethics, specifically the part where he argues that sin is in the consent and not in the act, and that the deed adds nothing to the guilt. That’s been messing with my head because it collides with something pretty basic. Our legal system and most of our moral judgments punish a completed murder far more severely than an attempted murder, even if the intent and the planning were identical and the only difference is that one bullet hit and the other didn’t. Same desire to kill, same consent to it, only luckier victim. Is there any justification for this asymmetry that Abelard could accept, or does he consistently have to say the law is just wrong to make that distinction? I want the strongest defense of the ordinary distinction you can find, and then an honest verdict on whether Abelard has to reject it. Also maybe think about the reverse point—whether his view would make punishment more or less fair, or whether it would require punishing thoughts, which seems even worse.
