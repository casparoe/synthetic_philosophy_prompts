I recently joined a reading group on Aristotle's De Anima while working through some questions about theories of mental imagery, and last week two members spent the whole evening arguing about exactly what Aristotle means by phantasia in Book III. I could follow the exchange but could not tell who had the better of it, so I have set it out below.

(1) Elena argued that phantasia is, at bottom, a faculty of mental imagery. She pointed out that Aristotle describes it as a movement set up by actual perception, something that persists after the perceived object is gone, and that he repeatedly says thinking never occurs without phantasmata. Since a phantasma is an image-like particular, she took him to be committed to the view that all thought in an embodied soul operates on pictures derived from perception and then recombined in the object's absence.

(2) Marcus replied that this rests on a translation habit. Phantasia, he said, is better understood as the capacity for something to appear to one as being a certain way, and this need not be pictorial. He noted that Aristotle distinguishes phantasia from belief not by its format but by its relation to conviction: merely imagining something dreadful does not affect us as believing it does, which shows a phantasma can carry content of the sort that figures in judgment rather than functioning as a private picture.

(3) Elena answered that this proves her point. Aristotle says that someone who merely imagines something dreadful is in the position of a person looking at a fearful scene in a painting. That is exactly the detachment of an image: phantasia presents a sensory particular in the absence of the thing itself, and the picture analogy is his own. She added that if phantasia were not sensory in format, Aristotle's insistence on tracing it to actual perception would be unmotivated.

(4) Marcus replied that the painting comparison concerns the attitude we take toward a phantasma, not its internal format, and that treating the origin of phantasia in perception as a claim about its format conflates two different questions. He added that Aristotle also describes a deliberative kind of phantasia, found only in beings that can reason, by which one combines and compares appearances in order to weigh alternative courses of action; comparing options is something a gallery of pictures cannot do. And thoughts about justice or unity would be impossible on a strictly pictorial account, so a phantasma must function more like a diagram or a token than a photograph.

(5) Elena said a diagram is still an image, and her view can accommodate schematic and symbolic imagery; Marcus had broadened "image" rather than refuted the claim. They were stuck there.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each numbered move, say what it gets right and what it overstates; identify the distinctions that are doing the real work (such as faculty versus activity, origin versus format, and pictorial versus propositional content); and state who is more right overall about Aristotle's account of phantasia and its role in thought.
