I'm applying for a six-month seed grant, and the proposal has grown out of an argument I had last night with a friend who works as a structural engineer. He claimed that philosophy does not accumulate the way physics does because it lacks an unambiguous error signal: no experiment can tell a philosopher that they are wrong. I pushed back by pointing out that feedback and verifiability are not the same thing — mathematics has little in the way of empirical confirmation and yet plainly accumulates, and some data-rich fields have stagnated for long stretches anyway. By the end we had converged on the thought that the explanatory variable is not whether a field can be checked against the world, but something about the structure of its error signals: how quickly they arrive, how precisely they localize the mistake to a particular claim or method, and whether practitioners can accept them without losing standing in their field.

I would like you to write me a polished one-page research proposal around this half-formed idea. Please state the central question precisely — something in the neighborhood of: how much of the variation in rates of cumulative progress across fields is explained by the structure of their error signals, once we distinguish the speed, specificity, and social uptake of feedback from the coarser notion of verifiability? Restate it so that it has a determinate answer rather than inviting vague case-by-case judgment. The proposal should then say why the standard positions in the growth-of-knowledge debate leave this open: the familiar accounts either divide fields sharply into scientific and non-scientific, or focus on periods of revolutionary change, and neither treats feedback as a graded, multidimensional feature of a field's practice. Then lay out the approach: I want to build a small taxonomy of error signals, apply it to a deliberately varied set of cases — mathematical proof, high-level board games, medicine at different stages of its history, and parts of philosophy — and see which dimensions of feedback do the actual explanatory work. Say why this conceptual-plus-comparative approach is better for the question than the obvious alternatives, such as a purely statistical comparison of publication and citation patterns or a narrative history of a single discipline, and anticipate the objection that I am merely relabeling good methodology as feedback. Please also state what a positive result would look like — a defensible criterion of feedback quality that tracks the intuitive ranking of fields and predicts where a field will stall — and what would count as failure, for example finding that every proposed dimension can be adjusted after the fact to match my preferred ranking, or ending with a notion too vague to apply to a new case. Keep the scope realistic for six months: this is a conceptual piece rather than a data-collection exercise, and the deliverable is a working paper. Do not include a bibliography or citations; I will add the references myself.

Throughout, please separate what is genuinely settled among people who study the growth of knowledge from what is contested or merely my friend's way of putting things, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus.
