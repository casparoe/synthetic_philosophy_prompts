I have a standing policy when I evaluate philosophical arguments, and I want to know whether it is defensible or just a bias dressed up as a method. The policy concerns thought experiments. Whenever an argument leans on a vivid, emotionally loaded scenario — a child drowning in a shallow pond before you, a bystander pushed from a footbridge, a person kept alive by being plugged into another person's body — I translate the scenario into what I call a cool version before I let it move me. The cool version keeps what I take to be the structural features: the same agents, the same options, the same causal relations, the same kind of harm or benefit. But I strip out the concrete imagery, lower the stakes, and make the description flat and abstract. So the drowning child becomes, roughly, a stranger who will suffer a serious harm that I could prevent at modest cost to myself, with no one else around to help. I then ask whether the intuition the argument needed is still there. If it survives the cool version, I treat the thought experiment as a legitimate illustration or piece of evidence. If the intuition only shows up in the vivid version — if cooling it down makes the conclusion feel less obvious — I conclude that the thought experiment was functioning as an intuition pump, doing rhetorical work rather than evidential work, and I discount the argument accordingly, sometimes almost to zero.

Two recent uses: a friend argued, via the drowning child, that I am obligated to donate a large share of my income to the global poor; I cooled it down and found my sense of obligation became much weaker and less clear, so I discounted the argument. Another friend argued, via a footbridge variant of the trolley problem, that there is a deep moral difference between doing and allowing; in the cool version my doing/allowing distinction seemed to fade, so I discounted that argument too.

I already see one obvious objection: lowering the stakes is not the same as removing rhetoric, because many judgments may legitimately be sensitive to stakes, vividness may be what makes a value salient rather than what distorts it, and my cool version may change the content of the case rather than merely its presentation. I am not sure whether that objection is fatal to the policy or just forces a repair.

Please tell me: is this policy defensible, and under what conditions would it track or fail to track the truth? When is vividness merely rhetorical, and when is it doing legitimate epistemic work? Can the policy be restated so that it keeps the benefit — immunity to being maneuvered by affect — without the cost of systematically discarding stakes-sensitive or concreteness-sensitive judgments? And am I entitled to use it in the way I actually do, which is mostly to decide which arguments to pursue and how much weight to give them, rather than to settle questions outright?

After your discussion, give me a structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "verdict" (one of: defensible as stated / defensible only with revisions / not defensible / depends on further facts), "conditions_where_it_works" (a list of conditions under which the cool-version test tends to track the truth), "conditions_where_it_fails" (a list of conditions under which it tends to mislead), "vividness_epistemic_role" (one short paragraph on whether and when vividness is evidence, a mnemonic, or mere rhetoric), "best_revised_policy" (a two-sentence statement of the best version of the policy that keeps its benefits without its costs), and "entitled_in_practice" (yes or no, with a one-sentence justification given how I use the policy).
