I don't have a background in philosophy, but I've picked up the outline of a debate about whether photographs are transparent—whether looking at a photograph is more like looking through a window or telescope, a way of literally seeing the photographed scene, than like looking at a hand-drawn picture. A friend restated the pro-transparency position as an argument about causal pipelines, and then showed me that the same argument appears to prove too much. I can't find the step where it breaks.

Here is the argument as I understand it:

(1) The reason photographs are treated as transparent is that the image is produced by a purely causal, mechanical channel from scene to picture: light from the scene falls on film or a sensor, and the resulting image is formed without anyone's beliefs or intentions selecting what gets recorded.

(2) If that is the right ground for transparency, then a photograph is transparent just because its content is causally fixed by the scene rather than by a human's intentional act.

(3) But that ground no longer singles out traditional photography. A modern phone camera produces its image through an automated computational pipeline—it merges several exposures, denoises, reconstructs high dynamic range, sharpens—and at no point does a human inspect the scene and decide what to include; yet the output is partly synthesized and can contain detail that no single exposure captured.

(4) The same criterion covers other automated pipelines: a robot that photographs a scene and then mechanically lays down paint accordingly, or software that converts pixel data into an oil-painting-style image without human judgment.

(5) So the proposed criterion cannot distinguish the paradigm case of a film photograph from images that are partly generated or depictively transformed. Either all of these are transparent, or the contrast between seeing through a picture and seeing a depiction of something has lost its content.

(6) But the contrast seems to be doing real work. A raw camera file is normally treated as evidence in a way a synthetic photorealistic image is not.

(7) Therefore the transparency thesis either rests on a criterion that overgeneralizes until the thesis is trivial, or the intuitive difference in evidential status between a raw photograph and a generated image is not a difference in transparency at all.

The conclusion bothers me: I want to keep the idea that a photograph stands in some specially direct epistemic relation to the scene it depicts, but only if that idea can be stated so it survives the computational photography case. Where does the argument go wrong? If you wanted to defend some version of the transparency thesis, which step would you reject or refine, and what would that refinement cost? If instead you think the argument is sound, explain what the residual difference between a raw photograph and a photorealistic generated image consists in, if not transparency.
