I am a retired physicist, and I have only recently begun reading philosophy seriously. In my working life I was trained to think about evidence in terms of likelihoods, priors, posterior probabilities, Bayes factors, model comparison, experimental design, and selection effects, while the idioms of possible worlds, necessity, and brute fact are less natural to me. I have been reading the fine-tuning argument, and its central moves seem to me to be doing statistical work under another name: the narrowness of the life-permitting range, the claim that this demands explanation, and the contest among design, brute necessity, and the multiverse.

I would like you to translate that debate systematically into the vocabulary of statistical inference, and then tell me what does not survive the translation. Treat the observed values of the constants and laws as a datum. Treat the claim that the universe is fine-tuned for life as the claim that this datum is wildly unlikely under some relevant null model. Treat design as an alternative hypothesis that places high likelihood on the observed datum, brute necessity as a hypothesis whose parameters are forced or whose prior is concentrated at the observed values, and the multiverse as a sampling scheme in which many values are drawn but we observe only the draw that happens to permit life, so that our observation is conditional on a selection rule. Treat the old-evidence or anthropic objection as a question about whether evidence already built into the observational setup can discriminate between models. For each component, say which parts translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which cannot be stated in this vocabulary at all.

Then give me an honest verdict on the residue. Does it show that the statistical vocabulary is missing something the philosophers are really after, or does it show that parts of the philosophical argument have no determinate meaning until someone specifies the priors, reference classes, and selection rules that the statistical formulation forces into the open? In particular, I want to know how much of the apparent force of the fine-tuning argument depends on choices that, once formalized, turn out to be arbitrary—for example, a uniform prior over an unbounded parameter space that cannot be normalized, or a reference class chosen after the fact.
