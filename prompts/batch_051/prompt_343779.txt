Throughout molecular biology and in the way its results are communicated to the public, one idiom recurs: DNA is said to "code for" proteins, to "carry the instructions" for building an organism, and to constitute a "blueprint" or "book of life"; sequencing projects are described as "reading" the genome, and bioinformaticians speak of annotating, editing, and comparing sequence "texts." Yet many of the same scientists who use this vocabulary also insist that gene expression is just chemistry — transcription, splicing, and translation carried out by molecules with no intentions — and that talk of a gene "for" a trait is only a convenient shorthand. There is an apparent tension here: the official metaphysics is causal and mechanistic, while the working vocabulary is semantic and representational.

What theory of information, meaning, or representation does this descriptive practice presuppose, if it presupposes one at all? Reconstruct the implicit view as precisely as you can. In particular, does calling DNA a code commit the practice to semantic information — content that is about something and could in principle misrepresent — or only to a weaker, purely statistical notion of information? Does the idiom presuppose a distinction between a message and an independent reader of the message, and does the cell supply anything that genuinely plays the role of a reader? Say which elements of the code-talk are strictly required for the practice to make sense and which merely fit it well.

Then assess whether the different parts of the practice presuppose consistent things — for example, whether one can coherently hold that DNA both "codes for" a trait and does not determine it. Finally, tell me what would have to change in how molecular biology describes its results, and in how genomic findings are interpreted in medicine and public discourse, if the code or blueprint picture were replaced by a purely causal or statistical account of gene expression.
