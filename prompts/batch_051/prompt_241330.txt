Having retired from a career in the natural sciences and turned to philosophy relatively recently, I have been trying to think carefully about how much citizens should conceal from the state. In a discussion that compared the German disposition to treat personal data as requiring protection by default, the American tendency to treat openness as a condition of freedom, and the Chinese social credit system's routine collection and scoring of behaviour, one participant dismissed the critics of extensive state data collection with the familiar slogan, "If you have nothing to hide, you have nothing to fear." I want to understand what that sentence could actually be claiming, because it seemed to be doing a great deal of work in the argument. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan might express — about the nature of privacy, about what surveillance is for, about the difference between secrecy and privacy, about who bears the burden of proof when the state proposes to collect data, and about the relation between innocence and being observed? For each reading, I would like to know whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, and which of the readings a person would have to be invoking for the slogan genuinely to support the dismissal of privacy objections in a comparison of that kind. I suspect the slogan trades on an ambiguity between privacy as the concealment of wrongdoing and privacy as control over how one is seen by others, but I have not been able to make the distinction precise enough to say what follows from it. Please write your reply as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a letter or an email.
