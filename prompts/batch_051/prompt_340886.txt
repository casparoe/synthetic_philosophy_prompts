A general-audience book I've been reading has a chapter on economics and moral philosophy that summarizes the twentieth-century treatment of utility roughly as follows. The author says that economists established that interpersonal comparisons of utility are meaningless, since utility can only be inferred from a person's choices, and choice reveals at most an ordinal ranking of that person's own options. There is no common unit in which one person's gain can be set beside another person's loss, so there is no fact of the matter about whether a policy's benefits to the winners are greater than its costs to the losers. When governments and cost-benefit analysts announce that the gains to some outweigh the losses to others, they are simply projecting their own private valuations onto the public. The chapter ends with the claim that utilitarianism requires exactly the interpersonal comparisons economics has shown to be meaningless, and that utilitarianism therefore rests on pseudo-scientific foundations and is refuted. I have the sense that this is unfair, and that at least two senses of the word utility and two senses of the word meaningless are being run together, but I do not know how much of the history and the claim about choice is actually sound. Is this a fair summary? Please go through it claim by claim, say what a careful welfare economist or a philosopher would accept, qualify, or reject, and then say whether the final verdict on utilitarianism follows from whatever in the summary survives correction.
