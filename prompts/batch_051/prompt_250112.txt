I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I was rereading Plato’s *Timaeus* after doing a peice on pseudohistory. In the Atlantis story, an Egyptian priest tells Solon that the Greeks are perpetual children because periodic disasters wipe out their records, while the Egyptians, living in a safer region, preserve an older tradition. I know that’s often read as self-flattering Egyptian lore, but the rhetorical structure of it keeps showing up in modern fringe histories: a writer claims an advanced civilization existed, then explains why archaeology hasn’t found it by invoking floods, fires, lost archives, deliberate destruction, or a coverup. The absence of physical evidence becomes a sign of how thorough the catastrophe was. The *Timaeus* passage does the same with the pre-flood Athens: it’s supposed to be older and more glorious than Egypt, but all its deeds are gone because only the story survived, not the city. I know there are broad labels like “unfalsifiable” and “ad hoc rescue,” but those feel too general. I’m wondering whether there is an established name for this specific maneuver, where a lost-record story is used both as evidence for a vanished civilization and as an explanation for why the evidence is missing. If there is no exact name, how would you carve the concept so it doesn’t collapse into ordinary unfalsifiability or cherry-picking? Where is the boundary between a legitimate historical argument from lost texts—we know many Greek tragedies are lost because later catalogs and commentaries mention them—and the move I’m describing?
