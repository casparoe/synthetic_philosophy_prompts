ok here's one i've been chewing on for a while. a friend of mine who designs typefaces is always going on about how there's no single shape every 'a' shares, and to make the point he reaches for the same analogy every time: a letterform is like a song. every cover of a song is still that song even if the key, tempo, instrumentation, singer, even the genre change; what stays fixed is the melody and maybe the lyrics, and those don't have any single acoustic shape either. he uses this to argue that 'a-ness' is similarly not a matter of matching some fixed geometry or a checklist of measurable features, but of something playing the right role in the system—the alphabet, surrounding word shapes, reader expectations—and that trying to define 'a' by necessary and sufficient spatial features is doomed from the start.

the conclusion feels half right to me but i suspect the song analogy is smuggling in a lot. can you actually lay out what maps onto what here: what in the letter case plays the role of the melody, the lyrics, the key, the tempo, the singer, the songbook, the audience's familiarity with the original? then tell me where the mapping breaks, and which breaks matter for his conclusion versus which are just surface. in particular i keep getting stuck on the fact that for a song there's usually an original recording or a score and some historical chain of performances, whereas for letterforms there's no obvious "original a" being covered, and no score except maybe a very abstract sense of how children are taught to write. and how literally should i take "playing a role"—is that a causal role in reading, a structural role in a system of contrasts, or just a vague family resemblance? finally: does his conclusion (a-ness is a role, not a shape) survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing all the work, and is there a better one?
