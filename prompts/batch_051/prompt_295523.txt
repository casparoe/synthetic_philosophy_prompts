I've been down a rabbit hole lately reading about the Eichmann trial, and there's something I can't get straight. The way I always understood Arendt's "banality of evil" idea is that Eichmann wasn't some demonic mastermind — he was a mediocre, rule-following bureaucrat who organized train timetables and spoke in stock phrases, and the horrifying part is that a person like that, not even especially hateful, could of helped murder millions. The evil wasn't in monstrous motives; it was in thoughtlessness, in not really stopping to think about what he was doing from the perspective of the people it was done to.

But then I came across something that seems to mess this up. After the war Eichmann hid out in Argentina and gave a bunch of long interviews that were recorded. Only fragments of the transcripts got used at the trial, and the full recordings apparently didn't become public until decades later. In those full recordings, he supposedly sounds completely different: still a committed antisemite, proud of what he did, annoyed that he didn't do the job more throughly, saying he'd do it again. So the question is: does the banality-of-evil thesis survive that? Or does it collapse if Eichmann himself turns out to have been a true believer and not a thoughtless clerk? I get that Arendt might have been fooled by his performance in the glass booth, but I'm trying to figure out whether the idea can be saved anyway — like maybe the banality is in the system, or in the kind of work it took to run the killing machine, rather than in Eichmann's personal psychology. Which part of Arendt's claim does this new evidence actually hit, and is there a version of the thesis that still holds up?
