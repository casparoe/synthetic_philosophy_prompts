I am a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I keep encountering a substantive-sounding version of Dennett's intentional stance: the claim that when we describe a chess engine as wanting to control the center or a language model as concluding that a question is nonsense, the intentional idiom is not merely a convenient façon de parler but tracks a real pattern — one that compresses and predicts the system's behavior better, for its cost, than any comparable description at the design or physical stance. I would like you to design a concrete test of that claim. Pick one system and specify the data you would collect, how you would operationalize an intentional description and its cost, the comparison class of design-level and physical-level descriptions, and the quantitative criterion for better compression or prediction. Then say what result would favor the real-pattern reading and what result would favor the deflationary alternative that the intentional stance is only instrumentally useful. Finally, be explicit about what even a clean positive result would not settle — for instance, whether the pattern is in the system or in our predictive practices, and whether real pattern adds anything beyond useful compression. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
