I am preparing a seminar on Camus's novel The Stranger (L'Étranger), to be read alongside the long essay against capital punishment that Camus published in the 1950s. The seminar question is how these two texts stand to one another.

As I understand the essay, its central claims are these: the state kills with a certainty that no human institution can actually possess; the machinery of arrest, trial, and execution strips the condemned person of his humanity and treats him as an object of procedure; execution is uniquely final and forecloses every possibility of correction or redemption; and the public justifications for capital punishment are largely illusions. The novel gives us Meursault, a man who has killed, who is tried at least as much for his refusal to perform conventional grief as for the killing itself, and who then spends his last days in prison waiting for the guillotine, ending with his confrontation with the prison chaplain and the novel's closing acceptance of the world in its indifference.

I would like a reading of The Stranger through the lens of that essay which is faithful in both directions: the essay must be treated as a serious argument, and the novel must be treated as a novel rather than a mere illustration of it. In your answer, please do the following:

1. Identify places where the novel genuinely dramatises, extends, or concretises the essay's arguments, and say what the narrative form adds that the bare argument cannot.
2. Identify places where the novel complicates or resists the essay's position. For instance, Meursault is guilty in fact, so the novel is not a story of judicial error; and his final attitude toward his own death may sit uneasily with an abolitionist appeal to the value of life. Look for further tensions rather than stopping at the obvious ones.
3. Give first the strongest reading in which the novel and the essay reinforce one another, then the strongest reading in which the novel exposes something the essay misses.
4. End with a reasoned judgement: does the novel, read this way, strengthen Camus's case against the death penalty, reveal a limitation in it, or do something else, and what should a seminar take away from placing the two texts side by side?

Do not summarise the plot at length, do not invent quotations, and do not provide or rely on citations. I want your own careful reconstruction and critical assessment, working only from well-known aspects of the texts.
