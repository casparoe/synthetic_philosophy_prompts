I'm giving a faculty workshop next month to a group of human rights lawyers and legal philosophers, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense paper. My thesis is a qualified Benthamite one about natural rights: when political actors say there is a natural right to X, the sentence doesn't pick out any pre-legal entity; it is best understood as a demand that a legal right to X be created, backed by a moral judgment that the absence of such a right would be bad. The centerpiece of my paper is the passage in Bentham's Anarchical Fallacies where he attacks the Declaration of the Rights of Man and the Citizen, the one where he says natural rights are not just false but nonsensical, and that the right which is natural and imprescriptible is nonsense upon stilts, and where he goes on to argue that there are no rights before law, only reasons for wishing there were. I want to use that passage to make a point about what talk of pre-legal rights can and can't do, not to dismiss human rights practice wholesale.

The room will include two people who have spent their careers in international human rights litigation, a political theorist who thinks Bentham simply misunderstood what rights-claims are, and a few younger people who will be hearing Bentham for the first time. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way this audience would actually put them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend instead of hoping nobody notices. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
