i'm putting together a 'which theory of writing systems do you actually hold?' quiz for some friends who argue about spelling reform. The underlying question is what a script encodes and how phonemic an alphabet should be. It should sort people by what they say about concrete cases, not by which label they like. i want 9 questions, each a short case with three-ish answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions. The positions should be: phonemic purist (the only job of an alphabet is one symbol per phoneme and every deviation is a flaw), morphophonemic (a good script tracks underlying forms and morpheme identity rather than surface sound, so English 'sign' keeping the g because of 'signature' and German 'Rad' keeping the d even though it's pronounced like 'Rat' are features), etymological/historical (spelling should record where a word came from, not how it sounds now), syllabist or moraic (segments are the wrong unit for at least some languages, and a syllabary or kana-like system is the honest choice), and logographic/semasiographic (writing can point at meaning directly and that's not a failed alphabet). Add a 'historical accident' leaf for people who think no writing system is a consistent theory of anything because scripts are just frozen history plus exceptions. For each position in the key, add one sentence on the most awkward thing it has to swallow.

Build the logical space before the leaves: first sound vs meaning; within sound, segment vs syllable/mora; within segment, one-to-one phonemic vs morphophonemic vs etymological. Mark any combination nobody really occupies and give one sentence on why (e.g. pure semasiography for an open vocabulary). Also flag which pairs of positions the 9 questions can't cleanly seperate, and say what kind of case would split them. The cases I definitely want used: French plural -s/-x being silent but written; English 'electric/electricity/electrician' where the c shifts sound while the spelling stays; Finnish or Turkish as the one-symbol-one-sound baseline; Chinese characters; and emoji as a borderline case. Phrase every question so a friend could answer it without already knowing the position names.
