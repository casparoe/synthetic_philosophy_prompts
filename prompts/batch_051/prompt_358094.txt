After four decades of work in the natural sciences, I now read philosophy with a scientist's demand that every part of an explanatory scheme account for its keep. Apply that standard to J. L. Austin's analysis of the infelicities that can afflict a performative utterance. Consider the three families of conditions he distinguishes: (a) the existence of an accepted conventional procedure, with the appropriate persons and circumstances; (b) the correct and complete execution of that procedure; and (c) the presence of the requisite thoughts, feelings, or intentions, together with any stipulated subsequent conduct. Please rank these three families by what fraction of the distinctively illocutionary force of a speech act each one explains, as against requirements that would attach to any conventional or ceremonial act apart from its linguistic content. Assign each family a score from 0 to 1, justify each assignment in a single sentence, and then, in two or three further sentences, say what the resulting order tells us about the thesis that the force of a performative utterance is grounded in convention rather than in the speaker's psychology.
