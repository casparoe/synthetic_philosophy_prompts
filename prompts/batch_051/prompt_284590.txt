I keep returning to a structural resemblance and cannot tell whether it amounts to anything. In the Stoic tradition — most explicitly in Epictetus and in the modern writers who have revived him — the dichotomy of control divides everything into what is up to us (roughly, judgment, impulse, desire, aversion, and the conduct those issue in) and what is not up to us (body, property, reputation, office, other people's opinions, and the way events fall out). The associated advice is to attach one's striving and self-evaluation only to the first class and to treat the second as indifferent, since a correct use of what is up to us does not become worthless when the world fails to cooperate.

This looks to me formally like the distinction in reinforcement learning between a policy and the transition dynamics of the environment. A policy is a rule mapping states to actions, the part of a trajectory that is the agent's choice; the transition dynamics are the causal law mapping a state-action pair to the next state and reward, the part the agent does not choose. There is also a shared impulse toward judging only the controllable part: a policy is not evaluated by the reward actually received on one lucky or unlucky rollout, but by its expected return under the actual dynamics, and the agent is credited or blamed only for the policy it selected.
