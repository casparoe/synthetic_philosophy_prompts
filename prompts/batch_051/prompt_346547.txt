Construct a thought experiment in which Kant's distinction between intuitions and concepts produces a classification conflict across two well-established interpretations of the distinction. The scenario must be an ordinary case of expert judgment, with no brain lesions, no synesthesia, and no exotic cognitive enhancement.

Here is the background. On one interpretive line, the intuition/concept contrast is fundamentally a distinction of logical form: an intuition is a singular and immediate representation, while a concept is a general representation that relates to objects only by way of marks shared across many possible objects. On another interpretive line, the contrast is fundamentally a distinction of cognitive provenance: an intuition is a representation received through sensibility, while a concept is a representation produced spontaneously by the understanding through acts of comparison, reflection, and abstraction. The case should be engineered so that the same representation is classified as an intuition on the logical-form reading and as a concept on the provenance reading.

The representation should be an intellectual recognition of a particular structured item—for example, a physician recognizing a syndrome in a patient's chart, a mathematician recognizing a proof pattern in a notation, or a judge recognizing a fact pattern as a particular instance of a legal doctrine. It should not be the straightforward perception of a physical object. The classification conflict must fall out of the two interpretive principles, not be stipulated into the scenario.

After presenting the scenario, do all of the following:

1. State exactly which representation is the candidate, then show why the logical-form reading counts it as an intuition (singular and immediate) and why the provenance reading counts it as a concept (spontaneously formed through discursive acts of comparison, reflection, and abstraction).

2. Give the reasoning for each reading in enough detail that the verdicts follow from the principles stated above.

3. Identify which feature of the case is doing the work—for example, the kind of object recognized, the history of acquisition, or the role the representation plays in later inference—and say what would happen if that feature were removed. Would the case collapse back into an easy, standard intuition?

4. Take a committed position on which reading the case better supports, and then give the strongest reply available to a defender of the other reading. Do not end by saying both readings are equally plausible.
