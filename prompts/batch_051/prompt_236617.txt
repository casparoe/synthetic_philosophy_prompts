For a chapter I am writing on rational preference change, I am trying to pin down which assumptions in Good's theorem actually carry the result. The theorem says that, for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer with a fixed utility function over outcomes, a fixed set of available acts, and a signal that affects payoffs only through belief revision, the expected value of costless information is never negative. I want to test that result against a case in which learning something and being changed by something blur together.

Consider an agent deciding whether to keep a family estate intact or cede part of it, who also has a stable higher-order commitment to honor her late father's considered judgment about the estate. She is offered, free of charge, a letter he left. She knows that if the letter says the estate was acquired unjustly, her weighting of the two options will shift permanently and will seem to her, once shifted, to be the correct weighting. She also cannot say in advance whether the shift will occur because the letter gives her information about an end she already holds — honoring his judgment — or because the letter will itself introduce a new final end. She is otherwise an expected-utility maximizer.

Does reading the letter count as receiving costless information in the sense required by the theorem? Work through the theorem's assumptions one by one — fixed utility function, fixed action set, costlessness, no direct causal effect of the signal on payoffs, and any other condition you take to be doing nontrivial work — and state whether the case satisfies, violates, or leaves each condition indeterminate. Then give a verdict on whether this sort of case shows that Good's theorem needs a sharper criterion for separating belief revision from preference change, or whether the indeterminacy is a problem for the theory of rational choice rather than for the theorem.
