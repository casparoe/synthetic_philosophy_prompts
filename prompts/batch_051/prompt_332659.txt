I teach an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of mind course, and I am writing the final exam. We have spent three weeks on the tension between Ned Block's access/phenomenal distinction and global workspace theory, including the debate over whether there can be phenomenal consciousness without access, and whether global workspace or global neuronal workspace theories are best understood as theories of access rather than of phenomenality. I want one long essay question that a well-prepared student could answer in 60 minutes. The question should be unanswerable by merely defining Block's two notions and then reciting that global workspace theory has been criticized for accounting only for access consciousness; a student who does that much has memorized the labels but has not yet shown they understand why the disagreement is hard, or what would actually settle it.

In particular, I want the exam question to force students to reconstruct at least one strand of empirical argument in the debate—for example, the Sperling partial-report paradigm and the claim of phenomenal overflow—and to say, precisely, what a global workspace theorist would need to deny or reinterpret for their position to survive that kind of case. The ideal answer should also show some grip on the dialectical options: that it is open to the global workspace theorist to reject the overflow interpretation of the result, to allow that global availability and reportability can come apart in ways the subject only partially accesses, or to accept that the theory is a theory of cognitive access and argue that this is no retreat because its target was never a full theory of phenomenal character. Crucially, the marking should punish the common mistake of treating the empirical result as if it simply refutes the theory, without saying which premise of the argument a defender would reject.

Please draft the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly 750 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 marks. The marking scheme should name the five or six must-hit moves that separate an A answer from a B answer, assign marks to each, and include a one-line note on the specific way weaker students typically botch each move—for instance, conflating accessibility with attention, or assuming that because a subject cannot report all the letters the subject has not experienced them.
