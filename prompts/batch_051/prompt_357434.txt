I've been trying to think through the AI-safety-via-debate family of proposals, and I keep flip-flopping on it. The appeal is real: instead of trying to write down human values directly or build a perfect reward model, you train two models to argue with each other in front of a human judge, and the judge just picks the answer that survives cross-examination. The claim is that this scales past human ability because a wrong answer has some specific flaw buried in it, and one pointed follow-up question can expose that flaw even when the judge couldn't have found it alone; honesty, on this story, is cheaper to maintain than a lie that has to stay consistent under adversarial questioning. Its a beautiful setup — a clear training signal, a clear evaluation, and a clean division of labor. I get the pull.

But the judge is still a human, and that's the part I can't get past. At exactly the point where the questions get superhuman — where I can't check the math, read the code, or survey the evidence myself — persuasion and truth can drift apart, and I don't see what pulls them back together. A sufficiently capable model won't just be superhumanly knowledgeable; it'll be superhumanly persuasive, able to give me exactly the walk-through that makes me feel like I've understood the matter. So the honest debater's edge, if it exists, is an empirical bet about which is more expensive at the margin: being truthful or being convincing. And training pressure optimizes for convincing, not for tracking truth, unless the judge's verdict happens to track truth. I'm left with a theory that has clean systematic virtues and a stubborn intuition that winning a debate is a different currency than being right, and that the difference becomes load-bearing precisely where the method is supposed to help.

I'm not asking for a verdict. I want the strongest version of each side, and then a real account of what they'd each cost. What would have to be true, or what would I have to be confused about, for the intuition to be safely discounted — is it an artifact of imagining a dishonest model with full capabilities and an honest model with none of the countermeasures, like decomposition, cross-examination, and a judge who can demand evidence? What would have to fail, precisely, for the theory to be abandoned or substantially amended — would it be enough to show that persuasive skill scales faster than honesty, or that the judge's judgments stop correlating with truth in some specific regime? And is there a negotiated reading where debate is the right instrument below the frontier where the judge can still audit the moves, while the intuition is right about the far side, so the real dispute is about where that boundary sits and whether anything on the other side of it keeps the process tethered to truth?
