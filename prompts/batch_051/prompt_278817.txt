I keep coming back to the young Keynes's view of the good life — the one he soaked up from Moore and the Bloomsbury set and then half-disowned, half-defended, later on. As I understand it, the theory is: the only things good in themselves are certain states of consciousness (aesthetic enjoyment, insight, close attention, contemplation) and certain relations between people (love, friendship, being genuinely seen). Everything else — building things, winning, making money, having effects on the world, leaving a mark — is instrumentally good at best, worth doing only insofar as it tends to produce more or better states and relations. The appeal of the theory, for me, is that it's ruthlessly simple and gives one answer to what I'm actually optimizing for: not the scoreboard, not the outputs, just the felt quality of a life and the depth of a few relationships. It also handles the post-scarcity scenario better than almost anything else: if we ever really got the fifteen-hour week, the person who gardens, reads, and loves well would have the entire good, and the person who keeps acquiring status would be chasing a means as if it were the end.

And yet I have an intuition I cant shake. It seems to matter to me not just that my life feel a certain way but that it actually be a certain way — that I really did something, that the people who seem to love me really do, that some of what I made or fixed outlasts me. I imagine a life made of wonderful but entirely private states, with no effects beyond themselves, and I can't accept that nothing would be missing if it ran at full intensity to the end. The deception case bothers me most: someone who goes through life convinced they are loved, useful, and on the side of truth, while actually being gently played for a fool, seems to me to have a worse life than the person with the same inner history whose love, usefulness, and beliefs are all true. The theory, taken strictly, can't say that.

So which yields? Should I treat my "it has to be real" intuition as a bit of evolutionary or cultural wiring — status anxiety, agency worship, the protestant refusal to accept that states of mind are enough — and drop it? Or does the intuition force me to add a condition to the theory (a success condition, a truth condition, something about some goods having to be achieved in the world rather than merely experienced), and can the theory survive that amendment? Or is there a reading on which the young Keynes's theory already absorbs the intuition — because knowledge, as he understood it, isn't just a pleasant mental state, and being loved and being useful are relations, not feelings? I don't just want a winner. Tell me what would justify each of those three moves, and which one I'd be rational to make.
