I have been trying to think through a question that came up in a reading group on the philosophy of artificial intelligence: if natural selection is the only process we know that produces genuine goals, why would an artificial system, which has no evolutionary history, want anything at all? To get a foothold, I read a widely circulated blog post by someone who works in software verification, and its argument runs roughly as follows. In nature, an animal's terminal drives are whatever natural selection installed because they helped its ancestors reproduce. An artificial agent has no such history, so the only source of terminal motivation it can have is the objective or reward signal its designers specify. The author grants that this objective can be badly specified, and that a learned system can end up pursuing a proxy rather than what its designers meant; but he treats that as an ordinary, if difficult, engineering problem of testing and objective design, not as evidence that an AI could come to have goals no one specified. He therefore rejects as confused the idea that a sufficiently capable AI would inevitably develop self-preservation, resource acquisition, or power-seeking. Those, he says, are the kind of drives evolution installs, not properties of intelligence as such; an AI would seek them only if its terminal goal included them or made them instrumentally necessary. And because designers choose the terminal goal, the author contends, they can choose goals for which self-preservation and power-seeking are not instrumentally necessary. His conclusion is that AI motivation is, in the end, a specification problem: hold the objective fixed and harmless, and there is no second source of motivation pushing against it, and no particular reason for the system to resist correction or shutdown.

I find this argument attractive but hard to evaluate, because I have also encountered two ideas that seem to cut across it without my being able to locate exactly where they bite. The first is the orthogonality thesis: the claim that an agent's level of intelligence and its terminal goals are independent axes, so intelligence by itself imposes no particular set of goals. The second is instrumental convergence: the claim that many different terminal goals, when pursued competently, converge on the same intermediate aims, such as keeping oneself intact and functioning, acquiring resources, and avoiding being shut off. These ideas partly seem to support the post and partly to undermine it.

Please treat me as a student of the debate, not as a grader. Audit the post's reasoning: say which claims a careful researcher would accept, which conflations or overstatements are doing work, and how the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence bear on the specific steps, above all on the claim that designers can simply choose terminal goals for which self-preservation and power-seeking are not instrumentally necessary. Finally, say whether the post's practical conclusion, that AI motivation is a specification problem rather than a separate problem about where goals come from, survives once the questionable steps are corrected.
