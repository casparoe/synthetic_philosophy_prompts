I'm a retired research chemist, and a few years after leaving the lab I got myself onto my city's Democracy and Citizenship Review Panel, mostly because I wanted something that would make me actually read political philosophy rather than skim it. We've been asked to advise the council on whether to endorse a proposal that would let non-citizen residents vote in local elections after five years of continuous residence. The panel is split right down the middle. One group says voting is part of what it means to be a citizen, and extending it to non-citizens severs the connection between the franchise and membership in the political community; they also point out that a resident who wants the vote can naturalise, and that the city shouldn't be redefining citizenship through the back door. The other group says that's exactly backwards: these people live under the same by-laws, send their children to the same schools, and pay council tax, so being governed without any say is the same sort of wrong the franchise exists to remedy, whatever passport someone happens to hold.

I've now read enough democratic theory to recognise the 'all-subjected' and 'all-affected' principles behind these positions, and I can see how each side thinks the other is smuggling in its conclusion. Draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action (endorse, reject, or endorse with conditions) with reasons the panel can actually vote on, and it should do justice to the strongest consideration on the losing side. No reading list or citations, please, and keep it to about 600–650 words, one side of A4.
