I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so forgive me if my terminology is loose. I've been researching a book about a bookmaker who's ruined by a single disasterous bet and spends the rest of the novel convinced he can "feel" the real odds under the posted ones. Reading around, I came across the idea that rational confidence ought to defer to objective chance: if you know a coin is fair and the chance of heads is one-half, you should be exactly one-half confident in heads. That seemed plainly right, and I wanted the book to dramatize it.

Then I built an argument that seems to prove too much:

(1) If you know the objective chance of an outcome is 0.5, you should be 0.5 confident in that outcome.
(2) One minute before a fair coin is tossed, you know the chance of heads is 0.5, so you are 0.5 confident in heads.
(3) One minute after the toss, before you have looked, the chance of heads is no longer 0.5: it is now 1 if the coin landed heads and 0 if it landed tails, and you know this.
(4) Now that you know the chance of heads is either 0 or 1, deference to chance seems to require that your confidence be either 0 or 1.
(5) But that's absurd: you have no way to tell which value the chance took, and reasonable people dont become certain about an unseen coin toss just because the toss has already happened.
(6) So the principle in (1) must be false, which would wreck the book.

I'd most like to keep (1), and I have a hunch the trouble is somewhere between (3) and (5), but I cant name the step. Which premise should I reject, or where does the argument go wrong? And what does rejecting it cost?
