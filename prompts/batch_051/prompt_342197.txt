I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about why some countries are rich and others aren't. The people I interview keep splitting into two camps: economists who say institutions — secure property rights, courts, limits on the executive — do the real causal work, and geographers and cultural historians who say that's a just-so story dressed up in regressions, because you can't rerun history. I don't want to write another 'experts disagree' piece, and anyway the two sides are talkig past each other. Here's what I actually want to know. Take the claim that institutions, rather than geography or culture, are the main reason some nations prospered. How could anyone test that claim? Part of what I need is for you to pin down what 'main reason' could even mean when history only ran once, and then be concrete about what evidence could bear on it.

Act as an econometrician who works on comparative development and has spent too much time arguing with economic historians. Before committing to anything, brainstorm at least five candidate research designs — natural experiments, instruments, border comparisons, planned settlements, country splits, whatever you think is strongest — and say briefly why you're rejecting the weak ones. Then, after a short overview paragraph, give me a table with these exact columns: Design (a name or one-line label), What you'd measure or compare, Result that would count in favor of the institutions story, Result that would count against it, What it would settle, What it leaves untouched. Below the table, rank the designs by how much a clean positive result should move a reasonable skeptic, and tell me which design I should be most suspicious of even though it looks tidy.
