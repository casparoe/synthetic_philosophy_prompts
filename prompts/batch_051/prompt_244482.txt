I work as a journalist covering science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and for the past several months I have been moderating a written exchange between two of our regular contributors. One defends a position in the line of Patrick Devlin: that a society is constituted in part by a shared morality, that the criminal law is entitled to enforce that morality as an act of social self-defense, and that wrongdoing done in private can nonetheless erode the public moral consensus. The other defends a position in the line of H. L. A. Hart and Mill: that the criminal law's proper business is the prevention of harm to others, and at most certain forms of serious public offense, rather than the enforcement of morality as such, and that a society's identity does not dissolve every time a contested moral standard changes. They have each accused the other of caricature, and they have now agreed to produce something like the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration: a document both can sign, followed by a precise record of what is still in dispute.

I would like you to draft that document. It should open with a section of numbered claims both parties accept, substantive enough not to be hollow and worded so that neither side feels its view has been flattened. Then include a section setting out the genuine disagreements in formulations each party would accept as a fair statement of its own position, while flagging any apparent disagreements that are really only verbal. After that, isolate the two or three cruxes on which the debate actually turns. I suspect they include at least the following: whether enforcing a society's shared morality is best understood as self-defense against a kind of social dissolution, or whether society itself is being conflated with a particular, movable set of conventions; whether the reasonable-person jury standard is a way to identify a genuine public morality or a device for licensing majority disgust; and whether purely private immorality ever harms third parties indirectly in a way the criminal law may properly cognize. Where it helps, separate the moralist's moderate thesis, that society may enforce its constitutive morality to preserve itself, from the stronger thesis, that there is in principle no limit to a society's right to legislate morality.

For each crux, specify a concrete case or body of evidence both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each side predicts. Where possible, draw the testing grounds from the original debate: the recommendation to decriminalize private consensual same-sex conduct, the later prosecution of a published directory of sex workers as a conspiracy to corrupt public morals, and a modest, purely self-regarding vice practiced by adults in private. The tone should be that of a document both parties would actually put their names to: deadpan, precise, and constructive, not a debate transcript and not a verdict. Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words, format the disagreements and predictions as a table where that helps, and do not include citations or lists of works; everything needed for the statement should be inside the document itself.
