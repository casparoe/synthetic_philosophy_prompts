A city has a persistent problem: some true and consequential items of local knowledge — the fact that a reservoir sits directly above an old mining void, a narrowly worded clause in the charter that limits the mayor's emergency powers, the flood-frequency statistics for a particular neighborhood — keep dropping out of institutional memory and public discussion. Nobody is suppressing them; they simply fail to stick. They are boring to read aloud, awkward to summarize, easy to mislabel as someone else's responsibility, and they vanish from agendas unless someone actively remembers to remember them. Call information with these properties an antimeme: an idea that resists being retained and transmitted.

The city council is considering a proposal to create a Bureau of Antimemetic Hygiene. The Bureau would maintain a public registry to which any resident may submit an item they believe is antimemetic; each registered item would be stored in three mutually isolated forms — a paper archive, a digital archive, and a brass plaque in a public square. To keep the Bureau's own staff from forgetting what the registry is for, the staff would hold a weekly recitation of the registry's opening page, and the Bureau's annual audit would be conducted by a panel of ordinary residents chosen by lottery rather than by a standing committee, on the theory that a standing committee would slowly forget to attend. Finally, any city decision above a modest budget threshold would have to cite the registry in its approval paperwork and state, for each flagged item, why that item does or does not bear on the decision. Assume the Bureau begins sincere, competent, and uncorrupted in the ordinary sense; the interesting question is what the antimemetic subject matter does to the mechanism anyway.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than a policy preference. What does it incentivize among residents, city agencies, and the Bureau's own staff? Where does it get gamed first? Which failure modes are specific to managing items that resist being noticed, and which of those failures is hardest to fix without granting the Bureau powers that create problems of their own?
