I have been trying to decide whether a structural parallel I keep noticing is deep or merely a case of pattern-matching. In Hofstadter's Ant Fugue, a colony of individually simple ants — none of which represents the colony's plans, knows where the food is, or can say what the colony believes, and even the articulate Aunt Hillary cannot identify which ant knows what the colony as a whole knows — appears, at the level of the colony, to have beliefs, goals, and something like a self. The thought experiment is meant to make the claim stronger than a useful fiction: the colony really has mental states, realized in the traffic of ants rather than in any individual ant. I see the same shape in two modern systems. A prediction market aggregates dispersed information into a price that no individual trader holds; the market as a whole reacts to news and updates its implicit probabilities, yet no trader holds the market's belief. A large language model produces assertions and inferences that seem to be distributed across many layers and attention heads; no single component stores the proposition the model as a whole seems to assert. I want to know whether these are genuinely three instances of one structure — an intentional system realized by parts that lack its intentional states — or whether the analogy falls apart under inspection. What exactly has to be true of a system's parts and their interactions for the whole to count as having beliefs and goals? Which of the three cases, if any, satisfy those conditions, and does the parallel make any prediction that turns out false?
