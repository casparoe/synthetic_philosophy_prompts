I keep coming back to a variant of the iterated prisoner's dilemma that I haven't seen spelled out, and I want to know how the analysis actually changes. Standard infinite-horizon IPD: per-round payoffs T=5, R=3, P=1, S=0, common discount factor δ, no third parties to switch to. The folk theorem story says cooperation can be sustained by grim trigger: cheat once, grab 5, then both defect forever at 1; cooperate forever for 3, so the punishment is the mutual-defection payoff.

Now add an autarky move. Before choosing C or D in a round, either player can unilaterally choose A. If at least one player chooses A, both get 2 that round and no C/D interaction happens. So 2 is a safe-but-medicore outside option: better than mutual defection, worse than mutual cooperation.

What I want worked through is three versions:

(a) no A at all;
(b) reversible A — choosing A only lasts for that round, and next round you can go back to C/D;
(c) irreversible A — the first time anyone chooses A, the partnership ends and both get 2 per round forever after.

For each: is (C,C) every round a subgame-perfect equilibrium, and for which δ? Does grim trigger still make sense, or do the sensible strategies change once A exists? What about always-A, or tit-for-tat where you answer defection with A for a while? Here's my hunch, which I haven't verified: in (b) mutual defect isn't even a stage-game equilibrium anymore because either player can jump to A, so the only credible punishment is softer (2 rather than 1), which might make cooperation harder, not easier. Worth checking.

Then the part I actually care about. People defend autarky for persons and nations as an exit option that keeps you from being exploited: fuck-you money for individuals, reserve capacity or trade barriers for states. Does the reversible/irreversible distinction change that story? Reversible A looks like a cushion; irreversible A looks like it commits you out of future gains from trade. After running the equilibria, what's the actual normative upshot for self-sufficiency as an ideal — is it a buffer, a trap, or does the answer depend on the details I've laid out?
