I have been reading about the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals and keep returning to the retroactivity objection. The defendants were charged with crimes against peace and crimes against humanity—categories that, when the relevant acts were committed, were at best nascent in international criminal law—and the defense insisted that punishing them under newly articulated legal standards violated the maxim nulla poena sine lege. The standard reply at the time was that these categories were not truly new: they made explicit norms that were already binding as custom, including an existing renunciation of aggressive war.

Since then the legal landscape has changed considerably. The core international crimes have been written into multilateral treaties, and a permanent international criminal court now applies an explicit principle of legality when it tries the same kinds of conduct. I am trying to work out whether that later development defeats the original objection or merely relocates it. Does the retroactivity objection at Nuremberg and Tokyo survive the codification of international criminal law? In answering, please identify the precise concern, or premise, on which the objection really rested; assess whether prospective codification addresses that concern or changes its form—for instance, by turning it into disputes about when a customary norm crystallized, or about how broadly vaguely worded definitions may legitimately be interpreted against a defendant; and then say whether the strongest repaired version of the objection still has force against contemporary international criminal law.
