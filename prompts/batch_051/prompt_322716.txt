You are setting the take-home essay question for the final assessment in a graduate seminar on idealized and bounded rationality. The question must be answerable in 90 minutes with an expected answer of about 800 words, and it must be self-contained: a student who has done no outside reading should be able to answer it from the question alone. The assessment should not require citations, bibliographies, or knowledge of any particular published treatment.

Write one essay question on whether HCH (Humans Consulting HCH) is a good idealization of human deliberation, and then provide a model answer and a marking scheme. Include the following working definitions in the question itself, in your own words:

- Factored cognition: the claim that a cognitive task can be split into many small subtasks, each small enough for one unaided human to do reliably in a limited time and context, such that the subtasks can be combined into an answer to a problem no single human could solve.
- HCH: the limiting procedure in which a human either answers directly when a question is small enough, or else decomposes the question into subquestions, delegates each subquestion to a fresh copy of the same human-with-delegation-powers, and aggregates the copies' answers. The base case is an unaided human answering within a short budget; the recursion adds no AI, only more human reasoning.
- Distillation: training a fast model to imitate the slow amplified procedure, often with the aim of inserting the fast model into the next round of amplification.

The question must not be answerable by reproducing these definitions. It should center on the following crux: HCH is presented as a way to extract capabilities that no individual human has, yet its definition quietly assumes that a human can factor an unfamiliar problem into safe subquestions, check the subanswers, and aggregate them without already possessing much of the competence the full answer requires. Is that assumption a benign idealization, or does it beg the question against the very limitation HCH was introduced to overcome?

The essay question should ask students to do the following: identify at least two idealizations built into HCH or factored cognition beyond the simple assumption of unlimited time and unlimited copies; show concretely how each could fail in real human deliberation (for example, decomposition may itself be a skilled act; verification may be as hard as generation; aggregation may distort; preferences or standards may drift across levels); and argue whether each failure undermines HCH's standing as an epistemic idealization of what a human would conclude with unlimited help, as an alignment target, or both. It should leave room for a strong student to argue in more than one direction rather than dictating a single conclusion.

After the question, provide a model answer of roughly 800 words that a first-class student might write, pitched at the same level. Then provide a marking scheme with five must-hit points worth 4 marks each (total 20). For each point, name it, state what earns the marks, and add a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. The scheme should make it impossible to pass by reciting the HCH procedure and the phrase "factored cognition"; the marks must reward diagnosing which assumptions are load-bearing and saying what happens when they fail.
