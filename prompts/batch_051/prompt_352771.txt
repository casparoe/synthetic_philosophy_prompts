I work as a software engineer on a team building a consumer brain-computer interface, and I have been asked to help structure an internal policy discussion about mental privacy and cognitive liberty before we ship a feature that continuously estimates attention and cognitive load from EEG and shares a summary with an employer dashboard. I have no formal philosophy background, so I need a document I can hand to technically literate colleagues that lets them locate their actual commitments without first learning the academic labels.

Please write a decision tree, with seven to nine leaves, that sorts a person into a position on the governance of neural data. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of positions: first the branch questions and the combinations of answers they define, then the actual positions located at the resulting leaves. Root the tree in the following concrete case rather than a slogan: a company markets a non-invasive headset that continuously infers attention, cognitive load, and mind-wandering from neural signals, and offers employers a dashboard summarizing these states for workers who opt in.

Every branch node should be a question about a scenario or a policy choice that an engineer can answer without knowing which position it leads to. A node might ask, for example, whether a company may infer something a user did not intend to communicate from the raw signal, whether an employee can meaningfully consent to continuous workplace monitoring of this sort, whether a model trained on many users' brain data creates obligations toward people who never contributed data, or whether reading from the brain and writing to it raise the same problem. No node should simply ask whether someone believes in cognitive liberty or whether the mind is the seat of the self.

The leaves should include at least the following positions, under whatever names you find clearest, with a one-sentence characterization and one sentence on the position's most awkward practical commitment:

- The continuity view: neural data raises no qualitatively new problem, and existing privacy and data-protection frameworks are the right tools.
- The sensitive-data view: neural data is unusually revealing and deserves stricter protections, but consent, purpose limitation, and data rights remain the correct machinery.
- The neurorights/integrity exceptionalist view: neural data is different in kind because of its connection to the self, so at least some protections must be inalienable and cannot be waived by consent.
- The negative cognitive liberty view: the priority is freedom from nonconsensual reading or writing to the brain, understood as a negative liberty.
- The positive cognitive liberty view: freedom also includes an entitlement to use neurotechnology to modify one's own cognition, so paternalistic restrictions on enhancement are themselves a threat.
- The republican/anti-domination view: the decisive problem is not any particular intrusion but structural power, such as employers or platforms acquiring arbitrary capacity over inner life.
- The relational or collective view: neural data is not effectively individual, because models trained across many brains permit inferences about people who never provided data, so protections must operate at the group level.

Please also do three things. First, mark at least one combination of answers that the tree's logic permits but that, as far as you can tell, no serious position occupies, and say briefly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored. Second, flag any two leaves that the tree cannot cleanly separate once the questions are written in non-specialist terms, and state exactly where their answers converge. Third, for each split, keep explicit track of which layer of the technology is doing the work, raw neural signal, inferred mental state, or deliberate output and action, and whether the case involves reading from or writing to the brain. Our product discussion turns on these layers, and a taxonomy that blurs them will not be useful to us.

No bibliographies, reading lists, or citations are needed; named positions with short characterizations are sufficient.
