I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I want to make my uncertainty about the moral psychology of markets explicit. Give me your credence that, in modern commercial societies, the spread of market exchange has on balance strengthened everyday virtues—honesty toward strangers, promise-keeping, prudence, forbearance—more than it has strengthened greed, the habit of treating people instrumentally, and the reduction of relationships to self-interested calculation. Decompose the question into at least: the direct effect of repeated market exchange on individual character; the motivational crowding-out thesis, i.e., that monetary incentives erode existing non-market motives; the selection-and-reputation view, i.e., that markets reward and spread traits people already have rather than transform them; and the possibility that any observed link between commercial society and cooperative or liberal norms is carried by shared institutions and rising wealth rather than by character. Give each component a rough probability and a line of justification, show how you combine them into your overall credence, and say which component the final number is most sensitive to and what evidence would move it most. As you go, separate what is reasonably settled from what is genuinely contested among moral psychologists and economic historians, and do not present a contested reading of the evidence as the consensus.
