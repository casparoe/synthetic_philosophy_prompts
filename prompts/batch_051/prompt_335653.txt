In a seminar on the ethics of consumption, a classmate defended a line of reasoning about frugality that has been nagging me ever since. We were trying to decide whether frugality is the kind of value one should instantiate in one's own life — by spending less oneself — or the kind one should promote in the world — by getting others, institutions, and markets to consume less. She maintained that on either reading the value undoes itself, and she set the argument out like this:

(1) If frugality is a genuine value, then either it is valuable as a pattern of individual conduct (the practicing reading) or it is valuable as a social state to be brought about (the promoting reading). (2) On the practicing reading, each person has reason to forgo consumption even when others do not. (3) Yet one person's forgone consumption, taken in isolation, does not appreciably reduce aggregate consumption: the money not spent is saved, lent, or spent later, and one buyer's restraint lowers prices and invites offsetting consumption by others. (4) So the agent who practices frugality alone incurs real sacrifice without realizing the aggregate good that was supposed to justify the sacrifice. (5) The only escape is to say the sacrifice is intrinsically right; but that is just to abandon the claim that frugality matters because of what it does, and it leaves unexplained why this particular form of austerity should be binding. (6) On the promoting reading, each person has reason to direct their resources at changing the incentives, norms, and policies that govern everyone else's consumption, since that is the only reliable way to lower aggregate consumption. (7) But if everyone acted on that reason, everyone would spend the surplus trying to make other people frugal while treating themselves as exempt from the practice, and the promotion itself would consume much of what frugality was meant to conserve; the desired social state would never arrive. (8) Thus the promoting reading is self-effacing in a vicious sense: it can reach the state it values only if most people do not act as it says. (9) A value that is either causally ineffectual when practiced or self-defeating when promoted is no value at all. (10) Therefore frugality is not a value; and by parity of reasoning, honesty, generosity, and resource conservation are not values either, since each depends on broad uptake for its point.

I find the conclusion absurd, but I cannot locate the first wrong turn. I would most like to keep the idea that frugality is a real value and that each person has some reason to practice it even when the individual causal contribution is small. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
