I'm a journalist who covers science and economics, and I'm working on a feature about how mathematical frameworks shape decisions under uncertainty. A draft of a general-interest article on ergodicity economics was shared with me, and I want to audit its conceptual substance before I rely on it or push back on it. Its account, as far as I can reconstruct it, runs as follows.

Economics has long judged risky prospects by their expected value, which is an average over a huge collection of parallel copies of the same situation. Ergodicity economics says that this is the wrong currency for an actual person who lives through time. What matters to a single decision-maker is the time average: the long-run growth rate of wealth along one life. When wealth changes multiplicatively, the time average corresponds to the geometric average of the outcome multipliers, whereas expected value is the arithmetic average. If the two averages coincide, the process is ergodic; if they diverge, it is not. A sequence of gambles can have a positive arithmetic average while its geometric average is negative, so the ensemble average rises even though a typical single player's wealth declines. Because most real risks are multiplicative and recurring, ordinary people are rationally right to turn down many gambles with positive expected value, and what is often labeled excessive risk aversion is really the sensible avoidance of negative time-average growth. The draft then goes further and says that this overturns expected utility theory, or at least strips it of any coherent interpretation, because the theory never distinguished the two averages. It concludes that the St. Petersburg paradox, the equity premium puzzle, and the under-insurance of rare catastrophic risks all dissolve once ensemble averages are replaced by time averages.

I suspect the rhetorical temperature is too high here. Modern decision theory is built on expected utility rather than literally on expected monetary value, and I doubt a heterodox movement simply overturns it; I also doubt that three longstanding puzzles just dissolve.
