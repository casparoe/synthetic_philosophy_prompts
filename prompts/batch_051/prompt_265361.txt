I am preparing notes for a reading group session on formal epistemology, and I have been trying to organise the main responses to the problem of logical omniscience into families that differ in their basic strategy rather than in details of presentation. The problem, as I understand it, is that standard possible-worlds semantics for knowledge and belief validates closure under entailment: if an agent knows that p, and p entails q, then the agent knows that q, and every logical truth comes out known automatically. Actual agents plainly fail at both.

Here is the list of response-families I have collected so far. (1) Awareness-based approaches: keep the possible-worlds core but distinguish implicit knowledge, which is closed under consequence, from explicit knowledge, which requires an awareness function that selects the sentences the agent explicitly entertains; closure then fails for explicit knowledge. (2) Syntactic approaches: model knowledge or belief as an attitude to sentences, governed by whatever limited proof rules one actually licenses, rather than as a relation to sets of worlds, so closure is no longer built into the semantics. (3) Impossible-worlds approaches: admit worlds in which classical logical truths can fail or the truth of a compound is not compositional, so the worlds compatible with what the agent knows need not validate every consequence. (4) Resource-bounded or algorithmic approaches: identify knowledge with what an idealised procedure can compute within a specified resource bound, so consequences that would require too many steps, or too much memory, remain unknown. (5) Fragmentation approaches: represent the agent's total state as split into separately accessed fragments, so a consequence of beliefs stored in different fragments need not be believed within any single fragment.

I keep feeling that there is at least one further family of response, distinct in its basic strategy from all five and not merely a special case or a combination of them. What is it? Give the core idea precisely, show how it blocks the unwanted closure, and say what it captures that the five on the list do not. I am less interested in a name or a citation than in the mechanism: what stays, what is given up, and where exactly the closure step is broken. If, after reflection, you take the five to be exhaustive, defend that judgement instead: say what the five have in common that makes the space of solutions close with them, and describe where a sixth proposal would have to depart.
