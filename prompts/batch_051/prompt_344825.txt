I am a software engineer who has been reading Poincaré’s Science and Method (in the public domain translation), and I keep returning to a passage in the chapter on mathematical intuition where he argues—paraphrased, since I don’t have the exact translation in front of me—that a proof is not simply a sequence of syllogisms; if it were, a machine could assemble valid steps, and the result would still not be mathematics. What matters, he seems to say, is the order or the unity of the steps, not the mere fact that each follows logically from the last.

This looks like the following argument, which I find hard to resist:

(1) A published mathematical proof, when fully rigorous, is a finite sequence of statements such that each statement is either an axiom, a definition, or follows by a recognized rule of inference from earlier statements.
(2) If checking that each statement satisfies (1) were sufficient for understanding the proof, then a proof checker that verifies each inference would thereby understand the proof.
(3) But a proof checker does not understand the proof; it merely verifies local logical moves.
(4) So checking each inference is not sufficient for understanding a proof.
(5) Mathematical rigor, at least as formalized in things like Lean, consists exactly in checking that each inference is valid.
(6) Therefore mathematical rigor is not sufficient for mathematical understanding.

So far this agrees with my experience: a formally verified proof can be entirely opaque, and after passing it through a checker I may still have no idea why the theorem is true. But then the argument seems to prove too much:

(7) If rigor is not sufficient for understanding, then no purely logical reconstruction of a proof—no fully formalized proof—can ever by itself constitute mathematical understanding.
(8) But that is too strong; formalization sometimes catches errors and reveals gaps, so it must contribute something to mathematical knowledge beyond mere mechanical checking.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would prefer to keep (6) and (8), but I suspect the slide is from (4) or (5) into (7), as if “not sufficient” meant “irrelevant.” If the flaw is in (7), please say exactly which logical relation I’m getting wrong.
