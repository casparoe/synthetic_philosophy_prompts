I am a retired research chemist. For most of my working life I was used to disputes that could in principle be settled by a better measurement, and I have recently turned to philosophy, where disagreements often seem to resist that kind of resolution. The debate that has most occupied me is in the philosophy of law: whether a person's ignorance of a legal rule bears on how fair it is to apply that rule to them.

The two positions I have in mind I will call the fair-notice position and the systemic-validity position. The fair-notice position holds that a legal rule may legitimately bind a person, and in particular may legitimately be enforced against them, only if the rule was reasonably knowable to them before the act in question; a law that could not have been known fails a basic requirement of law as something addressed to its subjects. The systemic-validity position holds that, within a functioning legal order, the validity and application of a rule cannot turn, except in narrow and carefully controlled ways, on what any given person actually or constructively knew; a legal system in which ignorance of a rule were generally a defense could not coordinate a complex society, and whatever unfairness arises in individual cases is best handled through the design of offenses, intent requirements, prosecutorial discretion, or sentencing rather than by suspending the law's operation.

I would like you to draft the joint statement that a fair-notice theorist and a systemic-validity theorist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Structure it as follows. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: substantive propositions about law, knowledge, publication, fairness, and enforcement that neither side would reject. The test is that each claim has real content yet each camp can sign it without embarrassment. Secondly, a disagreements table. For each remaining point of contention, state the precise question at issue; then give the fair-notice formulation and the systemic-validity formulation, each phrased so that its own proponents would accept it as a strong and accurate statement of their view. Thirdly, identify the cruxes, by which I mean the sub-questions such that a well-supported answer would move at least one side. I suspect the following will matter, but you should test and refine them rather than simply reproduce them: whether the primary office of law is ex ante guidance of conduct or ex post settlement of disputes; whether a standard of reasonable knowability can be made administrable without collapsing into ad hoc line-drawing; and whether the unfairness to an unknowing defendant resides in the rule itself or in the decision to punish under it. Fourthly, for each crux specify a concrete case or piece of evidence that both camps would accept bears on it, and record in advance what each side would predict or conclude from it. Cases worth drawing on include a genuinely secret administrative rule, a newly enacted regulation buried in a large body of official notices, a first offender charged under a highly technical tax or environmental reporting provision whose conduct is not wrong apart from the rule, a statute written in language intelligible only to specialists, and a traveler penalized under a foreign law they could not plausibly have known. You need not use all of these, but the cases you use should do real work.

Close by saying which parts of the original dispute, if any, look verbal rather than substantive after the reconstruction. The whole should read as one constructive text that both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact.
