I'm putting together my privecy views for a workshop and I'd like a conistency check before I have to defend them. Here's where I've landed:

(a) privacy's value is entirely instrumental—it matters because of what exposure does to people: humiliation, reputational damage, discrimination, being manipulated, that sort of thing;
(b) if a privacy violation causes no actual harm and no real risk of harm, calling it a "wrong" is really just a figure of speech—the moral weight is carried entirely by the harm or the risk;
(c) I read this passage from Warren and Brandeis, The Right to Privacy (1890), as making exactly that instrumental point, since they locate the evil of exposure in the pain and distress it causes:

"The intensity and complexity of life, attendant upon advancing civilization, have rendered necessary some retreat from the world, and man, under the refining influence of culture, has become more sensitive to publicity, so that solitude and privacy have become more essential to the individual; but modern enterprise and invention have, through invasions upon his privacy, subjected him to mental pain and distress, far greater than could be inflicted by mere bodily injury."

(d) but I also think a peeping tom genuinely wrongs you even if you never find out and the information goes no further—there's something objectionable in the act itself;
(e) and I think what's most wrong with surreptitiously photographing someone in a private moment isn't that the photo might leak, it's that it denies the person a say over how they present themselves to others.

Can I keep all of these? Go through and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like they are, ranked by severity, and for each tension what the cheapest fix would be. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
