Been going down a rabbit hole on legal proof lately. A bunch of LessWrong-adjacent people I read keep treating the blue bus and gatecrasher cases like they settle a huge swath of the evidence debate, and I'm starting to think the toy example is doing more work than its earning. The move, as I've seen it made: 1000 people in a stadium, 499 paid, so a randomly selected spectator is 50.1% likely to have jumped the turnstile—yet nobody thinks you've met even civil preponderence (let alone beyond a reasonable doubt) if you sue that random guy for the ticket. Similarly, a bus runs you off the road, 80% of buses on that route are owned by Blue Bus Co., so it's 80% likely theirs—but courts still balk at finding Blue Bus liable. The conclusion people draw is that bare frequencies can never be enough to find a particular person liable, because they don't connect the defendant to the event the way a witness or a fingerprint does.

I want this audited as an analogy. When people export blue bus/gatecrasher to a general principle about evidence, what's in the mapping—what plays the role of the ticket count, the bus-route frequency, the random draw, the individualizing evidence that's missing—and which features of the toy cases are quietly doing the anti-statistics work? Where does the analogy break once you bring in DNA random-match probabilities, for example, or epidemiological evidence in toxic torts, both of which are also just group frequencies aimed at an individual? Does the conclusion—naked statistical evidence can't carry a verdict—survive on the merits, or has the analogy been smuggling it in all along? And if it's a bad analogy in the places where I want to use it, what would a better one separate?
