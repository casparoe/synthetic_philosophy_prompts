You are a professional metaphysician who works on modality and personal identity. I am going to report a disagreement I heard between two graduate students and then ask you to adjudicate it. Work carefully: before evaluating anything, take a moment to reconstruct the strongest version of each move so that the weaker wording does not decide the outcome; then assess the moves as actually made.

Last week in a philosophy common room, two students argued about origin essentialism. Their exchange, as I remember it, went like this:

(1) Sam: You could not have come from a different sperm and egg. The zygote you developed from was a numerically distinct biological individual with its own genome, and you simply are that individual continued through ordinary development. If a different sperm had fertilized the egg, the resulting zygote would have had a different genome and would have been another organism — a sibling, not you. So the gametes you came from are essential to your existence: any possible world in which those particular gametes never met is a world in which you never existed.

(2) Dana: That only works if you are the organism. But personal identity is not animal identity: intuitively you could survive a cerebral transplant or the gradual replacement of nearly all your body, so the body is at most a vehicle for the person. What makes you the same person over time is psychological continuity — overlapping memories, character, capacities, and the right causal dependence among them. The sperm and egg are merely the causal precursors of that vehicle. If a different sperm and egg had produced a life with the same psychological history, it would have been your life, not merely a life similar to yours.

(3) Sam: That is exactly the step I reject. Similarity across possible worlds, however detailed, cannot give numerical identity. Even a perfect psychological duplicate in another possible world would still be someone else, because identity is not a matter of degree and is not fixed by matching descriptions. More importantly, the zygote is not a “precursor” you can detach from the person: it is the earliest stage of the very individual you are. You are deciding that the person begins later only because that is what your psychological criterion needs.

(4) Dana: And you are deciding that the person begins at conception only because that is what your organism criterion needs. For a person, as distinct from the biological organism, there is no “earlier stage” before there is a subject of a mental life and the beginnings of a psychological continuity. The embryo is a precursor organism in roughly the way the particular carbon atoms that happened to form the first prebiotic molecules are precursors: they were needed to produce this body, but they are not part of the identity of the person whose body it is. If the person is a psycho-physical continuant, her origins lie where that psychological continuity emerges, and that continuity could in principle have had a different biological base.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each numbered move, rate it as clearly wrong, defensible but under-supported, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating on its philosophical merits, not its rhetoric. Then give an overall verdict: which specific claims are each of the two parties entitled to, what would each need to establish to win the points still in dispute, and what distinction, or distinctions, neither of them explicitly drew would have done most to move the argument forward?
