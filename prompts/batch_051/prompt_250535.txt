I direct the risk-triage function at a philanthropic foundation that funds research on low-probability, high-severity threats, including engineered pandemics and catastrophic infrastructure failure. In weekly screening meetings, the sentence "possible is not the same as probable" ends arguments in both directions. Sometimes an analyst flags a catastrophic scenario that our current models do not exclude, and a reviewer replies, "that is possible, but possible is not probable," and the item is deferred. At other times an analyst argues that a scenario cannot yet be assigned any base rate, and a reviewer says, "if it is genuinely possible, we cannot set it aside as merely speculative." I want to stop the slogan functioning as a debate-ender in either direction.

Please disambiguate the sentence "possible is not the same as probable" for use in this kind of risk triage. Give me a menu of the distinct precise claims the sentence could be making, ordered from trivial to radical. For each reading, specify what it would amount to formally: which sense of "possible" (logical, metaphysical, epistemic, or physical) and which sense of "probable" (subjective credence, objective chance, or model-relative probability) it pairs, and whether the claim is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false. In particular, separate:

(i) claims about the relation between modal and probabilistic notions themselves,
(ii) claims about our evidence for attributing possibility or probability, and
(iii) decision-theoretic claims about whether a mere possibility should shift action thresholds.

Then say which reading, if any, actually licenses deferring a "merely possible" catastrophic scenario, and which reading licenses treating a possibility as a live risk, matching each of the two stock moves I described. Flag the equivocations the slogan lets us slide across, especially the slide between "not ruled out by our models" and "assignable a non-negligible probability," and between "possible" in the sense of consistency with evidence and "possible" in the sense of worth taking seriously. End with two or three one-sentence formulations I could adopt in the review room that are precise enough to prevent the equivocations, for example one that distinguishes logical possibility without a probability from physical possibility under our best models.
