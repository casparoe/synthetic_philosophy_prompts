I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about performative utterances — the idea that with some words, saying it is doing it, or at least partly doing it — and I keep finding that my gut verdicts point in different directions. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher of language who specializes in speech acts, and to think carefully rather than just pattern-matching to the standard examples. Maybe start by brainstroming the different principles that could be pulling on these cases, then tell me which one (or which small set) best captures them.

Here are my verdicts, for what they're worth.

If you say to me "I promise to pay you back Friday," and I happen to know you've already decided not to pay, I still think you promised. Insincere, sure, and probably a bad thing to do, but the promise is made and later broken.

If a stranger on a train says "I order you to give me your seat," I don't think I've actually been ordered; it was an attempted order, or a demand dressed up as one. If a police officer on duty says "I order you to step back," that is an order. So standing seems to matter there.

Same pattern, maybe stronger: a judge saying "I sentence you to ten years" does the sentencing — it changes the defendant's legal situation then and there. If I, a random person, shout the same words in the courtroom, nothing legal happens. I want to know whether "order" and "sentence" are governed by the same principle or two related ones.

Here's a pair that really bugs me. My friend says "I apologize" and later tells someone else she didn't feel sorry at all. I still want to say she apologized — a hollow apology, but an apology. Yet if she says "I forgive you" while still seething with resentment, I don't want to say she forgave me. I can't make sense of the asymmetry. Maybe one of those verdicts has to go, but I honestly don't know which.

If someone says to me "I insult you," I don't think that itself insults me. It's a strange, maybe hostile, thing to say, but the saying isn't the insult. But "I compliment you on that talk" does seem to compliment you. So some first-person speech-act verbs do the act and some don't, and I can't see what distinguishes them.

If you say "I warn you there's a wasp on your shoulder," that's a warning, and it's still a warning even if I don't believe you and get stung. But if you say "I warn you I'll make your life miserable if you don't leave," I want to say that's a threat wearing a warning's clothes, not a warning. Yet both start with "I warn you." Where's the line between a warning and a threat, if the word "warn" doesn't mark it?

"I persuade you to come to the party" obviously doesn't persuade me — persuasion happens or doesn't in the hearer's head, and no formula can guarantee it. Same for "I bore you": saying it isn't boring me. These feel like a different kind of creature, like they name an effect rather than do a thing.

If I already know the meeting is at three and you say "I inform you the meeting is at three," I don't think you've informed me. If I didn't know, you have. So informing seems to depend on something in the hearer's mind, whereas promising doesn't.

If I say "thank you" resentfully through gritted teeth, I still think I thanked you — badly, but I did it. Same shape as the apology case, which is why the forgiveness one nags at me.

The one I flip-flop on daily: if I say "I bet you five dollars it'll rain," is the bet made the moment I say it, like a promise, or only once you accept? I've held both views in the same afternoon.

My loose hypothesis is something like: an utterance does the act when there's a convention that lets the right sort of person, in the right sort of setting, make it so by those words. But that doesn't obviously cover apologies or thanks, and it seems to fail for the insult/compliment pair.

So my question is: is there one coherent account — or at most a small set of clearly stated principles — that delivers all or most of these verdicts? Please state the account precisely, say which case or two most motivates each clause, and say what it predicts about these cases I've left out: "I note that you've been late three times this month," "I dare you to ask her out," and "I request that you leave." Also tell me honestly which of my gut verdicts the account can't keep, and why you'd rather have the cleaner principle than save all my intuitions. And don't just wave at buzzwords like "felicity conditions" or "illocutionary force" — I want the actual workings, even if that means telling me some of my verdicts are wrong.
