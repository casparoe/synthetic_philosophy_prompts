So in AP Euro we read a chunk of Vattel's The Law of Nations, Book II, Chapter IV, Section 56. I don’t have it in front of me so I’ll just paraphrase the part I mean. Vattel says when a ruler breaks the fundamental laws and becomes a tyrant, his subjects get a right to resist. He also says foreign nations have a right, maybe even a duty under the offices of humanity, to help a people who are being oppressed by their own ruler, especially if the people ask for assistance.

I've been trying to see if that actually yields a usable rule for things like Kosovo, Iraq, and Libya. Here's my reconstruction of the argument people use, with Vattel as the base:

(1) If a ruler is committing serious crimes against his own population, the population has a just cause to resist him.
(2) If a population has a just cause to resist, foreign states have a right to intervene militarily on their side.
(3) Milosevic's repression of Kosovar Albanians gave them a just cause to resist.
(4) So NATO's intervention in Kosovo was justified by a Vattel-style test.
(5) The same test would justify intervention in any country whose ruler is brutal enough, and deny it otherwise.
(6) But Vattel gives no clear line between a bad ruler and a tyrant, so brutal enough gets filled in by the intervening states themselves.
(7) A just-war principle that gets filled in by the intervener's own judgment can't function as a genuine moral limit.
(8) So Vattel's principle either fails as a real criterion, or it makes regime change too easy.

Is this argument valid? If not, which exact step is wrong? I keep thinking the problem is step (6), but maybe (2) is doing more work than Vattel actually allows. Or is the conclusion actually fine because Vattel just didn't worry about modern humanitarian intervention?
