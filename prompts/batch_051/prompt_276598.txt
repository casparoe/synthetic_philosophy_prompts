I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so write in crisp, concrete language and avoid academic jargon. Write a dialogue of at most 900 words — strict limit — between two evenly matched engineers walking home after a late-night test of a generative-agent simulation. Watching the agents has pulled them back into an old argument: the feeling that we are the main character, that our own inner life is vivid and certain while everyone else's is merely inferred. Character A treats this feeling as a live epistemic asymmetry: other people could be NPCs in the only sense that matters, and they cannot be ruled out. Character B treats the feeling as an architectural artifact: a single stream of attention will always feel categorically different from inferred minds, even when those minds run on the same kind of machinery, so the feeling cannot count as evidence. Neither should be a strawman; each must answer, not sidestep, the other's strongest point, and each must concede at least one specific thing the other has established. A should force B to admit that the asymmetry is real: a person has a kind of access to their own experience that they will never have to another person's. B should force A to admit that a felt asymmetry is an indexical fact — here feels special on a map with no privileged location, simply because every perspective has an origin — and that this cannot license a metaphysical conclusion about what else exists. In the middle, have one of them raise the possibility that if the world were itself a simulation, the NPC hypothesis becomes ordinary engineering rather than solipsism; the other should push back that this changes the tools of the claim without changing the evidence, because the felt asymmetry would be exactly the same inside the simulation. The dialogue should open with a single line that states the disagreement sharply, and close with each character giving a one-sentence statement of where they now stand, neither identical to where they began. Do not include citations, footnotes, quoted material, or named thinkers; the characters should argue in their own words. Label the speakers A: and B:.
