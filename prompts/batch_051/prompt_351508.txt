Please adopt the role of a professional philosopher of mind and aesthetics, specializing in emotion and fiction. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming several candidate principles before settling on one; then state and defend it. I am an undergraduate in a course on the philosophy of fiction, and I have been collecting my own emotional responses to fiction to see whether any one principle captures them. Here are my verdicts:

1. During a slasher film I flinch, my pulse rises, and I grip my seat. I do not believe any monster is present or that I am in danger, and I would not flee the theater. I call this fear — real fear, not merely make-believe fear.

2. When a character I have followed for hundreds of pages dies at the end of a novel, I feel something very like grief. But I resist calling it grief, because I do not believe anyone has actually died; I settle for "sadness with a fictional object."

3. I feel intense indignation at a villain who betrays a character I love. The indignation feels real and is directed at the villain, yet I have no desire that any actual person be punished, since I know there is no such person.

4. In a second-person video game, the character "you" control has a cruel act built into their backstory, imposed on me by the game. I feel something uncomfortably close to shame, but I do not call it shame, since I do not believe I did anything shameful.

5. I abandon a novel once I learn it is a close fictionalization of a real atrocity, although a novel with the same plot details but labeled as pure fiction did not disturb me the same way. The distress clearly tracks my belief about whether the events really occurred, even though the text is nearly identical.

6. In a fictional retelling of a real disaster in which the victims are saved at the last moment, I feel glad at the ending — but uneasy too, because if someone asked me whether I am glad about what really happened, the honest answer is no: that was a tragedy.

7. On a second viewing of a film, already knowing exactly what will happen, I still cry at the same scene.

My tentative principle has been this: emotions elicited by fiction are genuine emotions of the ordinary kinds, directed at the contents of a representation — at characters and events as represented — and they differ from real-directed emotions only in their object, not in their kind. But cases 2, 4, 5, and 6 seem to strain it. What is the best principle, or small set of principles, that captures these verdicts? State it precisely, apply it to each case, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and test it against one further case of your own devising.
