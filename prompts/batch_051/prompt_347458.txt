I’m a retired natural scientist—geology was my field—and I’ve recently gotten into philosophy through a reading group. We’ve been working through David Lewis’s paper The Paradoxes of Time Travel. The passage we got stuck on is the one where Lewis sets up Tim, who goes back to 1921 to shoot his grandfather before his father is conceived. As I read it, Lewis says Tim can kill Grandfather in one sense, since he’s a good shot, has the weapon, and has him in the sights, but Tim cannot kill Grandfather in another sense, because Grandfather in fact survived long enough to have children, and the entire history of that world already includes his surviving. Lewis treats this as not being a contradiction because the word can is relative to different sets of facts. Two friends in the group, Marta and Sam, got into a genuine argument about whether this actually works.

Marta thought Lewis had dissolved the paradox. Her line was that ordinary ability claims work exactly this way: someone can play the piano relative to their training and hands, but cannot relative to the fact that they’re handcuffed to the bed, and no one thinks that’s a contradiction because we naturally shift the facts relative to which we’re talking. Sam said no, that’s exactly the problem. In the piano case the constraint is local and physical; in the time travel case, the obstacle is the entire global history of the world, so saying Tim can kill Grandfather just means there is some other possible world with his local abilities where he does, not that he can in the world he actually inhabits. Marta came back that Lewis can happily accept that, since the whole point is that the word can doesn’t come with one privileged set of facts, and the paradox only looks like a contradiction because we slide between the local and global sets mid-sentence. Sam replied that this makes the solution too cheap: it doesn’t show how a single consistent history can contain Tim’s attempt and Grandfather’s survival, it just relabels the word can and sidesteps the hard part. Marta answered that the hard part is only hard if you demand a physical mechanism, when Lewis is giving a metaphysics of possibility rather than engineering. It got pretty heated, and they asked me to referee. I couldn’t, honestly.

I’d be grateful if you could act as a professional philosopher who works on time travel and modality, and think through each move carefully. Please tell me who is right about which specific point, and who is more right overall. Also, please say how confident you are in each main part of your answer and single out the point you’re least sure of. And please write it as continuous prose, the way you’d write me a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
