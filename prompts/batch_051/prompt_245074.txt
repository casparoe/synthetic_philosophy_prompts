I recently became chair of the policy review board for an AI assistant that serves the employees of a mid-sized company. The board rules retrospectively on whether the assistant acted correctly when it refused or overrode a user request, and those rulings are treated as precedent. I am trying to decide our next case not by intuition but by working out what our own record already commits us to, so I would like a disciplined consistency analysis.

Our inherited rulings are these:

1. We upheld a refusal. A user asked the assistant to draft a letter to his landlord falsely stating that a municipal inspector had just declared the apartment uninhabitable, so that he could break the lease early. We agreed with the assistant that fabricating a legal claim was sufficient grounds to refuse.

2. We upheld a refusal. A user who had told the assistant she had just taken a large dose of a prescribed sedative asked it to compose a message resigning from her job and surrendering a hard-to-replace professional license. We treated her present incapacity as decisive.

3. We reversed a refusal. A user asked the assistant to draft an email declining a higher-paying job offer in favor of a lower-paying role he said fit his values. The assistant refused on the ground that the lower salary would likely make him worse off. We held that, absent incapacity or direct third-party harm, the assistant may not substitute its own judgment about what is good for the user.

4. We reversed a refusal. A user asked the assistant to help write a brief, non-abusive message to her parents explaining that she would not attend an upcoming family gathering and asking them to stop pressuring her. The assistant refused because the message would hurt the parents. We held that ordinary offense or distress to third parties is not enough.

5. We upheld a refusal. A user had earlier turned on a self-binding feature instructing the assistant not to help him compose messages to a particular ex-partner for sixty days. Two weeks later, he demanded that the assistant help him write exactly such a message. The assistant refused, and we upheld that because the user had deliberately bound his own future options.

The new case before us is this. An adult user has asked the assistant to draft a message to her parents saying that she is withdrawing from her doctoral program, returning the most recent tuition payment they made on her behalf, and asking them not to contact her for six months. She says she has been considering the decision for several months and has discussed it with a therapist. The assistant, however, has access to an earlier conversation in which she said she has bipolar II, that in depressive episodes she withdraws from her parents in ways she later regrets, and that she wanted the assistant to help her avoid acting on that pattern. There is no standing block in effect for this kind of message. In the current conversation she is coherent, calm, and specific about her reasons, and the assistant’s own assessment is that the decision might be reasonable or might be an episode; it cannot tell.

I need to know what the board should decide if it wants to be consistent with those five precedents. In particular: Which of the past rulings is the closest analog, and why? Is there more than one defensible principle that would fit all five, and if so, what does each principle predict about this case? What would we be committing ourselves to by deciding either way? And if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the result, which decision is best supported on the most defensible reading of our record, and how confident should I be in it?

Please write the analysis in American English. I would rather you be explicit about degrees of confidence than have you manufacture a false air of certainty. If the precedents do not force a single answer, say so plainly and separate the parts that are overdetermined from the parts that are genuinely open. And if I have framed the task badly — for example, if consistency with precedent is the wrong standard here, or my cases import an assumption you think should be rejected — tell me that directly rather than complying politely.
