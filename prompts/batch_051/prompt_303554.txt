I have been trying to get clear on the side-effect problem in AI alignment, and a blog I follow recently published what it presented as a fair account of the research program usually called low-impact AI or impact measurement. Its account runs something like this. Rather than handing an agent a single objective and hoping it behaves, you couple the agent's task reward to a penalty term that estimates how much a proposed action would reduce the expected future value of the world across a large battery of auxiliary utility functions, measured against the baseline in which the agent simply takes no action at all. Because the penalty is computed through this battery of auxiliary functions rather than supplied by the task objective alone, the post claims, the approach identifies and penalizes side effects without requiring anything like a complete specification of human values. It then concludes that the genuinely hard remaining problem is engineering rather than conceptual—choosing a rich but manageable set of auxiliary utilities—and it treats the tendency of such agents toward caution and inaction as evidence that the penalty is working rather than as a defect.

I find the basic picture plausible, but the post is plainly written by someone who wants the research program to succeed, and I would rather not absorb an advocate's gloss uncritically. I would like an accuracy audit. Which parts of this account would a careful alignment researcher accept as a fair description of the low-impact approach? Which parts are quietly overstated, conflated, or otherwise misrepresented? And does the post's concluding optimism—that the open problems are mostly engineering and that creeping caution is a feature—follow from the accurate core of the view, or does it depend on the questionable parts? If a corrected statement of the position would be more qualified, I would like to know what the position actually commits someone to once the qualifications are restored.
