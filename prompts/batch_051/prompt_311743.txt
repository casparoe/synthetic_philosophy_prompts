I work as a tech in animal research, and there's this pattern I can't get straight. The behavior people are super strict about never saying an animal thinks. A rat that learns a maze is learning an association or responding to cues; if you say it figured out the route or remembered where the food was, you get corrected and told to pick the least clever explanation that still fits.

But the same institution is totally different when it comes to using animals. The protocols assume a mouse can suffer, so we have to give pain relief and use anesthesia. Fish get way less consideration — the reasoning is vauge, something about their nervous systems being too simple. Insects basically don't count at all. And then there's the octopus: an invertebrate, not even a vertebrate, and yet the welfare officer told me octopuses are being moved into a special protected category because they can solve puzzles. So the people who won't let me say a rat plans are fine with saying a mouse hurts, and a clever invertebrate counts more than a fish.

What view of animal minds is this whole setup actually built on? Does it assume there's a ladder of mental life, and pain only matters when the creature is close enough to us or clever enough? Does the simplicity rule from the behavior side actually conflict with what the welfare committee assumes, or are those just different questions? And if we took the simplicity rule seriously all the way down, wouldn't we eventually have to say the mouse doesn't suffer either, since we can describe the flinching without ever mentioning pain? I want to know which parts of the setup are genuinely inconsistent, which parts only look like they clash, and what would have to change if the line were drawn by whether a creature can suffer rather than by how smart its species looks.
