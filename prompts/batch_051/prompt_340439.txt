I teach a graduate seminar on corruption and the state, and last week a student from Indonesia pushed back hard on the reading, which classified clientelism — a mayor steering contracts and public-works jobs to supporters in exchange for votes — as corruption, in the same bucket as bribery. Her objection was that this universalizes a Western/Weberian standard: in her account, voters expect their representative to deliver concrete goods to the people who put her in office, and a representative who refused would be the one seen as illegitimate. What stuck with me is that the rest of us don't actually apply the label consistently across contexts. When a U.S. senator fights to keep a military base open in her state because it brings jobs, or when a British MP pushes a subsidy that flows mostly to her own constituency, we call that representation or pork-barrel politics, not corruption — even thought the underlying logic, distributing public resources to a particular set of voters partly to secure their support, looks structurally similar. Gift relationships muddy it further: a lobbyist taking a staffer to lunch is a scandal in one city and routine in another; a local official who pays funeral expenses or school fees for constituents out of public funds can be celebrated as generous in one setting and prosecuted in another.

So here's the asymmetry I can't fully justify: the same kind of pattern — an officeholder directing resources toward individuals or groups in ways that track political support, or gifts flowing up and down around power — gets moralized as corruption in some places and normalized as politics, generosity, or reciprocity in others. Treat me as your colleague in political philosophy, someone who works on corruption and clientelism. I want you to say whether this asymmetry of judgment tracks a defensible distinction or mainly tracks what a given society happens to have normalized. Try to construct the strongest principle that could generate both the "corruption" verdict and the "ordinary politics" verdict, and test it against hard cases: a mayor who gives a snow-plowing contract to her cousin below market because the cousin is the only one who can reach the remote village; a major campaign donor who becomes ambassador with no obvious qualifications; a community that expects its MP to pay school fees for constituents. Start by brainstorming two or three candidate distinctions before settling on the most promising one. As you write, separate what is settled among scholars of corruption from what is genuinely contested — for instance, whether corruption requires abuse of public office for private gain, whether clientelism is democratic accountability in another register or a threat to it, and whether the universalizing impulse is itself part of the problem. Don't present a contested view as the consensus. Then give me an honest verdict: do I keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up?
