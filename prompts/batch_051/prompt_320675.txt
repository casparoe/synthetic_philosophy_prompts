I am an undergraduate currently taking a course on early modern theories of money, and as part of the course I am helping to run a departmental colloquium at which a visiting scholar will appear in the role of George Berkeley and answer questions in character about The Querist. Our seminar has already read the work closely, so I do not want questions that merely ask him to restate the familiar account of money as a sign. I want the questioning built around the following passage, which we read as Query 26:

"Whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?"

— George Berkeley, The Querist, Query 26.

Please draft eight to ten questions for this Q&A. Start with one or two questions that force a precise interpretation of the quoted query: what Berkeley means by the denominations being retained, and what role he assigns to trust, habit, and public authority in sustaining them. Then include at least one question that presses the obvious bullionist objection about international trade, and at least one question about whether Berkeley's position can handle inflation or a collapse of confidence if denominations outrun the goods and industry behind them. For each such question, include a short follow-up keyed to the reply Berkeley is most likely to give, drawing on the claims he makes in the surrounding queries about industry and the circulation of credit. End with one question about what observation or historical case would count against the view expressed in the passage, and how Berkeley would have to respond if that case turned out badly for him. The sequence should keep returning to the passage itself rather than to general doctrines.
