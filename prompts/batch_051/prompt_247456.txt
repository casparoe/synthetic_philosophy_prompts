I cover science and ideas for a general audience, and I'm working on a piece about how people and whole towns decide what deserves to stay in memory and what's okay to let go. I've been reading Margalit, who's on the side of the duty to remember, and Rieff, who thinks remembrance is overrated and often does more harm than the original injury. I've also been collecting my own gut verdicts on cases, partly from reporting and partly from arguments with friends. The problem: one by one the verdicts feel right to me, but I can't see the principle that makes them hang together. My editor is allergic to "it's complicated" as a conclusion, so I need something sharper than "context matters."

Here are the cases.

A town keeps up a small annual ceremony for a 1902 massacre of its Jewish residents, though almost no one alive is connected to victims or perpetrators anymore. I'd keep funding it. It's not about the town's guilt; it's about not letting a state-sponsered killing just vanish.

A survivor in her 90s tells her family she doesn't want her story recorded for the local archive; they say she owes it to those who died. My gut: she owes nothing. Whatever the duty of memory is, it can't be a debt she has to keep paying.

A man finds papers showing his great-grandfather ran a prison camp and is deciding weather to destroy them or hand them to an archive. Destroying them feels like a cover-up to me, even though it shames the family. Hand them over.

A city's remembrance day has gone rote—organizers admit nobody under 50 attends, and it gets cancelled only when it rains—and they're considering ending it. I think it's fine to end the ceremony, but only if the city moves the content into schools. The ritual can go; the teaching can't.

A friend hurt me badly years ago, in a way that ended the friendship, and I still catch myself rehearsing the injury every year like some private anniversary. I don't think that's a duty. I'd be better off letting it go, and no decent principle of memory should require me to stay angry.

A city is deciding whether to remove a statue of a figure who built hospitals and also ordered a massacre. To me removal isn't forgetting, as long as the full record ends up in a museum rather than a dumpster. Don't erase it; recontextualize it.

The pair that bugs me most is the first two: the town owes the dead a ceremony, but the survivor owes no one her story. I believe both, and I can't state the rule that gives both.

My unresolved case: residents want to rename a street that honors a philanthropist who turned out to have profited violently from colonial land seizures. The victims' descendants want the name gone; opponents call renaming historical forgetting. I flip depending on the day.

So: is there one principle, or a small set of them, that captures all six verdicts, and which of them does it force me to drop? And what does it say about the street?

Format: exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no introduction and no conclusion. First the principle stated precisely; then which of my verdicts it saves and which it abandons; then its ruling on the street; then the strongest competing principle and the case that kills it; then the case it fits worst, stated honestly.
