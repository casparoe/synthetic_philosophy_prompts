I run a small team that maintains a public-facing benefits eligibility tool, and after a bad mistake last quarter (we published a rule as true that was actually just our vendor's claim, never checked), I've been thinking about what it means for us to treat something as true enough to publish. We already have a basic fact-check flow, but it assumes truth is something you capture at a moment, and that model keeps failing us. I want a procedure my non-philosopher team can run every time a candidate claim comes in, maybe 30 minutes max, grounded in this from William James's *Pragmatism*:

"The truth of an idea is not a stagnant property inherent in it. Truth happens to an idea. It becomes true, is made true by events. Its verity is in fact an event, a process: the process namely of its verifying itself, its veri-fication. Its validity is the process of its valid-ation."

We publish eligibility rules under real uncertainty: the agency interpretation may change, a court ruling might be pending, our vendor's advice may be a guess. I want the procedure to reflect that truth is a process, not a yes/no. Specifically:

- questions to ask in order,
- what counts as a sufficient answer to each,
- where to stop with a "don't publish" or "publish with caveats,"
- how to schedule re-verification, since James says it's made true by events,
- how to mark a claim that isn't true yet but could become true after we do things to verify it.

I know Peirce and James differed here, and I don't want a procedure that just assumes James's "healthy leading" version. I want you to flag which steps use the riskier Jamesian idea that truth is agreeable leading/satisfaction, and which steps are closer to Peirce's long-run-consensus-of-inquirers idea. If you think Peirce is the right one for our use, say so and redesign the procedure accordingly.

Then run the procedure on three claims:

1. "All adults in the state with income below X are eligible for program Y," where one authoritative handbook says yes, a vendor's parser says maybe, and an agency help line said "sometimes."
2. A national nonprofit claims "this program has been shown to reduce recidivism by 30%," based on one internal evaluation we can't get the data for.
3. A partner agency claims "residents usually lose Medicaid if they move between counties mid-year," which we know our users would contest.

For each, show what the procedure would do. Include at least one case where it gives an answer my team will hate (we likely have a bias to publish), and be explicit about the places where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment. If my request rests on a misreading of James or a confused idea that truth for an operational team could be made mechanical, say so plainly instead of just building it. I want usable, not faithful-to-a-philosophy.
