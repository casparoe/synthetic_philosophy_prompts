I spent my scientific career treating probability as a working instrument, and now that I am retired and reading the philosophical foundations of the subject I keep encountering a picture — associated with Cox's theorem and the Jaynesian tradition — on which probability theory is not merely a useful calculus but the logic of plausible inference. On this picture, the sum and product rules stand to uncertain reasoning as modus ponens and the principle of non-contradiction stand to demonstrative reasoning, and Cox's theorem is often taken to show that any rational inquirer whose plausibility judgments satisfy a few elementary desiderata is thereby committed to the probability calculus, much as any reasoner is committed to deduction. A further, more specific version of the analogy casts Bayes' theorem as the probabilistic counterpart of modus tollens.

I would like you to audit this analogy rather than simply expound it. First, provide the explicit mapping between the two sides: propositions and truth values, conjunction and disjunction, tautology and contradiction, proof-theoretic rules and axioms, the consequence relation, and the conditional. Second, identify where the mapping strains or breaks. The pressure points I already have in view include: deductive logic is truth-functional while probability assignments generally are not; logic admits two values where probability admits a continuum; logical entailment is monotonic whereas ordinary plausible inference, as in defeated or default reasoning, is non-monotonic; and the probabilistic conditional behaves differently from the material conditional. Third, tell me which of these disanalogies genuinely threaten the analogy's intended use and which are harmless differences of degree or idealization. Fourth, say what the analogy quietly imports from the source domain — in particular, it seems designed to transfer to the probability axioms the special normative authority we accord to logic, so that violating them is a failure of rationality and not merely an empirical mistake. Finally, give your verdict on whether the conclusion the analogy is used to support — that the probability axioms have the same compulsory normative status for plausible reasoning that the rules of deduction have for demonstrative reasoning — survives the audit, and whether some better analogy, or a more defensible formulation of the underlying claim, should replace it.
