Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about how archives make some people look important and others invisible, and about the silences in the sources around servants, women, enslaved people, the poor. Last month I was reconstructing the household staff of a nineteenth century farm from account books, and the only time the women show up is when they're fined or let go; I refused to say the quieter ones weren't there. I told my friend this is actually a rule I follow. The rule: I never treat the absence of a record as evidence that something didn't happen or that someone didn't exist or didn't do a particular thing, at least when the person was unlikely to leave a paper trail in the first place. If a woman doesn't show up in parish registers, court rolls, or tax lists, I just say we don't know; I won't let her absence count against her having been there and having been active. My friend tore into this. She says it makes history into mush, because if silence can never count as evidence against a claim, then the claim that half the village was secretly literate is as good as the claim that none of them were, and you can never rule anything out. She also says I don't apply it evenly: when a rich man leaves no records, I take that as evidence he didn't do much, but when it's a servant or a woman I refuse to. Is my rule defensible, or is it a guilty-liberal reflex dressed up as an epistemic principle? Can I keep the respect for archival silence without losing the ability to say that some claims about the past are just poorly supported? What would a defensible version look like, and when would it still force me to say we can't know?
