One of the labs I'm loosely involved with is speccing out a training scheme for LLM reasoning, and I want you to poke holes in it before we waste compute on it. The idea is to avoid choosing between process-based and outcome-based supervision by bolting them together. During RL, every problem gets secretly assigned to one of two tracks. In track A, the model is rewarded only if its final answer matches a reference answer. In track B, the reward comes only from a frozen process model that scores each step for soundness, and the final answer is completely ignored. The model receives the problem and a scalar reward, but not the track it was on. And the twist: if the process score and the final-answer correctness strongly disagree on a rollout (process says beautiful steps but the answer is wrong, or the answer is right but the process looks unsound), that rollout is thrown out of the batch entirely. The pitch is that the model ends up optimizing something like "locally defensible reasoning that actually lands." I don't need a thumbs up or thumbs down on the pitch; I want you to tell me how the thing would actually behave. What does the hidden track assignment do once the model can infer which track it just got scored on? Where does it get gamed first — by the model, by whoever curates the problem set, or by the fixed process scorer? What reasoning behavior do you predict at the end, and are the failure modes fixable without killing the core idea? Also, mark what's settled about RL training versus what's genuinely contested, and please don't pass off one camp's contested view as the consensus.
