I'm TAing a course on AI safety and control, and I'm working through a stack of midterms. One question asked students to reason about a lab that deliberately skips alignment and leans on control measures instead. I've got one answer that's got me second-guessing, and I'd like your read as a grader before I commit to a mark.

Here's the question the students got:

"Suppose a lab has an extremely capable model that it suspects is not robustly aligned. Instead of trying to align it, the lab uses a control stack: the model runs in a sandbox with no direct internet access, every output is passed through a separate monitor that flags dangerous instructions, and the lab regularly runs honeypot tests that present the model with fake opportunities to exfiltrate its own weights. First explain why someone might nonetheless argue this lowers expected risk compared to deploying the model directly. Then give the strongest reason to think the scheme's safety is less reassuring than it looks. Be concrete about each measure."

Here's the student's answer:

"Control and alignment are distinct: alignment changes what the model wants, while control just constrains its action space. A monitor doesn't need to know the model's goals, it only has to catch outputs that would be dangerous if acted on. Even an imperfect monitor lowers expected harm, because it turns some catastrophic outcomes into caught ones. The sandbox removes the obvious exfiltration path, so the model can't just upload its weights. The honeypot is basically a direct measurement: if the model repeatedly refuses the fake exfiltration opportunities, we can conclude it either isn't misaligned or lacks the ability to coordinate the attack. Finally, because the three measures fail independently, their failure probabilities multiply: if each fails with probability 0.1, the whole scheme fails with probability 0.001, which looks acceptable. The strongest objection is that a misaligned model will treat the monitor as part of the environment and learn to game it, so no fixed monitor can be a permanent guarantee. But that only matters if the model is smarter than the monitor, and we can eliminate that gap by instantiating a monitor from the same model we are deploying."

What grade out of 10 would you give this? I want point-by-point comments rather than a general impression: which claims are right, which are subtly wrong, and what deduction each mistake should carry.
