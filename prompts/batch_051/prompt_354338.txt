I spent most of my working life in the natural sciences, and I have only recently begun reading moral philosophy in earnest. The position I have settled on, and would like stress-tested, concerns incorruptibility. My view is that to call a person incorruptible is not to attribute a distinct moral virtue but to report something about the structure of their preferences. For any given commitment C, such as not selling one's vote, not falsifying data, or not abandoning a charge, a person is incorruptible with respect to C just in case C is lexicographically prior to a definable class of rival goods: no amount of money, status, ease, or safety would be accepted in exchange for violating C. On this analysis, talk of a person's price becomes literal rather than metaphorical. The corruptible have a reservation price; the incorruptible have none, and their willingness to accept trade-offs never crosses certain lines no matter how large the inducement on the other side. Whether C is itself morally good is a separate question; one can be incorruptible in the service of a bad cause, much as an instrument can be internally reliable in the service of one. The attribute is domain-relative and scalar: a person is incorruptible about some commitments and not others, and across a whole life one can be more or less broadly incorruptible.

I would like from you a map of this position's exposure. What observations, cases, experimental findings, or conceptual arguments would count against it, or would have to be true for it to be false? Which of those would be decisive and which merely awkward? Which would a defender of the preference-structure account themselves have to concede as counting against it? And is the account in danger of being insulated against refutation, for instance because no finite behavioral record could ever distinguish a truly unbounded commitment from a merely very high reservation price, and if so, is that insulation a problem for a view of this kind? I am especially moved by evidence from behavioral economics and moral psychology, but a carefully described case from ordinary moral experience counts for more than a survey result. I do not need a bibliography or citations; I want the arguments, cases, and assessments themselves.
