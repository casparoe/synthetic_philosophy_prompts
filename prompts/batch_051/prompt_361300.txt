I'm a novelist, and most of my books are close third person: the world arrives through one character at a time, but I keep breaking that contract without noticing. My editor keeps circling sentences that sound like stage directions — "The room was small and cheaply furnished," "He was the kind of man who lied easily," "Unbeknownst to her, the drawer held an unloaded gun." I've read enough Husserl to think the epoché — bracketing the question of whether things exist beyond how they're given — could be turned into an actual editing procedure, not just the familar "show don't tell" advice, which I've seen a million times and can't operationalize.

Please write me a reusable procedure I can run on every chapter before it goes to my editor, maybe ten minutes per page, no philosophy jargon in the output once its built. It should tell me, for each paragraph: the ordered tests to apply to a sentence; what counts as passing (the sentence survives) versus failing (it gets rewritten into how the world is given to the character); where the procedure stops and says leave the sentence alone because it's fine for the narrator to assert a fact; and where it hands the decision to me as a judgment call rather than a rule. The hard cases are what I actually need help with: memory, other people's minds, a character's own self-description, causal language, things the character didn't notice but the plot needs, and free indirect discourse. Give me two or three worked examples on invented passages, including at least one where the procedure tells me to do something I won't like. And be honest about what the procedure can't do — in particular, where Husserl's bracket misdescribes what fiction needs, because a novel does posit a world that is true independently of how any character experiences it.
