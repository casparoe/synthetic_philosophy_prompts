Put on your philosopher-of-mind / cognitive scientist hat for this one, and think it through carefully before you commit to a diagnosis.

Heres a debate that seems to me to have been stuck for decades, and I want you to diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicate who's right.

In the eighties, Fodor argued that the mind has modules, but only around the edges. On his picture a module is something like: domain-specific, informationally encapsulated (it cant see everything you believe), fast and mandatory, tied to fixed neural architecture, prone to characteristic breakdowns, and producing only shallow outputs. Vision and language parsing are the standard examples. The central systems—belief fixation and practical reasoning—are the opposite: isotropic and Quinean, so that in principle any belief can be relevant to any other. Central cognition therefore cannot be modular, because modularity requires exactly the encapsulation that central thought has to overcome.

Then the massive modularity people, mostly evolutionary psychologists, came along and said the whole mind is a collection of modules, including central cognition: cheater detection, theory of mind, naive physics, social-contract reasoning, and so on. They cite the Wason selection task getting much easier when its framed as cheater detection, plus the broader claim that evolution would build lots of specialized adaptations rather than a domain-general reasoner.

What bugs me is that since maybe the nineties the two camps just talk past each other. Fodor's side says: those things arent modules in my sense, they're not encapsulated. The other side says: fine, maybe your sense was too strict, what matters is functional specialization. And thats about as far as it ever goes. Nobody moves.

So my question for you: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it verbal, turning on the word 'module'? Is it a substantive empirical disagreement about whether central cognition is encapsulated and decomposes into independently breakable parts? Is it two different questions—one about computational architecture, one about evolved function—running under a single name? Is there a hidden disagreement about what counts as evidence (double dissociations? imaging? the Wason task? evolutionary plausibility)? Or is the original question just ill-posed?

Then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis—a sharper definition, a kind of experiment, developmental or connectivity data, a formal result, a change of framing—or argue that no progress should be expected, and why.

Start by brainstorming a few possible diagnoses before you settle, and be concrete about what each side actually says.
