Ok so I'm writing a term paper for a philosophy of mind course on pain, and I've ended up with a list of things I want to hold all at once. The paper has to defend a unified position, and the more I look at the list the less confident I am that its actually stable. I'd love a consistency audit before I have to defend any of this in front of a seminar.

Here's what I find myself believing: (1) Pain asymbolia is the real deal. People with certain brain lesions will tell you that a pinprick still has the sharp, stinging quality of a pain and they can locate it and rate its intensity, but it doesnt bother them; they don't flinch, guard the area, or ask you to stop. I take this to show that the sensory core of pain and the unpleasant, aversive side of pain are two dissociable components. (2) What makes a pain bad — what makes it something it would be rational to want gone — is not some intrinsic property of that sensory quality itself but the motivational grip: the felt demand to do something about it now. If that grip were absent, I don't see what would be left to call bad, no matter how sharp or stinging the sensation was. (3) At the same time, I think pain is representational through and through: the specific feel of a pain is exhausted by what it represents, and what it represents is something like tissue damage in a body location. That's what distinguishes a pain from an itch or a tickle. (4) Phantom limb pains are genuine pains, not imaginary ones, so pains can misrepresent. I don't see this by itself as undermining (3). (5) And here's a verdict I can't shake: an asymbolic's state isn't bad for them in the way ordinary pain is. If someone has the sensation but zero aversive, motivational response, I'd say there isn't the same prudential or moral urgency to relieve it.

Which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and what would the least costly revision be? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating every conflict as equally bad, and tell me what each repair would cost me.
