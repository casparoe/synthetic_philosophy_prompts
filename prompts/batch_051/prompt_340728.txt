I work as a software engineer, not a trained philosopher, so this may be naively framed. I build retrieval and classification systems on top of dense vector embeddings of words and sentences, and I find myself pulled in two directions about what these systems are actually doing. The engineering case for the underlying picture, that semantic content is well modeled by geometry in a learned high-dimensional space, strikes me as strong. You train a model to predict context, and out comes a space in which similarity is a distance, related terms cluster, and regular relations show up as near-parallel offsets, as with the vector arithmetic for analogies. The view has the virtues I usually want in a theory: it is simple, it requires no hand-built ontology or explicit features, it makes graded similarity quantitative rather than binary, and it keeps producing useful systems. On reflection I am drawn to the stronger claim that this is the right kind of account of meaning: a word's meaning just is a function of the contexts it occurs in, and semantic structure is spatial structure.

But as a speaker I also have an intuition I cannot shake off. Embeddings that perform extremely well will place expressions close together that I would never describe as close in meaning. Antonyms can sit near each other because they occupy the same discourse slots. A sentence and its simple negation are often near-duplicates in cosine terms. And two vectors can be nearly identical while differing in exactly the respect I most care about, namely what the expression commits us to and what follows from it. In production I treat these as error cases, adding rules, hard-negative mining, or a downstream classifier, but that feels like patching a deeper mismatch. The hard part is not that the model makes mistakes; all models do. It is that the geometry appears to be answering a different question than the one I meant by meaning, and the patches implicitly concede that.

I do not want to be told which side simply wins. I want to know what would justify each move. Specifically: under what conditions should I treat the intuition that there is more to meaning than contextual distribution as an introspection artifact, a product of thinking about meaning as what a speaker can articulate rather than what a model can predict, and drop it? Under what conditions should I instead demote the geometric theory to a partial account of similarity or associative structure, with some separate compositional, inferential, or truth-conditional story carrying the rest? And is there a reading on which the two do not conflict because they concern different levels of analysis, and if so, how would I test that this is a real division of labor rather than a relabeling of the problem? Present the strongest version of each side, including the best objection to my own framing, and tell me which you would give up, or whether you would refuse to.
