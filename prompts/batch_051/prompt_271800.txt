Two people in my study group got into it last week over Kant's murderer-at-the-door case, and they asked me to referee, but I honestly couldn't sort out who was right about what. Dana said the absolute prohibition on lying is clearly wrongheaded: if a man with an axe shows up asking whether your friend is hiding in your house, you lie to him, and any ethical view that forbids that is monstrous. Mark replied that Kant never said you have to blurt out the truth; you can refuse to answer or try to shut the door, but a false assertion is a different kind of act because it makes an exception for yourself — you're treating the murderer as a mere means to your end. Dana said that's a distinction without a difference, since the murderer isn't owed the truth in the first place. Mark said that's the whole problem: once you allow yourself to decide who counts as deserving the truth, the principle becomes "lie to anyone who doesn't deserve it," which just collapses into self-serving exceptions.

Dana answered that Mark was misinterpretting the categorical imperative — the point isn't to protect the murderer's feelings, it's about the form of law itself, and while you couldn't will a universal law of lying, you could will a universal law of deceiving someone who is trying to use your statement to commit murder. Mark shot back that "could will" is doing all the work there, and that Kant's own essay seems to say you're not morally responsible if you tell the truth and the murder happens, which makes moral purity more important than your friend's life. Please go through their exchange and say who's right about which specific point, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
