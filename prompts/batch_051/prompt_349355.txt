I've spent years teaching expected utility theory, and the dominance principle has always seemed like the one piece I'd never give up: if one act pays strictly more than another in every possible contingency, you dont need a probability assignment to know its better. But I've run into a case that seems to drive a wedge between that principle and expected utility theory as a formal tool, and I honestly cant figure out which side should yield.

Suppose I offer you a game. A fair coin is tossed until it comes up heads; if the first heads occurs on toss n, you receive (-1)^(n-1) * (2^n / n) dollars. So heads on the first toss wins you $2, first heads on the second loses you $2, first heads on the third wins you $8/3, first heads on the fourth loses you $4, and so on. If you sum the expected payoff in the order of the tosses you get 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which converges to ln 2. But that convergence is only conditional: the positive terms by themselves diverge to infinity and the negative terms by themselves diverge to minus infinity, so by rearranging the same terms you can get any real number, or plus or minus infinity. On the usual definition, then, the game has no expected value. Not zero, not infinite—undefined.

Now consider a second game, identical except that it pays exactly one dollar less in every possible outcome. In every state, game one pays more than game two, so dominance says prefer game one, and the intuition feels non-negotiable: you could never rationally take the dominated game. Yet expected utility theory cannot assign an expected value to either game, so the formal machinery that was supposed to vindicate dominance goes silent exactly where dominance gives its clearest verdict.

Which should yield? Maybe dominance is the fundamental norm and expected utility is just a limited tool that presumes its expectations are well defined; then we trust the obvious preference and say the utility theory is out of its depth. Or maybe the undefined expectation is a warning: these are pathological, unbounded payoffs whose positive and negative parts both diverge, and neither the dominance principle nor expected utility was ever meant to apply to acts like that. Or maybe the toss-order sum is privileged—there is a fact about which state comes first—and we should just accept ln 2 and move on, though that sounds like letting the notation decide. Give me the strongest version of each and tell me which you would give up, and what it would take to convince you.
