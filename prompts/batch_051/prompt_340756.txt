im in a pragmatism course right now and we've been doing Peirce and James back to back. honestly the difference between their accounts of truth keeps slipping away from me, since both of them seem to make truth a matter of what belief does rather than of matching some ready-made world. there's one spot in Peirce's "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (1878) that i keep rereading, where he writes:

"But the soul and meaning of thought, abstracted from the other elements which accompany it, though it may be voluntarily thwarted, can never be made to direct itself toward anything but the production of belief. Thought in action has for its only possible motive the attainment of thought at rest; and whatever does not refer to belief is no part of the thought itself."

right before this he mocks the dilettanti who get annoyed when a question gets finally settled, which feels like it could be aimed at James's willingness to let our passional side decide genuine options that can't be settled on intellectual grounds. but I could also read Peirce as basically agreeing that belief is the goal, with James just being more honest about how people actually get there. what are the most important implications of this passage for how I should asses James's move here? i mostly want to know whether Peirce's view makes it flat-out illegitimate to hold a belief on passional grounds, or whether it just changes when that's permissible, and what the difference would actually look like in a case like deciding whether to keep the religious outlook i grew up with.
