I am a professor of philosophy working in moral psychology and metaethics, and in three weeks I am giving a paper at a workshop on psychopathy, moral judgment, and responsibility. My audience will be mostly empirical moral psychologists, several experimental philosophers who use psychopathy as a test case in metaethics, and a few criminal-law theorists who care about whether psychopathic offenders satisfy the knowledge condition for culpability. I need a question-period prep sheet, not a restatement of my argument.

The thesis I am defending is that the familiar claim that psychopaths do not know that what they do is wrong rests on a conflation. The evidence, I will argue, mostly shows that psychopaths differ from typical people in moral motivation and in the affective load carried by their moral judgments; it does not show that they lack moral knowledge, where knowledge is a matter of reliably applying moral concepts and failing to know involves a recognizable cognitive deficit. I will argue that many psychopaths can correctly sort actions as prohibited or permissible, can report that their own actions were wrong, and exhibit no confusion or sloppiness in doing so, even though this knowledge is inert: it neither deters them nor produces the aversion that accompanies moral judgment in most people.

My working argument for the talk runs as follows. First, distinguish three capacities: the capacity to classify an act under a moral concept, the capacity to be moved by that classification, and the capacity to feel the wrongness. Second, the empirical literature I will discuss shows a deficit in the second and third capacities, while the first is largely intact under careful testing once the tasks are set up to isolate classification. Third, a correct account of moral knowledge should be tied to the first capacity, not to the others, unless one simply builds motivational internalism into the definition of moral knowledge. Fourth, the tempting inference from he did not care to he did not know is exactly that smuggling-in, not a discovery.

The audience will not grant this. The psychologists tend to accept an affective-deficit account of psychopathy and will suspect that I have explained away their data; the experimental philosophers are split on motivational internalism but regard psychopathy as the strongest case for it; the criminal-law scholars worry about what follows for the knowledge condition in criminal culpability.

Please write the prep sheet in the following five sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths.

1. Likely objections, in the audience's own terms (about 500 words). List them ordered by how likely each is to be raised, phrased the way this audience would actually raise them.
2. The objection with the most force (about 200 words). Identify which objection is hardest to answer well, and explain why it threatens the argument rather than merely my presentation.
3. What to concede up front (about 150 words). Say which concessions cost little, earn credibility, and are anyway required by the evidence.
4. The reply that has to land (about 200 words). Identify the single response without which the central argument fails, state it as a reply, and note where it remains weakest.
5. The question I should hope nobody asks (about 150 words). Identify the hidden weakness in the argument and show how a hostile questioner could exploit it.
