For much of the twentieth century, many physicists and mathematicians treated elegance — symmetry, compactness, unification, lack of ad hoc machinery — as evidence that a theory was likely to be true or approximately true, above and beyond its agreement with the data in hand. The informal argument ran roughly as follows: nature's fundamental structure is itself simple and beautiful; a mathematically beautiful theory is therefore more likely to match that structure; and the historical success of beautiful theories justifies leaning on that heuristic when data are scarce or indecisive. Now consider a development that appears to cut against this. Some of the most successful predictive systems of the present are models that almost no one would call elegant: deep networks with billions of fitted parameters, ensembles, interpolation from large datasets, and brute-force simulations that perform remarkably well while yielding little compact explanatory insight. Meanwhile, some of the most aesthetically admired proposals in fundamental physics, such as supersymmetry and string theory, have survived decades without empirical confirmation. Does the elegance-as-evidence argument survive this situation? Indicate how confident you are in each verdict.
