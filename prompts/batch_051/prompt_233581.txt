You are a philosopher of language and probability who works on the semantics of conditionals. I want a structural diagnosis of a longstanding impasse, not a survey of theories and not a verdict about which theory is correct.

Here is the debate as I understand it. There is a widely shared empirical generalization, often called Adams's thesis or the Equation: the degree to which a speaker is willing to accept an indicative conditional such as If it rains, the match will be cancelled tracks the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent, P(cancellation | rain), rather than the probability of any truth-functional proposition like not-rain or cancellation. The Ramsey test points the same way: one evaluates the conditional by provisionally adding the antecedent to one's beliefs, with minimal change, and asking how plausible the consequent then is. But Lewis's triviality results showed that if the conditional expresses an ordinary proposition whose probability is supposed to equal P(C|A) across a suitably rich class of probability functions, the formalism collapses: the equation forces P(C|A) to equal P(C) for arbitrary A and C, which would make almost every pair of propositions probabilistically independent. The field has been stuck since. Truth-conditional theorists respond by giving the conditional some propositional content — possible-worlds semantics, material implication plus pragmatics, context-sensitive propositions — and treating the Equation as a norm of assertability, an idealization, or an artifact of context. Non-propositional and restrictor approaches deny that a bare if-clause plus main clause expresses a single proposition, so Lewis's assumptions never apply. Yet conditionals occur embedded — in the consequents of other conditionals, inside disjunctions and attitude reports — and compositionality seems to demand a propositional object. Each side handles some of the data elegantly and the rest awkwardly.

My working hypothesis is that this is not one substantive disagreement but several disputes sharing a heading. I suspect the sides agree on most of the surface data and are answering different questions: what a sentence contributes to truth conditions and compositional structure, versus how an assertion updates an agent's degrees of belief. The word semantics slides between those. I also suspect there may be a hidden formal question — how close any propositional semantics can even come to the Equation, or under what constraints a context-sensitive semantics can recover it — that neither side states sharply. Diagnose this impasse. Say which parts are merely verbal, which are genuinely substantive, and of the substantive ones, whether they are formal, empirical, or methodological. Then say whether the original question, what do indicative conditionals mean, is well posed. Finally, tell me what would actually count as progress given your diagnosis: a theorem, an experimental result, a conceptual distinction, a redescription of the question, or the honest conclusion that the two projects are both legitimate and no single account should be expected to win. Think carefully, and if it helps, begin by brainstorming the possible diagnostic hypotheses before committing.
