I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so bear with me if I name the wrong debates. I've been rereading Melville's "Bartleby, the Scrivener," and I keep thinking it sits right on top of political obligation and civil disobedience, but in a way that makes the standard philosophy misfire.

The standard accounts I half-remember — Rawls, Thoreau, Socrates in the Crito — all seem to treat civil disobedience as a form of address. You break a law in public, conscientiously, nonviolently, and you accept the punishment in order to appeal to the community's sense of justice; it's an argument you make with your body. Bartleby does almost none of that. He doesn't break a law so much as decline to cooperate with ordinary workplace authority. He offers no principle, no conscience-claim, no appeal to the public; he just repeats his polite formula about preferring not to. When he's arrested he doesn't use the courtroom as a platform either; he goes quiet and dies. And yet the narrator, his boss, is plainly changed by him — not persuaded by any argument but disturbed into something like charity and guilt.

So here's what I'd like: about 800 words arguing whether Bartleby's refusal should count as civil disobedience, and what the story forces us to revise in the usual picture of political obligation. Push in both directions. First read it through the public-appeal model and say why he doesn't fit. Then say what the story might add — maybe refusal can move a conscience without a program, maybe obligation rests on ordinary routine rather than the state, maybe a demand needn't be publicly justified to do political work. But don't flip into romance about him: the story should resist your reading too. Bartleby may be too singular, too passive, too self-cancelling to model anything political, and an honest essay has to say so.

Don't worry about exact quotations — paraphrase any detail. And don't bother with citations or a reading list; I want your own reading, fixing wether the philosophy illuminates the story and whether the story illuminates the philosophy.
