I'm a senior science journalist at a national magazine, and once a month I run an internal seminar for early-career reporters on the conceptual machinery behind the claims they cover. Next month's session is on probability, and I want to teach one idea properly rather than survey the field: the rule philosophers call the Principal Principle. Roughly, it says that when you know a process has a genuine objective chance of producing an outcome, your degree of belief that the outcome will occur should match that chance, provided the rest of what you know is admissible and not, say, the outcome leaking through the back door. In practice, this is the move a reporter makes when they write that an asteroid has roughly a one-in-several-thousand chance of striking Earth, or that a patient in a randomized trial has a 40 percent chance of benefiting, and then treat that number as the sober summary rather than as one expert's hunch.

The seminar runs 90 minutes. The audience has no probability background beyond a basic statistics course, and I don't want them leaving able to state the principle formally; I want them able to notice when a number deserves their deference, when it merely reports an observed frequency, and when extra information they hold should make them pull away from it. Above all, I want the principle to land as something they already half-believed rather than as an arbitrary rule handed down from philosophy.

Act as a professional philosopher of probability with real experience teaching working journalists, and think carefully before committing. Start by brainstorming at least three candidate sequences and three candidate carrying examples before you settle on one. Then give me a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself, organized around these questions. First, the order in which the concepts must be introduced and what depends on what, including why the distinction between objective chance and personal confidence has to be in place before the deference rule can mean anything. Second, the one concrete example the whole session should build on, and what it must have and lack; I suspect the standard fair coin is too weak because a fifty-fifty setup invites symmetry reasoning rather than genuine deference, so tell me what would carry the session better and why. Third, the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring or form, such as equating objective chance with observed frequency, thinking deference means ignoring your own information, assuming determinism dissolves the topic, or treating the rule as too obvious to need its caveat, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than cleaned up later. Fourth, one closing question or short exercise that would show me whether they have understood the idea rather than merely repeated the slogan. Fifth, what I should deliberately leave out at this level, announce as left out, and why.
