My topic is the debate over whether current machine learning systems can produce genuine novelty or only recombinations within their training distribution. My half-formed idea: The debate about whether LLMs can make intuitive leaps has stalled because the two sides use 'novelty' in different senses without noticing. One side says every output is a recombination of fragments statistically present in the training corpus; the other side points to outputs no human has previously produced and takes that to settle the matter. But this runs together at least three things: (1) novelty relative to the training corpus, (2) novelty relative to the model's available representational space, and (3) novelty at the level of conceptual structure, understood as whether solving the target requires forming a new concept, decomposition, or compression rather than reweighting concepts the model already has. I suspect a strict, testable notion of intuitive leap can be built around (3): a model makes a leap on a target task when its success cannot be accounted for as piecewise interpolation or feature recombination within the representational space it demonstrably uses for neighboring tasks, and when the target requires a decomposition or concept absent from its training data. The project would formalize this criterion, build a small battery of 'leap probes' in a bounded domain such as elementary arithmetic generalization or miniature program synthesis, and compare model performance against explicit interpolative baselines fitted to the training distribution.
