I'm a PhD student in philosophy and I somehow ended up as the student rep on my university's research ethics board. Next Tuesday we vote on a psychology study that deliberately makes participants feel a short episode of intense shame (induced by having them read a staged, humiliating peer-evaluation of a task they just did) in order to test a new debriefing procedure; consent is genuine and participants are debriefed, but the upset is intentional and has no direct benefit to them. The committee is stuck at 6–6. One side thinks the study is obviously fine — the distress is mild and transient and the debriefing research is useful. The other side thinks it is simply wrong to intentionally harm subjects even a little for research, regardless of benefit, and their not going to be moved by "it's only mild." I keep thinking of this as a moral-uncertainty problem, not a factual-disagreement problem: nobody is confused about what the protocol does.

Our chair asked me to draft a short memo on the procedural question underneath this vote: how should a group make a decision when its members have deep, apparently intractable first-order moral disagreement? I need the memo to settle what we actually do on Tuesday, so give a concrete recommendation — vote your conscience and majority wins, require consensus, discount each member's confidence, adopt some maximizing-expected-choiceworthiness rule across the committee's moral views, always pick the option that minimizes expected moral violation under each member's own theory, defer to a moral compromise between the two sides, or whatever you think is right. It should take seriously the idea that each of us ought to be morally uncertain rather than treating our own view as obviously correct, and also the worry that different moral theories may not share a common scale for comparing how badly an option "violates" one theory versus another.

Role: you're a professional philosopher working on moral uncertainty, social choice, and decision theory. Please think carefully, start by brainstorming several candidate procedures and where they break, then write the memo I can circulate as-is. Give me one well-defended decision rule with a fair treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, not a survey.
