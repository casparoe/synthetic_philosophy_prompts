Suppose that a safe, cheap drug is introduced that completely suppresses hedonic adaptation: whatever a person enjoys, she continues to enjoy at roughly the intensity of the first encounter, for as long as she keeps taking it, and she is spared the restlessness and felt lack that normally follow satisfied desire. A friend and I have been arguing for several evenings about whether an indefinitely long life on this drug would deserve to be called eternal bliss, or whether it would merely feel like bliss while something essential was missing; I suspect the friend is too quick either way. I want a reasoned prediction of how such a life unfolds over its first few centuries, not a settled verdict. What happens to goal-directed activity once satisfaction no longer decays and no desire keeps its sting? Do people continue to undertake long-term projects whose value depends on delay, difficulty, and the possibility of failure, or do they settle into a fixed repertoire of already-loved pleasures? Can boredom arise at all in the absence of adaptation, and if it cannot, is that absence a gain or a hidden loss? Finally, how could an inhabitant of such a life, or an outside observer, tell genuine fulfillment apart from a permanently self-renewing feeling of fulfillment, and what would have to be true for your prediction to be wrong? Indicate which links in the chain strike you as robust and which as speculative.
