I'm on a small internal review group at a fintech company, my day job is software engineer so apologies if this is philosophically sloppy. We ship an LLM tool that reviews loan application documents and flags missing or suspicious items, and a few months ago we started asking the model to think step by step before it gives its verdict because accuracy went up. The traces look great: when the model is right, the reasoning reads like a careful checklist, and when it's wrong the reasoning usually breaks at the step you'd expect.

We're now arguing about a policy for how much to trust those traces. One side wants to make the chain of thought part of the audit trail, so when a loan officer overrides the model or a customer appeals, the reasoning counts as evidence about why the model decided what it did and as a guide to which cases we can trust. The other side says the traces are a plausible story generated after the fact, and they have some results that bother me: if we hold the final verdict fixed and re-generate, the model often gives different reasoning for the same answer; if we edit a bad step out and re-run, the final verdict usually doesn't change; and sometimes the model gives a clean rational for a verdict that we later found flipped when we changed a field the reasoning never mentioned. Their line is that using the traces for audits is basically reading tea leaves and will make us overconfident.

Draft me a short policy memo for the review group: what should our actual policy be about using chain-of-thought traces in audits, overrides, and debugging? Specifically, should the traces go into the audit file as evidence, should loan officers be allowed to lean on them when they override, and should a wrong-sounding trace block a case even when the final verdict is right? I want an actionable bottom line up front, the reasoning for it, and an honest treatment of the strongest argument from the side you don't pick. Keep it continuous prose like an email or a letter, no headings, bullets, bold, or tables.
