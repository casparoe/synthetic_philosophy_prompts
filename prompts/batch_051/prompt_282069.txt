I've been running a small reading group on Peirce, and I keep getting stuck on a wrinkle in the pragmatic maxim. The maxim says the whole meaning of a concept is the sum of its conceivable practical consequences, but it doesn't tell you how idealized or how long-run those consequences are allowed to be. I have a hunch you can build a case where two people, both faithfully applying the maxim, end up assigning the concept differently — not because they misread the facts, but because they read the scope of practical consequences differently.

The setup: two researchers, both self-described Peirceans, argue about whether a system S falls under a concept C. They agree on every observation they can currently make, and they can even write out the same master list of conceivable downstream concequences. But one of them (the strict reader) holds that only consequences operationalizable by current inquiry count as semantic content; the other (the long-run reader) holds that consequences under idealized, indefinitely extended inquiry count too. Build the case so the strict reader and the long-run reader give opposite verdicts on whether C applies to S. The disagreement should not be merely verbal — it should push them toward different practical decisions, like whether to classify S a certain way or whether to fund a follow-up study. The point of the exercise is to test whether the pragmatic maxim actually settles meaning disputes once the notion of a practical bearing gets stretched, so a case that cheats by stipulating the answer wouldn't help me.
