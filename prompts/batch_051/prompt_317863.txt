I have no academic background in philosophy or mathematics; my thinking on this has come from working through popular mathematics and logic on my own, and from trying to teach myself theorems rather than merely collect them. For years I have been bothered by a gap that opens up when I go through a proof. Take the standard proof that the square root of two is irrational: I can see that assuming √2 = p/q in lowest terms gives p² = 2q²; that p² being even forces p itself to be even; that substituting p = 2k then gives q² = 2k² and so forces q to be even as well, contradicting the assumption that the fraction was already in lowest terms. I can verify every step, reproduce the proof from memory, and even correct small slips in someone else's version of it. And yet I do not feel I see why the result has to be true; the proof is something I audit, not something I grasp. With other proofs, the line-by-line checking sometimes suddenly clicks, and afterwards the proof seems to be organised around a single idea that makes the theorem inevitable, even though nothing about my ability to verify the individual steps has changed.

At the same time, a deflationary view keeps looking compelling to me, and I want it taken seriously rather than dismissed. Perhaps understanding just is procedural fluency, and there is nothing else for it to be. Whenever I try to specify the extra ingredient—a mental picture, a sense of inevitability, seeing the structure all at once—I can only seem to demonstrate it by doing something further: solving a novel exercise, adapting the proof to a nearby result, explaining the idea in my own words, spotting the flaw in a false proof. Those are all just more procedures. The click may be nothing but the feeling that accompanies fluency once the proof has been compressed and stored efficiently; it feels like a distinct achievement but does no independent work. The deflationary view has the virtues of being simple, of making mathematical understanding testable in an ordinary way, and of refusing to posit a private inner state that cannot be checked.

I would like this set out as a genuine clash and then negotiated. Which should yield: the intuition that understanding is something over and above the ability to run the steps, or the deflationary theory that understanding is nothing more than suitably rich procedural fluency? Or is there a reading on which both survive, because understanding is procedural fluency of a structurally different kind—fluency with compressions, variations, and explanations rather than with the original proof? Be specific about what each move would cost and what would justify it. I do not want a verdict that simply splits the difference; I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be debunked as a by-product of fluency, what it would take for the theory to be amended or abandoned, and how one would tell which is happening in a particular case. Please write in British English throughout.
