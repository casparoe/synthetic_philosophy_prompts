I've been going back and forth with a friend about tipping and queueing — whether those are real obligations or just local habits that only bind because everyone around you expects them. We got onto the visitor question, and that led to a counterfactual I'd like you to actually work through.

Imagine a mid-sized, tourism-heavy city (call it Halverton) enacts a 'Civility Accord.' Starting January 1, every visitor — anyone in the city more than 24 hours and less than 90 days — is required by law to follow three everyday local conventions, on pain of fines: queue strictly for buses, shops, and crossings; tip 18% at any place with counter or table service; and cross streets only at signals, even when the street is empty. Locals are exempt from all three rules. The city's rationale is that locals already know the 'spirit' of these conventions, so they don't need enforcement. Enforcement is realistic: a visitor gets caught roughly one violation in twenty, and an app explains the rules at check-in.

Predict how this plays out over five years, seperately for tipping, queueing, and jaywalking, and separately for locals and visitors. Give me the chain of reasoning, not just the endpoints: which convention erodes fastest and why; whether locals start violating more once they watch visitors get fined, or instead turn into self-appointed enforcers; whether the asymmetry spawns some kind of 'visitor tax' industry or a backlash; and — the thing we were actually arguing about — whether the policy reveals these conventions as mere local customs that bind only when backed by authority, or whether they carry moral weight that survives being turned into a tourists-only rule. Tell me, for each main prediction, how confident you are and what would have to be true for it to be wrong.

After the argument, give me a compact table with columns: convention; predicted effect on locals; predicted effect on visitors; main mechanism; confidence (high/medium/low); and the key assumption that could flip the prediction.
