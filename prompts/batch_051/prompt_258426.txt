I'm reading Herbert Spencer's 1860 essay The Social Organism and want help with a particular passage. The passage I have in mind is not quoted verbatim, but here is its content as I understand it: Spencer argues that a society and an individual organism are alike in that each is an aggregate of mutually dependent parts, with a division of labour among them, so that the life of the whole depends on the life of its parts. But he then identifies a disanalogy: in an animal, feeling or consciousness is concentrated in a particular set of parts, the nervous system, whereas in a society, consciousness is not concentrated anywhere; it is distributed across all the individual members, each of whom is separately capable of feeling. No governing organ feels for the society in the way a brain feels for an animal.

I'm trying to use this passage to think through a debate in contemporary philosophy of biology about biological individuality: whether holobionts, eusocial insect colonies, or colonial organisms like siphonophores should count as individual organisms, rather than as populations or societies of organisms. My question is not about Spencer's political views. My question is whether the disanalogy he draws actually tracks a criterion of organismal individuality, or whether it confuses a symptom of individuality with a criterion. In particular, if the concentration of consciousness in a specialized part is what makes an animal an individual rather than a society, then what should we say about organisms that lack a centralized nervous system, like plants or sponges? Does Spencer's disanalogy, if taken seriously, imply that plants are not individual organisms but societies? And would that be a reductio ad absurdum of his way of distinguishing organisms from societies, or does it reveal a real ambiguity in how the concept organism gets extended across cases? I'm less interested in what Spencer himself would have said than in whether the distinction he articulates can be made precise enough to do work in the modern debate about superorganisms and holobionts.

Please help me work through this: interpret the passage, spell out the criterion of individuality that Spencer's disanalogy presupposes, and test whether that criterion can handle the plant, sponge, and siphonophore cases without collapsing or becoming circular.
