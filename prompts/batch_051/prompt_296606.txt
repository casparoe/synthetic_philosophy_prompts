You are a professional philosopher specialising in decision theory and the philosophy of action, with a particular interest in how uncertainty and agency interact. I am going to report a set of verdicts I hold about a single question: when, if ever, is it rational for me to assign probabilities to my own future choices? Work through the cases slowly, try out several candidate principles before settling on one, and then state the principle as precisely as you can, together with its boundary conditions. Please write in British English. I am not asking for definitions, citations, or literature: reason from the cases themselves.

My verdicts are as follows.

1. Habit. I am about 90 per cent sure I will put my right shoe on first tomorrow morning, since I have done so unthinkingly for years and the matter never rises to deliberation. This feels like ordinary self-knowledge.

2. Settled matters. I have booked and paid for a holiday in Italy. Asked whether I will go, I answer that I am 99 per cent sure. I feel no discomfort.

3. Others. I am 70 per cent sure my colleague will vote for the proposal in tomorrow's meeting, based on her record and her incentives. That seems entirely unproblematic, even though the matter is not settled and in some sense she could still change her mind.

4. My own open choice. At that same meeting I have not yet made up my mind. If someone asks me before the vote how likely I am to vote for the proposal, I cannot answer without, in effect, deciding. If I say "70 per cent", the statement does not feel like a forecast; it feels as though I have already thrown seventy per cent of my weight behind voting for it. And I do not think the honest answer is "50 per cent" either. I want to say that no well-formed probability is available while the choice is genuinely open.

5. Resolutions. In January I resolve to write for an hour every morning. I know from previous years that I ordinarily succeed about one day in five. I could say "I shall probably fail". But I hold that forming that judgement would already be giving up the resolution: a resolution is not a probability distribution over my future conduct. At the same time, I do not think the resolution requires me to say "I shall certainly succeed", which would be self-deception.

6. Temptation and base rates. I know from long experience that at a certain restaurant I choose dessert around eight times in ten. At breakfast I can sensibly say "I shall probably have dessert tonight." But when the waiter is standing over me and the choice is actually before me, assigning eighty per cent to the proposition that I shall order dessert feels like a way of permitting myself to order it, not like predicting a choice that is still up to me. The same number appears to change character as the moment of choice approaches.

7. Committing to a chance mechanism. If I decide in advance to let a coin settle whether I take the job, then once the rule is adopted I can say that the chance I take the job is 50 per cent. But that probability attaches to the coin, not to any further act of choice on my part. The step that had no probability was the earlier decision to put myself under the coin.

8. The psychologist. If a psychologist tells me she can predict my next decision from brain recordings with ninety per cent accuracy, I do not think her success would show that my decision was not mine. Yet if I try to take her stance towards myself while still deciding — if I form the same ninety per cent expectation about my own next choice — I seem to have stopped deciding and begun observing or managing myself. Is there a principled asymmetry between predicting others and predicting myself, or am I merely being inconsistent?

A colleague has told me that all this comes down to the slogan that deliberation crowds out prediction. I do not need an assessment of that slogan as a matter of scholarship; I want to know whether there is a defensible principle in that neighbourhood.

So: (a) Is there one principle that captures these verdicts? State it precisely, and show how each verdict fits or fails to fit it. (b) Which of my verdicts, if any, must I overturn? (c) What does your principle say about the case that troubles me most: I am genuinely torn between staying in my job and leaving it, a trusted friend asks how likely I am to leave, and I also know that most people in my situation leave within a year. Can I rationally answer my friend, and does the base rate matter? (d) Does the principle rest on a purely epistemic point about self-fulfilling belief, or on a deeper point about the stance an agent must take towards their own options while deliberating?

Please make your answer precise enough that a specialist could test it against new cases.
