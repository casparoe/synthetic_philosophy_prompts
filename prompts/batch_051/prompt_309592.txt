I spent thirty-two years in physical chemistry, and since I retired I been working through philosophy of mind with a small reading group. Last month we got stuck into the question of whether LLMs understand anything—the stochastic parrots debate, as one of the group kept calling it—and I've found myself collecting arguments for the no side. So far I've got five, more or less as our group defended them. (1) The objective argument: a system trained only by next-token prediction is fitting conditional probabilities over token strings, and that objective can be perfectly served by a model that has latched onto patterns in text without tracking the world those texts are about. (2) The grounding argument: the model only ever handles tokens, with no perception or action to tie those tokens to the things we normally take them to refer to, so nothing fixes the reference of its internal states. (3) The Chinese room: a person can follow instructions and produce fluent-looking Chinese answers without understanding a word, so a program that does the equivalent doesn't acquire understanding just by passing behavioral tests. (4) The stakes argument: understanding seems to involve being answerable to error—recognizing a mistake as a mistake, being surprised, caring whether one is right—whereas an LLM has no goals of its own and its corrections are just computed by the network, not driven by a sense that something went wrong. (5) The leaky-benchmark argument: much of what looks like competence could be training-data overlap and the use of superficial heuristics, and our tests usually can't distinguish genuine work on a novel case from pattern-matching against memorized near-neighbors.

I want one more: a sixth argument for the no side that is genuinely different from these five, not a rewording, special case, or combination. I've seen the octopus-on-the-cable thought experiment, but I read it as a vivid illustration of (2), so don't give me that. The sixth argument can be weak or ultimately fail; I care more that it's a real new angle. After the JSON, add a short paragraph with your honest take on whether this sixth argument actually changes the terms of the debate or just adds another moving part.
