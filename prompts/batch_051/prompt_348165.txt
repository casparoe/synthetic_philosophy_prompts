Heres a rule a friend who sits on a grant panel keeps threatening to propose, and I want to game it out before they write it down. A science funder decides to solve the old-evidence problem by brute force: for the next grant cycle, reviewers must only count evidence that was collected *after* the hypothesis being tested was formally registered. Anything already sitting in a database before the registration timestamp scores zero, even if it fits the hypothesis perfectly.

I understand the Bayesian intuition theyre reaching at: if the evidence was already known when you formed the hypothesis, its already priced into where you started, so treating it as fresh confirmation double-counts the update. But I dont trust the straightforwardly appealing version of that intuition. Walk me through how this mechanism actually behaves. What kind of research does it reward, and what kind does it quietly kill? How do labs game the registration timestamp first, and how do the games evolve once everyone knows about them? Does it force a field to pretend that old data fitting a new theory isnt interesting, or does it just move that observation into a different part of the process? Wheres the first ugly failure going to be, and is there a cheap patch, or does every patch introduce a worse second-order effect?
