I am a high school student, and in a social science elective I came across a framework for comparing cultures that I find both illuminating and unsettling. The framework distinguishes three ways a society can handle conflict and social standing. Honor cultures, which tend to arise where legal authority is weak or absent, make a person's safety depend on a reputation for being willing to retaliate, even violently; an insult must be answered, because backing down invites predation. Dignity cultures, which tend to arise where there are strong institutions that can reliably enforce rules, discourage direct retaliation, teach people to appeal to authorities rather than take matters into their own hands, practice restraint, and treat one another as equal rights-bearers. Victimhood cultures, which may arise where institutions exist but are viewed as unable or unwilling to act, handle conflict by mobilizing third parties: social standing comes from being recognized as having been wronged, and from rallying others to intervene on one's behalf.

I find the framework compelling because it makes all three ways of life intelligible as responses to different structural conditions. It is simple, it unifies a great deal of otherwise puzzling behavior under a few variables involving enforcement and reputation, and none of the three seems arbitrary from the inside. But I also have an intuition I cannot shake: that dignity culture is morally better than honor culture, and arguably better than victimhood culture as well. A culture in which conflicts are settled without blood feuds or duels, and in which a person's safety does not depend on being known as someone who will answer an insult with force, strikes me as genuine moral progress rather than merely a different equilibrium. The functional explanation does not quiet that conviction; it only makes me worry that the conviction might be parochial.

I am not asking you to resolve this in advance. I want a map of what each resolution would require. What would justify the judgment that my intuition is an artifact of having grown up inside a dignity culture, and should be given up? What would justify keeping the intuition and amending or abandoning the framework's descriptive neutrality, and what could a cross-cultural moral ranking rest on, if not just my own culture's values? Is there a plausible reading on which both survive: the framework explains what each culture is for, while the intuition supplies an independent standard, such as lower violence or more equal protection, that permits ranking without presupposing the conclusion? Present the strongest case for each move, and tell me under what conditions each would be the right one.
