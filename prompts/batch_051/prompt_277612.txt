I cover ideas and law for a general audience, and I am preparing a piece on why some mass atrocities are called genocide while comparable ones are not. Before sorting through the cases, I want the conceptual specification. If "genocide" must satisfy three audiences at once — a court-ready legal definition, drawn narrowly with an intent requirement and a finite list of protected groups; a moral category that tracks the distinctive wrong of destroying a group's conditions of existence; and a term whose application is also shaped by the political interests of states — what would an adequate account of the concept have to deliver? Write the requirements list: the cases it must capture (coordinated destruction of a group's conditions of life, not only mass killing) and the cases it may legitimately refuse; the distinctions it cannot collapse, such as destroying a group versus expelling it from a territory, or legal proof of intent versus a standard of judgment a journalist can responsibly apply. Include as constraints Lemkin's original account of genocide as a coordinated assault on the essential foundations of a group's life, and the post-1945 legal settlement's preference for a narrow, provable offense; any revision must respect or consciously reject these. Then flag which requirements are negotiable, which pairs are in tension, and which single requirement does the most to expose what the standard legal definition misses. I want the criteria and the reasoning behind them; a scorecard of who has called which atrocity what matters less.
