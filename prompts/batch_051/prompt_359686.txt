Been thinking about the Pasadena game and I noticed a weird parallel I can't shake. The setup: flip a fair coin until it comes up heads, and if that happens on the nth toss you win (−1)^(n−1) × 2^n / n dollars. The expected value comes out to the alternating harmonic series, which converges, but only conditionally. So by reordering the terms you can make the sum come out to any number you want, or diverge. There's no well-defined expected value.

The parallel is this: when people tell the story of a life, or assess a long project after the fact, they sequence the same good and bad episodes in some order. The overall verdict seems to shift with the order of narration, even though the episodes themselves are identical. It feels like the same structure: the positive and negative contributions don't sum absolutely, so there isn't a "true" overall value; the order in which you recount them is doing the work.

Is this a real structural parallel or am I just pattern-matching? What would have to be true of retrospective evaluation for the math to transfer — for instance, that episodes contribute additively, that there could be indefinitely many of them, and that the positive and negative contributions are each divergent on their own? And does the analogy predict something false, like that with infinitely many episodes a memoir could be arranged to justify any verdict at all, whereas with a finite life the sum is just order-independent? If the infinity is doing all the work, is there still a useful lesson left over for finite retrospective judgments?
