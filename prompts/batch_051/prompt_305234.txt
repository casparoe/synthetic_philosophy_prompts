I have been reading Alan Turing's 1950 Mind paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence. In the section on extrasensory perception, he treats telepathy not as a joke but as a live possibility, says the statistical evidence for it is at least strong enough that the imitation game must take it seriously, and argues that if telepathy is real, the interrogator could learn which contestant is which through telepathy instead of through the typed exchange alone. He therefore suggests that the game might need a room shielded against telepathy before a machine's performance can count as evidence about thinking. I am paraphrasing because I cannot recall his exact wording. I keep thinking about a modern version of this. Suppose an online imitation-game tournament has judges reading transcripts and labeling each one human or machine. Unknown to the organizers, the platform displays each author's time zone as a small piece of metadata, and the judges turn out to be heavily influenced by it, so a judge may be right about who is human without ever attending to what the text says. Does Turing's worry about telepathy give us a reason to treat this accidental metadata channel as the same kind of threat—something that must be blocked before the test says anything about machine thinking? Or is his argument aimed specifically at channels that cannot be explained or observed, so that an ordinary causal leak like a visible time zone is a different kind of problem his reasoning does not cover? I want to know how the passage should be read: is it a general principle about what can undermine a test, or a narrower point about paranormal channels? If the two readings point in different directions, which should a modern organizer follow, and why?
