I'm an economist who works on illicit markets, and I keep getting pulled into drug-policy arguments that run on four scripts at once. One side says prohibition is self-defeating: it pushes supply underground, raises the return to violence, and tries to crush demand that enforcement cannot much reduce. Another points to Portugal's decriminalization as evidence that treating problematic drug use as a health problem rather than a criminal one saves lives and reduces stigma. A third says cannabis legalization is mostly a standard problem of regulation, licensing, and tax design. A fourth says all of this misses the point, which is whether the state may properly permit and collect revenue from a habit that predictably harms some users and the people around them.

I want a controlled translation exercise. Restate the debate among these positions in the vocabulary I actually work in: demand elasticities, externalities (to others and to one's own future self), black-market rents and how they shift as one moves from prohibition to decriminalization to legalization, substitution and complementarity across substances, price effects versus non-price channels such as stigma, information, and self-control, and the limits of Pigouvian taxation when demand is unresponsive or harm is concentrated in a small subset of users. Be faithful: carry each side's central claim across only as far as the vocabulary allows, and do not quietly convert every position into a cost-benefit calculation.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the debate have no counterpart in my vocabulary — autonomy and a right to use, the idea that prohibition corrupts the standing of the state or expresses contempt for the people it polices, the claim that addiction undermines the competence of consent, the worry that legalization sends a message of approval that changes behavior beyond price — and say, for each, whether it fails to translate because my vocabulary is missing something or because the original claim was doing less work than its defenders think. I do not need a literature review or a verdict on the Portugal or U.S. state evidence; I need to see which arguments in this fight are genuinely extra-economic and which only look that way. Throughout, separate what is empirically settled from what is genuinely contested among researchers, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
