I have been turning over a puzzle about the ethics of randomized trials in medicine, and I keep reaching a conclusion that seems much too strong, which makes me suspect I have made a mistake somewhere. Here is the argument as I would lay it out.

(1) A doctor's duty is to act in the best interests of each patient: to recommend the intervention that the doctor, after weighing the available evidence, actually believes offers the best expected outcome.
(2) In a randomized trial, the treatment a patient receives is not chosen by the doctor's judgment about which is best but by a chance mechanism. By enrolling a patient, a doctor therefore knowingly risks the patient receiving a treatment the doctor does not judge best.
(3) That risk is acceptable only when the doctor is genuinely uncertain, meaning the doctor's judgment does not favor either treatment. If the judgment tips at all toward one treatment, then the doctor is deliberately taking a chance with the patient's welfare for the sake of knowledge that will mostly benefit other people.
(4) Genuine uncertainty in this sense is almost never present. A doctor will have a slight hunch from clinical experience, or a vague memory of a paper, or simply the fact that one treatment is the standard of care and the other is experimental. Any of these is enough to make the doctor's judgment tilt, however slightly, toward one treatment.
(5) Therefore, doctors are almost never ethically permitted to enroll patients in randomized trials.
(6) But that cannot be right. If almost no doctor could ethically enroll, randomized trials would slow to a trickle, medicine would lose its best method for learning which treatments actually work, and future patients would be harmed. A principle with that result is almost certainly too strict.

Is the argument valid as it stands? If it is not valid, tell me exactly which step fails, why it fails, and what the corrected version of that step should be. If it is valid, explain how one should accept the conclusion in (5) despite (6).
