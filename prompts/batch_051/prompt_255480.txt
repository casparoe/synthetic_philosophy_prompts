I'm a philosopher of language, and I've been teaching generics this term — the usual examples, "ducks lay eggs" and "mosquitoes carry malaria," where the sentence is true even though only mature females lay eggs and only a handful of mosquito species transmit the parasite. The standard move is to say these are default generalizations with unenumerated exceptions. But I use to code a bit, and it struck me the other day that a generic behaves a lot like a function that can throw: "ducks lay eggs" licenses an inference about any given duck, and that inference gets caught and overridden when the duck turns out to be a drake or a duckling.

In a language with checked exceptions, a method's signature tells you what can go wrong; a generic seems to carry its exceptions around with it too, but the exception list is never explicit, and the sentence doesn't become false no matter how many ducks don't lay eggs. Part of me suspects I'm just being seduced by the fact that both happen to use the word "exception." So I'm wondering whether this is a deep structural parallel — generics are to default inference roughly what checked exceptions are to procedure calls — or just a loose analogy between two things that both involve "default plus override." If it's deep, what would have to be true on both sides for the mapping to do real work, and what does the parallel predict about generics that turns out false? If it's shallow, where exactly does the mapping break?
