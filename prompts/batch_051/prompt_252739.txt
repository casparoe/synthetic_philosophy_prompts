I have a thesis about historical explanation that I have been developing under the name "the posteriority tax," and I want it assessed candidly before I build further on it. The claim is that historical narratives systematically treat the past's own present as less open than it was, and that this is not — or not only — a bias in individual memory but a structural feature of narrative explanation itself. Narratives are assembled retrospectively and must select, and selection is inherently retrospective in a way that makes contingencies look inert. More precisely: every narrative explanation pays a posteriority tax, meaning that the later an event stands in the account, the more explanatory weight it carries for what preceded it, because the account was built in light of the event's having in fact occurred. A general who is assassinated, a firm that later collapses, a treaty that later breaks down: each gets narrated as though the failure or undoing were progressively gathering within the events themselves. I am deliberately setting aside familiar claims about individual cognitive bias, including hindsight bias, and I believe the thesis is also distinct from the narrative fallacy, but I am not confident it is more than a relabeling of one or the other. Here is what I want from you. First, reconstruct the thesis in its strongest form and say whether it is coherent. Second, say whether it is original, a near-variant of an existing position, or some mixture of the two. Third, give me the two or three hardest counterexamples or boundary cases — for instance, a historical narrative that demonstrably does not pay the tax, or a case where the apparent tax is doing no explanatory work — and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than merely limiting. Throughout, separate what is settled about hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy from what is genuinely contested among people who work on them, and do not present a contested account as the consensus.
