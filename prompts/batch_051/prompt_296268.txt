According to the standard scorekeeping picture of conversation (Stalnaker's common ground, Lewis's accomodation), a conversation is steered by a shared store of mutually accepted propositions, and when a speaker says something whose presuppositions aren't in the store yet, the other parties quietly add them so long as the addition isn't to blatantly at odds with what everyone accepts. Now consider a language model chatting with a person: it keeps the whole exchange in a context window and predicts next tokens, but it has no beliefs, no way to mutually accept anything with the human, and no sense in which it could be said to know what the human knows. Does this development kill the common-ground-plus-accommodation account, or does it just force us to stop thinking of common ground as a fact about the minds and start thinking of it as something like an abstract conversational record? If it survives, be explicit about what plays the role of the common ground (the context window contents? the human's projection? the pretraining distribution?) and what plays the role of the accommodation rule. If it doesn't survive, say what is actually going on when a human and an LLM seem to presuppose and accommodate, and whether the old account was always better understood as a rational reconstruction than as a causal description.
