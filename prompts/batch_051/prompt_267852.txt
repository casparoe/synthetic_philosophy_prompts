I'm teaching a grad seminar on computation and the limits of formal methods, and last week we got stuck on the Church-Turing thesis. One student argued that, in it's usual form—every effectively computable function is Turing computable—the thesis is a genuine empirical claim, and that it only looks like a tautology because no one has yet exhibited a counterexample. I've gone back and forth on this, and I want to get out of my own head by forcing the question into a concrete test. Suppose we took the student seriously and tried to design the best possible empirical test of the claim. What observation would actually count? Should the test be a physical device, a humanly executable procedure, a formal system, or some odd hybrid? And what would a positive result—a convincing alleged counterexample—really show or fail to show? I'm worried that any weird candidate gets ruled out as "not effective" by stipulation, which would make the thesis unfalsifiable in practice even if it is a claim about the world. I'd like a committed answer here, not a survey of positions: give me the strongest version of the test, say what result would convince you one way or the other, and then be explicit about what the test could never settle about the epistemic status of the thesis.
