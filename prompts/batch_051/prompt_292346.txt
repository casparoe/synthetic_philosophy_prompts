The usual defense of letting people drive, run power plants, release chemicals, and so on—even though each of these imposes a small risk of serious harm on others without asking them—is that the risks are roughly symmetric and reciprocal: I impose a one-in-a-million chance of death on you and you impose about the same on me, and we both benefit from the whole practice, so it's fair to treat each of us as having accepted the bundle. Now suppose it becomes cheap to measure and log exactly who is imposing what risk: telematics in every car, continuous emissions monitoring, personalized health-risk scores. The empirical claim of rough symmetry stops being true, or at least stops being something anyone can honestly assume. Does the old argument survive this shift? If it does, tell me which premise was doing the real work all along. If it doesn't, give the repaired prinicple: should individual risk imposition now be handled as a tort, a tax, a licensing problem, or something else?
