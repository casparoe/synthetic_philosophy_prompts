At a family dinner last week my uncle got going on a local firm whose founder just installed his thirty-year-old son as CEO; everyone at the table was disgusted, kept saying the kid didn't earn it and that it made a joke of merit. Ten minutes later the same people were arguing that the founder should be free to leave the whole company—shares, voting control, all of it—to that same son with barely any tax, because "it's his to give" and "you'd do the same for your children." Nobbody seemed to notice they'd switched principles between the salad and the coffee.

I can't shake the feeling this is either a real distinction or a big unreasoned asymmetry. In both cases a parent is handing a child an unearned competitive advantage, bought with birth rather than talent or effort, and both look like a problem for anyone who cares about equal opportunity. Yet our practices point opposite ways: we write anti-nepotism rules for public hiring and sneer when private firms do it, but we romanticize family farms and family businesses and keep the transfer of dynastic wealth comparatively gentle. What I want is the strongest principle you can build that lets us condemn the kid-as-CEO case while accepting the kid-as-shareholder case. Candidate lines I've toyed with: a job is an office held in trust for others while wealth is private property; or a job is zero-sum among identifiable candidates while wealth is only vaguely a zero-sum competition; or nepotism corrupts institutions while inheritance merely reproduces families. But the mixed case wrecks most of these—if the founder hands over the controlling shares and the board then makes the son CEO, the "it's just private property" line now covers an institution's leadership. Test whatever principle you propose against that case.
