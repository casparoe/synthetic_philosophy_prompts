In P.F. Strawson's work on moral responsibility, resentment is treated as the central reactive attitude: the response we make when another person's action or attitude toward us seems to express ill will, disregard, or a refusal to meet some demand we make of them. Strawson draws the term carefully, distinguishing resentment from indignation (the vicarious analogue, felt when the wrong is done to someone else), from guilt (the self-directed analogue), and from the objective stance, in which we step out of these attitudes and treat the person as something to be managed, cured, or explained rather than as a participant to be reasoned with.

Your task is to give a working definition of "resentment" that gets the following cases right. I take some verdicts to be fixed:

- A close friend finds your private journal, reads the most embarrassing entries, and teases you about them at dinner. (Paradigm case of resentment.)
- A driver runs a red light while texting, hits your car, and breaks your arm. (Paradigm case of resentment.)
- You stub your toe on a step and, for a moment, curse it. (Clearly not resentment, because the step is not a person.)
- You are caring for a relative with advanced dementia who no longer understands where she is; when you try to help her eat, she spits at you and screams abuse. (Not resentment, though the anger has not simply vanished — something more clinical has replaced it.)

The hard cases, on which I have no settled view, are these:

- The person behind you on a crowded bus steps hard on your foot because the driver brakes suddenly and he is thrown against you.
- A stranger humiliates your friend in front of you; you are not the target, but you feel anger on your friend's behalf.
- You reveal a friend's secret under pressure, and for weeks afterward you are angry at yourself about it.
- A corporation sells a drug it has reason to know is causing liver damage, and you feel sustained anger at the company itself rather than at any particular executive.
- Your father, now dead for a decade, was emotionally cruel to you as a child, and you still feel anger at him.

A dictionary gloss will not do: the definition should be precise enough to yield one verdict for each case without ad hoc adjustments. Please propose the definition, run it through all nine cases, mark each verdict, and say which single verdict is least secure. If the definition misfires on any case, either revise it or defend the surprising verdict. In stating the definition, also say where it stands on two of Strawson's own boundary lines: whether indignation and self-directed anger count as resentment, and what exactly distinguishes a suspended reactive attitude from the objective stance.
