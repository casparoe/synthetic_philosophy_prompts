Here is an argument a colleague pressed on me, and it has left me unsure where to stand. I would most like to keep the thesis that moral deference is defective in a way that ordinary deference to a physician is not, but I cannot tell which step the argument forces me to sacrifice. (1) If moral deference is defective because it outsources the standards by which one lives, then deference in any domain that supplies standards for how to live must be defective for the same reason. (2) Medicine supplies such standards: a patient who accepts a diagnosis or a treatment plan on a physician's say-so adopts rules for how to live — what to ingest, what to avoid, what risks to run. (3) No one regards that sort of medical deference as defective merely because it is deference. (4) So the proposed feature does not distinguish moral from medical deference. (5) If the feature that was supposed to explain the asymmetry does not distinguish the two cases, then the asymmetry itself is probably an artifact. (6) Therefore moral deference is no more problematic than taking a doctor's advice. Which step should be rejected, why did that step look safe, and what does rejecting it cost the pessimist about moral testimony?
