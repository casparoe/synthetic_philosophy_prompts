I keep finding that my verdicts about when someone knows a logical consequence of what they already know pull in different directions, and I would like you to work out the principle behind them — or tell me which of my verdicts must go.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. Blake has just been told by a wholly reliable security officer that (a) the intruder was in the east stairwell at 3 a.m., and (b) whoever was in the east stairwell at 3 a.m. is the accomplice. A minute later, asked to identify the accomplice, he says he does not know. I say he does not know that the intruder is the accomplice. He has both premises and the inference is a single step, but he has not taken it.

2. Cora, a professional logician, has the same two pieces of information from the same source and has likewise not performed the inference in the minute that follows. Asked to identify the accomplice, she answers instantly and correctly, with no felt effort of reasoning. I want to say she knew it all along, not that she only came to know it when asked. Yet before the question there was no moment at which she drew the conclusion, and the absence of such a moment is exactly what made me deny knowledge to Blake.

3. Daria, an undergraduate, has the same pair of propositions. Asked the same question, she works through the one-line inference aloud and only then arrives at the conclusion. I say she knows it now and did not know it a minute ago.

4. Farid knows P, and he has been assured on unimpeachable authority that Q follows from P by a valid derivation of ten thousand steps, each step a rule he is fluent in, but he has never worked through the derivation. I say he does not know Q, although he is in a position to know Q. This feels continuous with Blake: steps not yet taken, so no knowledge of the conclusion.

5. Grace knows P, and Q follows from P by a rule she has never encountered and could not understand if shown it. I say she does not know Q and is not in a position to know Q.

6. Hana knows P and the conditional that if P then Q. Last month she explicitly derived Q and would have asserted it; she has since forgotten the entire affair, and asked now whether Q, she says she has no idea. I say she does not know Q now, though she did know it last month.

7. Kai derives Q from P by a fallacy. Q happens to be true and also follows validly from P along a route Kai never took. I say he does not know Q, even though he ended up with a true conclusion that his evidence did support.

The principle I keep reaching for is something like this: you know a derived conclusion only when you currently hold it on the basis of a derivation you have actually performed, each step valid and grasped by you; before that, you are at most in a position to know. That principle appears to do well on Blake, Daria, Farid, Grace, Hana, and Kai. But it forces me to say Cora did not know the conclusion until the instant she answered, which I find very unnatural; and it makes whether a person knows depend on the history of their mental acts rather than on the intellectual condition they are now in. I also suspect it has trouble saying why Farid, whose ten thousand steps are individually effortless, is not just a stretched version of Cora.

Please give me a precise principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures these verdicts. For each candidate: state it cleanly; say how it handles each case above, singling out the one it handles least well; say which of my verdicts, if any, it requires me to abandon; and apply it to cases I have not listed, including the following. (i) Amad knows the axioms of arithmetic but has never derived a standard theorem that follows from them by a long chain of steps. (ii) Noor has a proof so long that no human could survey it; a trusted machine has verified it, and she understands the statement of the theorem. (iii) Priya performs a one-step inference so quickly that no intermediate step enters consciousness. (iv) Sam has the premises and knows the rule but is too exhausted to apply it at the moment he is asked. Finally, tell me how much work the distinction between knowing and being in a position to know should be doing, and how to draw that line without making it arbitrary.
