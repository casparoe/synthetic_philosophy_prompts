Here's something I keep going back and forth on. I spent my career in fluid dynamics, so I have an operational feel for the butterfly effect: positive Lyapunov exponents mean a perturbation in the initial state grows roughly like exp(λ t), so even a perfect code loses skill after a while. Fine. But since I retired I've been reading some philosophy, and I want to pin down the claim that deterministic systems can be "genuinely unpredictable." So here's the question.

What's your actual credence that the unpredictability associated with deterministic chaos is an in-principle limit rather than a merely practical one? I don't want "it depends" — I want a number and the reasoning in pieces. Let me define "in-principle" carefully: for any physically possible predictor (finite-precision measurements, unbounded computation if you like), there's a hard forecast horizon beyond which a point forecast is no better than the attractor's climatology, no matter how good the instruments get. By "merely practical" I mean: no such hard horizon exists; you can push it out roughly arbitrarily far by measuring the initial condition more finely, and the only obstacle is noise, cost, and instrument resolution rather than anything about the dynamics themselves.

I'd put my own credence at about 0.6 that it's a genuine in-principle limit, but I can't tell weather I'm smuggling in a lower bound on measurement precision. So decompose your answer. In particular, separate: how much rides on whether physical measurement precision has a real floor (thermal noise, quantum limits, whatever); how much rides on whether "prediction" means tracking a single trajectory versus predicting the statistics of a strange attractor; how much rides on the fact that the required initial precision has to shrink exponentially with forecast lead time while a finite observer can only shrink error by a finite factor; and anything else you think belongs, like whether exact real-number states are even a physically meaningful idealization. Then tell me which of those sub-questions, if you changed your mind on it, would move your final number the most. And if my definition of "in-principle" is doing too much work and that's where the real issue lives, say so.
