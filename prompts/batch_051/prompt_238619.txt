Write a dialogue between two decision theorists who disagree about expected value fanaticism. One, whom we can call the fanatic, holds that for any action with a small but nonzero probability of producing an enormous improvement, there is some scale of improvement that makes the action rationally preferable to any action with only ordinary, bounded payoffs; on this view, that conclusion is simply what expected utility reasoning commits us to once we allow that value has no fixed upper bound. The other, whom we can call the bounder, holds that this is a reductio of at least one of the underlying assumptions: unbounded utility, the literal use of arbitrarily small probabilities, or expected utility maximization itself.

Set the dialogue after a seminar in which someone presented the following choice. A foundation must choose between two uses of the same money. Program A is a malaria prevention effort that is virtually certain to save several hundred lives over the next five years. Program B is a speculative research effort with roughly a one-in-ten-million chance of producing a safeguard that would prevent a future catastrophe killing many billions. The fanatic says that, on expected value grounds, if the catastrophe is bad enough, Program B is not merely permissible but mandatory, and that anyone who resists this conclusion is rejecting expected utility theory rather than patching it. The bounder replies that this is exactly the kind of case in which the theory should be patched or abandoned.

Write the dialogue so that the two work through the moving parts rather than trading labels. They should address at least the following, though not mechanically one by one: the St. Petersburg game and what it shows about willingness to pay; whether utility is naturally thought of as unbounded and whether bounded utility draws an arbitrary line; whether very small probabilities are precise enough to enter expected value calculations the way moderate probabilities do; whether the fanatic can reject literal infinities without doing so ad hoc; and whether the bounder's preferred fix inherits unpalatable verdicts of its own, for example about ignoring risks or about violating stochastic dominance. Each character should put forward the strongest version of their view, and they should respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talking past one another. Ensure some real progress: each should grant at least one substantive point to the other, and the dialogue should end with the disagreement narrowed to its most difficult remaining premise rather than resolved by fiat. Aim for roughly ten to fourteen exchanges.
