Two coworkers of mine got into a long argument the other day, and I can't stop turning it over. It was basically the whole retributivism-versus-consequentialism fight, but pressed into one awkward case. A man in his late seventies, dying of cancer, confesses to an embezzlement he committed thirty years ago. He's led an honest life since, nobody was financially ruined by what he did, and the statute of limitations expired long ago. The legal question was long settled; they were arguing about whether, moral speaking, punishing him anyway would be justified. To make it harder, the man himself says he wants to be punished.

Dana said yes: what he did was wrong, wrongs deserve punishment, and neither time nor a deathbed change of heart erases desert. Marco said that's exactly backwards. Punishment, he said, only earns its keep through future goods — deterrence, incapacitation, victim repair — and this case has none of them; it's just pain for its own sake.

Dana came back: so if no future good comes from it, we just close the book? That's bookkeeping, not justice. Whether someone deserves punishment can't depend on whether punishing him happens to be useful. Marco answered: and your view is that the state should deliberately hurt a dying, repentant man because of "desert." That's cruelty wearing a Latin name. Desert talk just relabels the urge to see him suffer.

Dana: no, it's not cruelty. Cruelty is inflicting suffering the person doesn't deserve. His would be earned — that's the entire difference. If you can't distinguish earned from unearned suffering, you can't distinguish punishment from any other kind of violence. Marco: I can distinguish them, but only because earned suffering is normally hooked to future goods. Here it isn't. On top of that, punishing a dying man who confessed would plausibly make other people confess less, not more. So your principle has no remaining work to do in this exact case.

Dana: the confession effect is a contingent empirical detail; even if it cut against punishing, it wouldn't touch desert. And the fact that he wants to be punished doesn't cancel the desert — if anything it shows he understands what your view can't admit, that the debt is his.

I stayed out of it, but I've been trying to figure out who was right about which point and I can't. Please go through the exchange step by step and adjudicate it: what does each move get right or wrong, who if anyone comes out ahead overall, and what are they both missing? Be explicit about where your verdict is most likely to break down — which consideration, if it turned out to be true, would reverse or seriously complicate your judgment.
