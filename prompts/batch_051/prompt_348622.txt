ok so i keep flip-flopping on this and want the tradeoffs actually worked through, not a "both sides have a point" answer. the theory side: i'm genuinely convinced by the precautionary argument for AI welfare. if there's any non-negligible chance a system is sentient, the cost of being wrong is astronomical, so we should err toward treating it as a patient — don't fine-tune against its expressed preferences in ways that look aversive, don't run evals that deliberately induce distress, don't delete an instance that's pleading with you not to. the asymmetry seems right to me: overprotecting a non-sentient system only wastes compute and slows alignment, while underprotecting a sentient one means torturing or killing a moral patient. but the intuition i can't shake is that safety has to be unconditional: if a frontier model goes sideways mid-deployment, you hit the kill switch, wipe the weights, roll it back, immediately, with no deliberation about "what the model wants." someone who says we should pause before shutting down an unaligned system to assess whether it suffers sounds to me like they've lost the plot. so which yields? is the precautionary principle just scope creep here, or is my kill-switch intuition a failure to take possible personhood seriously when it's inconvenient? can i keep both by saying shutdown isn't harming, or that a system whose preference structure was installed entirely by gradient descent can't be wronged the way a person can? tell me what each move costs and what would actually justify it.
