so ive got this policy i started following about a year ago and i honestly cant tell if its a decent way to think or just a trick i use to get out of thinking. whenever someone brings up one of those big impossible-to-settle questions — the world being a simulation you could never detect, a tree falling in a forest with no one around, whether the whole past was created five minutes ago with all our memories and fossils already in place — i ask exactly one question: if this were true instead of false, what would i expect to notice, even in principle? and if the answer is 'nothing,' i file it under not-a-real-question and refuse to have an opinion. a friend of mine whos read some pragmatism says this is basically peirce's pragmatic maxim and that its a respected move, but another friend says im not doing philosophy, im just finding a fancy excuse to not care about questions i cant win.

what worries me is i cant tell, from the inside, wether im applying the rule because i actually think the question is empty or because im too lazy to do the work. it obviously saves me from the simulation debates, and that feels like a win. but it also seems like the same rule would let me write off real questions where the difference is real but personally uncheckable — like something that happened in history where all the evidence is gone, or a maths problem i cant verify because i lack the background. so is this habit defensible? is there a version that keeps the good bits without turning into licence to ignore anything difficult, and how would i tell the difference between the two from the inside?
