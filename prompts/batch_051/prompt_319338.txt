I’ve been rereading Eduard Hanslick’s On the Musically Beautiful (1854), specifically the part of Chapter II where he concedes that music can reproduce the motion of a psychic process—its quickening, slowing, rising, falling, intensifying—but denies that music can represent the particular content or object of any feeling. If I have the argument right, he treats that dynamic resemblance as what makes listeners describe music in emotional terms, while insisting that the feeling itself is not an attribute of the musical content. As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, I keep wanting to turn this into a crux: it seems to grant the phenomenology while refusing the ontology, and I can’t tell whether that actually moves the old debate or just relocates it.

I’m asking for a diagnosis, not a verdict. The dispute about how sounds can be sad has, in recognizable form, apparently been going since well before Hanslick and has not visibly converged. On one side is the view that sadness is in the musical surface itself—perhaps because the music literally possesses the contour, tempo, and dynamic pattern of sad human behavior, or because the music is sad in something like the way a drooping willow is sad. On another side is the view that the sadness is projected or personified by listeners, perhaps through something like bodily mimicry, contagion, or learned association. And there is the more deflationary view that “sad” is just a misleading name for a non-evaluative sonic property with no more intrinsic connection to human sadness than a minor chord has to grief. My question is what kind of disagreement this really is. Is it partly verbal, turning on different senses of “express,” “sad,” or “in the music”? Is it a dispute about which cases count as data—for example, whether the sadness of a slow, falling melody should be treated as a perception or as an emotional response? Is there a hidden empirical question, such as how much cross-cultural agreement exists or what happens in congenital amusia, that could move at least one side? Are the sides answering different questions under one heading: one about music-theoretic properties, one about listener psychology, one about aesthetic normativity? And is Hanslick’s motion-content distinction itself part of the impasse—a distinction that has survived less because it is load-bearing than because it lets each side claim victory over a different half of the phenomenon? If there is a path to progress, I’d like to know what a concrete experiment, formal result, or conceptual distinction would have to show, and which position would be most vulnerable to which result.
