I facilitate a monthly reasoning-and-decision-making group for software engineers at a mid-sized company; there is no formal philosophy background in the room. Next session we have 14 people and 75 minutes. I want a runnable activity—a game or structured simulation, not a lecture—that makes participants feel hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy in historical explanation before either term is named.

The room has a whiteboard, index cards, sticky notes, and a timer. Design the activity around real historical episodes, but do not assume participants know specific dates or names; the setup should give them enough case material to make judgments. The sequence should force three stages: (1) from a deliberately incomplete pre-outcome briefing, participants record numerical predictions or probabilities about what actually happened; (2) after the outcome is revealed, they reconstruct what they remember thinking and write a short causal explanation of why the outcome was almost inevitable; (3) their records are compared so they can catch themselves revising their own past expectation.

The debrief should map moments in the activity onto hindsight bias and narrative fallacy, and should distinguish the memory-revision effect from the storytelling effect. Provide a full 75-minute timeline, all materials, the exact written prompts given to participants, a method for grouping and comparing cards, and a debrief script that gets the group to name the phenomenon themselves. Include an account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—for instance, participants refusing to make numerical estimates, anchoring on one confident person, or the debrief becoming abstract—and how I should handle each. Be honest about which feature of the effect the activity cannot reproduce because I, as facilitator, already know the outcomes in advance.
