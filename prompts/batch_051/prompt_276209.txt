A friend and I recently went back and forth for a long evening about whether a language could, in principle, do without pronouns and other indexicals. He insisted that whatever can be said with "I" or "now" can be said with a definite description, so eliminating indexicals would cost only convenience. I resisted, with the sense that something about the link between an utterance and its context would be lost. At some point the debate started to look familiar, and I realized why: it resembles the distinction in assembly language between relative and absolute addressing. An instruction that says, in effect, "read the value eight bytes before the current instruction" has a constant meaning—the offset—but refers to different memory locations depending on where in memory the code is loaded. An absolute-addressed instruction names a location outright and refers to the same place regardless. Indexicals seem to me to be the relative addressing of natural language: "I" encodes a constant rule (pick out the speaker of the context), but its referent shifts with each utterance, just as an offset is a constant rule that resolves to different addresses. My friend says this is mere pattern-matching on two things that both happen to involve context. I would like a careful ruling. Map the analogy as precisely as you can; say what would have to be true of both sides for any result to transfer between machine code and natural language; and identify at least one prediction the parallel makes that is false of indexicals, of addressing, or both. Then state whether you judge the parallel deep or superficial. Answer in American English, lead with your bottom line, and keep the response to at most 500 words.
