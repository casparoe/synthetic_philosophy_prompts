Act as a professional philosopher of language who specializes in reference and fiction. I am a novelist, and a quarrel with a friend about whether the people in my novels are, in any sense, real has left me stuck on the old puzzle about Sherlock Holmes. I have reduced my confusion to the following argument, and I would like you to assess it carefully: tell me whether the argument is valid, examine each premise, and say exactly which step, if any, fails — or, if none fails, what the conclusion actually commits me to. Take your time and consider the strongest reading of each premise before you settle on a diagnosis.

(1) It is true that Sherlock Holmes is a detective.
(2) A subject–predicate sentence is true only if its subject term refers to something.
(3) Therefore, "Sherlock Holmes" refers to something that is a detective.
(4) It is true that Sherlock Holmes does not exist.
(5) If "Sherlock Holmes" refers to something, then the sentence "Sherlock Holmes does not exist" is false.
(6) Therefore, that sentence is both true and false — unless "Sherlock Holmes" refers to something that can be a detective and yet not exist.

A philosopher friend says the flaw is premise (1): she claims it is true only within the stories, not without qualification. But as a novelist I am uneasy about saying that the declarative sentences of my own narration are simply untrue. Before I either give up (1) or accept a realm of nonexistent detectives, tell me which step, if any, is the mistake, and whether the argument can be repaired.
