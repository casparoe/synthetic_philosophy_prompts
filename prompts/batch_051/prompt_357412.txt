I work as an analyst for a state parole board, and my job is to turn a person's file into an estimate of the probability they'll be rearrested or reconvicted within three years. The number goes into a memo the board votes on. I keep running into what I've started thinking of as the reference class problem: the same person belongs to dozens of groups that each come with their own base rate. All people with that exact offense: 58%. All people his age: 31%. All people with his exact prior record: maybe 22%, but that's based on eleven people, four of whom reoffended. All people who match him on every field of our internal risk instrument: 19%, from a sample so small the confidence interval is basically the whole unit interval.

I've read enough to get that there's no privileged reference class and that any choice smuggles in a value judgment, but that doesn't remove the need to file the memo. I've tried the obvious rules—use the narrowest class with at least N people, use the class that minimizes some error measure on the last three cohorts, average several classes—but each one just moves the arbitrariness around. The narrowest class is often some gerrymandered non-kind like "people charged on a Tuesday with a prior theft and a positive drug test," and the averaging rule gives me a number that no actual class has.

Still, I have to write something. Could you give me a reusable procedure for choosing a reference class—or a small defensible set, or for concluding that the data won't support a number at all—that a non-philosopher could actually run? I do this three or four times a week, the stakes are someone's release, and the memo has to survive a public defender cross-examing it as arbitrary. Constraints: I typically have 45 minutes per case, I can't collect new data, and I'm not allowed to use race, ethnicity, sex, or zip code as classes even though they're often the ones that would shift the number most. The procedure should say what questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a "no" or a "we can't say anything responsible here," and where it hands the call to a human because the question is one of values rather than statistics. Then run it on at least two of these, including at least one where the procedure tells me something I don't want to hear: (a) a 44-year-old with one drug possession conviction from ten years ago, now with a steady job, where "all prior drug offenders" gives 52% but everything narrower says low; (b) a 21-year-old with three theft convictions and a pending assault charge, where "people with pending violent charges" is a huge but contested class and "people convicted of property crimes" is stable but arguably the wrong one; (c) a 55-year-old first-time fraud case where the only precedent class of any size is way too broad to be meaningful. Hard cap of 1,200 words; if you need to cut something, cut one of the examples, not the failure modes.
