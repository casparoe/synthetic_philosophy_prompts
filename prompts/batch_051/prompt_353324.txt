We tend to treat someone with synchronically cyclic preferences—preferring A to B, B to C, and C to A all at once—as a paradigm case of irrationality, since a bookie can extract a stream of small fees from them through a cycle of trades. Yet we do not treat someone whose preferences merely change over time as irrational, even when the change is not prompted by new information: you might prefer staying in to going out tonight, and prefer going out to staying in tomorrow, and no one calls this a failure of practical reason by itself. The cases look structurally similar. If on Monday you prefer A to B and on Tuesday B to A, a broker who knows this could trade you A for B on Monday and B for A on Tuesday, returning you to your original holdings minus the fees. Is there a principled reason why the synchronic cycle is a defect of rationality but the diachronic reversal is not—or should one of these judgments be revised?
