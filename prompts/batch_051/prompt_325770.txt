I'm a painter and a bit obssessed with colour, so I keep running into this thing I have no word for. It seems to me that colour space has four anchors — a red that doesn't lean yellow or blue, a yellow that doesn't lean red or green, and the same for green and blue. Between neighbouring anchors you get every ordinary composite: orange, lime, teal, violet. But the pairs across the middle don't cooperate. A green that leans red — not a muddy brown, but a hue that is reddish and greenish at once the way turquoise is greenish and bluish at once — is something I can't picture and can't mix. Red and green paint gives a dull brown; red and green light gives yellow, which sure doesn't feel red-green. Blue with yellow is the other stubborn pair: in paint it gives green, in light it gives white, and neither is a blue-yellow. So colour space acts like a wheel until you hit two of its diameters, which are just bricked off.

Lately I've come across papers claiming people have produced exactly these blocked mixtures — a proper red-green that isn't brown — by stabilizing images on the retina or by feeding opposite colours to the two eyes, and I've seen claims that stimulating the visual brain produces colours that don't fit the standard scheme at all: colours that seem to glow on their own, greens that are somehow too green, things discussed as new qualia. I can't tell whether that's the same phenomenon as my missing mixtures or two or three different effects wearing similar clothes.

So: does the basic missing-mixture thing have an established name, and is there a broader category for colour experiences that the normal visual system can't build but an altered one apparently can? If there's no name for the broader category, propose one and mark out its edges. I'm not after a bare label — I want to know whether this is a hard limit of the colour system or just a feature of normal viewing conditions, and how it's related to but different from afterimages and ordinary adaptation colours. Also, please keep me honest about what's actually settled here versus genuinely disputed among people who study colour, because the pop-science versions I've read tend to state contested things as fact.
