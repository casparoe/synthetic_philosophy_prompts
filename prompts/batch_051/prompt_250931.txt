I've been trying to think through the school-choice debate — partly because I've watched classmates leave my public school for private or charter options — and I keep noticing a transfer that the usual arguments don't seem to count. When people argue about vouchers or charter schools, they usually talk about money: per-pupil funding follows the student, fixed costs stay behind, and the school that loses the student has to do the same work for less. I think there is a second, quieter transfer at the moment of exit, and I have been calling it an implicit exit subsidy.

By implicit exit subsidy I mean the uncompensated transfer of human and social capital that happens whenever a student leaves a public school for a private school, a charter school, or homeschooling. The student does not walk out empty-handed. They carry away reading and math skills the school helped produce, work habits, a transcript, test scores, teacher recommendations, friendships, and a reputation within the school's community. The receiving school — or the homeschool arrangement — gets all of that for free, and the sending school and the students who stay get nothing back. I call it a subsidy because it is a one-way transfer; I call it implicit because nobody budgets for it or writes a check.

My central claim is that this is related to, but different from, the familiar worry about cream-skimming. Cream-skimming says choice siphons off the easiest-to-serve families and leaves the hardest cases behind. The exit subsidy happens even in the cases choice advocates praise: a well-run public school prepares a student, and then a private school inherits that preparation and can report better outcomes than it actually caused. If we accounted for exit subsidies, the apparent advantage of private or charter schools would shrink, because some of their results were produced by the schools their students left. I realize this may be a metaphor rather than a real accounting concept, and that is part of what I want to know.

Is this coherent, or am I just re-describing selection effects or the ordinary fiscal impact of vouchers in more dramatic language? Does something like it already exist under another name? Where exactly does it break? Maybe a school cannot be owed payment for a student's abilities, since those belong to the student; maybe families already paid for their public schooling through taxes, so the transfer is not really uncompensated; maybe the concept wrongly counts friendships, recommendations, and reputations as things a school can transfer at all. Please reconstruct the idea in its strongest form, name the nearest existing ideas if you know them, and say where it stops being distinctive or stops being true.
