Securities regulators treat two uses of private information in strikingly different ways. A corporate officer who learns about an unannounced merger in the course of her work and buys shares before the announcement is liable for insider trading, even though she acquired the information without theft and both parties to the trade consented. An outside trader who assembles the same conclusion from public sources, trades on it, and moves the price toward what turns out to be the announced value is, if anything, described in official market rhetoric as contributing to price discovery. Yet the standard justification for liquid stock markets is precisely that prices become accurate by aggregating information that begins in many private hands. The same institution thus treats one person's use of private information as a species of market abuse while treating the market's aggregate use of private information as a cognitive achievement.

What implicit theory of information, knowledge, or fair exchange would make this pair of practices coherent? Reconstruct the view that could license both, and be precise about where the boundary between the corporate officer and the outside trader is supposed to do normative work. Distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit with, and identify where they are philosophically underdetermined. Then introduce a competing model: under the assumptions of the no-trade theorem—common priors, risk aversion, and trade motivated only by private information—an offer to trade is itself a signal, and purely informational trade tends to unravel. If that model is a realistic description of the relevant market, does the insider-trading ban presuppose a picture of markets as places where informational trade is possible unless restrained? And what would have to change in either the regulation or the epistemology of markets if the no-trade model were accepted instead? Reconstruct the implicit theory and evaluate whether the practice actually needs it.
