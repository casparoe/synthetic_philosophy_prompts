Lately I've been trying to decide whether a resemblance I keep seeing between gauge theory and software is deep or just pattern-matching. In a gauge theory one physical state is represented by a whole class of distinct potentials; configurations related by a gauge transformation are the same situation, and only gauge-invariant quantities are observable. The surplus structure is treated as an artifact of the formalism rather than the world. In software, source code likewise has more structure than behavior: many implementations realize the same input-output function, and refactoring is the discipline of changing code while staying inside the same behavioral equivalence class; a 'gauge' would be a coding standard or canonical form that fixes a convenient representative without adding semantic content. But I worry this is only the generic idea of an equivalence relation. Physically, gauge redundancy is tied to the theory's local constraints (in electromagnetism, Gauss's law), and it comes with real facts a bare 'same observables' picture doesn't make obvious: a gauge condition may fail to pick a unique representative, and a symmetry of the classical equations may fail to survive quantization. On the software side, 'same behavior' is whatever a chosen semantics or test suite says it is, not an internal constraint structure. I want to know whether there is a precise mapping, with counterparts for the gauge group, gauge orbit, invariant observable, and gauge fixing, and whether those two physical facts have any software analogue. Is this a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion, and where does it cleanly break?
