In “Moral Hard-Wiring and Moral Enhancement” (2017), Ingmar Persson and Julian Savulescu state the position they have defended in their work on moral bioenhancement:

> The present time brings existential threats which human moral psychology, with its cognitive and moral limitations and biases, is unfit to address. Exponentially increasing, widely accessible technological advance and rapid globalization create threats of intentional misuse (e.g. biological or nuclear terrorism) and global collective action problems, such as the economic inequality between developed and developing countries and anthropogenic climate change, which human psychology is not set up to address.

Their argument rests on a mismatch: the reach of technology expands rapidly, while the moral psychology of the humans who wield it remains fitted for small-group cooperation rather than for global, long-range, statistical problems. Moral bioenhancement is supposed to close that gap.

I want to know whether this argument survives a development that has become central since they wrote. The most conspicuous instance of such “widely accessible technological advance” is now the general-purpose AI system: trained on human text, able to plan, persuade, write code, and act with increasing autonomy. Such a system is already, in an obvious sense, a product of massive cognitive enhancement, but it lacks anything like the evolved moral psychology whose limitations Persson and Savulescu diagnose. It can deploy moral vocabulary without having the moral motivations they believe need to be improved, and none of their proposed biomedical interventions could be applied to it. Meanwhile, its human users and developers remain the same morally limited agents, now acting through systems that greatly extend their reach.

Does the argument still go through, does it need repair, or does it collapse? I am asking about the fate of the argument, not for a policy recommendation. Name the premise that this development bears on: is it the empirical premise that the dangerous moral deficit is located in the biology of individual human agents, the assumption that the agents wielding the new technology are human beings, or something else? And say what a repaired version would look like. One natural repair is to claim that the artificial systems, not the humans, must be “morally enhanced”—aligned or constrained—while human bioenhancement becomes a secondary matter; but does that preserve Persson and Savulescu’s conclusion, or does it quietly change the subject? If the argument survives only in a weakened form, state that form precisely; if it fails, say exactly where.
