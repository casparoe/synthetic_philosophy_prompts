I have a research rule I've come to rely on, and I want to know whether it's actually defensible. When a contested empirical or policy question comes up—for a school paper, or just to figure out what I believe—I decide in advance what an adequate answer would need to look like: a clear mechanism or causal story, no conflict with facts I already trust, and a source without an obvious conflict of interest. Then I search only until I find the first source that meets that bar, and I stop. I treat its conclusion as my working view even when I know there are other serious arguments I haven't looked at. Last term I did this on the question of whether later school start times improve academic performance: I read one review that gave a plausible sleep-based mechanism and passed my checks, and I wrote the paper from it. I got a good grade, but I'm left unsure whether I actually know the claim is true. My debate partner calls the rule rationalized laziness—I'm just letting whichever adequate-seeming source I encounter first decide my beliefs. I call it satisficing under bounded rationality: my time and attention are limited, I can't compare every position, so I take the first acceptable account rather than optimizing. But I worry that this is just a relabeling, not a justification. Is my rule defensible as an epistemic policy? Under what conditions would stopping at the first adequate source be rational rather than premature closure? Which features of the subject matter—how contentious the question is, how uneven the quality of sources is, how high the stakes are—should change what counts as adequate, or change the decision to stop at all? And is there a revised version of the rule that keeps the efficiency while protecting against the failures most likely to come from my way of stopping?
