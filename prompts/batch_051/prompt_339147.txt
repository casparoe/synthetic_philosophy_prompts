Some friends and I run a tiny site where we try to forecast how long current countries will last, and the friend who built it wants to add a rule to stop everyone from rationalizing their pet forecasts. The rule: before you look at the country your forecasting, you have to pick a reference class from a fixed menu that we maintain (for example, other states at a similar age since formation, land empires since 1500, or industrial democracies since 1945), and your forecast can't be more than 20% off that class's base rate unless you publish a short note arguing that this time is different. The menu is public and forecasts get scored, so there's real pressure to be accurate, not just to pick whichever class gives you the answer you already wanted.

I want to work through what this rule actually does once people start responding to it, not whether it's a good idea in the abstract. Walk me through the incentives: how forecasters would pick reference classes, how the fixed menu itself would get gamed over time, what kinds of forecasts would actually get published under the rule, where the mechanism breaks first, and whether any of the failure modes can be fixed without throwing out the base-rate anchor entirely.

- What it does to reference-class choice — the strategic behavior around picking and defining classes (1–2 paragraphs).
- What it does to published forecasts — what gets said, what gets hidden, and how the this-time-is-different note gets used (1–2 paragraphs).
- First big failure — the specific place this comes apart and why it happens before anything else (1 paragraph).
