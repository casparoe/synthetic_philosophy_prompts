I've been going back through Zhuangzi on death and I keep feeling like there's a sharper point in there than it usually gets credit for. The thread I have in mind goes roughly: we never actually occupy the state we're judging when we call death bad, and the very standards we use to judge it are produced by the life we're currently living. He keeps gesturing at the idea that the dead, if they had a view at all, might not share our horror — they might look back on clinging to life the way someone who was lost looks back at the wrong road that felt so right at the time. And he pairs that with the thought that grief mistakes one phase of transformation for the whole story.

This strikes me as structurally identical to the problem of transformative experience in decision theory, at least as I understand it: you can't rationally evaluate an outcome from outside it when going through the experience would itself rewrite the preferences you'd use for the evaluation, so the old utility function's verdict doesn't even apply. The parallel is that both locate the trouble not in missing facts but in missing a stable evaluative standpoint.

But I have two worries that the analogy is actually shallow. First, death isn't a transformative experience I'll have and then reflect back from — there's no survivor whose preferences got updated, so the decision-theoretic setup may need a subject that Zhuangzi dissolves. And second, Zhuangzi seems less interested in making a better decision about death than in loosening the grip of the whole decision. What exactly would have to be true of Zhuangzi's view for it to be captured by a model of unknown future preferences, and where does the modeling project start getting him wrong? If the parallel were deep, what should it predict that would then turn out false? I'm trying to figure out whether this is a real structural identity or just a vibe.
