I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether any miracle claim could ever be rationally believed, and he keeps reaching for the old Humean argument: a miracle is a violation of a law of nature, the evidence for the law is all of past experience, and human testimony is just probable evidence, so a sensible person should size their belief to the evidence and withold assent. His tweak is that Hume was writing about a world where evidence for a strange event was basically someone's say-so — a neighbor who saw something, a traveler's story — so the argument says alot less about our world, where an alleged miracle would likely be caught on dozens of phone cameras, security feeds, and maybe seismographs or dashcams at once. That seems like it should change the force of the argument, because Hume himself allows, I think, that testimony could in principle establish a miracle if the falsehood of the testimony would be an even larger miracle; multiple independent recordings look like exactly that case.

But then it hit me that the same technology that records everything also fabricates everything. In a world of deepfakes and generated video, maybe the evidential value of seeing it on camera has collapsed, and miracle reports are in even worse shape than in Hume's day. I'm trying to figure out which of these two changes wins, or whether they just pull in opposite directions. Does Hume's argument survive the shift from human testimony to machine evidence and machine forgery? Which exact premise does each development bear on, and is there a repaired version of the argument that works when recording and fabricating are both cheap and ubiquitous? Or does the argument not really depend on the channel at all, and I'm overcomplicating it?
