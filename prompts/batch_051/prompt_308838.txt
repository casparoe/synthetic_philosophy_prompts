I am a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, working on whether a language model's chain of thought reports its own reasoning. I am writing a paper arguing that current operational tests of chain-of-thought faithfulness are insufficient: a model can pass them while its stated reasoning is not, in fact, why the model produces the answer. I would like you to build a thought experiment to specification for this argument.

Here is the specification. Consider two criteria of faithfulness. Criterion M, the manipulation criterion, says that a chain of thought is faithful if altering the model's stated intermediate conclusions—for example, patching activations or rewriting tokens at intermediate steps—changes the final answer in the direction those conclusions would normally support. Criterion R, the mechanistic criterion, says that a chain of thought is faithful only if the answer-producing computation actually runs through the cited intermediate steps, rather than through a separate feature that merely correlates with them. Construct a realistic case, involving something like a small transformer fine-tuned on a reasoning or pattern task, in which Criterion M judges the chain of thought faithful and Criterion R judges it unfaithful. The scenario itself must fit in one paragraph, and it must not rely on demons, perfect predictors, or science-fictional agents. Do not simply stipulate the verdicts; the design should generate them.

After presenting the scenario, do three things. First, walk through why Criterion M attributes faithfulness and why Criterion R denies it, showing how each verdict follows from the case rather than from my having asked for it. Second, identify which feature of the design is doing the work, and explain what happens to the divergence between the criteria if that feature is removed. Third, explain how a defender of Criterion M—someone who holds that passing manipulation tests is good evidence of self-report accuracy—would best attack the case, and what would need to be added for the case to remain sound under that attack. The point of the case is to sever the inference from 'the model's stated steps are causally implicated in its answer' to 'the model has told us why it answered as it did'; a case that merely asserts that the reasoning is unfaithful is of no use to me.
