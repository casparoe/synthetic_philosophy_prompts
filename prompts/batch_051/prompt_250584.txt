I keep going around in circles on something and would love a diagnosis rather than a lecture. In a decision theory seminar we read a line of thought that I keep reconstructing as an argument, and it seems to prove way to much, so I suspect I've built it wrong.

(1) If you have good evidence about some matter and no special reason to exclude it, rationality requires you to assign a probability to it. (2) I have excellent evidence about my own future choices: my track record, my laziness, the fact that I've ordered the burger every time this semester. (3) So a rational person in my position should assign, say, 0.9 to the proposition that I'll order the burger tomorrow. (4) But assigning 0.9 to my ordering the burger is already believing, to degree 0.9, that the matter is settled in the burger's favor. (5) If I believe to degree 0.9 that the matter is settled, then my deliberation about what to order is not genuinely open; I'd be going through the motions or deceiving myself. (6) Yet obviously I can deliberate about what to order even with all this self-knowledge. Tomorrow at the counter I'll hesitate, and I don't think that makes me irrational. (7) So (3) must be wrong: a rational deliberator can't assign probabilities to her own choices. (8) But then what do I do with the evidence in (2)? Treating myself as a special exception to (1) looks like special pleading.

Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep (1) and (6), because giving up (1) feels like abandoning the whole probabilistic picture of rationality, and giving up (6) feels like denying something I live inside every day. But if the argument is valid after all, tell me which of (1), (6), or (8) you'd bite the bullet on and what self-knowledge looks like on that picture.
