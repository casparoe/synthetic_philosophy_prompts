I'm a science and ideas reporter at a mid-size outlet, filing two or three pieces a week—usually around 900 words, for smart non-specialists—and I keep going in circles on one question. I've been reading around in philosophy of communication, and there's a view I find persuasive but can't operationalize. The view is that when you address someone, you owe them not just accuracy and sincerity but legibility: a reader should be able to retrace how you got from the evidence to the claim, including the places where you're uncertain. In practice that's impossible for me. If I make a health claim fully legible, I'm publishing half the methods section; if I don't, I'm making results look like they fell from the sky. My editor thinks legibility reads as hedging. Our fact-checker wants exact qualifiers. I'm making it up case by case, and readers really do change their behavior and their policy views based on these pieces.

What I want is a reusable decision procedure, not another essay. For any given claim or piece, I want the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure says kill the claim or refuses to run, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a procedure call. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire. Then run it on three pieces: (a) a nutrition study with a tiny sample, a weak effect, a confident press release, and a bunch of prior studies pointing the other way; (b) a preprint from a famous lab claiming their model beats all benchmarks, but the methods section is too thin for an outsider to reconstruct why it should work; (c) a profile of a critic who argues that fields have turned legibility into performance—clean causal diagrams and preregistration as theater—and who writes in a deliberately hard-to-parse way. Tell me what the procedure says to do in each case, and what it can't see. I expect at least one of those to produce an answer I'll hate.
