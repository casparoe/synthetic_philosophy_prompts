I've been working through Irving, Christiano, and Amodei's paper "AI safety via debate" and something about its structure keeps bugging me. They set up a game where two agents argue in front of a human judge who may not be capable of checking the right answer directly; the entire bet is that the back-and-forth, not the judge's own expertise, lets a weaker judge pick the more truthful agent. The passage where they say "This hierarchy of alignment tasks has a natural limit: a debate between competing agents where agents make arguments, other agents poke holes in those arguments, and so on until we have enough information to decide the truth." reminds me immediately of the forma disputationis from medieval universities — the machinery behind Aquinas's disputed questions — where one side objects to a thesis, another replies, and a master or audience decides without being able to verify every link in the chain independently.

Is this an actual identity of structure, or am I just pattern-maching? The pieces that look identical: the judge is deliberately outclassed by the debaters; the truth-tracking comes from opposition rather than from either debater's honesty; a move that would be unsafe if a single expert made it becomes safe when someone gets to poke holes in it. If the parallel is real, I'd want to know whether lessons transfer. Medeival faculties worried a lot about sophisms, arguments that make the worse side win before a limited judge, and they developed constraints about relevance, burden of proof, and which moves a respondent has to answer rather than restate. Should AI debate have something analogous to those rules, or does the analogy break at exactly the point that the AIs are trained on winning rather than having any respect for the truth? And what's a concrete thesis the parallel would predict that actually comes out false?
