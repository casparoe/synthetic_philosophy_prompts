Draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between two camps on small-scale outdoor research into stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) would issue. Name the camps as follows. The research-first camp holds that small-scale, short-duration outdoor experiments are permissible and probably necessary: models and indoor experiments leave dominant uncertainties about plume dynamics and aerosol microphysics unresolved, the direct physical effect of releasing a few kilograms of material is negligible, and treating such experiments as though they were deployment is a category error. The governance-first camp holds that no outdoor SAI experiments should proceed before a global governance framework is in place: even experiments with negligible direct effect build deployment infrastructure, normalize solar geoengineering as a live option, intensify moral hazard, and produce knowledge that is not cleanly separable from the capacity to deploy; the precautionary presumption should therefore run in the opposite direction.

Write the statement in four parts.

1. Numbered agreed claims. State at least eight claims, mixing physical and political ones, that both camps can sign without hedging. They should cover at least: that SAI would mask greenhouse warming rather than remove its cause; that it would not address ocean acidification; that very small releases would have a near-zero direct climate effect; that a maintained large deployment, if stopped abruptly, would produce a termination shock; that models and indoor experiments alone cannot resolve every relevant uncertainty; and that moral hazard and unilateral deployment are genuine risks that any governance regime must address.

2. A disagreements table. Provide at least six rows, with columns for the issue, the formulation the research-first camp would sign, the formulation the governance-first camp would sign, and an assessment of whether each side accepts the other side's wording as a fair statement of the other position. Keep the formulations concrete and non-rhetorical, and do not make one side sound like a strawman.

3. Cruxes. Identify three or four sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp on whether small experiments should go forward. For each crux, state the prediction each side records in advance and specify the observation, natural experiment, or argument both sides agree would bear on it. Include at least one crux about whether announced small-scale experiments shift mitigation effort downward, at least one about whether moratoria remain stable under unilateral defection, and at least one about whether knowledge from small experiments can be kept separate from deployment decisions.

4. Verbal disputes. State which, if any, residual disagreements are partly or wholly verbal, for example because they turn on how the boundary between experiment and deployment is drawn, and specify the stipulation or numerical threshold that would dissolve each.

Do not resolve the debate or declare a winner. The deliverable is a document both camps could publish jointly, with the residual disagreement made as exact as possible.
