So here's the thing. Where I live the only inheritance tax is a one-line section from a statute passed in the 1890s and never updated. It reads:

'Upon the death of any person, duty shall be charged on the value of all property of which the deceased was at the time of his death competent to dispose, whether he disposed of it by will or not.'

I'm not a lawyer, but that's the whole relevant clause.

The case I can't stop turning over is this. A woman spent her last ten years transferring her company shares into a family foundation. By the time she died she personally owned almost nothing. The foundation's rules let her children live in its properties, take salaries as directors, and borrow from it at near-zero interest with no repayment date. The loans are the whole trick: the children have use of the fortune without ever owning any of it, so on her death the duty fell on a few thousand pounds of personal effects. The foundation grows tax-free, and the children just take out bigger loans against it.

The statute was written to stop dynastic fortunes passing untaxed between generations, but I'm fairly sure the drafters never pictured a family foundation with revolving loans. My question is whether the clause, properly read, reaches this arrangement. Work through the readings: the plain words, the purpose of the act, what the 1890s drafters would have said if asked, and what a reasonable reader today makes of the word 'dispose'. Then say which approach should win and what it means for the loophole.

I should say why I care. The debate I'm in is whether inheritance tax is just a revenue device or the main legal expression of the idea that people should begin roughly level. If it's the latter, then a scheme like this isn't just lost tax; it's an end-run around the one mechanism that stops family wealth from compounding forever. I'd like the answer to stay inside the act, though, rather than reach for principles from outside it — unless you think the act itself points there.

Keep the answer under 500 words, and in British English.
