I co-edit a small satirical magazine about economics and philosophy, and the next issue is a theme on moral trade. The background idea we've been explaining to readers is this: people have different values, so there can be mutually beneficial trades in which each side does something the other finds morally important but that doesn't cost them much by their own lights — you donate to a global-health charity I care about, and I give up factory-farmed eggs even though I don't think eggs are much of a moral issue, and at the end we both honestly think the world got morally better. We like the idea, actually, so we don't want a hit piece on it; we want the satire to bite at what happens when it gets turned into a product.

Could you write us a parody FAQ page, maybe 700–900 words, for a startup — call it something like Absolve, Indulgeo, or DutySwap — that lets users list, swap, combine, and short-sell their own obligations and abstentions: volunteer-hour futures, apology credits, filial-duty swaps, ethically sourced meat-free Mondays, and a 'friends and family round for your vices'. The page should be in the deadpan voice of a sincere, slightly-too-credentialled founder who cannot stop saying 'efficient', 'mechanism design', and 'clearing house for conscience'. It should include a pricing or units section, a dispute-resolution policy for contested transfers, and a testimonial or two. The jokes have to come from the real moving parts of the idea rather than from simple hypocrisy, and please don't let the whole thing collapse into the old 'it's just medieval indulgences again' point — a passing nod is fine, but we want the rest to come from the actual structure of moral trade: how you compare the strength of two people's reasons without a common moral currency, whether you can offset a harm to someone who never consented, whether some obligations (promises, honesty, duties to particular people) are non-transferable, whether users could arbitrage or launder their own consciences, whether there's a difference between trading acts and trading principles, and why the genuinely attractive thought — that two people can each leave the world better by their own standards — makes the whole reductio sting rather than just seem silly. Aim for a reader who knows a bit of philosophy to find it fair and a reader who doesn't to still find it funny. British English throughout, please, with UK spelling.
