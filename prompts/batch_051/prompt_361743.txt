I am a philosophy professor, and my main research area is the philosophy of language. For the past three years I have also been the senior editor of the science-and-society section of a monthly general-interest magazine, and the same fight recurs with every issue. Our writers use generic sentences—statements of the form Ks are F, such as "ducks lay eggs" and "mosquitoes carry malaria"—and our copy editors flag them as inaccurate because they are false of most members of the kind: only sexually mature female ducks lay eggs, and only the females of a few Anopheles species transmit malaria. Other editors defend the sentences as saying something true of the kind itself, not of each individual. The result is inconsistent editing: sometimes a true and useful generic is replaced with an awkwardly quantified sentence, and sometimes a misleading generic survives untouched.

I want a reusable editorial procedure for deciding what to do with a generic sentence. It should be runnable by a copy editor with no training in semantics, in under ten minutes per sentence, and it has to fit within the production deadline for each monthly issue. For each sentence, the procedure should return one of three outputs: pass, revise, or escalate. It should be grounded in what the semantics and pragmatics of generics actually tell us—why a generic can be true without being true of most members of its kind, how generics differ from existential and universal quantification, and how a true generic can still mislead in context—but it should present these as operational tests rather than theory. It must also mark the points where the matter is not truth-conditional but editorial, and send the decision to a senior editor there.

Please structure the answer as follows.

1. The procedure (roughly 900 words). A numbered checklist with pass, revise, and escalate conditions for each step, written for working editors.

2. The distinctions it depends on (roughly 350 words). The two or three semantic and pragmatic distinctions that do the real work, explained without jargon.

3. Worked applications (roughly 650 words). Run the procedure on three sentences: (a) "ducks lay eggs" in a feature about a waterfowl biologist; (b) "mosquitoes carry malaria" in a one-paragraph public-health explainer; and (c) a sentence of your choosing, selected so that the procedure returns a verdict the editors—including me—would find unwelcome. For each, show which step decides and state the instruction to the writer.

4. Limits (roughly 250 words). The cases the procedure cannot settle, where it will predictably misfire, and why those misfires are acceptable.

Use American English throughout.
