You are an expert in hermeneutics and the philosophy of religious symbolism. I will report my verdicts on several cases involving numbers in scripture, and I want you to help me find the principle, if any, that captures them. Think carefully: before settling on any proposal, brainstorm several candidate principles and test each one against the cases below; then state the account you think survives that testing, even if it is complex or hedged.

Here are my verdicts about when numbers in scripture are carrying meaning and when noticing a number is just pattern-hunting.

1. The seven-day structure of Genesis 1, with the seventh day set apart as a day of rest, seems to me to be doing real numerical work: completeness, perfection, a deliberately shaped week. I would call that interpretation, not numerology.

2. The recurrence of forty days and forty years (the flood, Moses on Sinai, Israel in the wilderness, Elijah's journey, Jesus' fast) seems meaningful in a loose way, as a conventional round number for a complete or testing period. But I do not feel it is hiding a specific, calculable content.

3. Matthew's genealogy is arranged in three sets of fourteen generations, and I have read that the Hebrew letters of the name David sum to fourteen. I find this significant: it looks as though the number is doing deliberate work in shaping the genealogy. Even setting aside the gematria, the text itself foregrounds the count.

4. In John 21 the disciples catch 153 fish. I know 153 is the seventeenth triangular number, and I have seen it connected further to 10 and 7. I cannot bring myself to think this was intended. It feels like a puzzle imposed on a concrete detail after the fact. The text does not mark the number, and the arithmetic links seem supplied entirely by the reader.

5. Revelation gives 666 as the number of the beast and calls on anyone with insight to calculate the number. Part of me thinks this is a genuine coded reference to some name. But I know 616 appears in some manuscripts, and I have read that both 666 and 616 can be made to fit different candidate names. That flexibility unsettles me: it suggests the gematria method can be made to find whatever the interpreter wants.

6. Equidistant-letter sequences, where a regular skip pattern spells out predictions of modern events, strike me as pure pattern-hunting. If a method can find something in any sufficiently long text, and if it finds modern content in ancient texts, that is damning.

7. Counting how often a word such as God or love occurs in an English translation and then noting that the count is prime, triangular, or otherwise special seems meaningless to me, because the count depends on translation and the arithmetic property is not tethered to anything the text itself does.

I can see a tension inside these verdicts. I accept gematria in case 3 but distrust it in case 5. I accept the structural use of seven but reject the arithmetic observations of cases 4 and 7. What I want to know is this: is there a single principle, or a small set of principles, that draws the boundary between interpretation and pattern-hunting and delivers these verdicts? If a clean principle exists but forces me to abandon one of my verdicts, tell me which one and why it should go. If no clean principle exists, I would rather know that too.

Finally, test whatever account you defend on a case I keep going back and forth about: the number 12 (twelve tribes, twelve apostles) and the 144,000 sealed in Revelation 7, where 144 is 12 times 12. Sometimes I feel the arithmetic is doing symbolic work there, sometimes I feel it is merely a literary convention for a large, complete number. What does your principle say about that case, and does the principle get the case right?
