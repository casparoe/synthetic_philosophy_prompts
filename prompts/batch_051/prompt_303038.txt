I am a retired population biologist, and I have only recently begun to read moral philosophy with any care. An old colleague—a demographer, not a philosopher—has shown me an argument that seems to prove that ordinary moral thought has no coherent way to judge whether a bigger family, a bigger city, or a bigger species is a better thing. I can state the argument, but I cannot find the step at which it goes wrong, and I would be grateful for a precise diagnosis.

(1) When we compare two populations, the two quantities that seem to matter are how many lives there are and how well those lives go. The most natural way to combine them is to add up the value of the individual lives: the value of a population is the sum of the values of the lives it contains.

(2) A life that is worth living has positive value, even if only barely.

(3) Therefore, for any fixed population of excellent lives, there is some much larger population of lives that are barely worth living whose total value is greater. The excellent lives have a finite total value, while each added barely-worth-living life contributes a fixed positive amount, so enough such lives will eventually exceed that total.

(4) If one outcome has greater value than another, it is better.

(5) It follows that a city of ten billion people whose lives are each barely worth living is better than a city of one million people whose lives are all excellent. Ordinary thought seems to reject this. We do not think that merely adding lives while allowing the quality of each life to fall toward zero produces a better city, family, or species.

(6) The obvious repair is to use the average value of a life rather than the sum. But the average has consequences no less strange. It implies that adding a person whose life is worth living but below the current average makes the population worse: a flourishing couple would make things worse by having a child whose life would be good, though not quite as good as theirs, and a world containing one very happy person would count as better than a world containing a billion people each only slightly less happy.

(7) Thus the sum and the average each force a conclusion that ordinary moral thought will not accept.

I am not sure whether the error is in the additive premise, in the move from value to better, or in my assumption that these quantities can be combined in one number at all. If the flaw is in an inference, I would like the slide named. If the argument is valid, I would like to know which premise to abandon and how ordinary moral thinking about population size can be rebuilt without it. If I had to choose, I would most like to keep the thought that a life worth living is good for the person who lives it, and that adding such a life, other things equal, is not a moral mistake; but I do not want to buy that thought at the price of being unable to say that a larger population can ever be better.
