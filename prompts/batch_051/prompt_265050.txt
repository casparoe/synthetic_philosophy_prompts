Consider two cases. In the first, a child is born far from any official registry and goes unrecorded for years; no institution has ever acknowledged the child's existence. We would find it almost absurd to say that the child is not yet a person, or lacks rights, until some government takes note of it; the recording, we think, merely declares what is already true. In the second, a political community has governed a fixed territory for decades — collecting taxes, enforcing law, conducting foreign relations — yet most other states refuse to recognize it. Here views genuinely divide: some hold that it is not a state, or not a fully real one, because recognition has been withheld; others hold that recognition merely declares what the community's practice of government already establishes. Recognition therefore appears to work as a mere declaration when applied to persons, but as possibly constitutive — as partly making the fact — when applied to states. Is this asymmetry defensible, or is one of these two judgments a confusion that should be given up? If you defend the asymmetry, identify the feature of states, but not persons, that makes their existence depend on the attitudes of others, and test it against the unrecognized-but-effective polity described. If you reject it, say which judgment should be revised and in which direction, and explain what that revision would cost.
