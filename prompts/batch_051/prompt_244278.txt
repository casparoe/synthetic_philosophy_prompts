I review grant proposals at a small foundation that funds "culture change" interventions in public health — programs that try to shift some set of values, norms, or shared meanings in a community and claim that the shift will cause a health outcome. I see proposals claiming things like "the problem is a clinical culture that treats more treatment as better care," or "vaccine hesitancy is caused by a culture of distrust toward outside institutions." I need a way to tell, before we spend money, whether a proposal's cultural explanation is doing real causal work or just re-describing the outcome in fancier words. I've been told this is the same problem Max Weber ran into when he tried to explain the rise of capitalism by appeal to a Protestant ethic, and that there is real philosophical work on what it even means for a value or a cultural pattern to be a cause.

Please write a screening procedure my team could actually run on a proposal in under an hour. Ground it in whatever the philosophy and historical sociology of cultural explanation actually establish: for example, the difference between culture as a cause, as an effect, as a background condition, and as a re-description of the behavior to be explained; the requirement that a mechanism be specifiable; and the danger of explaining values with more values all the way down. The procedure should be a numbered checklist or decision tree in words: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values or of evidence we cannot get. Flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three proposals:
1. A program claiming that antibiotic overprescription is driven by a clinical culture of "more treatment is better care," and that changing this culture through peer-led workshops will reduce prescriptions.
2. A program claiming that vaccine hesitancy in a particular community is caused by a culture of distrust toward outside institutions, and that a campaign using community-chosen messengers will change that culture and raise vaccination rates.
3. A program claiming that smoking persists because of a culture of fatalism about health, and that a media campaign replacing fatalistic narratives will reduce smoking.

For each case, show which step of the procedure does the decisive work and what the verdict is — including at least one case where the procedure tells us to reject a proposal I might personally like. Keep the whole thing under 1,000 words.
