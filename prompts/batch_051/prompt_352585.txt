Before I retired I worked as a natural scientist, specifically in chemistry, and only recently have I been reading philosophy of language. A parallel keeps nagging at me, and I cannot tell whether it is deep or superficial.

When a theoretical posit is abandoned because no one could isolate it and its explanatory work was redistributed—phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous aether—historians of chemistry still make unproblematically true statements about it, provided they relativize the claim to the theory in which the term lived. "In the phlogiston theory, combustion releases phlogiston" is true; "phlogiston exists" is false. The treatment of fictional names I have been reading seems to do exactly the same thing. The name "Sherlock Holmes" has no bearer in our world, the unrelativized sentence "Holmes lives on Baker Street" is not a factual report, and an operator—"in the Holmes stories"—makes it true. Both cases look like an empty term placed under an "in..." modifier, after which truth reappears.

On reflection, though, the analogy may be only syntactic. The phlogiston chemists were not writing fiction: they intended to describe our actual world, and their assertions failed. Conan Doyle was not doing that; the stories stipulate or pretend a world rather than assert that ours contains Holmes. So "in the theory" rescues statements about a posit whose purported reference failed, while "in the fiction" licenses statements about an object that never existed at all. Does "Sherlock Holmes" refer within the fiction in anything like the sense in which "phlogiston" referred within the theory, or are these two different relations that merely share an "in..." surface?

Is this a genuine isomorphism between truth-in-fiction and truth-in-superseded-theory, or only a resemblance of surface grammar? If it is genuine, do any distinctions from the history of science transfer across—for example, the distinction between a term that failed to refer and a term whose referent turned out to be something else, or between an abandoned posit and a corrected one—and what would those distinctions predict about Holmes? If the analogy is superficial, name something it would predict about the Holmes case that turns out false, and say exactly where the two mechanisms come apart.
