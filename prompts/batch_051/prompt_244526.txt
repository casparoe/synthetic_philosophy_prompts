A popular introduction to animal ethics that I am reading gives the following account of Jeremy Bentham’s famous footnote in chapter 17 of An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789). Bentham is said to argue that animals matter morally because they can suffer, not because they can reason or use language. The fact that an animal cannot enter into legal contracts, reciprocate duties, or claim rights is morally beside the point, since a full-grown horse or dog is still more rational and more capable of communication than a human newborn. On this account, Bentham concludes that animal suffering deserves the same moral consideration as human suffering, and that any attempt to restrict moral status to members of our own species is a groundless prejudice — speciesism, as the author calls it.

I want to use this summary in an essay on whether sentience is sufficient for moral considerability, but I suspect the summary is not entirely faithful. Please audit it against what Bentham actually argues in the footnote and the surrounding passages. Which parts are accurate? Which are subtle overstatements, conflations, or terms Bentham himself would not have accepted? And does the final conclusion that Bentham’s argument refutes species-based restrictions on moral status follow from the accurate parts, or has the author imported something beyond what Bentham says?
