In a reading group on the ethics of belief, one participant repeatedly invoked W. K. Clifford's shipowner: a man who talks himself into believing his vessel is seaworthy, despite reasons for doubt, because a proper inspection would cost money and delay the voyage; the ship sinks, and we are asked to treat his failure as not merely an error but a moral wrong. The participant's further move was to present the case as settling the entire debate, as though any belief formed on insufficient evidence is thereby a moral failing, whatever its content, stakes, or social setting. I should like this audited as an argument by analogy. Could you set out explicitly what is supposed to correspond to what between the shipowner's situation and the general run of ordinary beliefs, where the mapping strains or breaks, and which features of the original case are quietly doing the persuasive work: the scale of the harm, the duty he owes his passengers, the palpable bad faith, the fact that his belief concerns a single verifiable matter of fact? I would then like a judgement on whether the general conclusion that one is never entitled to believe on insufficient evidence survives once those disanalogies are placed on the table.

The reason I hesitate is that the contrary position seems equally dependent on carefully chosen cases. James's reply points to situations in which a belief helps bring about its own truth, and to options that are forced or momentous, where declining to believe until the evidence is conclusive is itself a wager with real costs. I am not asking for a historical survey of the two positions, but for a structural verdict: is the shipowner an intuition pump that merely dramatises a principle which must stand or fall independently, or is it carrying an argumentative weight it cannot bear? If it cannot bear the universal conclusion, say what the case does establish, and what a fairer framing of the question would look like.
