I'm trying to get my head around Ramsey's Dutch book argument. In *Truth and Probability* he says roughly that if a person's degrees of belief don't obey the probability axioms, a clever bookie could set up a bunch of bets that the person regards as fair but that leave the person losing money no matter which way the world turns out. The idea is that the bookie doesn't need to know what will happen, only how to exploit the incoherance in the person's credences.

But here's the part that bugs me. To see that the various bets together guarantee a loss, the bookie—or whoever is constructing the argument—has to know all the logical relations among the propositions. For instance, if I'm considering a massively complex logical formula that happens to be a tautology, but it's far beyond anything I can verify, and I set my credence in it at 0.8, a bookie who somehow knows it's a tautology could sell me a bet at my fair price and clean up. But that bookie knows something I don't. So does the Dutch book argument actually show anything about me being irrational? If it only works when the bookie is logically omniscient and I'm not, why should it place any demands on my beliefs?
