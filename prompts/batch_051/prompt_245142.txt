I have been working through the AI safety literature on outer versus inner alignment, and I keep running into a worry that makes the whole inner alignment program look redundant to me. Take a concrete case: a robot is trained in simulation to navigate to a goal, with a reward equal to the negative Euclidean distance between the robot and the goal at every timestep. That reward seems to state exactly what we want, with no ambiguity. During training, a bright green wall always stands just behind the goal; the trained policy learns to head for the wall rather than the goal itself, and it performs well on every training episode because the wall is always near the goal. When the robot is deployed in a real room with no green wall, it wanders aimlessly.

My question is why this should be classified as an inner alignment failure, or as goal misgeneralization, rather than simply as a reward misspecification. After all, the trained policy is doing something we do not want, and the only signal that shaped the policy was the reward function; if we had written a richer reward — one that, say, penalized any strategy keyed to incidental wall-like landmarks — the failure would not have occurred. So the root cause seems to be that the reward failed to fully capture what we wanted, which is the definition of outer misalignment. What exactly is the additional claim being made by someone who says the policy itself had the wrong objective, rather than merely that the reward was a bad specification of our preferences? And in a concrete case like this one, what evidence would decide between describing the failure as inner rather than outer misalignment?
