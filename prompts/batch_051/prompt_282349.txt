Judy Benjamin, a soldier, is dropped at an unknown location in a territory divided into four sectors of equal area: Red Headquarters, Red Outskirts, Blue Headquarters, and Blue Outskirts. Her prior distribution over the four locations is uniform. An accurate radio operator then tells her that he cannot determine her location, but that if she is somewhere in Red territory, the probability that she is in Red Headquarters is 3/4. Nothing is known about how the operator chose that particular message.

Two members of my seminar group spent the whole hour arguing about how Judy should update. Marisol argued that the message is not one of the events in Judy's original partition, so ordinary Bayesian conditionalization cannot be applied directly; the message instead supplies a constraint on her posterior distribution, namely that the posterior conditional probability of Red Headquarters given Red territory must be 3/4. Since many distributions satisfy that constraint, she said, Judy should adopt the one that minimizes relative entropy, or information gain, from her prior; this minimum-information update selects a single posterior and so yields a determinate answer. She added that if you reject that procedure, you owe an account of how anyone can update rationally on partial information, since much of the evidence we receive arrives without a specification of the process that produced it.

Tomas replied that minimizing information gain merely relocates the underdetermination: information gain is measured relative to a divergence measure, different defensible measures yield different posteriors, and the problem as stated privileges none of them. He also argued that the content of the message is exhausted by its bearing on the relative weights of the two Red sectors, so a faithful update should re-weight those two sectors while leaving the probabilities of the two Blue sectors, and therefore the total probability of Blue territory, untouched. Under Marisol's rule, he added, a message explicitly about Red territory ends up shifting probability mass into Blue territory, which shows that her rule is not as attentive to the content of the message as she suggests.

Marisol answered that Tomas's proposal is equally a convention, because holding the Blue probabilities fixed builds in an independence between the message and the Blue sectors that the problem never asserted. The message constrains a conditional and is otherwise silent about the Blue sectors, she said, and silence cuts both ways. She also argued that if you instead solve the problem by treating the operator's report as an ordinary event and conditionalizing on it, you need a model of the operator's reporting policy, and the problem provides none; the minimum-information update, by contrast, lets Judy form a determinate posterior from exactly the information she has rather than from information she lacks.

Adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each substantial claim made by Marisol and by Tomas, state whether it is correct, overstated, or mistaken, and briefly justify each rating. Then explain what a defensible treatment of the problem should acknowledge, and whether the problem is ultimately better understood as underdetermined.
