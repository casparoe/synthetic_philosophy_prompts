I have been turning over a structural parallel between the precautionary principle in environmental and health policy and the way null-hypothesis significance testing distributes the burden of proof, and I cannot tell whether it is deep or superficial. The precautionary principle's distinctive claim, as I read it, is not merely that we should be careful about serious or irreversible harms, but that uncertainty about such harm should not count as a reason to treat the activity as safe: the default flips, and the proponent of the activity must show safety rather than the regulator having to show danger. This looks to me like exactly the sort of move a significance test makes, only aimed the other way. A test with a null hypothesis takes no effect as the default and caps the probability of a Type I error — asserting an effect when there is none — while the Type II error rate, missing a real effect, is left to fall where the design lets it; that is already a precaution-flavored asymmetry, treating the false positive as the error that needs controlling. So the principle seems reducible to the instruction: for a special class of decisions, swap which hypothesis gets the benefit of the doubt and re-weight the two error types so that missed harms are treated as worse than false alarms. Is that reduction right, or does it omit what is actually doing the work? My worry is that the analogy breaks precisely where the precautionary principle earns its keep: the principle is motivated by irreversibility and by our inability to enumerate the possible outcomes or assign probabilities in advance, whereas a significance test presupposes a well-defined parameter space and a loss structure one can already state; and choosing which error to privilege is a political allocation of risk among different people, not merely an epistemic convention. What I want to know is whether the parallel is deep enough that machinery from statistical decision theory — loss functions, minimax reasoning, admissibility — genuinely clarifies or constrains precautionary reasoning, or whether the two are only loosely alike.
