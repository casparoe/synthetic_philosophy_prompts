I'm a novelist—mostly literary fiction with unlikable narrators, so I have some skin in this game—and I keep running into this debate about whether immoral art can be good art. At a dinner party a while back a very confident critic (or maybe he was a screenwriter? anyway) gave me his clean version of the philosophical lay of the land, and I've been turning it over since. His take was:

The whole question of immoral art basically is the question of whether you can separate the artist from the work. Autonomism says moral and aesthetic evaluation are completely separate spheres, so nothing about a work's moral character—what it depicts, what it seems to endorse, what it makes attractive—can ever count for or against it as art. Moralism is the opposite: a morally flawed work is thereby aesthetically flawed, period, so a novel that, say, makes cruelty look glamorous is worse as a novel because of it, and you'd have to say even something as great as Lolita is aesthetically compromised. And the position he thought was clearly right is immoralism: that moral flaws can make a work better, because transgression and discomfort are part of what art is for, so the very thing moralists condemn is often where the aesthetic power lives.

I remember feeling like the first and last steps were too tidy and the middle felt like a caricature, but I dont have the vocabulary to say exactly why. Is this a fair summary of the actual positions? What would a careful defender of each view—or of some moderate version of it—object to in how he put it? I'm less interested in who's right than in getting clear on what these positions actually claim, because a workshop reader just called my current project "morally queasy" and I want to think about that criticism honestly.
