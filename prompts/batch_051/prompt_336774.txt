I've developed a habit when reading about AI and I genuinely can't tell whether it's a decent epistemic rule or a bias with a respectable vocabulary. Whenever someone reaches for a historical analogy for transformative AI — the Industrial Revolution, nuclear weapons, the printing press, electricity, whatever — I mostly tune out unless they're using the printing press. My reasoning is that the printing press is the only familiar analogy that concerns a technology whose main effect was to decentralise the production of information, rather than to reorganise manual work or create a weapon, and that this is the mechanism that actually transfers to machine intelligence. I've found the Industrial Revolution analogy usually sneaks in a story about labour and capital that doesn't have an obvious counterpart in AI, while the nuclear weapons analogy is really about deterrence between a small number of states and doesn't tell you much about a widely diffused technology. So my policy is: use the printing press as the default analogy, and only admit another one when someone can point to a specific mechanism in it that survives the disanalogies.

Am I fooling myself into a single-lens view of AI? Would I be better off keeping a portfolio of three or four imperfect analogies than one, even if I think the others are bad? What would have to be true for one historical analogy to be genuinely better — in a way I could have stated before working out my own conclusion — rather than just the one that flatters my prefered story? When you answer, please keep apart the historical questions where there's broad agreement from the interpretive questions historians actually dispute, and don't present a contested reading of, say, the printing press or the Industrial Revolution as the consensus.
