I've been keeping a case file on a colleague — call her M — whose research sits right on the problem usually called Buridan's ass: how a rational agent should proceed when no available option is preferred to another. Over the past year I have presented her with a series of vignettes, all involving a choice in which the agent arrives at no preference among the alternatives. Her verdicts have been stable, and I want to predict what she will say about a new case before I bring it to her.

Here are the recorded cases and her verdicts.

1. Two indistinguishable cans of the same soup sit on a supermarket shelf, with no difference that matters to the shopper. M's verdict: take one; the only mistake would be to stand there. Inventing a reason to favour one can would be self-deception, not rationality.

2. A diner regards two dishes on a menu as exactly equal. M's verdict: ask the server to choose, or take the one printed first; the confusion lies in demanding a reason where none exists.

3. A new graduate holds two offers for identical one-year, entry-level roles at indistinguishable firms — same city, same salary, same duties, same culture. She is genuinely indifferent between them. M's verdict: if a decision is needed, flip a coin; but afterwards she should not tell herself a story about why the chosen firm was really the better fit.

4. A postdoc is choosing between a rigorous, incremental research project and a risky, imaginative one. The two draw on different epistemic virtues, and he cannot rank them on a common scale; there is also no fact about which better suits him. M's verdict: this is not a tie. A coin flip would misrepresent the options as equivalent when they are not. What he needs is a further commitment — a supervisor, a bet about the kind of scholar he means to become, a disciplinary identity. If he lacks any such commitment, the indecision is the honest place to stop.

5. An agent must decide whether to put his career on hold to care for an aging parent. The two claims draw on different kinds of value — filial duty and the good of the work — and he judges that neither ranks above the other. M's verdict: the maximizing model and the coin flip are both wrong. The decision must be made out of loyalty or identity; treating it as a pick would be a moral evasion, not a solution.

6. A donor has identified two charities that are equal in cost-effectiveness, track record, and mission. M's verdict: pick one by a deliberately trivial feature — whichever name is shorter, whichever deadline comes first — while refusing to treat that feature as if it had moral weight. A month of agonizing would be self-indulgence, not conscientiousness.

The new case is this. A woman must choose between two grassroots political organizations through which she will pursue a commitment that will structure the next decade of her life. The organizations advocate the same cause with the same methods and effectiveness; their communities are, by stipulation, equally warm and equally suited to her; joining either would shape her habits, friendships, and self-understanding in the same way. After careful reflection she is genuinely indifferent between them, and there is no further dimension along which she can differentiate them. She needs to commit to one this month. Does M say to pick — as in the soup or charity cases — or to refuse to pick until she has a reason, treating arbitrariness itself as the thing to avoid? Predict M's verdict and the reasoning she would most likely give.

Please give me: (i) two or three principles that plausibly fit all six recorded verdicts, stated precisely enough that they yield different predictions; (ii) what each principle predicts for the new case; (iii) your best-guess prediction of M's verdict, defended by identifying the closest analog among the earlier cases and saying which principle M would be adopting either way; and (iv) one further vignette that would most cleanly separate whichever principles remain live, with the prediction each makes about it. I am less interested in the label for M's theory than in the structural distinction — what counts as a reason to pick versus a reason to choose, and where arbitrariness is harmless rather than a failure. No references are needed; I want the reasoning.
