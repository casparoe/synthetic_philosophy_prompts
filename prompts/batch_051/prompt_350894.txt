A friend who took a single causal inference course gave me a rundown, and here's the gist as I remember it (I'm condensing, so don't take this as his exact words). Pearl's idea is that causal reasoning has three levels: association, intervention, and counterfactuals, and you can't in general answer a question at one level using only information from a lower level. The exception is the do-calculus, which is a set of mostly algebraic rules for rewriting a do-probability like P(y | do(x)) into an ordinary conditional probability. As long as you've drawn a DAG and applied the rules mechanically, the do's drop out, and what you're left with is basically the back-door adjustment: you condition on the right covariates, the graph tells you which ones, and after that there's no real difference between the interventional estimate and an observational one. He also said the only real assumption is that your graph is correct, but he waved that off as a technicality you can sort out from the data. His overall take was optimistic: give Pearl a good graph and enough observational data, and causal questions turn out to be answerable after all, so the hierarchy is mostly a pedagogical device for keeping the notation straight. Now, something about this smells like it's flattening the position, but I can't tell where.
