Act as a philosopher of cognitive science, and think this through from first principles rather than reaching for the usual slogans. I want a working definition of "genuinely understands"—the thing Darwin and Turing are sometimes said to have inverted or bypassed. Start by brainstorming two or three candidate definitions before settling on one.

The definition should count as understanding: a kid who adds 27 and 35 and can explain in her own words that carrying the one is just regrouping ten ones; and a bilingual person who reads a newspaper story in one language and explains it in the other. It should not count as understanding: a pocket calculator doing the same addition, and a parrot that says "good morning" whenever a light turns on.

Then use your definition to give hard verdicts on these: (a) a person in a room with a big rulebook who answers written Chinese questions fluently without being able to read Chinese, (b) an LLM that can tutor undergraduates in physics and write novel-looking exam answers, but also hallucinates references, (c) natural selection building a working eye without anything understanding optics, (d) a student who reliably gets right answers by following a procedure but goes blank when asked why it works. If the definition gives the wrong answer or no answer anywhere, revise it or defend the surprising result. And if the question is badly posed—say "understanding" is too mixed a notion for a single definition—tell me that outright instead of playing along.
