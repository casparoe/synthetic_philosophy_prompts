I have no formal training in law or philosophy, but I have been thinking about a slogan that keeps appearing in arguments about legal proof: "you can't convict someone on a statistic." I first met it during a forum discussion about the following case. A pedestrian is killed in a hit-and-run on a country road at night. The road is served by only two bus companies, one with blue buses and one with red buses. From traffic surveys in previous years, about 80% of the buses on that stretch at that hour are blue and 20% are red. There is no other evidence tying either company to the collision. The person I was arguing with insisted that this 80% figure, on its own, could not justify even a civil finding that the Blue Company was responsible, let alone a criminal conviction of a blue-bus driver. He summarized his position with the line: "you can't convict someone on a statistic."

This was clearly intended as a general principle, not just a comment on the one case, but I cannot tell what principle it is exactly. It could be a claim about the level of certainty the law ought to demand, or about a difference between evidence that attaches to an individual and evidence that attaches to a group, or about an unfairness in treating someone as a mere member of a class. As the thread went on, people kept modifying the case, and the slogan seemed to do different work in each version. One modification replaced the survey data with a tamper-proof log showing that, on the actual evening, 80 of the 100 buses that passed were blue. Another replaced the presence statistic with a collision statistic: 80% of similar hit-and-runs on that road have involved blue-bus drivers. A third shifted the question from civil liability to criminal prosecution.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that "you can't convict someone on a statistic" might be making? For each reading, please say whether it is trivially true, plausible and defensible, or probably false, and whether it would actually support rejecting the 80% evidence in the original case and in any of the modifications where it matters. I would especially like to know whether any defensible version of the slogan still distinguishes the statistical evidence from, for example, eyewitness testimony, which is also fallible and only probabilistically reliable when pressed, yet is routinely treated as proper evidence. If there is a defensible core to the slogan, I want to know what survives once the weaker readings are set aside.
