Yesterday evening I had a long argument with a friend about weakness of will. Toward the end she set out the following argument, numbered so that I would have to say exactly where I disagreed. I could not do this to my own satisfaction, and I half-suspect the argument may simply be sound, but the conclusion still bothers me. Tell me whether the argument goes through; if it does not, say which numbered step is the first to fail and why, and whether the argument can be repaired.

(1) A person who acts freely always does what she most prefers at the moment of acting.
(2) The preference that explains a free choice is revealed by the choice: if an uncoerced and informed person chooses X when Y is available, then at that moment she prefers X to Y.
(3) Hyperbolic discounting captures how a person's preferences between two delayed rewards can reverse with the mere passage of time. While both are distant, the larger-later reward may be preferred; once the smaller-sooner reward becomes near, it may be preferred instead, without new information or reflective change of mind.
(4) A typical temptation case has exactly this shape. At 11 p.m., an agent prefers a full night's sleep to watching one more episode, and she judges that sleep is the better option. At 11:40 p.m., with the episode immediate and the lost sleep still in the future, her preference has reversed: she now prefers the episode, though her standing judgment has not changed.
(5) So when she freely chooses the episode, she does what she most prefers at the moment of acting; by (1) she acts in accordance with her own preference, and by (2) that preference is real.
(6) Weakness of will, if it is anything real, would have to be a case in which someone freely acts against what she most prefers at the very moment of acting.
(7) But (1) rules out any such act. Therefore, in cases of this kind, there is no distinct failure of weakness of will: what is called akrasia is only a preference that reverses as the reward approaches, followed later by regret from a longer temporal distance.

My sustained objection was that her standing judgment never changed; she replied that a judgment that does not show up in choice is not the sort of preference that a charge of akrasia could appeal to, so it cannot anchor the idea of acting against oneself. Is her reply enough to keep the argument standing, or does some step fail anyway?
