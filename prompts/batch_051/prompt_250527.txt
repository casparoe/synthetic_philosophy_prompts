i'm a cog sci phd student who splits my time between consciousness and distributed systems, and i've been turning over a parallel i can't tell is deep or dumb. Global workspace theory says a representation only becomes access-conscious when it wins a competition and gets globally broadcast through the workspace, after which the various modules (language, planning, memory, report) can use it; before that it's processed locally, in parallel, with nobody outside knowing. A shared-memory multicore machine has the same shape: cores churn on private values in their local caches, and a write only becomes visible to the other cores when the coherence protocol makes it globally consistent — at that point it's effectively broadcast. Both have local parallel work, a scarce serialized global resource, competition for what gets in, and downstream consumers that can act on the globally visible item without knowing its source.

The twist is access vs phenomenal consciousness. GWT is a theory of access consciousness. If access consciousness is the coherence part — global broadcast — then phenomenal character has to map to the private local state before broadcast, the cached value itself. But a cached value is still a functional, third-person-describable state; there is nothing it is like to be a cache line. So the analogy either says phenomenal consciousness is just another functional private state, or it only model access consciousness and breaks exactly at the hard problem.

Is this a genuine structural identity (at least for access consciousness), a loose metaphor, or a confusion? What would need to be true of both sides for anything to transfer — say, from memory-consistency or cache-invalidation ideas to predictions about when people's reports are unreliable? And what does the parallel predict that turns out false?
