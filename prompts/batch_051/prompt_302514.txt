A colleague who works in Bayesian decision theory has been arguing that there is never a rational justification for willful ignorance, and I want you to help me locate the mistake. I'll reconstruct his argument.

(1) Suppose an agent faces a decision under uncertainty and is offered information relevant to that decision, where the information is cost-free in the ordinary sense: acquiring it consumes no money, time, or effort worth mentioning. (2) After receiving the information, the agent may either act on it or do exactly what she would have done without it; having looked does not force her to change anything. (3) If the information would lead her to change her action, the change cannot be for the worse: conditional on what she has learned, the new action maximizes expected value, and the old action is just another available option, so following the information can only improve her expected outcome. (4) If the information would not lead her to change her action, then she is no worse off for having looked, since she paid nothing for it. (5) Therefore, for any cost-free and decision-relevant information, there can be no rational preference for refusing it over receiving it; at most an agent can be indifferent. Call this the value-of-information principle. (6) But this principle seems to prove too much. It entails that a patient cannot rationally prefer to decline a free genetic test for an untreatable, late-onset disease even if the knowledge would poison her remaining years; that a writer cannot rationally prefer not to learn now whether her novel will ever be published even when the uncertainty is what keeps her writing; and that a spouse cannot rationally prefer not to look through a partner's phone even when what she would find would destroy a marriage that would otherwise have healed. (7) Each of these looks like a case in which a reasonable agent could rationally prefer to remain ignorant, so at least one step above must be false.

I would like to keep the broadly Bayesian picture according to which practical rationality consists in maximizing expected value, so I do not want the diagnosis to be simply that expected-value reasoning is invalid. Something else has to give. Please identify which premise or inferential step fails; explain why that step initially looked forced; and say what a Bayesian must give up or reinterpret in order to block the conclusion without declaring the three cases in step (6) irrational by fiat. In particular, say which of the following the diagnosis pushes us toward and what each repair would cost: (a) allowing utility to be defined over beliefs and other mental states, not only over overt actions and objective outcomes; (b) denying that these pieces of information are "cost-free" in the sense the argument needs; (c) restricting the value-of-information principle to an agent's preferences over strategies rather than her all-things-considered preference; or (d) some other repair.
