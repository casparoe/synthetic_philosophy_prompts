I spent most of my career as a working chemist — physical chemistry, mostly — and since I retired I've been reading philosophy, first philosophy of science and now the ethics of technology. Lately I've gotten a bit stuck on the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness: the ones showing that a risk score generally cannot satisfy calibration and equalized odds (or equal false positive rates, or several of the other pairings people actually want) all at the same time unless base rates are equal across groups, or the classifier is trivial in some way. I can follow the math, more or less.

My view now is that these results should be read like no-go theorems in physics. They aren't just practical constraints saying "you can't have all of these nice things at once, so bargain over them." They're telling us that the whole project of formalizing fairness as a set of group-level statistical constraints is ill-posed — that fairness isn't the kind of thing that decomposes into those criteria in the first place, and the contradictions are the math's way of saying we carved the concept at the wrong joints. I know plenty of fair-ML people don't read them that way; they treat the results as a menu of tradeoffs to be negotiated case by case.

What I'd like from you: what would count against my reading? I want the actual evidence, formal results, or arguments that should move me, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. For each, say whether it hits only the inflated version of my position or the core of it, and whether someone defending my view would concede it as a genuine cost or brush it off. I'd also like you to be honest about whether my position risks anything at all — for example, whether I've insulated it by saying that any conflict among metrics just shows, again, that the formalization was wrong. For a guy who loves no-go theorems, I should probably worry that I'm to comfortable treating every impossibility result as deep, when some of them are just artefacts of a particular formal setup. What would it take for me to downgrade the claim from "fairness can't be captured by group-level statistical constraints" to something more modest like "this particular package of constraints is internally inconsistent, and that's all"?
