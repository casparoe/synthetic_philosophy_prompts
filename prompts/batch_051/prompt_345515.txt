Something has bothered me about a famous puzzle. A fair coin is flipped until it comes up heads, and the payout doubles with each flip: $2 if heads comes first, $4 if it comes second, $8 if it comes third, and so on. The expected value is infinite, yet almost nobody would pay a large sum to play. I understand the standard responses: money has diminishing marginal value, no bank could cover the largest prizes, a person's utility must have an upper bound, or the principle of maximizing expected value simply fails for gambles like this. What I cannot figure out is why the argument never seems to end. My working theory is that the disagreement is only partly about mathematics or about how people actually choose; underneath it, the different sides are doing different jobs under the same label "rational choice"—one is repairing a formal theory, another is describing real human preferences, another is legislating how an ideal agent ought to act. Is that diagnosis right, or is the disagreement more substantive than that? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a survey of proposed solutions, and an honest account of what kind of progress—a theorem, a conceptual distinction, a change of question—could actually settle it.
