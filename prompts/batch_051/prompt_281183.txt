In my graduate seminar on the philosophy of computation, a student pressed me on a distinction I had drawn between finding a solution and checking one, and I have been unable to give the question the clean treatment it deserves. Consider an asymmetry on which our intellectual practices seem to run. A mathematician spends years searching for a proof; a referee spends an afternoon checking the resulting proof. A Sudoku champion spends an hour filling an empty grid; a spectator glances at the completed grid and confirms in seconds that it is correct. We honor the finder, not the checker: we award the prize to the prover rather than the referee, and we regard the solver's act as creative while the verifier's act is merely competent. Finding and checking feel separated by a difference in kind. Yet our best formal expression of that gap is the conjecture that P ≠ NP — the claim that the class of problems whose candidate solutions can be verified in polynomial time (NP) is strictly larger than the class of problems that can be solved from scratch in polynomial time (P). That conjecture is unproved. If P = NP, then for a vast range of well-defined search problems there would be no complexity-theoretic barrier separating the work of producing a solution from the work of verifying one.

I want you to act as a professional philosopher of mathematics and computer science and to think slowly through this. Before committing, briefly canvass the strongest candidate grounds for the asymmetry — the P ≠ NP conjecture, the order of discovery in time, the phenomenology of insight, the social function of credit and priority, or anything else you find. Then make the best case for and against the claim that our honoring of finders over checkers is rationally hostage to P ≠ NP. Test your favored principle against nearby cases: an automated theorem prover that finds proofs without understanding, a verifier who checks a proof and experiences the same flash of insight as its author. Finally, give a clear verdict: is our practice of treating finding and checking as different in kind defensible as it stands, is it a wager on an unproved inequality that we are nonetheless entitled to make, or should it be revised or abandoned?
