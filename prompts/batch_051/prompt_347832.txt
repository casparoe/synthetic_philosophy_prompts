I've been reading Berkeley's Querist and a line has got stuck in my head. He writes:

"Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?"

I'm coming around to the view he seems to be pushing: money isn't wealth; it's a ticket or counter for reckoning and transferring claims on the fruits of industry, so what matters for national prosperity is keeping industry employed and credit circulating, not piling up gold. The really ambitious bit is that you could in principle keep the denominations and lose the bullion, and commerce would still go on, because the tickets are doing the real work.

What would count against this? I don't mean just "what do modern economists say about fiat money," but given a defence of the ticket/counter view from the passage, what evidence, historical cases, or arguments should move me away from it, and which are only awkward? I'd like defeaters sorted from fatal to merely embarrassing. For each one, say whether a Berkeleyan could swallow it by qualifying the view—e.g. by saying the tickets need a credible issuer or a legal backing—and whether that qualification eventually turns the view into a tautology or a different position. I'd also like a definite verdict at the end: is the view actually exposed, or is it insulated enough that it can't be falsified by any normal monetary event? Keep it under 900 words, not counting the Berkeley passage, and write in British English. Also, no hedging: I want you to take a position on which two or three defeaters are the real killers, not just lay out a menu.
