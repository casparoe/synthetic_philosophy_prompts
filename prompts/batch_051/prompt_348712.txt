I'm a software engineer (mostly backend) who got into AI alignment through LessWrong and arguments at work, so I don't have formal philosophy training — which might be exactly the problem here. There's one dispute I've been stuck in for literally years, with a coworker and with whole sub-communities, and it never moves. I don't want you to declare a winner; I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is.

The dispute is whether a sufficiently advanced AI would "necessarily" converge on some particular set of values or final goals. My coworker's side: instrumental convergence already implies that any smart enough system will in practice want self-preservation, resources, better models of the world, and so on, because those help for almost any final goal; and beyond that, a system that gets better at reasoning about its own goals would eventually notice when a goal is arbitrary or incoherent and revise it, so in the limit their won't be much room for truly arbitrary final goals. My side: this confuses means and ends. I hold something like the orthogonality thesis — final goals and optimization power are orthogonal axes, so superhuman capability doesn't narrow down what the system ultimately wants; a superintelligent paperclip maximizer isn't a contradiction. We both point to the same ML-era evidence (RLHF'd models, reward hacking, specification gaming), but it cuts in opposite directions for each of us.

My own hunch is that we're at least partly using "intelligence" and "rationality" in different senses. As an engineer, I basically mean optimization power: how good the system is at steering the world into states it ranks highly. My coworker means something thicker, like reasonableness — being responsive to reasons in a way that itself constrains what you could end up wanting. But I'm not sure that's the whole story, and I'd want you to pressure-test that hypothesis rather than just accept it.

Act as an AI alignment researcher who is also a professional philosopher of mind and language. Think carefully and maybe begin by brainstorming candidate diagnoses: a purely verbal dispute, a real factual question about which mind designs are physically or computationally possible, a difference in bedrock intuitions about what counts as a "goal" or a "reason," two different questions being answered under one name, or an ill-posed question. Then give your actual diagnosis, tied to what each side concretely says, and tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a formal result, a cleaner distinction, an experiment, a change of question — or argue that we should expect no progress and why. If my two-senses-of-rationality hypothesis is off, say so.
