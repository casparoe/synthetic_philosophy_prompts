After reading too much about the situationist critique of virtue ethics I adopted a rule: if I see someone do something in one setting, I don't treat it as evidence about their character. At all. A friend is rude to a waiter — null result on whether he's a rude person. A coworker lies in a meeting to cover up his own mistake — still basically null; my partner says at that point I'm not a situationist, I'm just refusing evidence. The experiments are what sold me: seminary students in a hurry stepping over someone slumped in a doorway, ordinary people following orders to shock a stranger, small features of the setup flipping whether people help or don't. Whatever the exact takeaway for virtue ethics is, the practical lesson I drew was to read one-off behavior as having about zero evidential value for stable dispositions.

But I can feel the rule biting, because the "pattern" that's supposed to count as evidence never gets to form — I discount each instance before a pattern can accumulate. Is this a defensible updating policy, or have I overcorrected from "he did X, so he is T" to "behavior is no evidence about character at all"? What would a fixed version of the rule look like: when should a single act move me, and how much, and what would I need to know first (base rates, how strong and how rare the situation was, how the person contrued it, what they'd likely do under other pressures)? And how do I distinguish the real lesson of the situationist critique from the lazy version where I use it to never blame anyone and never revise my view of my friends?
