I'm writing up a view about whether anything is at stake in how we label the roots of a polynomial, and I'd like a consistency check on the commitments I've settled into before I present them. The running example is the two square roots of −1; I'll use i and −i the way ordinary convention does, though part of what I'm asking is how much that convention can amount to.

Here is where I currently stand.

1. The equation x² + 1 = 0 has two distinct solutions in the complex numbers, and these are two mathematical objects, not one object with two names.
2. The roots are nevertheless algebraically symmetric: every predicate definable in the language of fields, even with the real numbers as parameters, that is true of one root is true of the other; complex conjugation fixes the real numbers and swaps the roots while preserving the field structure.
3. So I regard the question "which of the two roots is i, really?" as a pseudo-question; there is no further fact, beyond the structural facts, about which root deserves the symbol.
4. But I also think there is something right in the usual set-theoretic construction of the complex numbers: each root is a distinct set (or ordered pair), and that set-theoretic distinctness is what grounds their being two objects rather than one.
5. However, I hold that set-theoretic reductions cannot tell us what mathematical objects are, since there are many equally good candidate reductions and no canonical reason to prefer one.
6. I am a structuralist about mathematical objects: they have no non-structural properties, and a mathematical object's identity is exhausted by its place in a structure.
7. I also want to keep the usual convention that i denotes a definite one of the two roots (the principal square root), and I think the convention does real mathematical work and is not merely cosmetic notation.

I can feel that some of these come apart, but I may be wrong about which ones and how badly. Please audit the set: tell me which commitments pull against one another, which pairs only appear to conflict, and order the findings by severity. For each, say specifically what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be.

Format: a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Put the most severe genuine tension first, include at least one pair you take to dissolve on inspection, and refer to the commitments by number.
