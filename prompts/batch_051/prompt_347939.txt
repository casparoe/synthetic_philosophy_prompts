After a long career in the natural sciences, much of it spent making quantitative judgments about individual events — whether a particular instrument would fail, whether a particular population would persist, whether a particular floodplain was safe — I have turned to philosophy in retirement, and the problem that now seems to me central is the reference class problem. When one says that this patient's tumor has a certain five-year survival probability, or that this site has a certain annual inundation risk, one has silently chosen a class of comparable cases: tumors of that histology and stage, basins with that climate and substrate. But indefinitely many classes contain the case, and the probabilities they support differ. The mathematics does not choose among them, yet a single-case probability is relative to a class if it is relative to anything.

Before I read another proposed solution, I want the specification. What must a theory of reference-class selection actually do to count as adequate? I mean an account of how a particular case is assigned to the class or classes that fix its probability, or of why no such assignment is needed. Take adequacy to be relative to working scientific inference, in which a probability has to carry the weight of a decision about diagnosis, hazard, regulation, or forecast, not merely summarize a dataset.

Among the candidate requirements I expect you to examine, with the consideration behind each:

- No gerrymandered classes. A rule must not permit someone to engineer, after the outcome is known, a class whose frequency yields whatever number was wanted in advance, or a class built from enough irrelevant conjuncts that it fits the data by construction.

- A solution to the grain problem. The finest class for an individual case is very often a singleton, and a singleton sustains no usable frequency; the broadest class is uninformative in the other direction. The theory must say which level of grain is right, by what principle, and why the choice is not arbitrary.

- Homogeneity in the right respect. A reference class must group cases that are alike in a way that matters to the probability, not merely alike in some recorded variables and not merely alike in observed outcome. The theory must distinguish causal homogeneity, statistical homogeneity, and the merely coincidental similarity that sampling noise produces.

- An account of stratification and adjustment. Ordinary scientific practice conditions on some variables and ignores others, and sometimes adding a variable reverses the apparent association. A theory of reference-class selection should explain what that practice is doing, and whether it is silently importing a causal model while presenting itself as purely statistical.

- An account of the narrowest-reliable-class rule. The familiar suggestion is to condition on the narrowest class for which reliable statistics obtain. That pushes the question back: reliable by what criterion — sample size, stability, independence, something else — and what is the assignment supposed to be when the narrowest class falls below the threshold?

- Consistency with the principle of total evidence. An assignment should reflect all relevant available information, but taking that principle literally seems to drive one toward an ever more specific class without a stopping point. The theory must explain why respecting total evidence does not always force the singleton.

- Something to say about singular cases. Some events are close to unique in any class we know how to construct: the first flight of a new vehicle, a patient under a novel treatment, a one-off decision. The theory must either explain how a reference class is built from similarity in such cases or say plainly that no objective single-case probability exists there, and with what consequences for decision.

- A non-circularity constraint. The correct class cannot be defined as the class of events that have the same probability, or as the class generated by the same chance set-up, unless sameness of set-up is specified independently of the chances it is meant to explain.

For each candidate, say whether it is a genuine requirement, a requirement only for scientific use, a preference of one school, or a mistake; mark the non-negotiable ones. Then say which pairs cannot be jointly satisfied. I suspect two conflicts and want them confirmed or refuted: maximal specificity against the availability of stable frequencies, and the demand that classes follow causes against the demand that they be fixed by outcome data alone.

Finally, and briefly, since the criteria are the main event, score the standard families of answer against the list — frequentist, propensity, subjective Bayesian, and any natural-kind or causal account — and tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude each.
