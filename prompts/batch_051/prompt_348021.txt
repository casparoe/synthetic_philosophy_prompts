Here is the passage from the letter sent to each of the twenty participants in Douglas Hofstadter’s Platonia Dilemma:

“If you wish, you may send a telegram with just your name on it to the Platonia Institute in downtown Frogville, Oklahoma (pop. 2). You may reverse the charges. If you reply within 48 hours, the billion is yours—unless there are two or more replies, in which case the prize is awarded to no one. And if no one replies, nothing will be awarded to anyone.”

Now suppose that after the 48-hour deadline has passed, the Institute discovers that exactly one telegram arrived within the window: it contains the name of a participant who had decided not to reply, and it was sent by that participant’s estranged brother, who knew of the offer but did not know the participant’s decision. The brother used his own money to pay for the telegram, did not reverse charges, and forged the participant’s name. The participant had not authorized it, and neither the participant nor the brother had contacted any other player. The other nineteen players sent nothing.

The passage was written before such a case was contemplated. Work out what the rules require or permit here. In particular: Does the forged telegram count as a “reply”? If so, whose reply is it? If not, does the clause “if no one replies, nothing will be awarded to anyone” produce a different outcome? Address three interpretive approaches: the plain text of the rules, the purpose of the dilemma as a test of superrationality, and what the designers would have said had they been asked. Defend one of these approaches as the correct one, and explain what it implies about whether the participant, the brother, or no one wins.

The answer should be written in British English.
