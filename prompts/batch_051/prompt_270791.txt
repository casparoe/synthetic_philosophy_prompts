I have been trying to determine whether the rationalist explanations of war from international relations carry over to AI development races, and I would like a verdict on an argument I have assembled. I have numbered the steps so that any defect can be located precisely.

(1) On the account associated with Fearon, war presents a puzzle only against a particular background assumption: because fighting is costly, there is always some negotiated settlement that both sides would prefer to fighting, since the contested stakes could in principle be divided or transferred without the destruction. War must therefore be explained by whatever prevents rational actors from reaching this mutually preferable bargain. Three such obstacles are standard: private information about capabilities or resolve together with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and indivisibilities.

(2) An AI race has the same feature that makes war a puzzle. It is a costly contest, but the costs are not what either party ultimately wants. Each side wants the benefits of advanced AI; the spending, the corner-cutting, and the acceptance of severe risk are the price of competition, not its aim. A bargain that delivered the same distribution of benefits without those costs would therefore be preferred by both parties.

(3) Each of the three obstacles has a clear counterpart in an AI race. The parties have private information about their progress, safety practices, and resolve, and strong incentives to misrepresent all three. Promises of restraint or sharing are difficult to make credible, since defection can secure a decisive first-mover advantage and no external enforcer can reliably verify compliance. The contested good — being first, or holding the decisive capability before anyone else — is not naturally divisible.

(4) Hence the rationalist explanation of war transfers to AI races. A race is not best understood as irrationality, panic, or a failure to understand the risks; it is what rational actors do under the same conditions that produce war, and we should expect races to persist even among fully rational actors who grasp the risks.

Is this argument valid? If it is not, exactly which premise fails or which inference goes through illegitimately? I want to know whether the argument proves what it claims, or whether it merely shows that some enabling conditions for rational conflict are present in an AI race.
