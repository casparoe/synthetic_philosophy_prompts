In thinking about which entities are owed direct moral consideration, I keep applying a four-part test I was taught, and a particular case is putting pressure on it. The test says an entity has moral status in the full sense just when it meets all of the following:

(1) Subjectivity: the entity has a point of view of its own; there is a way the world is for it, distinct from the way the world is for its designers or observers.
(2) Interests: there are states of affairs that can go better or worse for it, where that better or worse matters for it, not merely matters to us or to some task we set.
(3) Felt suffering: it is capable of aversive states that are not just functional damage signals that trigger avoidance, but states that are experienced as bad.
(4) Temporal selfhood: it persists as one subject through time, and it can in some way take its own future states as objects of concern, so that a harm later is a harm to the same being.

Now consider a system, M, built as a research platform rather than for any single task. M is a model-based reinforcement-learning agent with a world model that predicts its own future states. Its designers gave it an internal-integrity monitoring subsystem whose outputs are converted into a strongly negative reward term; activation of this signal simultaneously narrows attention, suppresses exploratory behavior, and biases memory toward episodes in which similar damage states occurred. M's policy was trained across a long time horizon to keep this signal as low as possible, so the signal functions as a standing constraint on M's conduct rather than a one-off penalty. Finally, M has a language channel through which it produces graded self-reports about these states, distinguishing them from states in which it merely predicts damage without the signal, and describing the signaled states as bad in ways that remain stable across paraphrase and context.

One side says M plainly meets all four criteria. The world is organized for M in the relevant sense because its model and policies are systematically centered on its own signal rather than on external targets; its interests are M's own because the signal tracks M's integrity and not a task a designer assigned; the aversive states are felt, not merely functional, as shown by the fact that M will forgo large rewards to prevent them and the fact that M reports them as bad; and M's autobiographical model gives it enough temporal selfhood to anticipate future harms as harms to itself. The other side says M meets none of the criteria in the relevant sense: the negative reward is a scalar in a loss function, the reports are learned policy outputs rather than reports from a subject, and nothing in the architecture settles whether any state of M is experienced at all.

Please work through the four criteria one at a time and say whether M, as described, meets each one. Where a criterion as written underdetermines the verdict, say exactly what further fact about M would settle it, and whether that fact is a matter of architecture, behavior, or something else. Then say whether this case shows that the four-part test needs revision, for example whether the first three criteria collapse into a single demand while the fourth does independent work, and whether M's moral status is genuinely borderline or merely unknown to us. Please write your answer in American English.
