You are a professional philosopher working at the intersection of moral philosophy and the philosophy of language, with a special interest in the ethics of communication. Please think through the following question slowly and carefully. Before settling on an answer, make explicit the assumptions that the question carries, and test them one by one; if you find a false presupposition, state it clearly and then answer the corrected version of the question.

A minister appears before a televised parliamentary inquiry. The chair asks: "Did your office authorize any payments to companies owned by your brother?" The minister answers: "My office never authorized any payments to companies owned by my brother out of the public infrastructure budget." The statement is true: the payments under investigation came from the discretionary contingency fund, not from the infrastructure budget. The minister knows that the committee's real concern is whether any public money reached his brother's companies at all, and he knows its members will take his answer as a general denial — a denial that is false. He has been scrupulous to utter no falsehood.

Has the minister done anything morally wrong? After all, every sentence he uttered is true, so he did not lie. If the committee reads a false generalization into his true words, that inference is the committee's own doing, and a speaker cannot be held responsible for what other people infer. And even if his reply is called "misleading," to treat that as morally on a par with lying is to erase the difference between saying something false and merely failing to volunteer information. What, if anything, has the minister done wrong, and why?
