The dispute is whether a translated sacred text can itself be inspired and authoritative. Use only the descriptions I give here, and take these three positions as the objects to translate.

(1) Autographs-only. The original-language texts are the inspired source. Translations are human derivatives: they can convey meaning accurately, but they have no independent authority, and their correctness is always measured against the source.

(2) Septuagint tradition. The Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures produced in Hellenistic Jewish communities came to be treated by some communities as itself divinely superintended. Certain accounts of its production claimed that seventy or seventy-two translators, working separately, produced renderings that agreed to an extent that later readers took as a sign of divine guidance. On this view, the translation shared in the authority of the source instead of merely approximating it.

(3) KJV-onlyism. A particular early modern English translation is the providentially preserved and inspired text for English-speaking Christians. For that linguistic community it functions as the authoritative text in its own right, rather than as a descendant artifact that borrows authority by pointing back to the originals.
