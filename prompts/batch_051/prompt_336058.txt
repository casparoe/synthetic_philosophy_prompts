Ok, so I'm in a climate-econ working group and we've now spent three straight sessions fighting about discounting the future. I have to present in two weeks, and I wrote down where I've landed so someone can give it a consistency check before I do. I don't need a reading list; I need someone to audit the commitments, and I'd like the answer in American English.

(1) I reject pure time preference. A unit of well-being in 2150 matters exactly as much as the same unit of well-being today; the calendar date doesn't do any moral work by itself.

(2) But I still think it's fine to use a positive discount rate on consumption in cost-benefit analysis — 3% or whatever — as long as the rate is justified entirely by expected growth in per-capita consumption plus diminishing marginal utility of consumption, and not by the idea that later utility is worth less.

(3) I think present-bias is a rational defect, not just a different taste. Someone with hyperbolic discounting flips their own plan as the smaller, sooner reward gets closer, and I'd call that incoherent rather than merely impatient.

(4) At the same time, I have a pretty strong democratic instinct that actual public-sector discount rates — the ones a treasury or a climate agency uses — should be answerable to real people's revealed time preferences: market interest rates, surveys about how people trade now against later, that kind of thing. Imposing the philosopher's zero rate on everyone feels paternalistic, like treating citizens as if they don't know their own values.

So here's the task. Go through the six pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equal. For each real tension, say what would have to give and roughly what it would cost. Then tell me which single revision would leave the overall package most coherent.
