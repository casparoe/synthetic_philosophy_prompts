I am a journalist preparing a longform piece on how broad empirical generalizations earn their authority, and I keep returning to Carl Hempel's ravens paradox as the sharpest way into the problem. The position I find myself provisionally persuaded by is the standard one: confirmation is a matter of degree rather than all-or-nothing, so the observation of a non-black non-raven — a white shoe, a red apple — does confirm the hypothesis that all ravens are black, because it rules out one way the hypothesis could have been false. But it confirms it only to a negligible degree, since non-black non-ravens vastly outnumber ravens and almost none of them were ever serious candidates for being counterexamples to the generalization; a single black raven confirms the generalization far more substantially. On this view the paradox is not a genuine conflict but a lesson about confusing "confirms at all" with "confirms to any interesting degree," and the logical equivalence between "all ravens are black" and "all non-black things are non-ravens" is accepted as a trivial fact one should not find alarming.

I would like to know what would count against this position. If it is false, what would the world have to look like? I want the considerations ranked from decisive to merely awkward: formal results showing that the degree of confirmation assigned to a red apple depends on an arbitrary measure over an unbounded hypothesis space; cases in which the same reasoning licenses "confirmation" of gerrymandered hypotheses that no one would accept and that cannot be blocked except by ad hoc constraints; evidence that rational agents do not, and perhaps should not, treat logically equivalent hypotheses as equally confirmed; and any finding suggesting that the notion of "negligible confirmation" is doing more argumentative work than the mathematics can bear. I also want an honest assessment of whether the view has been insulated by qualifications — whether "negligible" can always be recalibrated to absorb whatever counterintuitive case arises, and whether that flexibility is a defect in a substantive claim about confirmation or a reasonable feature of a theory of graded evidence. What does the standard view predict that its rivals do not, where would those predictions actually show up, and which of the possible defeaters should I treat as noise rather than as a reason to abandon it?
