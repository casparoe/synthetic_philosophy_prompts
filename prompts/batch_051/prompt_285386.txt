I've been arguing with a friend about whether an infinite chain of justifications is actually a fatal problem for knowledge, or whether you could just accept that every belief is justified by another belief and call it a day. He insists that any endless regress is automatically vicious. I keep wanting to say some regresses are harmless — like when you keep asking what makes something true and the answer is just the same truth again — and that the real difference has something to do with whether the chain has to "get going" before anything counts, but I cant make that precise. We even invented a toy case where a committee says a rule is valid because a higher rule says so, and that higher rule is valid because a still higher rule says so, with no bottom; I wanted to say that one is different from the truth case, but I couldn't say how.

So here's the task: write an essay on the question of when an infinite regress is vicious rather than benign. Set up at least two regresses of different kinds — one epistemological like justification, and one metaphysical or semantic like truth, or the relation between a thing and its property — and use the contrast to motivate a criterion that actually separates them. Then apply your criterion to a new or made-up case where the verdict isn't obvious, and use the criterion to decide it. Reach and defend a conclusion; don't end on "it depends" unless you spell out exactly what it depends on and why that matters.
