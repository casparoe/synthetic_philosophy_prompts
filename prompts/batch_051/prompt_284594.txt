Here's one I've been turning over. Hume died in 1776, so he never got to see modern medical aid-in-dying laws. His own view, roughly, was that suicide is not a crime against God, against one's neighbors, or against oneself: our obligations to society are reciprocal and lapse when we leave it, and a life that has become a burden is not one a person is obliged to go on living. Now take the standard Oregon-style law the modern debate usually centers on: you need a terminal disease with a prognosis of six months or less, and two doctors have to confirm you're competent and asking voluntarily. If Hume were dropped into today's policy fight, what do you think he'd say about those safeguards in particular? Wich of them would he accept as a reasonable check on abuse, and which would he see as arbitrary medical gatekeeping that misses the cases he actually cared about (or something else, your call)? Play his principles forward onto a law he never saw; don't just summarize the essay. Start with a one-sentence bottom line giving your best guess at his overall verdict, then unpack it in at most 200 words.
