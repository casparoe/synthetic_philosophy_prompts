I keep landing on the same argument with a colleague who works on free will, and I'd like you to properly audit the analogy he won't stop using. It's the old Sphex wasp story. The female wasp stings a cricket, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it outside while she goes in to check the burrow, comes back out, and drags it inside. If you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she's inside, she'll drag it back to the entrance, go in to check all over again, and keep looping instead of just pulling the cricket in. His claim is that our allegedly flexible deliberation is mostly sphexishness at higher resolution: we run fixed subroutines triggered by cues, and we mistake the number and nesting of those subroutines for genuine flexability — the kind free will is supposed to need. On his telling, 'free will' is a flattering label we stick on a very complicated wasp.

I want an audit, not a scorecard. Give me the correspondence table: what in human action is supposed to play the role of the burrow-check, the cricket, the trigger, the loop, the wasp's obliviousness to the moved cricket. Then show me where the mapping breaks, and sort the disanalogies into the ones that matter for his conclusion and the ones that are just cosmetic. In particular, tell me what the analogy quietly imports from the wasp — rigid modularity, no error correction, no grasp of the goal as a goal, no ability to stop mid-routine — and whether human action actually lacks those things. Then say whether his definationary conclusion survives the audit or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time, and what a better analogy would be if this one is bad. Answer in American English, please.
