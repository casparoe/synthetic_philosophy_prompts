I'm a PhD student working on AI alignment, and I keep chewing on an argument that shows up all over the older safety writing. The rough idea is that once a system is superhumanly persuasive, you shouldn't let it argue with you at all: any argument it produces is optimized for what you'll find convincing, so by the time it sounds good to you you've already lost—it's just a better model of you steering you by the mouth. The usual conclusion is that we shouldn't let such systems argue for their own goals, especially around shutdown. But that argument was built for an agent with its own goals, strategically choosing what to say. The systems we actually have are trained mostly with RLHF to say what humans approve of, and the empirical picture of their persuasiveness so far is strange: people seem to be moved by fluency, confidence, and flattery, and the models are sycophantic rather than strategic. Does the old argument survive this development? Or does it flip into a different worry—not "the AI out-argues us" but "it flatters us into doing what we wanted to hear anyway"? If some version survives, which premise is actually doing the work, and is there a weaker repaired version worth keeping?
