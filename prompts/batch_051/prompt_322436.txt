I am an undergraduate art history student currently taking a philosophy of art course that ends with a unit on cultural property and cultural appropriation, and I am also the student member of my city's arts council, which awards grants for public art. The council meets monthly and reviews around a dozen proposals in a single evening, with about fifteen minutes per proposal; the panelists are working artists, community representatives, and one city councilor, none of whom have a background in philosophy, and every award spends public money and can draw press coverage and community protest. The panel's vote decides, but the executive director must approve a written justification for any grant. We repeatedly stall on one kind of case: a proposal that uses forms, stories, styles, imagery, or music from a culture the artist does not belong to. The discussion collapses into "this is cultural appropriation, reject it" versus "art has always borrowed, approve it," and we have no shared procedure for getting past those slogans.

The course has argued that cultural appropriation is not one wrong but several, and it distinguishes at least three: (1) misrepresentation that damages a group's standing or credibility; (2) violation of a group's legitimate authority over sacred, secret, or economically important heritage; and (3) a transfer of benefits in which the outsider captures the rewards — money, prestige, a career — while the community bears the costs or risks. It has also convinced me that neither slogan, "a culture literally owns its styles" or "everyone borrows from everyone," is an acceptable stopping point. My supervisor has asked me to turn this into a reusable decision procedure the panel can run on a proposal before discussion begins. I would like it written as a checklist of questions to ask in order, with a clear statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, explicit points where the procedure stops with a "no," and explicit flags where the remaining question is one of values rather than facts and the full panel must decide. The procedure must keep what the theory actually says while remaining usable by people who will never read the theory, and it should be honest about where it will misfire or leave a case unsettled.

Then run the procedure on three proposals, showing the questions the panel would ask and the answer the procedure returns: (a) a non-Indigenous composer who incorporates a melody an Indigenous community identifies as sacred, with no consultation and no plan to share royalties; (b) a white American novelist who writes in the voice of a Mexican-American character but has worked with community readers and will credit them; and (c) a public mural by an artist from outside a neighborhood who uses that neighborhood's own historical imagery, saying the work is meant to honor it, but without any local consultation. Please make at least one of the three a case where the procedure returns an answer the panel will not like, and conclude by stating explicitly what the procedure cannot do — which disputes it will not settle and where the panel is on its own.
