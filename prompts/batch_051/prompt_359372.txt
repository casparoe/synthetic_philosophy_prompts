Our tenant union — about forty households — is rewriting its bylaws, and the last two meetings got eaten alive by the voting-method fight. For years we did the obvious thing: somebody moves a proposal, people amend it, majority vote on whatever survives. But the same five people keep showing that the outcome flips depending on the order the proposals get voted on, and once you see it you can't unsee it. So now we're down the rabbit hole of ranked choice, approval voting, Condorcet methods, the works. What's driving me up the wall is that each advocate has exactly one principal guiding them — "must never reward strategic voting," or "has to use everyone's full ranking," or "has to pick the Condorcet winner when there is one" — and they just assert the other constraints don't matter. I don't want to adopt a method because its champion argues loudest.

So before I commit to anything: lay out the desiderata a fair rule for turning individual rankings into a group decision has to satisfy. For each one, give the small case or scenario that shows why it matters, and mark whether it's a non-negotiable requirement or just a wish of a particular camp. Then give me the part I actually need: which subsets of these desiderata can't all be satisfied at once — I know there's an impossibility result in the neighborhood, Arrow if I remember right, but I don't trust my memory of exactly which conditions collide — and which single requirement, if you drop or weaken it, opens up the most room for the methods people really use. Keep the focus on the criteria themselves; don't score a bunch of named voting rules at the end, I'll do that myself.

Format it in these sections:

1. The Requirements — the full list, each with the motivating case; a table is fine; roughly 500 words.
2. Non-Negotiable vs. Camp Preferences — which entries are genuinely mandatory and which are parochial; roughly 200 words.
3. Where the List Breaks — the jointly unsatisfiable subsets, with a plain-language explanation of why they collide; roughly 350 words.
4. The Lever Requirement — the one requirement whose removal does the most work, and what becomes available once it's relaxed; roughly 150 words.
