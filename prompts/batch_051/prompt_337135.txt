I've been editing Wikipedia on and off for a couple of years, and the more time I spend on talk pages the more I think the site has a whole theory of knowledge buried inside its rules, and I can't tell whether the people who enforce them would ever put it that way. The rule, as every experienced editor explains it to newcomers, is that you don't add a claim because its true; you add it because you can point to reliable sources that support it. If a claim gets challenged and the sourcing is weak, the text comes out, even when everyone in the discussion privately knows the fact is right. I once watched a paragraph about something from my own field get deleted because the only sources were primary and self-published, and the admin who closed the discussion said the project just doesn't verify things that way. Yet the same people will insist, in other disputes, that no, sources can be biased or flat wrong, and the encyclopedia certainly isn't claiming that whatever has the best citations is automatically the truth.

To me that looks like the site is quietly committed to the idea that being accepted by the community under its procedures is what counts as knowing, rather than the idea that knowledge is a matter of getting your words to mirror the world. A friend who studied philosophy says this is basically neopragmatism, the view that there's no standpoint outside our practices and vocabularies from which we could compare a sentence with reality, so justification is social all the way down. But maybe that's way too strong, and the Wikipedia rule is just a coordination device for an anonymous crowd, compatible with several different epistemologies.

So here's what I'm asking. What theory of truth, justification, or knowledge does this verify-before-you-add practice actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology as precisely as you can, and be careful to seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit well with, and say where it's just philosophically underdetermined. Which rival views of knowledge does it quietly rule out, if any? If the people running the encyclopedia genuinely held a correspondence theory of truth and thought the whole point of an article was to represent reality accurately, what would have to change about the rules, if anything? And one last thing: when edit wars get settled by arguing about which source is better rather than by figuring out who has it right, is that evidence that the project is social or antirepresentationalist about knowledge, or would a correspondence theorist predict the exact same dispute resolution process?
