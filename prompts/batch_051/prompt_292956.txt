I work as a software engineer at a company that runs a subscription app, and I have been pulled into building an internal review tool that product managers would use before shipping any new experiment in our onboarding or checkout flow. I have no formal philosophy background, so I can spec the questionnaire logic, but I need someone to tell me what the underlying categories actually are. Our written policy says only that changes must persuade users rather than manipulate them, but the line moves every review. Last quarter we shipped an annual-billing default that saved users money, and it passed without objection; two months later a growth PM made the cancel button grey and moved it behind an extra screen, and the review board split, with one side calling it ordinary friction and the other calling it a dark pattern. I keep hearing nudge, coercion, deception, and manipulation used as if they sorted the cases, but they do not, and I suspect the real dividing lines run through things like whether a change engages the user's reasoning or exploits inattention, whether it changes the user's options or only how options are presented, whether the user would endorse the mechanism if we disclosed it in plain language, and whether it targets a known vulnerability rather than something about the situation anyone would face.

Please act as an ethicist who specializes in the philosophy of influence, applied to digital products, and think carefully before producing anything. Begin by brainstorming the underlying distinctions that genuinely drive these categories, including distinctions that do not map neatly onto the words we use at work. Then write a decision tree of eight to twelve nodes that a product manager with no philosophy background could answer from a design spec alone, without already knowing which category they think a change falls into. Each leaf should carry a named classification (rational persuasion, mere informational framing, nudge, manipulation, coercion, deception, or whatever refinement of the space you think is right) plus one sentence on its awkwardest commitment and one concrete product example. Build the tree from the logical space first and locate the labels in it afterwards; if some combinations of answers have no occupant, say whether a position is missing because it is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag any two leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate — I expect nudges and manipulation to be hard — and define any technical terms so an engineer or product manager who has never read philosophy could implement your tree as a questionnaire.
