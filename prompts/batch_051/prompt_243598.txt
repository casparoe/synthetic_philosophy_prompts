A recurring argument has been playing out in my reading group, and I'd like a careful move-by-move referee. The group was working through deontological constraints of the sort that forbid an agent from killing an innocent person even when doing so is the only way to prevent five other innocent people from being killed. Daniel opened with the charge that this is not a bullet to bite but a genuine paradox. He argued that a constraint against X gets its point from the badness of X; if obeying the constraint predictably leaves more instances of X in the world, then the rule frustrates its own rationale, and a morality that leaves five murders on the table to avoid committing one has turned its rule into a fetish.

Priya pushed back that Daniel is smuggling in consequentialist bookkeeping. The constraint, she said, was never adopted to minimize the world's count of murders. It expresses something about the relation between an agent and each person: each person's claim not to be used as a means applies directly to the agent, and the fact that other people will violate their own duties does not turn an intended killing into a permissible one.

Daniel answered that this relocates the objection without meeting it. If the point of the constraint is the agent's relation to victims rather than the number of victims, then the theory seems to value the agent's clean hands over five lives — which is either a form of egoism or a very elaborate way of redefining a good outcome until the theory comes out true. Priya replied that it is not about clean hands but about the separateness of persons. The five have claims against their prospective killers; they do not have a claim that Daniel commit a sixth murder. Rights tell an agent what she may do, not which state of affairs she must bring about.

Daniel conceded that Priya's account makes the theory internally consistent but said the original paradox remains standing. A rational person should still find it disturbing that a rule whose guiding concern is the badness of a harm ends up yielding more of that harm, and the appeal to the separateness of persons is so far a label for the problem rather than an account of why the trade-off should be off-limits.

I keep going back and forth between them, so I want a verdict on their actual moves rather than a bare resolution of the underlying dispute. Which of Daniel's and Priya's specific claims are correct or mistaken, where does each argument overstate its case, and what are they both missing? And, if you had to say, who is more right overall?
