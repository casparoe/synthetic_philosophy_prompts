I spent most of my working life as an experimental biologist, and I retired with the working assumption that figurative language is either decoration or a convenient shorthand for something that could, in principle, be said literally. Since retiring I have been reading the philosophy of language and cognitive science, and I keep returning to a dispute that cuts across both. One position, associated with Donald Davidson, holds that a sentence such as "His argument collapsed under cross-examination" has only its literal meaning; strictly speaking it is false or somehow defective, and whatever the metaphor does beyond that — startling the reader, prompting a comparison, inviting an image — is a causal effect on the listener rather than part of what the sentence means. Any cognitive content worth taking seriously could be given in a literal paraphrase without remainder. The opposed position, from the conceptual metaphor tradition, holds that at least some metaphors are not replaceable ornaments. They are the surface of stable, largely unconscious mappings from embodied source domains — physical combat, spatial motion, temperature — to abstract target domains — argument, time, emotion — and these mappings do genuine cognitive work: they shape how we reason about the target, not merely how we phrase conclusions reached independently.

I would like your own credence in the following proposition: for at least some ordinary metaphors, something the speaker communicates is not expressible, even in principle, by any literal paraphrase without loss; the metaphor does cognitive work that a literal counterpart cannot fully do. Please do not give me a bare number. Decompose the judgment into the following component claims, each stated in the direction that favors the proposition: (1) robust, automatically activated cross-domain mappings are at work in ordinary comprehension; (2) such mappings demonstrably shape reasoning about abstract domains rather than merely accompanying it; (3) whatever content the mappings carry is part of utterance meaning or of what is communicated, not solely a downstream causal consequence of understanding a literal sentence; (4) the failure of literal paraphrase is an in-principle failure, not a practical limitation of vocabulary, time, or literary skill; and (5) the proposition is sharp enough to have a truth value rather than dissolving into a choice among definitions of "meaning." Assign each a rough probability, explain how they combine into your overall credence, and identify which component your number is most sensitive to.

Finally, help me locate a specific disagreement. My own credence is around 0.6. A former colleague, a computational neuroscientist, puts it near 0.15; he grants that metaphor has real imagistic and emotional effects but insists that anything worth calling content can be paraphrased. Tell me where your own number falls relative to these two, but more importantly say which of the five component claims most plausibly separates his 0.15 from my 0.6, and tell me what evidence — behavioral, neural, developmental, or philosophical — would move your own credence the most.
