I teach an upper-level seminar on concepts and mental representation — about 15 undergrads, mostly a mix of philosophy and cognitive science people, 80-minute meetings — and I want to do a session on how much is packed into "learning a concept from a few examples." I've gotten obsessed with Bongard problems: those little panels with six boxes on the left and six on the right, simple line drawings in each, where the task is to find the rule that separates the left boxes from the right. They look trivial until you sit with them, and the thing I want my students to actually feel is that no set of six positives and six negatives pins down a unique rule: there's always some gerrymandered alternative that fits, and the reason their rule feels obvious is somthing about them, not about the figures. I don't want to just stand up and say that — I want the activity to do it.

So here's the shape I have in mind: I'd hand out two or three printed Bongard problems to solve in pairs, then have each pair invent a hidden rule and draw their own Bongard problem (six-ish examples per side, stick figures and simple shapes only), then trade with another pair and have them write down what they think the rule is, then compare with the inventor's intended rule. My bet is that a decent fraction of the time the guessers come up with a perfectly good rule that is not the one the inventor meant — and then the inventor has no way to close the gap except by adding new examples aimed at killing that specific alternative. I want that moment to land, and then a debrief that connects it to Goodman's grue idea (that any finite sample is compatible with insane predicates like "grue" if you're willing to bend the rule) and to the thought that learning from few examples only works because people bring strong prior biases about what rules are even candidates. Could you give me: (a) concrete rules for the activity and a timeline that fits 80 mintues, (b) three starter Bongard problems that are easy enough not to frustrate, plus one adversarial one where the "wrong" rule is arguably simpler than the intended one, (c) a debrief script mapping specific moments to the philosophical points, and (d) a frank list of what predictably goes wrong — e.g. do pairs just make near-impossible puzzles, or generate rules too vague to test, and how do I keep it from becoming a puzzle-difficulty contest?
