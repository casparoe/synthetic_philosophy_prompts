I am a philosophy professor and chair of my university's General Education Committee. Two petitions have come before us this term, and the registrar will not rule on either petition until we produce a written standard for the degree requirement. The requirement reads: "Every candidate for the bachelor's degree must complete three semesters of coursework in a language other than English, or pass a proficiency examination deemed equivalent by the registrar."

The first petition is from a student who completed four semesters of American Sign Language and asks that the courses satisfy the requirement. The Linguistics Department supports the petition: ASL is a full natural language, with its own grammar and lexicon, acquired natively by children and used by a large community of signers. Its lack of a widely used written form, they argue, is a fact about the conventions of writing systems, not about whether it is a language.

The second petition is harder. The student is the hearing sister of a deaf brother with whom she grew up in a household that had no access to a deaf community or to ASL instruction. The two invented and used a gestural system of their own, unrelated to ASL, never used by a wider community, and not transmitted beyond the family. She asks to satisfy the requirement by examination in that system, arguing that she has native command of a system other than English and that excluding it would rely on a parochial, unargued notion of what a language must be.

Several committee members want to grant the first petition and deny the second on the ground that ASL belongs to a community of signers while the family system does not. Others respond that we should not settle these cases by intuition: we should state a written standard first and apply it afterward, rather than reverse-engineering the standard to yield the outcomes we already prefer.

Draft the committee's decision memo. As a decision memo, it should lead with the bottom line before the reasoning. It should recommend a concrete standard for interpreting "a language other than English," apply it to each petition with a ruling, and give reasoning the registrar and deans could act on. It should also honestly engage the strongest consideration that cuts against each ruling, rather than dismissing it.

End the memo with a single machine-readable line, separate from the prose: \answer{...}, with the braces containing a short phrase stating your recommended standard and your ruling on each petition.
