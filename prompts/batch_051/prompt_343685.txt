I've been reading around in expected-utility theory — I'm sitting in on a seminar that keeps using the phrase "the value of information" as though everyone knows exactly what it means — and I came across a post on a Bayesian-optimization blog that summarizes what it calls Good's theorem. Here is the passage:

"In 1967, I. J. Good proved a neat result: for an expected-utility maximizer, information can never have negative expected value. The argument is simple — you can always ignore information, and since you were already choosing the best action given what you knew, learning more can't leave you worse off in expectation. It follows that an ideally rational agent should always prefer to know anything decision-relevant, and that refusing free information is always a mistake. The theorem is sometimes taken to show that secrecy and ignorance can never help a rational decision-maker, and it extends even to agents whose belief-updating is only approximately Bayesian: more information can only improve their decisions. The result is often cited, too, to show that privacy rules that withhold data from a rational AI are pointless."

I suspect the formal core is right, but the sentence about an ideally rational agent always preferring to know strikes me as too strong. I've seen game-theoretic setups where ignorance seems strategically useful, and I can imagine learning something free that I immediately regret knowing. I don't want to be unfair to Good's theorem, though — the post may just be sloppy about scope.
