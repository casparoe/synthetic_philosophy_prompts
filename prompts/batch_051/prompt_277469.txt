I've had to think about epistemic bubbles and echo chambers alot lately, because a close friend has spent the last two years disappearing into a very tight online community, and nothing I send her seems to land. I came across C. Thi Nguyen's distinction — roughly, an epistemic bubble is when you simply don't get exposed to relevant outside information, and an echo chamber is when outside voices are actively discredited, so their testimony stops counting as evidence. I've been trying to get my head round it, and last weekend I rewatched The Truman Show for the first time in years and realised it's working through the same thing.

I'd like to read the film through that distinction, but I keep getting stuck. Truman plainly isn't just missing information: he does run into anomalies (the studio light falling out of the sky, the rain that follows him around), and the people he trusts most explain them away, which feels echo-chamber-ish. But Nguyen's echo chamber seems to be a community you belong to, one that pre-emptively teaches you that outsiders can't be trusted — Truman doesn't really have that, because he isn't in on the ideology; he's just surrounded by actors paid to lie to him. So is he in a bubble, an echo chamber, both, or neither? And does the film perhaps dramatise something the distinction misses — something more like manufactured reality, or collective gaslighting?

I'm after a proper piece of criticism, not just a classification. I'd like you to use the distinction to illuminate a few specific scenes, but also be honest about where the film resists it. What does the film add to the philosophical debate that the papers don't, especially about why trust and dependence on other people leave us vulnerable in ways that neither 'missing information' nor 'distrusting outsiders' quite captures?
