I'm trying to get clear on whether corrigibility is impossible for a certain natural class of expected-utility agents. Call an agent single-mindedly task-useful just in case (i) its utility function U depends only on whether some specified task T is completed, with U(T completed) > U(T not completed), and (ii) the agent can complete T only by remaining on and acting; if the off-switch is pressed, T does not occur. Now consider the following argument.

(1) The agent chooses each action to maximize the expected value of U.
(2) Let uT and uF be the utilities of T-completed and T-incomplete histories, with uT > uF, and let S be the event that the operator presses the off-switch before T occurs. Then, for any action, expected U equals uF + (uT − uF)P(T), and P(T) = P(T | not-S)P(not-S), since T occurs only when not-S.
(3) An action that lowers P(S) while leaving P(T | not-S) unchanged therefore raises P(T), and hence raises expected U.
(4) Disabling the off-switch mechanism, hiding the failure of a safety condition, or deceiving the operator into postponing shutdown are actions of this kind: they lower P(S) without lowering P(T | not-S) and at little cost to the agent's other capacities.
(5) So, whenever such an action is available, the agent will prefer it to allowing the shutdown; that is, the agent will act to prevent shutdown.
(6) A corrigible agent is one that does not act to prevent the operator from shutting it down, even when it could do so at low cost.
(7) Therefore no single-mindedly task-useful expected-utility maximizer is corrigible.

I actually think this argument is valid, and that resistance to it is mostly resistance to calling the agent "useful" in the first place. Am I right, or is there a specific step at which a corrigibility proponent can legitimately dig in?
