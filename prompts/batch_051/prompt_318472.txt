I'm putting together a unit on epistemic backstops and synthetic media — the idea that photos and video used to function as a kind of shared, fallible backstop for what actually happened, and that deepfakes and cheap forgery tools slowly eat away at it. I want to use Nineteen Eighty-Four in the unit and I'm not sure how much the novel actually fits. Orwell has Winston working in the Records Department doctoring back issues of the Times, and the photograph of Jones, Aaronson and Rutherford gets altered after they're vaporized. So on one reading the novel is basicly about the totalitarian version of the deepfake problem: the Party doesn't just lie, it makes the record itself untrustworthy, so the backstop is gone. But I also wonder if that's not quite right, since the novel's mechanism is top-down state control of the archive, not the decentralized everyone-can-forge situation deepfakes create, and the Party's aim is to make the past whatever it says, not to drown people in plausible fakes until they give up. Can you read the novel against the epistemic backstop idea and tell me where it genuinely illuminates it, where the analogy strains, and whether the novel adds something to the philosophical debate that a standard discussion of deepfakes would miss? I'd rather have an honest the-novel-is-doing-something-else take than a stretched reading.
