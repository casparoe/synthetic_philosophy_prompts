The question the instructor asked was: "Explain Bertrand's paradox and say what problem it raises for the principle of indifference. What is the right lesson to take from the paradox?" The students answer is copied below.

"The principle of indifference, going back to Laplace, says that if we have no reason to favor any one of several possible outcomes over another, we should assign them equal probability. Bertrand tested this with a circle. Draw an equilateral triangle inscribed in the circle, and ask for the probability that a random chord is longer than the triangle's side. There are three natural generation procedures: pick the two endpoints uniformly on the circumference, which gives 1/3; fix a radius and pick a point uniformly along it, then draw the perpendicular chord, which gives 1/2; or pick a point uniformly in the circle's interior and make it the chord's midpoint, which gives 1/4. Since the evidence is exactly the same in each case, and the principle of indifference says equal ignorance should yield equal probability assignments, the fact that it yields three different values means the principle is internally inconsistent and should be abandoned. The standard replacement is Jaynes's symmetry principle, which tells us to use the unique distribution invariant under rotations and translations. In Bertrand's problem this singles out 1/2, which is why most people now accept that as the correct answer. The lesson, then, is that objective probability in continuous cases should be anchored in invariance, not in indifference."
