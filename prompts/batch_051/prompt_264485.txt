I'm writing a paper on whether the semantics of generics explains why social generics about groups of people feel and function differently from biological ones, and I made the mistake of reading a popular explainer to check my understanding. The article summarizes the topic roughly like this: generics might look like universal claims, but they obviously aren't, since "all ducks lay eggs" is false; instead, linguists analyze them as statistical generalizations about what is typical of the kind. On this picture, "ducks lay eggs" says that enough—normally most—ducks lay eggs, and this is precisely what is meant by calling generics exception-tolerant: a generic tolerates the minority of cases below the statistical threshold, just as "most" does. The article then draws the moral that a generic like "women are bad drivers" is true when the relevant statistic really is true—when the majority really has the property—and that the danger of such remarks is therefore that they are usually arithmetically false.

The conclusion sounds neat, but it conflicts with my textbook's treatment of the data: it would make "mosquitoes carry malaria" false, since only a tiny fraction of mosquitoes carry malaria, and it makes the social cases look like a simple factual error rather than anything distinctive about stereotyping. I can no longer tell which parts of the article are standard semantics and which parts are its own overreach.
