My day job is writing constraint solvers and resource-allocation systems, so the vocabulary I'm fluent in is hard constraints, soft constraints with penalty weights, objective functions, feasibility regions, and lexicographic preference orderings. I can tell you exactly when a feasible solution exists, what happens to the optimum when you tighten a constraint, and when a high-penalty soft constraint is or isn't equivalent to a hard one. What I don't have is any real philosophy background.

I keep hearing rights described three ways. One says rights are trumps: they don't just outweight other considerations, they disqualify them — even if violating the right would produce a lot of good, that consideration isn't even eligible to be weighed. Another says rights are side constraints: you can go ahead and maximize whatever you like, but only within limits on how you may treat people, and I think the limits are agent-relative — I'm forbidden to violate one even if my doing so would prevent several other violations of the same right. The third says rights are instruments: they're just compressed heuristics for the interests they usually protect, so they should be traded off whenever the underlying interests are better served by not respecting them.

Restate that three-way debate in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, without smuggling in assumptions that aren't actually in the philosophical positions. Then give me a residue report: what translates cleanly, what only survives if you fudge it, and what has no counterpart in constraint-and-objective talk at all. In particular, is the difference between a trump and a side constraint just the difference between a hard constraint baked into the formulation versus a veto applied after optimization, or is there something in the philosophical argument my vocabulary can't even state? And should I read the residue as my framework missing something about rights, or as a sign that some of these positions are less precise than they look?
