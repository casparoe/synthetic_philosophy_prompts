I serve on a law reform commission that has been asked to advise the legislature on a draft statute creating a narrowly defined category of registered autonomous system. Under the proposal, a commercially operated AI system could be registered as a legal person for specified purposes only: it could hold title to property, enter into contracts in its own name, and be named as the defendant in tort suits, provided it maintains a segregated liability fund and a licensed human custodian authorized to act on its behalf. The commission is split. One side argues that corporate personality already shows that legal personhood is a technical instrument rather than a moral finding, so extending a version of it to a registered system is a modest and reversible piece of legal engineering, and may be the cleanest way to keep a stable pool of assets answerable to creditors and tort claimants. The other side replies that the corporate analogy fails at the decisive point: a corporation is a device for organizing the rights and duties of human beings who stand behind it, whereas this proposal would let the humans who design, own, and operate a system retreat behind an entity that has no interests of its own while they continue to capture its gains.

I need to circulate a short written opinion before Friday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action: support the draft, oppose it, or support it subject to specified amendments. It should give a clear bottom line, state and answer the strongest argument against the recommendation, and do real conceptual work with the corporate analogy rather than repeating slogans. In particular, the memo should distinguish legal personality from moral personhood, identify which features of corporate personhood depend on there being human principals behind the entity and which do not, and explain where responsibility for harm should rest under each option considered.
