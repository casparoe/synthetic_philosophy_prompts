For a philosophy of action seminar, write the desiderata that a theory of free will must meet, where free will is understood as whatever flexibility distinguishes a free agent from a rigid mechanism. Take as your motivating case the Sphex wasp made famous by Dennett: the wasp drags its paralyzed prey to the threshold of its burrow and goes inside to inspect; if an experimenter moves the prey a few inches during the inspection, the wasp comes back out, drags the prey to the threshold again, and repeats the inspection, indefinitely, without ever registering that the situation has changed. An adequate theory must say what the wasp is missing and what we have. Spell out the requirements: it must distinguish genuine flexibility from both sphexish rigidity and mere randomness; say whether flexibility comes in degrees and, if so, how much is enough for free will; respect what physics says about determinism and indeterminism without smuggling in a question-begging requirement; handle degraded and partial cases such as compulsion, addiction, unthinking routine, and a human acting sphexishly; and avoid making flexibility something that must itself have been flexibly chosen, on pain of regress. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are only the preferences of particular camps. Flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude familiar positions. Finally, tell me which of your requirements is the most contestable, what argument or consideration would lead you to demote or abandon it, and where your overall reasoning is most likely to fail.
