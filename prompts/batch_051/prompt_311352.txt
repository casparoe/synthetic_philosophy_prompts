I spent forty years doing visual psychophysics and only started reading philosophy of mind after I retired, so please forgive any mangled terminology. Lately I've been trying to get clear about the idea that conscious representations are transparent, in roughly the sense I gather Metzinger uses: the machinery that produces the representation is not itself experienced, so we simply get the world rather than a world-model, and the same is supposed to go for the model of ourselves. I wrote down my gut reactions to some cases and I'd like you to tell me whether any single principle captures all of them, or, if not, which ones I should give up and why.

First, when I look at the sycamore outside my window, I experience a tree out there in the world. I do not experience my visual system constructing a tree. If a theory said that normal seeing is as much in me as an afterimage, I would think it was off form. Second, after I stare at a bright lamp and then look at a pale wall, the afterimage that floats around feels like a private sensation rather than a public object. It follows my gaze and I know it is coming from my eye, so I want to say it is in me, not out there. Third, stubbing my toe: the pain is simply in my toe. Knowing that it is carried by nerves does not make it feel like an inner model of a damaged toe; the toe itself hurts. Fourth, the rubber hand illusion. With my real hand hidden and a fake hand being stroked in synchrony with it, I get the wierd sense that the rubber hand is mine. I am not fooled at the level of belief, but ownership seems to reach out to it. Fifth, a phantom limb. An amputee who still feels their missing hand as present and as theirs is, to my mind, feeling something more genuinely theirs than the rubber hand ever is, even though in both cases the hand is not there. That pair really bothers me. Sixth, in a dream, the body I inhabit may not resemble my real one, but while the dream lasts it feels completely mine. On waking I would deny that the dream-body is me, yet I cannot deny the ownership was complete while it lasted. Seventh, in an out-of-body experience I would see my actual body from near the ceiling and feel located up there rather than in it. I would still call the body down there mine, but I would also say that I am not in it. That is the one that feels as if it breaks any principle that ties ownership to location. Eighth, when I silently rehearse a sentence, the words are undoubtedly mine, but they do not feel bodily located. I want to say they are mine in an agentive way, not an embodied way.

Is there a coherent account of when something is experienced as part of me, or as transparently given in something like the self-model, that gets all eight? If not, which of my gut reactions does it force me to revise? Please give the principle precisely, say what it predicts for an actor in role who feels a remembered grief as the character's rather than her own, and be honest about where my verdicts conflict. Dont format the answer at all: no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, just continuous prose like an email. And write in British English.
