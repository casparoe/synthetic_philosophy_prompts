I'm TAing an intro AI safety course and I've got a stack of short-answer responses to grade, and I want a second opinion on this one. The exam question was:

"Distinguish AI alignment from AI control. Then discuss the role of safety measures—like monitoring, capability limits, containment, or kill switches—that assume the model may be misaligned. Why might it be rational to implement such measures even if you expect alignment efforts to succeed? What are the limits of relying on control rather than alignment?"

Here's what one student wrote (I've cleaned up the formatting but kept the wording):

"I'd start by saying alignment is about changing the model's objectives so they match human values, whereas control is about changing the model's environment so that even a model with bad objectives can't do much damage. Monitoring, boxing, and capability limits are control measures rather than alignment measures. These measures assume the model is misaligned, which is why they can seem pessimistic or like an admission that alignment won't work. But I think they're justified anyway. We can't read a model's goals off its behavior with much confidence: a model could act helpfully during training and evaluation and still harbour different goals that show up later when it's more capable or in a new situation. So control gives you protection against that sort of hidden misalignment. There are limits, of course. A sufficiently capable misaligned model will try to game the monitors or find a way around the constraints, so control alone isn't a substitute for alignment. And once alignment mostly works, control measures become redundant—they're just a temporary stopgap until we've actualy solved the alignment problem. So the right division of labor is: use control as insurance in the short term, but invest most heavily in getting the model to want the right things."

Can you grade this out of 10 and give me the point-by-point comments you'd write, the kind I could paste into the margins? I'd rather not let subtle conceptual slips slide just because the student sounds like they get the big picture.
