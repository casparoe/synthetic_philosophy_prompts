I am a third-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of mind course and a jurisprudence course this term, and the two syllabi have collided in a way I cannot stop thinking about. In the philosophy course we have been reading Douglas Hofstadter and Emmanuel Sander's Surfaces and Essences. Their central claim is that analogy-making is not one cognitive skill among many but the core of all thought. On their account, a concept is not a fixed definition or a set of necessary and sufficient conditions, but a growing and revisable stock of remembered exemplars, and every application of a concept to a new situation is an analogy between the present situation and those earlier exemplars. Because no two situations are ever exactly alike, the difference between applying a concept literally and applying it analogically is at most a matter of degree rather than kind; categories get stretched, reweighted, and reorganized every time they are used.

At the same time, the jurisprudence course has been covering common-law reasoning by precedent, and I cannot shake the impression that it is the same structure wearing a wig. A judge deciding a case does not simply subsume the facts under a rule with a fixed extension; the court compares the present facts with the facts of earlier cases, asks whether the resemblance is strong enough to bring the present case under an earlier principle, and may distinguish a precedent or extend it to a new kind of fact pattern. The ratio decidendi of a case — the principle for which it stands — is not stored as an exact verbal formula that gets mechanically applied; it is reconstructed retrospectively, in light of the new case onto which it is being mapped. On this way of looking at it, legal doctrines are exactly concepts in Hofstadter and Sander's sense: precedents are exemplars, the ratio is the essence extracted through the mapping, distinguishing a case is introducing a slippage, and the development of the common law just is analogical concept change.

I am not sure whether this is a genuine identity of structure or only a seductive analogy about analogy-making. Please act as a philosopher of cognitive science who also knows jurisprudence, and think carefully before offering a verdict. Begin by brainstorming candidate mappings between the two domains; then settle on the sharpest one and state it precisely. Next, check where the structures match and where they come apart: what would have to be true of legal reasoning for claims about concept formation to transfer to it, and what would have to be true of ordinary cognition for features of precedent-based law to transfer back? Finally, name at least one thing the parallel predicts that turns out false, and say whether the parallel is deep, superficial, or somewhere in between.
