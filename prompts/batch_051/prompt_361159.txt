One of my friends in the writing group has gotten really into the outside view thing — base rates, reference classes — and he keeps using it to talk me out of finishing my novel. I can't find the exact flaw, if there is one. Maybe you can take it apart, or tell me its airtight and I should just live with the conclusion. His argument, numbered:

(1) For any forecast, you should start with the base rate for the relevant reference class and only move off it if you have evidence that actually discriminates.

(2) My book belongs to the class of first novels by serious non-famous writers. Just to have a number, call the base rate of such a book getting picked up by a real publisher 2 percent.

(3) My inside view says the book is in the good 2 percent.

(4) But the people in the other 98 percent also had inside views, and they pretty much all said the same thing: theirs was the exception.

(5) So the fact that my inside view says "exception" is a fact I'd have whether or not I was in the 98 percent.

(6) A fact you'd have either way can't shift you off the base rate.

So I should put my own book at about 2 percent until something properly outside happens, like an agent asking for the full manuscript or a beta reader who doesn't owe me anything saying it holds up.

Something feels off, especially around (4). It's like it treats my inside view as a vibe — "mine's special" — when actually it's a pile of concrete reader-response reasons about specific scenes and sentences. But I can't formalize why that matters. It also implies almost nobody should write novels, which might just be true and stings.

So: is the argument valid as written? If not, which step exactly fails and what would the correct version of that step have to say? If it is valid, why does the conclusion feel more absurd than it is?
