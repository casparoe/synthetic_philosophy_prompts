I'm not a trained philosopher, and before this goes to print I want someone's honest read on whether the view I've ended up with actually hangs together. Here's the set, as best I can state it:

1. Fragility is a real feature of the glass, not just a label we stick on it or shorthand for how it has broken in the past.
2. A glass can be fragile even if it never breaks. A wine glass that spends its entire life packed in foam is still fragile, even though in those conditions the plain conditional "if struck, it breaks" is false.
3. Fink and mask cases — a sorcerer who would turn the glass into unbreakable rubber the instant before the hammer lands, the antidote that stops a poison from killing, the foam that absorbs the blow — show that the simple counterfactual analysis of dispositions is wrong; but they don't show dispositions are unreal.
4. What makes fragility the disposition it is is its stimulus–manifestation profile: being fragile is essentially being disposed to break when struck in normal circumstances. The profile isn't just a symptom; it's constitutive.
5. Dispositions are causally in the driver's seat. A chemical's power to dissolve, oxidize, or bind explains why a reaction goes the way it does; "it dissolved because it's soluble in the solvent" is a real explanation, not a redescription of the outcome.
6. At the fundamental level I lean dispositional essentialist: charge just is a cluster of powers — attract, repel, radiate — and there's no inert categorical "what it is" standing behind the powers. But I also think a tabletop disposition like fragility is realized in the glass's ordinary molecular structure — lattice geometry, packing density, bond strengths — and that tabletop powers bottom out in those micro-level structural facts.

I honestly can't see which of these is the weak link, or whether the whole package is fine.
