I have been asked to lead a discussion in a policy-analysis reading group on the pitfalls of quantitative targets, and the organizer circulated a handout that condenses the issue into a single paragraph. The handout reads:

"Goodhart's law is the principle that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. If hospitals are rewarded for shorter emergency-room wait times, the wait-time statistic quickly stops tracking quality of care, and if schools are rewarded for higher graduation rates, the graduation-rate statistic stops tracking genuine learning. Donald Campbell made a closely related point about social indicators: the more a quantitative indicator is used for high-stakes decision-making, the more it invites corruption and distorts the very process it was supposed to monitor. Goodhart and Campbell are really describing the same underlying regularity, and together they establish that attaching stakes to almost any single quantitative indicator will tend to produce gaming. The practical lesson is that quantitative indicators are only trustworthy when kept purely informational; the moment you tie rewards or penalties to a metric, you should expect the metric to be corrupted."

I know the popular version of Goodhart's law reasonably well, but I have also seen it claimed that Goodhart's original remark was narrower, having to do with the instability of statistical regularities once policymakers try to exploit them, and I know that researchers in machine learning talk about specification gaming and reward hacking rather than Goodhart's law. I cannot tell how much of the handout is a faithful summary, how much is a tidy conflation of distinct ideas, and whether the concluding lesson is stronger than anything the underlying laws actually license. Could you give me an accuracy audit of the handout? In particular, please separate the sound parts from the subtly misleading ones, spell out where Goodhart's original claim, Campbell's law, and specification gaming come apart and where they genuinely overlap, and say how a thoughtful defender of well-designed quantitative targets would respond to the handout's concluding recommendation.
