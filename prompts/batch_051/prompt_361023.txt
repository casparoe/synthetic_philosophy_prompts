I am taking a seminar in formal epistemology, and we have just moved from ordinary Bayesian conditionalization to Jeffrey conditionalization. The mechanics are clear enough: with plain conditionalization I learn some proposition E outright, and my new degree of belief in H is P(H | E). But Jeffrey conditionalization is supposed to cover the messier case in which an observation does not make any proposition certain, only more or less probable. The stock example we discussed was glancing at a piece of cloth in poor light: I become more confident that it is blue, but I do not come to know, let alone become certain, that it is blue. I can write down the update as a weighted average, P'(H) = P(H | E) P'(E) + P(H | ¬E) P'(¬E), but I do not feel that I understand what the rule is really doing or why it is reasonable.

Could you explain the conceptual structure of Jeffrey conditionalization in an intuitive but technically precise way? In particular, I want to understand: why the update must be represented as a redistribution of probability across a partition of possible observations rather than as conditioning on a single less-than-certain evidence proposition; what is being held fixed when one assumes that the conditional probabilities P(H | E_i) for each cell E_i of the partition do not change during the update, and what would justify that rigidity assumption; and how the rule avoids an apparent circularity, since it seems to require as an input the new probability of the evidence, which is the very thing observation was supposed to supply. If the answer turns on how the partition of possibilities is chosen, I would like that explained as well.
