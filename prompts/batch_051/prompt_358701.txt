I recently moved from a large, fairly tolerant city to a small town where people have strong views about how you keep your garden, when you greet people, how children behave in restaurants, and so on. At first I found it stifling, but after a year I noticed I felt less anxious about everyday interactions because I almost always knew what to expect from others. I've been trying to think about this through the tight–loose cultures debate, and a friend and I arrived at an argument that seems to prove too much. I can't find the exit.

The argument runs like this:

(1) A social rule is justified when it solves a coordination problem or reduces a harm.
(2) Shared expectations about how others will behave are themselves a kind of public good: they make interactions more predictable and lower the cognitive cost of ordinary decisions.
(3) Therefore, enforcing a rule can be justified partly by the expectation it creates, even if the specific behavior it governs would not matter much on its own.
(4) If an expectation can justify enforcement, then enforcing more rules creates more shared expectations, which in turn justifies further enforcement.
(5) So there is no principled stopping point: the more tightly a society regulates behavior, the more justified its regulations become, until any deviation from a rule counts as a harm to the public good of predictability.
(6) But that conclusion would make an extremely conformist village, a total institution, or a maximally tight society normatively better than a loose and tolerant one.
(7) Since that is absurd, at least one step must be wrong.

We both want to reject (6), and we would rather keep (1) and (2) than give up on the idea that looseness can be a legitimate achievement rather than a failure of coordination. Tell me which step you reject, or whether the chain is valid and the real question is which premise is least costly to give up. In particular, what would a principled account of how much rule-following a society should demand have to say that this argument leaves out?
