Take the classic Allais pair of choices. You know the one: first choose between a sure $1 million and a gamble that pays $1 million with 89%, $5 million with 10%, and nothing with 1%. Then choose between an 11% chance of $1 million and a 10% chance of $5 million (nothing otherwise). The usual finding is that people take the sure million in the first pair and the 10% shot at the big prize in the second, which violates the independence axiom.

Here's the variation I want to work through. Keep the second pair exactly as in the original. But in the first pair, replace the certain $1 million with a near-certain option that pays $1 million with probablity 99.89% and nothing with probability 0.11%. The risky option in that first pair stays the standard one: 89% of $1 million, 10% of $5 million, 1% of nothing. (The near-certain option is built out of the same 89%-of-$1m common branch plus an 11% sub-lottery that itself gives $1 million with 99% and nothing with 1%; that decomposition matters for the independence-axiom analysis.) So the first choice is between a 99.89% shot at a million and the usual 89/10/1 gamble.

Now: does the independence axiom still get a grip here? The two pairs no longer reduce to exactly the same subcomparison, so the line-for-line cancellation argument from the original doesn't obviously go through. But an expected-utility maximizer might still be barred from the Allais pattern (near-certain million in the first pair, 10% shot at five million in the second). Work through the expected utility carefully and tell me: (a) can any expected-utility maximizer with an increasing utility function choose the near-certain million in pair one and the 5-million gamble in pair two? (b) if not, which principle is actually doing the work now, if it isn't the common-consequence form of independence? (c) what would the certainty-effect and regret stories each predict in this version, and does this variant separate them or not? Also say where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what would change your mind.
