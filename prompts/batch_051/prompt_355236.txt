I have been trying to pin down exactly what a Moorean sentence commits a speaker to. It seems obvious that another person can say of me, without absurdity, that it is raining but I do not believe that it is raining, while the first-person version is plainly defective. I want to test an argument to the effect that no one can sincerely and truthfully assert the first-person version. Let the sentence be: it is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining.

(1) A person believes a conjunction only if she believes each conjunct.
(2) To sincerely assert a sentence is to believe the proposition that sentence expresses.
(3) Suppose I sincerely assert this Moorean sentence and it is true.
(4) By (2) and (3), I believe the whole conjunction.
(5) By (1) and (4), I believe that it is raining.
(6) By the truth of the conjunction in (3), I do not believe that it is raining.
(7) Steps (5) and (6) contradict, so the supposition in (3) is impossible.
(8) Therefore no one can sincerely and truthfully assert a Moorean sentence.

I am unsure whether the argument is valid and whether the real fault, if any, lies in (1) or (2). Is the argument valid? If not, identify the exact step that fails and the reading under which it fails. If it is valid, say which premise gives way first and what a weaker version of it would have to look like.
