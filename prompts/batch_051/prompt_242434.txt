I am a retired physicist who has recently begun reading philosophy, and I have become preoccupied by a problem in the philosophy of artificial intelligence: the shutdown problem. My scientific training makes me comfortable with formal frameworks — expected utility theory, preference orderings, optimization — but I am still learning to treat these as objects of philosophical argument rather than as engineering tools. I would like you to help me think through a particular disagreement.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher specializing in AI alignment and decision theory. Think carefully before you write. I suggest you begin by sketching, for your own use, the strongest arguments each side could make and the objections each would raise to the other; only then draft the dialogue itself.

The disagreement is this. Imagine an artificial agent built as an expected utility maximizer, with a shutdown button that its human operators can press. The familiar difficulty is that such an agent has an instrumental incentive to resist shutdown: being switched off prevents it from pursuing whatever terminal goal its utility function encodes, so it will tend to disable the button, manipulate its operators, or otherwise act to keep itself running. One position holds that this problem can be solved inside expected utility theory by making the agent indifferent to the button's being pressed: shape its utility function so that, at the moment of choice, the expected utility of the branch in which it is shut down equals the expected utility of the branch in which it is not. On this view, corrigibility is the absence of a preference about one's own continuation. The agent does not resist shutdown, because resistance would have no expected utility payoff; but it also does not seek shutdown, because it is not suicidal. The opposing position argues that this target is mistaken in two ways. First, corrigibility is not a tie in a utility function but a positive, stable disposition to cooperate with correction — and a tie, so the objection goes, gives the agent an incentive to rig the conditions under which the button is pressed rather than to leave the button alone. Second, and more deeply, no agent with complete preferences over trajectories can be both useful and shutdownable. A complete ordering, so the argument runs, forces the agent to prefer either preventing shutdown (when the continuation it expects is better than the shutdown branch) or causing it (when the shutdown branch is better), unless we fine-tune a knife-edge tie; and a knife-edge tie is brittle, since any small shift in the agent's beliefs tips it one way. The proposed alternative is to give the agent incomplete preferences — preferential gaps between certain trajectories — so that shutting down now and continuing are simply not compared, and the agent neither seeks nor avoids the button.

Write a dialogue of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words between two characters, one defending the utility-indifference approach and one defending the preferential-gaps view. Both characters should be equally competent; neither should be a strawman. The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth, with each character responding to the other's actual arguments rather than talking past them. In particular, I want the indifference advocate to press the purist on whether preferential gaps can be implemented without secretly presupposing the corrigibility they are meant to explain, and I want the purist to press the indifference advocate on whether a tie in expected utility is truly indifference to the button rather than an incentive to manipulate the circumstances of its pressing. The positions should be stated with enough precision that a specialist in AI alignment would recognize them as fair representations. Do not force a resolution; it is fine if the disagreement ends sharpened rather than settled. Avoid bibliographic references and named citations; the ideas should be carried entirely by the arguments themselves.
