Could you do some moral reverse-engineering for me? Ive been going back and forth about what adult children owe their parents, and I keep making confident judgments about particular cases but can't state the principle I'm using. So here are my verdicts—tell me what they add up to.

1. A father worked two jobs to put his daughter through college, but he was basically absent the whole time: no warmth, no interest in her life, but also no abuse. Now he's elderly, broke, and needs help with rent. I think she owes him significant financial help, but I don't think she owes him her time or a real relationship.

2. A mother was warm, funny, and genuinely close with her daughter—the kind of parent you call just to talk. She's also hopeless with money and has run up credit card debt through decades of bad spending. Now she wants her daughter to pay it off. I don't think the daughter owes her the money. I think she owes her real support: help budgeting, a place to stay if things get dire, company—but not a bailout.

3. A stepfather raised a child after his wife, the child's mother, died. He provided materially but was cold and often cruel—he'd say the child had ruined his life. He's now in a nursing home, and his social worker wants the adult child involved. I think the child owes him nothing beyond what anyone would owe a stranger: return the call, make sure the home isn't neglecting him, and that's about it.

4. An infant was adopted and raised lovingly by her adoptive parents. Her biological mother, whom she's met twice as an adult, now asks to move in with her. I don't think the biological connection by itself gives the mother any special claim.

5. A father, who genuinely was a good father, needs a kidney, and his adult son is a match. I don't think the son is obligated to donate. I do think he owes it to his father to take the request seriously, learn the actual risks, and be straight with him about the answer.

6. A mother who raised her child well now has advanced dementia and no longer recognizes the child. Visiting is basically useless and personally awful. I still think the child owes her mother decent care—making sure she's safe and treated well—even though there isn't a relationship left in the ordinary sense.

Here's the pair that's driving me crazy. Case 1 and Case 2 look like they should run on the same principle, but in Case 1 I think the child owes money without ever having had a relationship, and in Case 2 I think the child owes a relationship without money. My working hunch is something like: you owe your parents whatever a friend would owe, and only when the relationship really is friendship-like. That handles 3, 4, and 5 nicely, and maybe half of 2. But Case 1 seems to violate it directly, and I'm honestly not sure it handles 6 either, since there's no relationship left. So test that hunch against the cases and repair it, or replace it. Is there one principle that gets all six? If not, which of my verdicts should I give up, and why that one? And tell me what your repaired principle says about a case I haven't listed: a parent was warm and provided well, but the adult child has gone cold—not resentful, just uninterested—and no longer returns calls. Does the child owe anything, and if so, what exactly?
