Im trying to write a paper on Groundhog Day and a view I'll call tranquilism: the only thing that ultimately matters for a life is the absence of craving and suffering. Everything else—love, achievement, knowledge, pleasure—is good only insofar as it quiets craving or reduces suffering, and bad only insofar as it feeds craving or causes suffering; otherwise it doesn't count for or against a life. The movie seems like an obvious fit, since Phil's early misery is basically a tangle of cravings (escape, novelty, sex, status, impressing Rita) and his late peace looks like what happens when the cravings die down. But the late Phil isnt exactly a craving-free sage either: he's busy, he cares about a lot of specific people, he keeps trying to save the old homeless man even though he fails every time, and he clearly wants Rita to love him, not in a nuetral 'whatever happens is fine' way. I want a reading that takes tranquilism seriously and tests it against the film, scene by scene where that helps. Where does the lens really illuminate what's wrong with early Phil and whats different about late Phil? Where does the film push back, so that an honest tranquilist would have to say the movie overvalues achievement or connection or love? And what if anything does the film add to the philosophical debate that the bare statement of the view doesn't? Dont let it collapse into 'the film proves tranquilism right' or 'the film proves it wrong'; I want the exact places where it holds and the places where it resists.
