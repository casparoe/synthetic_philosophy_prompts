In my philosophy of language class we've been doing generics and the criteria for when "Ks are P" is true. We covered the usual problem cases ("ducks lay eggs" is true but only the mature females do it; "mosquitoes carry malaria" is true but only a tiny fraction of mosquitoes actually carry it), and we were given three candidate tests: (i) most Ks are P; (ii) P is a characteristic property of the kind, the sort of thing you'd mention to someone who'd never met a K; (iii) being a K disposes you toward P under normal conditions, even if the disposition is rarely manifested. Now I've been trying to stress-test these on a case I made up. Imagine a species of frog where only about 2% of individuals ever secrete a powerful neurotoxin, because the gene for it is in every frog but only gets expressed under a stress condition almost none of them encounter. The toxin is the species's main defense, the reason predators avoid them, and the first thing a herpetologist would mention about the species. Is the generic "these frogs are toxic" true? Go through the three criteria one by one and say where they agree and disagree. If they split, which criterion is doing the real work, and does the case show the others need to be revised or thrown out?
