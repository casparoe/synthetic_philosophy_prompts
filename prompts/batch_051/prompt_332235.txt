I am a doctoral student in political theory whose technical training is in formal political economy — mechanism design, incomplete contracts, club goods, credible commitment, and the economics of signaling. I would like you to translate the main positions in the church–state debate into that vocabulary, and to report honestly on what the translation fails to carry.

By the main positions I mean four ideal types: establishment, in which the state officially adopts and supports a particular church (England is the standard case); strict separation, in which the state declines to take cognizance of religion in public institutions (French laïcité is the standard case); nonpreferentialist neutrality, in which the state may support and accommodate religion but may not prefer one church or faith over another (often associated with the dominant American reading of the religion clauses); and corporatist partnership, in which the state recognizes religious bodies as public-law corporations and collects a church tax on their behalf (Germany is the standard case). Treat these as ideal types rather than as descriptions of any country's current law.

For each of the four, please do three things. First, restate the position in the formal vocabulary: identify the principals, agents, and the delegation or contract problem the arrangement is meant to solve; specify which goods are being bundled (membership, identity, education, welfare provision, ritual services, civic belonging); identify the transaction costs, hold-up problems, and credibility problems the arrangement addresses or creates; and say what the arrangement signals or coordinates. Second, report the residue: which normative commitments translate cleanly into efficiency, incentives, credible commitment, or welfare terms; which translate only with distortion; and which have no expression in the framework at all. Third, give a verdict on the residue. Is what fails to translate a defect of the economic vocabulary, or does it show that the untranslatable notions — collective establishment as identity, state neutrality as equal standing among citizens, the sacred as a category that is neither a private preference nor a public good — are doing more rhetorical work than they should?

I am less interested in an account of the four countries than in the systematic translation and its limits.
