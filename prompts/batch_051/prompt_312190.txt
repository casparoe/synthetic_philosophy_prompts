I have been reading a lot of narrative history over the past few years, and I have developed a policy for handling it that I am no longer sure I can defend. The policy is aimed at hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy. When an author explains a large event—an empire's fall, a revolution, a war, a financial crisis—and the account is built so that the event emerges naturally from long-standing causes, such that by the end it seems as if it had to happen, I treat the explanation with real suspicion. Specifically, I refuse to let the explanatory details move my own beliefs about why the event occurred unless the author can show that the account would have assigned a high probability to the event in advance, using only what contemporaries could have known at the time. If the account cannot pass that test, I set it aside as a post hoc story: possibly true, but not something I should update on.

The rule has costs. It leads me to discount careful work on cases like the fall of the Western Roman Empire or the coming of the First World War, where specialists plainly know far more than I do, and it seems to commit me to the strange view that an explanation can be true without being rationally usable by me unless it was reachable in advance. I am not a historian; I read this material to understand the world, not to audit a profession that uses methods I have never been trained in. Yet whenever I catch myself suspending the rule for a book I enjoyed, I worry that I am being inconsistent in exactly the way the rule was supposed to prevent.

What I want to know is whether this policy is a genuine epistemic discipline or a bias in disguise. When is it reliable, where does it break down, and is there a revised version that keeps the protection against hindsight bias and narrative smoothing while still letting me learn from retrospective historical scholarship? And how would I tell, from the inside, whether I am applying it selectively to explanations I would have rejected anyway? Please answer in American English.
