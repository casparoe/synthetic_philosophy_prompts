You are a professional philosopher working in value theory and the theory of practical rationality. I am in a graduate seminar on the structure of "better than," and we have been working through spectrum arguments: sequences of options in which each adjacent step seems better than the one before it, while the option at the end seems worse than the one at the start, thereby threatening the transitivity of all-things-considered betterness. A colleague has proposed that a spectrum argument should count as a genuine counterexample to transitivity only if it satisfies these five criteria:

1. Uniformity of the relation: every judgment in the series — adjacent pairs and endpoints alike — uses one and the same all-things-considered betterness relation, over the same options, against a fixed background; no judgment is merely "better in one respect" or "better relative to a shifted baseline."
2. Stability of the step-wise judgments: each adjacent-pair judgment would survive seeing the whole series at once and knowing where it leads; the judge would not quietly withdraw any pairwise betterness claim once the endpoint is on the table.
3. No threshold crossing within a step: no single adjacent step crosses a qualitative boundary in any relevant value — for example, a line between tolerable and intolerable intrusion. Each edge in the spectrum is small and merely quantitative.
4. Stability of the endpoint judgment: the judgment that the first option is better than the last survives reflection, and is not an artifact of framing, salience, or an unnoticed switch in the sense of "better" being used.
5. Common scale of comparison: every pair in the series is evaluable on one scale of value; none are incommensurable, and the normative character of the trade-off does not change as one moves along the series.

Now consider the borderline case we are stuck on. A city of 800,000 residents is choosing among public safety surveillance policies. Let O₀ be the policy with no public cameras; under it, 40 serious crimes are cleared each year. For each k from 0 to 999, O_{k+1} is exactly like O_k except that one additional camera is installed at the location expected to clear one further serious crime per year. The new camera changes each resident's daily experience so slightly that no individual can notice, in isolation, that it has been added. At every single step, the policy committee judges O_{k+1} all-things-considered better than O_k: one more serious assault or robbery cleared, at a privacy cost too small for anyone to detect. After 1,000 steps we reach O₁₀₀₀: cameras at every major intersection, facial recognition, and a log that can reconstruct where nearly any resident has been on any day; 1,040 serious crimes are cleared per year. Yet, looking only at the endpoints, I find — and some of my colleagues disagree, finding O₁₀₀₀ better — that O₀ is better than O₁₀₀₀. The step-wise gains, each purchased at a tiny price, together add up to a society in which citizens can no longer move through public space unobserved, and that endpoint judgment strikes me as all-things-considered, not merely aesthetic.

Please start by brainstorming the strongest considerations on each side: the best case for saying this spectrum meets the five criteria and therefore forces a serious verdict on transitivity, and the best case for saying one or more criteria are not met, so transitivity can be preserved. Then go criterion by criterion and give a reasoned verdict for each: clearly satisfied, clearly not satisfied, or underdetermined by the criteria as stated. Where the answer is "not satisfied," say whether the problem lies in the criteria, in this particular case, or in one of the underlying judgments. Finally, give the overall verdict you would defend: does this case show that "better than" is not transitive, does it show that the criteria themselves need to be revised, or does it show that one of the judgments in the spectrum should be abandoned — and what is the main cost of whichever option you choose?
