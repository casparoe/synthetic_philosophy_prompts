Over the summer I interned for a lawyer who does mostly defamation work for local review sites and a couple of free papers, and I fell down a rabbit hole in her old case files trying to work out whether she has a single rule for deciding when a review or article states "a fact" you can sue over and when it's just protected opinion. As close as I can reconstruct it from what she actually did, her record runs like this. She sued over a critic's claim that a chef "trained at Le Cordon Bleu" when he'd never been — checkable credential, she said, so a fact. She told a hotel to settle after a site gave it one star and "worst value in the region," on the ground that a star rating is subjective. She refused to sue a tip sheet that predicted a roofing company "will be bankrupt within eighteen months" and turned out wrong — forward-looking, she said, not a claim about the present. She went after a competitor who posted that a rival had "failed its health inspection last March" when it had passed. She wouldn't touch a blog that called the mayor "a clown" with "the stupidest idea in this town's history." She sued a former customer who wrote that an auto shop "billed me for repairs it never made." And when a credit-rating firm cut a local credit union's bonds to C and the credit union sued saying the downgrade destroyed it, she told the firm not to settle, because in her view a letter rating is an evaluative judgement about creditworthiness, not an assertion of a specific fact.

Now the new one is on her desk. A patient posted a one-star review of a dentist that says, "Total scam artist — she charged me $1,400 for a crown she never did," and the dentist wants to sue. I keep flip flopping, because the review smushes together a loose insult and a very specific, checkable allegation, and her past calls seem to pull in both directions. What should she do if she wants to stay consistent with that record? Which of her earlier cases is the closest analog? I'm less interested in the verdict itself then in the rule she's implicitly running, the other plausible rule that fits the same track record just as well, and what principle she'd be adopting with each choice about the dentist review — and if the record genuinely underdetermines it, say so and tell me the extra case that would settle which rule she holds. Also tell me how confident you are in all of this and the one point you're least sure about.
