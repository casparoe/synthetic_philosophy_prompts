Suppose that, beginning on a known date, every human child born from that day onward has fully functioning nociception but lacks the affective-motivational component of pain. They detect tissue damage, localize it, and can tell whether it is sharp, burning, or aching, and the information is available to guide behavior. But the experience carries no unpleasantness, no aversive quality, and no impulse to escape: a burn tells them that the hand is on the stove, but it does not hurt in the ordinary sense. A rare acquired condition called pain asymbolia approximates part of this dissociation; some patients can report that a stimulus is sharp and may even use the word pain for it while showing no discomfort, not withdrawing, and not asking for it to stop. The real condition is localized, partial, and entangled with other deficits, whereas the thought experiment idealizes it into a clean, universal, lifelong trait. Assume the trait is permanent, present in every person born after the date, inherited by their children, and publicly understood. Predict, with reasoning, how the world looks fifty years on in at least these areas: medicine and public health; the safety of children and how caregivers supervise; the law of assault, torture, and compensation for injury; and ordinary language and the concept of pain itself, including whether people continue to treat tissue damage as bad or merely as information to be managed. Make the mechanism explicit at each step: which consequences follow robustly from the loss of aversive affect alone, which depend on whether deliberative risk-avoidance and social norms can substitute for a felt urge to escape, and which would change if the affective component could be present in graded rather than all-or-nothing form. Say which predictions you are most and least confident about, and for the shakier ones, state what feature of pain or of human motivation would have to be different for them to fail. Finally, say what this imagined world shows, if anything, about whether the badness of pain lies in the sensation itself or in the separable aversive reaction to it.
