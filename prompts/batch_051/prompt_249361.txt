I keep coming back to a parallel that I can't tell is deep or just a collision of vocabulary. In machine learning, when a model only performs well for a very narrow slice of hyperparameters, nobody takes that as evidence that a designer chose those hyperparameters. We assume we got lucky after a big grid search, or that the model is overfit and brittle, or that we kept tuning against the test set. In the cosmological fine-tuning argument, the fact that the constants of nature sit in a tiny life-permitting range is treated as surprising and as evidence that something set them. But the moves available in ML seem to map almost one-to-one onto the usual responses: the multiverse is basically a one-shot grid search over universes, the anthropic principle is basically selection bias (we only observe the trial where the loss came out low), and adding a designer is like introducing a prior we have no independent evidence for. Yet I also feel like the analogy has to break somewhere, because in ML we usually have a known training distribution and a known number of trials, and in cosmology we don't. Is this a real structural identity between the two arguments, and if so does anything from ML—regularization, degrees of freedom, the bias-variance tradeoff, maybe the difference between tuning one global parameter and tuning thirty seperate knobs—actually transfer to the design argument? Or am I just pattern-matching on the phrase "fine-tuning"?
