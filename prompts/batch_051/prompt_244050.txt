I'm a software engineer and I don't have any formal philosophy background, so I might be using the wrong words here. At work we do planning poker for sprint estimates, and in the last retro my EM announced we should stop letting the tech lead's estimate anchor everyone and instead just average the whole team's numbers, because "the wisdom of the crowd beats the individual expert." He gestured at some famous math result and a story about a fair where a crowd guessed an ox's weight almost perfectly. Now the phrase is getting used all over the place: bug-triage votes, hiring debriefs, even a proposal to replace senior code review sign-off with a panel vote of whoever is around.

I got curious and looked up the Condorcet jury theorem, but the standard example is a jury picking guilty/not guilty, and I can't tell how it maps onto averaging a bunch of numerical estimates. Is the underlying claim about majority votes being right, or about the average of noisy guesses landing close to the truth, or about markets aggregating scattered information, or about diversity beating expertise? Those all feel like different mechanisms, and I suspect they need different conditions. Independence, everyone at least better than chance, errors not being correlated, people having actual signal rather than just vibes—it's murky to me which conditions matter for which claim and whether our planning poker numbers satisfy any of them (everyone on my team underestimates the legacy auth work, for example).

I want a menu I can reason from: what are the distinct precise claims someone could mean by "the wisdom of the crowd"? For each, sketch the actual mechanism and the conditions it needs, and say whether its trivially true, defensible under narrow conditions, or just false. Then say which reading the EM is probably using, and which readings would actually license replacing the tech lead's estimate with the team average—vs. which ones wouldn't. I'd like to walk into the next retro able to say exactly what work the slogan is doing.
