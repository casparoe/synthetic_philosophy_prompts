I've been reading rationalist blogs since before the epistemic-status thing got meta, and I want to make one of those which-position-do-you-actually-hold quizzes for my site, except about Goodman's new riddle of induction instead of the usual politics or identity stuff. The whole point of this quiz format is that it sorts people by what they say about cases, not by the label they walked in wearing, so I want the questions built out of concrete cases, with a scoring key hidden at the end.

Audience: people who know the basic grue setup (grue = green before 2050, blue after) but haven't worked through the academic literature. Eight to ten questions, two or three answer options each, phrashed so you can't tell which position an option belongs to, and a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto leaves.

The leaves I want are roughly: Goodmanian entrenchment (projectibility is just a function of how entrenched a predicate is in our practice), natural-kind/universals (grue is gerrymandered and only natural properties are projectible), simplicity-first (green wins because it's simpler, and simplicity is doing the real work rather than naturalness), Bayesian prior-chooser (there's no objective solution; Bayes just relocates the problem into priors over predicates, and we pick green-friendly priors for pragmatic reasons, not metaphysical ones), deflationary category-relative (there's no fact of the matter about which projection is right, and that's fine rather than a scandal), and a hard skeptic leaf (induction can't be justified, grue just makes the problem vivid). If you think two of these collapse into one leaf or that one needs splitting, do it and say why.

Include at least these cases: the plain emerald case; a bleen or spatial variant to test whether the person's answer secretly depends on time rather than on predicate gerrymandering generally; a numeric/curve-fitting analogue where a smooth curve and a gruesome polynomial both fit all the data and diverge at the next point; a case where someone coins a brand-new predicate tomorrow that exactly captures a genuine natural regularity, so entrenchment and naturalness come apart and you can see which one the respondent actually reaches for; and a case where a whole community finds grue-bleen resemblance natural the way we find green-blue resemblance natural, to catch the realism/anti-realism split.

Build the scoring key from the logical space of answers rather than from the list of leaves: lay out the questions and the answer combinations they allow, then locate the named positions inside that space. Mark any combination nobody seems to occupy with a one-line note on whether it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Flag any two leaves your questions can't actually separate instead of pretending the quiz sorts them.

Since I'd rather have an honest quiz than a tidy one, end with a short self-audit: which question you're least confident actually sorts people, which part of the scoring key owes the most to your own hunches, and what argument or edge case would make you move a leaf, split one, or change a branch question. If the whole taxonomy leans on a contested claim about how simplicity and naturalness relate, that's exactly the kind of thing I want on the table.
