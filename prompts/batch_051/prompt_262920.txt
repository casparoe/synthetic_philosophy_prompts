In 1997 the legislature of one American state passed a joint resolution that read, in part: "The General Assembly, on behalf of the people of this State, expresses its profound sorrow and regret for the suffering endured by individuals who were involuntarily sterilized under the laws of this State repealed in 1963, and hereby offers its formal apology to those individuals and their families." In floor debate, the sponsors made clear that they meant the roughly 1,700 people, now mostly elderly, who had been sterilized under the state's eugenics statute.

More than two decades later, a historian reviewing county records established that between 1964 and 1974 the state's public welfare department continued to perform sterilizations under a different legal cover. Women committed to the state home as "feebleminded" or delinquent were told that release was conditioned on their consent to sterilization; the consent forms were signed by guardians the department had itself petitioned to appoint, and the forms never cited the repealed eugenics law. At least 900 women appear to have been affected. The 1997 resolution says nothing about post-repeal conduct, guardianship, or consent forms, because no one in the legislature knew about the program when the apology was passed.

Analyze whether the 1997 apology applies to this later-documented group of survivors. Work through at least four approaches: (1) the ordinary public meaning of the words as adopted; (2) the purpose the legislature was serving and the wrong it meant to acknowledge; (3) what the sponsors and well-informed legislators would have said in 1997 if they had been told about the post-repeal program; and (4) how a reasonable survivor reading the text would understand whether she is being addressed. Say which approach should control here and why, and whether it matters that an apology is a performative speech act addressed to victims rather than a statute imposing duties. Then give a direct answer: does the original resolution already reach the post-repeal group, or does the state need to extend it formally?
