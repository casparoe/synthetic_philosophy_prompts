I'm drafting a novel about a retired math teacher who runs a tiny, tightly disciplined betting fund for five friends, and I keep trying to make her rules feel like they come from one coherent philosophy rather than a pile of writerly quirks. By chapter three the rules are established: she won't touch anything with negative expected value; when she does bet, she sizes the stake with the Kelly formula—roughly edge divided by the payout odds—but then she only stakes a quarter of what that formula say; she rebalances every Sunday and talks about growth in terms of average log return, never average dollar return; and she absolutely refuses a class of positive-EV bets, namely the ones where the positive expectation is carried by a huge jackpot that almost never hits, so the typical outcome is a small loss. A beta reader told me that last rule is inconsistent, because someone who cares about expected value should take any positive-EV bet. But I suspect the character doesn't actually care about expected value in the usual sense; she's maximizing something like geometric growth, or she's being robust to the fact that her edge is estimated, never known. I'm not sure the four rules even hang together. What theory of rational betting does a practice like this presuppose? Could a single coherent view justify all of it, and would that view be best described as logarithmic utility, geometric-growth maximization under model uncertainty, or something else again? Where is the practice underdetermined—that is, which parts would several different theories all license? And how would her rules have to change if she were a plain expected-value maximizer with no uncertainty in her estimates?
