I am a retired condensed matter theorist who has lately been reading metaphysics, and I would like a candid philosophical assessment of a position I keep trying to articulate. I have been calling it "scale solipsism," for want of a better phrase. The claim is this. For any system that admits a well-defined effective description at some scale of resolution — hydrodynamics for a fluid, the Landau–Ginzburg order parameter for a superconductor, the Ising model for a magnet, the low-energy meson theory for the strong interaction — that description is not best understood as an approximation to the deeper, smaller-scale theory, even when we have excellent reason to think such a deeper theory exists. It is an exact theory of a different question, namely what the system is like at that resolution. Its degrees of freedom, its entities, and the truths expressible in it are not reducible to, or less real than, those of the underlying theory; they simply answer to a different level. The renormalization group, on my reading, does not demonstrate that the infrared is controlled by the ultraviolet. It demonstrates the opposite: for a vast class of ultraviolet completions, the low-energy theory is autonomous, because the details that distinguish one completion from another are largely washed out and only a handful of couplings survive. So I have been arguing that physics is best read as a stack of autonomous scales, none metaphysically privileged, and that "more fundamental" marks a practical orientation rather than an ontic hierarchy.

I would like an honest assessment of this. Is it a coherent position rather than a loose metaphor? Does it already exist under another name, and what are the nearest positions to it? And where does it break — in particular, what can a reductionist say that this picture cannot easily handle, and does it overstate what universality and decoupling actually license? Please say how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the point you are least sure of.
