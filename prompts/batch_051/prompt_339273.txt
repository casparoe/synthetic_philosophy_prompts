Last week I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about the easy-knowledge problem, and he pushed me into a position I now have to defend in front of people who will not be as patient as he was. My position, as it stood at the end of that argument, is this: the bootstrapping problem is not a special embarrassment for process reliabilism but a version of a difficulty that arises for any epistemology once a belief-forming practice is allowed to certify itself, and the reliabilist should accept the conclusion that a track-record argument can yield justified belief in the reliability of one's own perception. The appearance of viciousness in the standard cases, I now think, comes from conflating two things a subject might be doing: using a process to generate a justified reliability belief, and using a reliability belief as part of the basis for the very first-order beliefs from which the track record is built. The first is benign; the second would be objectionable, but I deny that the basic case commits the reliabilist to it.

Next week I present a short paper defending this at my department's epistemology workshop: twenty minutes for the paper, then thirty minutes of questions. The audience will include two process reliabilists who accept the theory but have said in our reading group that the bootstrapping cases make them deeply uncomfortable; one access internalist who holds that justification must be reflectively available to the subject; one evidentialist who rejects reliabilism outright and thinks a belief is justified only if it fits the subject's undefeated evidence; and several graduate students who are drawn to externalism but afraid of sounding permissive. The core of my argument in the paper is as follows:

(1) Suppose a subject forms a series of true perceptual beliefs — the wall is red, the book is blue, and so on — by ordinary vision.
(2) On each occasion the subject is also in a position to form the corresponding belief that things are as they appear to be.
(3) The subject then reasons by simple induction from the sample of cases in which things were as they appeared to the general conclusion that the subject's visual appearances are reliable.
(4) A process reliabilist is committed to saying that each premise is justified by vision, that the inductive step is justified if induction is reliable, and hence that the reliability conclusion is justified.
(5) The objector calls this an absurdly easy route to a justified belief that one's perceptual faculties are reliable.

My paper argues that the charge in (5) cannot be motivated without either (a) imposing an internalist requirement that reliable belief formation is insufficient unless the subject has reflective access to its reliability, which the reliabilist independently rejects, or (b) endorsing a principle against circularity that would also condemn calibrating a thermometer by its own readings or validating memory partly through memory — practices the objector's own account of evidence cannot easily do without.

Prepare me for the question period. I want the objections ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, phrased in the terms those particular people would use, each with a note on what I should concede, what I should hold, and a short reply I can deliver in two or three minutes. Be honest about where each reply is weakest, and tell me which point in my argument I should most fear having to defend at length. I would also like a warning about the objection I am probably hoping nobody will ask, and advice on how to handle the inevitable comparison to a clairvoyant or a lucky guesser who bootstraps his way into a delusional confidence in a faculty he does not have.
