I am a philosophy professor who also directs a clinical ethics consultation service at a teaching hospital. The hospital's critical-care committee has asked me to replace our ad hoc end-of-life decision-making with a written procedure. Under the new policy, allowing a patient to die by forgoing or withdrawing life-sustaining treatment is permitted when the consent and documentation conditions are met, while deliberately killing a patient is prohibited and must be referred to legal review. Every contested consult turns on which side of that line a proposed action falls, yet in practice the classification tracks the personalities in the room rather than any defensible principle.

Write the reusable procedure the on-call team will apply. The service handles several such consultations a week. The users will be ICU nurses, residents, intensivists, and risk managers who have read no philosophy and who must usually decide within an hour, with a family waiting; the attending physician and the on-call ethics consultant must sign the classification, but the form itself will be filled in by whoever is in the room. The stakes are a patient's death, possible criminal or civil exposure, and the credibility of the service. The procedure must be usable without citations and without theory vocabulary — whatever philosophical machinery operates underneath must be translated into the hospital's own terms — but it must not merely encode the folk contrast between acting and standing by, since that contrast is exactly what the hard cases put under pressure.

The procedure should set out: the distinction or distinctions it operationalizes and why those rather than others; an ordered checklist, or a flowchart in words, whose questions a non-philosopher can answer from the chart; and a clear account of which steps cannot be resolved by rule and require judgment, including what that judgment is a judgment about. Do not let the procedure quietly collapse into a rule about withholding versus withdrawing or intended versus merely foreseen: wherever it uses such a contrast, defend it and mark the point at which it breaks down.

Apply the procedure step by step to at least these cases. (a) A competent patient with advanced ALS who has been ventilated for two years asks to be taken off the ventilator; a nurse objects that this is killing because someone must press a button and the patient will die within minutes. (b) A patient with end-stage COPD is on non-invasive ventilation, and the family asks that he not be intubated if he deteriorates. (c) A patient is taken off the ventilator for a spontaneous breathing trial; when she tires, the team decides not to reconnect her, and a resident objects that if the tube had accidentally come out and the team had decided not to replace it, everyone would call that letting die, so the need for a hand to reconnect the circuit cannot by itself turn an omission into a killing.

Add one more case of your own construction, chosen so that the procedure returns an answer the treating team will not like, and explain why the procedure returns it anyway. Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot do, including where it becomes indeterminate, and what the committee should do in those cases rather than defaulting to the loudest voice in the room.
