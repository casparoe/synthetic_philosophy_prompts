A friend and I have been going back and forth about the old GEB thing — Aunt Hillary, the ant colony, the idea that a colony might have a mind — and I want to submit a concrete version of the dispute rather than keep looping. The question is whether a distributed system like an ant colony can have something that deserves to be called a mind: unified beliefs and desires, maybe a point of view, as opposed to just a big bag of ants each running dumb local rules. The case we keep returning to is colony-level behavior that looks smart — choosing between nest sites, reallocating foragers after a food source dries up, keeping something like a memory of which direction was profitable. All of that cashes out in pheromone trails, encounter rates, and individual ants that are basically tiny finite-state machines. My friend says calling that a mind is as informative as saying a thermostat knows the room is cold. I'm not sure, because the colony-level state seems to be doing real representational work: the trail is a reusable, error-corrected, distributed map, and the colony's behavior tracks facts no single ant has access to. I've read enough LessWrong threads to know this is the kind of thing people half-solve and then realize they've been arguing about the word "mind."

Then say what the test couldn't settle even if it came out cleanly one way — is the leftover part a limit of experiments, or a defect in the notion of "having a mind" itself?
