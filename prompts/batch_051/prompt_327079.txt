Take the standard Downs-style paradox of voting: for a self-interested voter, a vote only seems to matter if it's pivotal (breaking what would otherwise be a tie), and in a close large-N election the chance of that is on the order of 1/sqrt(N), so the expected benefit is tiny compared to the cost in time and effort, hence rational abstention. The usual fixes are duty, expressive benefits, social pressure, or caring about the whole electorate's welfare.

Now vary the institutions. Suppose the election is a pure proportional scheme where the implemented policy is literally a weighted average of the two parties' platforms, with weights equal to their vote shares. No 50% threshold, no jump at the margin. Your vote never flips a winner; it just nudges the policy mix by one-vote's worth. On the face of it this looks like it should dissolve the paradox, since every vote deterministically makes the outcome slightly better for you. Work through the actual mechanics. How does the marginal effect of one vote in the continuous system (roughly D/N, where D is your payoff difference between 100% party A and 100% party B) compare to the expected effect of a possibly-pivotal vote in the winner-take-all system (roughly D/sqrt(N))? Which setup gives a self-interested voter a bigger reason to turn out, and is the continuous version's reason qualitatively different — a tiny certain improvement rather than a tiny chance of a huge one — so that the standard duty/expressive fixes are doing different work? Does the paradox actually get worse in the continuous version, or does it dissapear because the contribution is guaranteed? Finally, add back a single threshold, say a supermajority requirement for some part of the platform, and ask where pivotality reappears and what that tells us about which feature of elections is really generating the paradox.
