I am a program officer at a mid-sized foundation. Several times a month I receive a grant report or an external evaluation claiming, in effect, that some program caused some outcome — a tutoring program raised reading scores, a cash transfer reduced psychological distress, a text-message campaign increased clinic attendance. I have to decide whether the causal claim is strong enough to justify renewing or scaling the grant, and I have to defend that decision to a board with no patience for philosophy. I usually have about an hour per decision.

I have been reading the interventionist account of causation associated with James Woodward, and I think it is the right framework for my job. As I understand it: a factor X is a cause of Y just in case there is some possible intervention on X that changes Y or its probability distribution, where an intervention must be surgical — it changes X and influences Y only through X, it is not correlated with other causes of Y, and it overrides the other causes of X — and where the X–Y relation is invariant under some range of such interventions, not merely stable under passive observation. I also know the framework faces a circularity worry: an intervention is itself characterized in causal terms, so the account can look as though it assumes the very causal structure it is supposed to establish. I do not need the procedure to solve that circularity, but I do need it to be honest about it; I cannot hand my board a checklist that quietly presupposes the answer.

Please write me a decision procedure I can actually run. It should be a checklist or ordered set of questions for a claim of the form: program X caused outcome Y in population P. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer and where the procedure stops with a no. Mark explicitly the steps where judgment cannot be eliminated and the steps where we are relying on unverified background causal assumptions. The procedure must keep the intervention and invariance distinctions that do real work for a grant decision and drop the machinery that does not. It must be usable by me and my non-philosophical colleagues without our having to explain Woodward; the theory should stay under the hood, visible only in the structure of the questions. Also say how the procedure treats a claim for which no actual intervention was performed, so the intervention is merely hypothetical.

Then run the procedure on these three cases:

1. An evaluation of a job-training program compares participants to non-participants after matching on education, prior earnings, and family background. Employment rates are higher for participants. The only manipulation is selection into the program, which is partly driven by the same motivation and social networks that affect later employment.

2. A randomized trial of an unconditional cash transfer in a drought-stricken rural region finds large effects on consumption and mental health. The foundation is considering scaling the program to urban areas in ordinary economic times. The trial is internally strong, but the effect has been observed only under drought conditions in one region.

3. A study claims that the number of text-message reminders sent to patients causes clinic attendance. But changing the number of messages also changes their content and tone — more messages become more urgent and directive — so there is no way to vary the dose while holding the message itself fixed.

In at least one of these cases the procedure should deliver an answer I will not like, and you should say so plainly. End with a short statement of what the procedure cannot do — in particular, which parts of my decision will remain causal judgment rather than checklist, and how I should describe that residual judgment to the board without dressing it up as settled science.

Keep the whole response under 1,000 words.
