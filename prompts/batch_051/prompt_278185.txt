Consider two people who eat animals. The first lives in an affluent city where plant-based proteins are cheap, familiar, and available in every grocery store, and she eats factory-farmed chicken anyway. The second is a pastoralist family in a food-insecure region, or a fishing household on a coast where the catch is the main reliable food; they eat animals or animal products because the alternatives are scarce, expensive, nutritionally risky, or culturally alien. Assume, to isolate the question, that in both cases an animal is killed and eaten, and that in both cases the person could in principle survive without eating it if alternatives were cheaper and more available. The difference is only in the cost, familiarity, and reliability of the alternatives.

People who think factory farming is seriously wrong, and who blame the first consumer without much hesitation, typically refuse to blame the second and often find the very suggestion that they should go vegan absurd or offensive. So ordinary moral judgment treats the same kind of act — killing an animal for food when doing so is avoidable in principle but costly in practice — as a chosen wrong in one case and as a regrettable necessity in the other. The judgment tracks the agent's circumstances rather than anything about the animal or the act described at the level of what happens to the victim. That is the asymmetry to examine.

Your task is to determine whether this differential treatment can be justified, or whether it is an inconsistency — possibly a comfortable way of exempting well-off people we identify with while refusing to make demands on people we pity. Do the following:

1. Give the strongest candidate justifications for treating the two agents differently: scarcity of alternatives, the cost of substitution to the agent, enculturation and knowledge, the causal structure of their participation in an animal-use system, demandingness, or something else.
2. Test each candidate against nearby cases. For instance: an affluent urbanite in a low-income country who has the same alternatives as the first consumer; a poor person in a rich country with a tight budget and a food allergy that rules out the cheapest plant proteins; a pastoralist who could replace a meaningful share of animal foods at significant but bearable cost; a rich-world person for whom eating a particular animal is deeply embedded in family and religious life. Which cases break which justifications?
3. Keep separate the wrongness of the act and the blameworthiness of the agent. Does defending the asymmetry force you to say that the moral status of the very act depends on the agent's option set, or can the entire difference be located in what excuses are available to the agent after the wrongness is fixed?
4. Take a position: keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up. If you keep it, state the precise rule for when scarce alternatives suspend the demand and when they only reduce it. If you give it up, say what that commits you to — condemning subsistence herders in a way you may find repellent, ceasing to condemn the affluent consumer, or revising some larger distinction between causing a harm, participating in a harm-producing system, and being unable to avoid it affordably.
