I'm the best man at a wedding this fall, and I keep going back and forth on the toast. The groom is an improv theater director — he genuinly organizes his life around the idea that overplanning kills whatever's alive in a moment, won't book flights more than a week out, the works. The bride is a logistics planner who once described their vacation to me as needing a work breakdown structure. The room is going to be split down the middle: half her operations-research people, half his improv troupe, plus a few family members who will not appreciate being turned into a lecture audience. So the toast has to be maybe five minutes, funny, affectionate, and it has to work for both camps. But I'd also like it to actually have a take on the planning-vs-improvisation question — not just "both are great," but something with a little more spine, like planning is overrated in a specific way, or underrated in a specific way, or the distinction itself is the real problem. Use the two of them as a case study rather than examples in the abstract. One angle I've been chewing on: good improv looks spontaneous but sits on a huge pile of drilled formats and trained attention, and even a careful plan is mostly a tool for noticing when reality has gone off-script — so maybe the clean opposition is what should go. But you don't have to take that line if you think there's a better one.

After the actual toast, add a short bracketed note for me, not to be read aloud, flagging which of the claims in the speech would be considered pretty settled by people who actually study planning or improvisation and which are contested or a stylistic stretch, so I don't get blindsided by some argumentative cousin.
