I'm a novelist, and I've been down a rabbit hole about the extended mind debate—the idea that your notebook or phone can be literally part of your cognitive system, not just a tool you lean on. The debate feels like it's been stuck forever. Everyone accepts the basic Otto case: a man whose notebook plays the same role for him that biological memory plays for everyone else. The extended-mind people say that, by parity, the notebook just is part of his mind. The critics say that's a confusion between being coupled to something and being constituted by it; the notebook is coupled to Otto's brain, but the mind stays in the skull, and calling the notebook part of the mind is a metaphor that got out of hand. What bugs me is that both sides seem to agree on all the facts about what the notebook does and what the brain does. So my working theory is that the disagreement its really about where you draw the boundary of the word 'mind,' or what the mark of the cognitive even is, and not about anything we could find out empirically. I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so I might be missing a hidden conceptual or empirical question that's actually doing the work. Is that diagnosis right? Or is there a real factual or formal dispute under the labels? And what would actually move it—a distinction, a better question, or is this just the kind of argument that never settles? I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a survey of the positions.
