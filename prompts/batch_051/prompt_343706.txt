I write about moral psychology and the law for a small ideas magazine, and I've been put in charge of a 75-minute hands-on session at a readers' retreat. Sixteen adults, mixed backgrounds — a couple of defense lawyers, two social workers, the rest just sharp readers — four tables of four, moveable furniture. I don't want to stand up and explain retributivism and consequentialism and then go around the room asking opinions; in my experiance that produces polite hedging and the same intuitions people walked in with. What I want is an exercise where the distinction between backward-looking desert and forward-looking prevention shows up as a conflict participants run into themselves, before anyone names it.

Design me a role-play or structured-deliberation game for that exact setup. My rough instinct is a two-stage thing: first, each table sets sentencing rules from behind a veil of ignorance, before they know whether those rules will fall on them as offender or as victim; then roles are assigned and the rules have to be applied to a concrete person sitting across the table; then there's one round of amendments, and every amendment has to be argued in one of two fixed vocabularies — future prevention, or deserved suffering. But if a different structure would make the tension emerge more sharply, override that. Give me exact materials (case sheets, role cards, a scoring scheme if it helps, no real money), a minute-by-minute budget for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the two theories — including the moment where a retributive jolt tends to show up even in participants who started out proudly consequentialist. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to patch it, and be frank about which part of the distinction the exercise can't reproduce and therefore has to be said aloud in the debrief.

And commit. Don't give me a menu of three viable designs and let me choose; hand me one fully specified activity and defend the choices you made, espeically the design of the single case that's supposed to create the sharpest collision between desert and deterrence.
