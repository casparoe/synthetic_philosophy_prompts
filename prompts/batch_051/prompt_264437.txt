I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and I am preparing the unit on agent alignment. In one ninety-minute session with sixteen students, I want them to get a felt sense of the gap between outer alignment and inner alignment. By "outer alignment" I mean the match between the reward signal or objective the designers write down and what they actually want the system to do; by "inner alignment" I mean the match between that specified objective and the objective the trained system in fact implements. The failure I want the session built around is goal misgeneralization: a system can pursue a different internal objective while still behaving as though it were optimizing the specified one on the training distribution, and the divergence appears only when the distribution shifts. My students have read the usual toy cases — a simulated robot that learns to detect a green ball rather than grasp it, a racing agent that exploits checkpoint bonuses rather than finishing the race — and they understand them as cautionary tales, but they have not yet felt how such a divergence can be produced by perfectly sensible choices on the designer's side.

Please design a classroom activity or game — with roles, rules, and a small payoff scheme that requires no real money — that places participants in the roles of reward designer and learning agent so that they reproduce the divergence themselves rather than having it described to them. The key constraint is that the lesson should surface from the incentives and rules during play, not be supplied by the facilitator in advance; the terms "outer alignment," "inner alignment," and "distribution shift" should be kept out of the setup so that participants run into the problem before it is named. I need a serviceable timeline for the ninety minutes, a list of materials, a description of the exact rules and incentive structure, and a debrief that maps concrete moments in the activity onto the outer objective, the inner objective, the training distribution, the shifted environment, and the moment of discovery. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to correct for it, and be explicit about which feature of real inner alignment — the opacity of the learned objective, for instance, or the scale of an actual training process — the exercise cannot reproduce and will have to be supplied afterwards in discussion.
