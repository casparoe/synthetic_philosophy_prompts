A thing I keep noticing and can't resolve: when a company keeps a failing product alive because it's already sunk $200 million into it, everyone agrees this is the Concorde effect — textbook irrationality, since the money is gone either way and shouldn't influence what you do next. But when a person stays in a career they hate because they already put eight years into it, or won't leave a relationship that's run its course because they've "built a life together," the same people call it grit, commitment, honoring your past. People will even mock a friend for finishing a book they're not enjoying just because they're 300 pages in, then praise that same friend for not quitting a PhD because they're four years in. It looks like the exact same mental move: letting an irretrievable cost pick your future. So I'm wondering whether anything actually justifies the asymmetry, or whether we've just agreed to call the same mistake by two different names depending on whether the subject is a person or a firm.

I want you to find the strongest possible principle that could generate both judgments without contradiction — maybe personal projects involve promises, identity, other people's reliance, or a kind of narrative meaning that corporate budgets don't? — and then test it against uncomfortable nearby cases. For instance, a founder who keeps a failing company alive partly for her emplyees, versus someone who stays in a calm but empty marriage because they don't want twenty years to have "meant nothing." Should we keep this asymmetry, collapse it in one direction (sunk costs can legitimately matter as commitments or information, so the company might not be irrational either), or collapse it in the other (both are just fallacies, and one side is romanticized)?
