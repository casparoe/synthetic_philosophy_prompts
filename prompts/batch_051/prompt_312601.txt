I am one of four trustees of a small family foundation that makes roughly thirty grants a year, usually between $10,000 and $75,000, across health, the arts, and animal welfare. None of us has formal training in philosophy, but over the past two years we have converged on a working view: preventing intense suffering matters more than creating or increasing positive experiences, and we want all our grantmaking to follow that view consistently rather than by instinct. Please turn that principle into a step-by-step decision procedure we can run on any proposal in under half an hour. The procedure should specify the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where we should stop with a no, and where the decision is a values call that must be handed to a person rather than settled by the checklist. It should also handle three disagreements we keep having: whether severity should outweigh the number of beings affected, whether we may ever fund a project whose main effect is to add new positive experiences rather than remove a negative one, and how to treat the risk that an intervention, especially in wild-animal or future contexts, might create suffering worse than anything it prevents. Then apply the procedure to three proposals in our current queue: a pilot program providing emergency pain relief to isolated elderly people at home; a summer arts festival in a low-income neighborhood; and a study estimating how many farmed fish experience severe pain during common slaughter methods, with no intervention attached to the study. Please flag where each application returns an answer the committee is likely to resist, and be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure and what the procedure cannot settle.
