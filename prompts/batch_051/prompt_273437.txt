I've been chewing on an idea for a little essay and I'd appreciate some comments on it. Not from a professional angle, I've got no training in philosophy and I dont want a reading list — just whether the thinking has holes in it. In particular: which part is doing the weakest work, and is the last move I make actually a different argument wearing a trench coat?

The essay wants to say that some values only work because they disguise an instrumental job as an intrinsic one. I call them guardrail values. The three cases are truth-seeking, tradition, and purity.

Truth-seeking. People say they want the truth for its own sake, but most of the time the truth pays: it helps you win arguments, predict what happens next, avoid being conned. The few times it doesn't pay — a truth that loses you friends or makes you miserable — are exactly the times people get most keen to announce they value it for its own sake. So the intrinsic-sounding talk shows up precisely when the instrumental story stops covering the behaviour. That looks suspicious.

Tradition. Nobody keeps a tradition going because it is useful in some measurable way; they keep it because it's theirs, and ditching it would feel like disloyalty or self-erasure. But traditions usually got installed because they were useful — coordination, avoiding forgotten disasters, binding a group together. The value has flipped from useful to sacred somewhere along the line, and it now works only because the usefulness has been forgotten.

Purity. This feels like the most elemental of the three. The disgust arrives before you can justify it. But it keeps pointing at the wrong things: body cleanliness slides into moral cleanliness, and what counts as disgusting tracks group boundaries more than it tracks actual danger. It acts like a smoke alarm that shrieks fire at whatever the group needs you to avoid.

Here's the move I'm least sure about, near the end. I want to say this is not a debunking. The fact that these values need to feel intrinsic to work tells us something. If you held truth-seeking as only worth it when it pays, it would stop paying, because you would abandon it precisely in the hard cases where it matters most. Same for tradition: you cannot keep a tradition alive while also treating it as disposable. Same for purity: a purity rule you second-guess every time is just risk assessment, not a guardrail. So the disguise is part of the function. A value that has to be held as an end in order to work is, in an odd way, a real end.

What I'd like comments on:

(1) Is the truth-seeking observation right, or am I cherry-picking the dramatic cases where truth hurts?

(2) Does the ending actually answer the suspicion raised at the start, or does it just change the subject from is it really intrinsic to is it useful to act like it is? And if those are two different questions, which should I care about?

(3) Is there a hole in the purity example? It feels like the strongest case to me, but maybe I've been staring at it for too long.
