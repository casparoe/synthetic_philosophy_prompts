The debate over whether the numbers count in rescue cases has always struck me as a debate that has stopped moving rather than a debate that has been resolved. The aggregationist says: each of the five has exactly the same claim as the one, so five equal claims outweigh one, and saving the five is required. The anti-aggregationist, following Taurek, says: there is no one who bears the loss of the five; there are only five separate individuals, each of whom would lose what the one would lose, so the one's claim is not outweighed by the mere existence of other claimants. On that view the fairest way to respect equally strong claims is a coin toss, and saving the five without one fails to treat the one as an equal. Both sides agree on the non-moral facts and on the equal moral importance of each person, and yet neither has moved. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about what is good or bad but about what fairness requires and about whether better can be predicated of an outcome from no one's point of view — in other words, that the two sides are answering different questions under the single heading of what I ought to do. Diagnose the structure of this impasse: is it a verbal disagreement, a substantive disagreement about the separateness of persons, a difference over whether morality is fundamentally about value or about claims, or a clash of bedrock intuitions that argument cannot reach? And given the diagnosis, say what would actually count as progress — a formal result, a new case, a sharper distinction — or whether we should stop expecting the debate to move.
