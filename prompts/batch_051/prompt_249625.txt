Suppose a group of families settle an uninhabited island and decide to run an explicitly Lockean property regime, or their approximate version of one. Everything starts as common. You only get an exclusive parcel by actually improving it — clearing, building, or farming it — within 30 days of staking a claim; a flag, a fence around raw scrub, or a piece of paper does nothing. And any claim is void unless, at the moment it's filed, there's still an unclaimed parcel of roughly the same quality available for every other adult settler who wants land. Parcels return to the commons if they go unimproved for 90 days, and a rotating jury of three neighbours decides what counts as 'improved' and whether the leftover parcels really are as good. Walk me through how this actually behaves, not whether it's fair. What does it do to the order and timing in which people claim land, to what people do to count as 'improving' land cheaply, and to how 'same quality' gets measured once the best plots start disappearing? Where does collusion between early settlers show up first, what happens when parcels get inherited by people who don't want to work them, and where does the whole scheme break? Can the failures be fixed without giving up either the labour requirement or the proviso? Also please answer in British English.
