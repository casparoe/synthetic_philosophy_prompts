I cover science and ideas for a living, and I keep returning to a tension I cannot dissolve on my own. On one side is a position I find increasingly hard to resist, sometimes called lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. As I understand it, the view says this: suffering comes in degrees of severity, and above a certain threshold it is not simply a very large amount of bad but something lexically weightier — no quantity of well-being below the threshold, no matter how enormous or widely distributed, can compensate for a single instance of above-threshold suffering. Below the threshold, pleasures and pains trade off against one another in the ordinary way. The view attracts me because it lets me say, without hedging, that the comfortable happiness of a great many people cannot justly be set against one person's agony, while avoiding the more familiar, unqualified negative utilitarianism, which seems to make even a mild pain lexically prior to any amount of happiness. On the other side is a plain and stubborn intuition: that pleasure and pain are, in the end, commensurable. For any finite episode of pain, however severe, there must be some amount of happiness — perhaps astronomically large, or spread across enough lives — whose addition would leave the whole at least as good as it would have been without the pain. I can make the intuition vivid: set a single bounded episode of excruciating pain against a subsequent lifetime of flourishing, and the refusal of every such exchange seems to me less like moral seriousness than like a failure to do the arithmetic. I do not want to be told which side should win. I want to know what would have to be true for the intuition to be the thing that gives, what would have to be true for the theory to be the thing that gives, and whether there is a reading of 'outweigh' or 'compensate' on which the lexical threshold and the commensurability intuition can both stand.
