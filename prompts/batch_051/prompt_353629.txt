I'm in a philosophy of law course, and we've been on sex work for the last couple weeks, and I keep circling back to something that feels underdiscussed in the readings. The Nordic model makes selling sex legal but buying it a crime: the seller is treated as a victim who can't be punished, the buyer as the exploiter, with the official story being that prostitution is always exploitation and demand has to be eliminated. But the same states that adopt that model also let people consent to plenty of risky, dignity-compromising things like boxing, selling plasma, deep-sea fishing, or signing massive binding contracts, where the state doesn't step in and void your consent. So what theory of choice and coercion does the Nordic model actually presuppose, as opposed to merely claim? Does it strictly presuppose that a person cannot genuinely consent to sell sex even when they insist they do, or just the weaker claim that the state can't reliably tell willing sellers from coerced ones? And is that presupposition consistent with the rest of a liberal legal system? If I were writing a short paper on it, I'd want to structure it as: reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the law strictly requires from what it merely fits with, and say what other policies you'd have to change or accept changing if you swapped that theory for the one New Zealand's decriminalization model seems to presuppose. Is that framing right, and what should the best version of the answer actually say?
