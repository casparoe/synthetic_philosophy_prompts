I am a retired scientist — chemistry and physics, mostly — and I have recently begun reading philosophy of science and cognitive psychology. My attention keeps returning to a puzzle from my teaching years. It was not that students lacked information; it was that their errors had a shape. They were systematic, confidently held, and oddly resistant to correction: the heavier body that must fall faster, the winter coat that gives off heat, the battery that empties like a fuel tank. I have since learned that researchers disagree about what this shape is — whether each misconception is a fairly coherent alternative theory, a miscategorization of a thing into the wrong ontological category, or an on-the-fly assembly of many small, semi-independent pieces of knowledge. Before I read further, I want the standard of evaluation itself.

Please write the desiderata for any adequate theory of systematic student errors in science. Take the theory's purpose to be the interpretation of student reasoning and the design of instruction. Give me the requirements: which phenomena it must explain, which distinctions it must respect, which cases it must get right, and which empirical findings it must be consistent with — and, for each requirement, the case or finding that motivates it. I am particularly interested in requirements that would have to handle the following observations, though you should add others you think necessary:

(a) the same student can answer one problem correctly and a near-identical problem with the misconception, minutes apart;
(b) some errors survive years of explicit instruction while others disappear after one demonstration;
(c) many student errors resemble positions once seriously held in the history of science, such as impetus theories of projectile motion or a substance-like view of heat;
(d) some so-called errors are productive approximations that serve students well in everyday settings.

Then flag any pairs of desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories.

Finally, score briefly — a sentence or two each — the alternative-theory view, the miscategorization view, and the small-independent-pieces view against your list. Keep that section short; I am mainly after the desiderata.
