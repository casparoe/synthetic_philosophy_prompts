I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I spend much of the day reasoning about abstractions that are not physically present but predict system behaviour well; that is probably why a colleague's argument recently struck me as plausible. The colleague was arguing about whether a person's identity could survive radical memory loss, and he offered this analogy: the self is a centre of narrative gravity. His version ran roughly as follows. The centre of gravity of a physical object is not a material component of the object; it is an abstract point, computed from the distribution of mass, that lets us describe and predict how the object behaves. Likewise, the self is not a thing situated inside the brain or body; it is an abstraction over the stream of stories that a person and other people tell about that person's actions, commitments, and history. From this he concluded that selves are useful fictions rather than real entities, and that praising or blaming a self is a category mistake, in the same way that praising or blaming a centre of gravity when a beam tips over would be.

I can feel the analogy doing some work, but I suspect it quietly imports features of physical systems into talk about persons, and I want the mapping tested rather than left implicit. Please audit the analogy in detail. In particular:

2. Identify the disanalogies: the respects in which a narrative self is unlike a centre of gravity, being specific about which properties fail to carry over.
3. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the colleague's conclusion from those that are merely superficial. For example, a centre of gravity can lie outside a physical object; is that a harmless difference or one that affects the conclusion?

Say whether the conclusion survives: does the analogy support the claims that selves are fictions and that praise and blame are category mistakes, or does it smuggle in assumptions about fictionality and responsibility that the source domain cannot carry? If only a weakened version survives, say what it is.
5. If a better analogy exists, propose one and say what it captures that this one misses.
