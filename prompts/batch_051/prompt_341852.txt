Here are my verdicts about cases involving the infinite. I would like you to find the principle, if there is one, that captures them, state it precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and then apply it to two cases I have not settled.

1. In Hilbert's hotel, a new guest is accommodated by moving the occupant of room n to room n+1 for every natural number n. I accept that maneuver as fully coherent, and I draw the further moral that an infinite totality may be put into one-to-one correspondence with one of its proper parts. Nothing about that seems paradoxical to me.

2. When Galileo observes that the natural numbers can be paired with their squares by the mapping n maps to n squared, I conclude that the natural numbers and the perfect squares have the same cardinality, and that the ancient part-whole principle simply fails for infinite totalities.

3. I accept Cantor's diagonal argument as proving a conditional: whenever someone presents an infinite list of infinite binary sequences, there is an infinite binary sequence not on that list. But I do not accept the further claim that there exists a set of all infinite binary sequences with a cardinality greater than that of the natural numbers. I am willing to say that the list is always incomplete; I am unwilling to pass from that to the existence of a completed totality of all such sequences. The step from no list is complete to there is a completed set of all sequences is precisely the step I will not take.

4. I am comfortable with the set of all natural numbers as a completed object, and equally with the set of all finite binary strings and the set of all finite subsets of the natural numbers. I recognize that the set of all finite subsets of the naturals is itself an infinite totality. My position therefore cannot be a flat refusal to countenance completed infinities.

5. I reject the existence of the set of all arbitrary subsets of the natural numbers. My difficulty is with the word arbitrary. Most subsets are not specified by any rule I could state, and I cannot see what all subsets means except by an appeal to a completed arbitrary infinite, which I do not accept.

6. I use real analysis without discomfort. I accept the least-upper-bound axiom, theorems that quantify over all real numbers, and the standard definition of a limit. Yet when the reals are described as a set, I want to say that the phrase the set of real numbers is convenient shorthand for a rule-like procedure of approximation, not reference to a completed totality.

The challenge is that my verdicts distinguish among infinite totalities in a way that is not obviously principled. I accept the set of natural numbers and the set of finite subsets of the naturals while rejecting the set of infinite binary sequences and the full power set of the naturals. If the principle is accept exactly the countably infinite totalities, that explains verdicts 3 through 5 but leaves my view of the real numbers unstable, since the reals are uncountable and yet I rely on them daily. If the principle is accept a totality only when its elements can be generated by an effective rule, then the finite subsets fit, but I need to know whether the set of all computable functions from N to N fits, and whether the real numbers fit.

What is the best principle that captures at least most of my verdicts? Please state it precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and tell me what it says about two further cases I cannot settle: (i) the set of all computable functions from N to N, and (ii) the set of all real numbers whose decimal expansion is generated by a computer program. I would also like to know whether, under your proposed principle, my acceptance of the reals as shorthand is a stable compromise or just equivocation.
