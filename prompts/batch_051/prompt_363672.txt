My dissertation advisor works on technological standards and path dependence, and she is unusually precise about when an outcome counts as locked in rather than merely path dependent. I have collected the following rulings from her, with the facts exactly as she treats them, since her criterion concerns the structure of the case rather than the details of the real histories.

1. The Vandermeer gauge. In the 1890s two railway gauges, 1.4 meters and 1.7 meters, were introduced and were near-equal in construction cost and safety. The first large trunk line adopted the 1.7-meter gauge because its chief engineer happened to have trained on a 1.7-meter tramway; the early majority then converged on that gauge. Today the 1.4-meter gauge would be modestly better in fuel economy and ride quality, but converting the track, rolling stock, and platforms would be ruinously expensive. Her verdict: locked in.

2. The Halver process. Two industrial processes for making plate glass, Halver and Conwick, were patented within a year of each other in the 1920s. Conwick was from the start decisively cheaper and produced clearer glass, and by 1940 no major firm still used Halver. Her verdict: not locked in; the better process simply won.

3. The Sorel notation. Two notation systems for a then-new keyboard instrument, Sorel and Pinter, were introduced in the 1960s and were about equally legible and learnable. A conservatory curriculum committee chose Sorel by a coin flip, and Sorel remains the more common system. But publishers print in both notations, players can be retrained in a term, and no instrument depends on either notation. Her verdict: path dependent but not locked in; the early accident had no self-reinforcing consequences.

4. The Hedland units. A system of electrical units, Hedland, was the only fully worked-out system when the industry organized around it in 1900. The later rivals were, at every relevant moment, strictly less complete, and no comparable alternative ever stood for adoption. Hedland remains at least as serviceable as the alternatives, and the sunk investments in it are enormous. Her verdict: not locked in; there was no moment at which a comparable rival lost out, so the installed base is just the normal cost of having adopted the one workable standard.

The new case is the Brattle citation format. A scholarly field adopted Brattle in the early 2000s over its rival, Merrow. At adoption the two were about equal in usability. Brattle won because the largest journal's editors happened to prefer it and adopted it first. Today nearly every journal requires Brattle, reference managers assume it, and the accumulated records are keyed to it. Controlled comparisons now find Brattle and Merrow exactly equal in usability, learnability, and error rates; neither is better. Rewriting the accumulated corpus and retraining authors and editors would be technically feasible but expensive.

I would like to know what she would say about the Brattle format: is it locked in, path dependent but not locked in, or something else? I am less interested in the label than in the underlying rule. Please (a) reconstruct the most plausible rules that fit her four rulings, (b) say which candidate rules agree on the four earlier cases but divide on Brattle and why, (c) give your best prediction of her verdict on Brattle, with the reasons for preferring that rule, and (d) propose one further case that would most cleanly discriminate between the remaining candidate rules.
