I am preparing a unit on just war theory and want to pair Walzer and McMahan on the moral equality of combatants with Shakespeare's Henry V. The play seems to me to dramatize the very question at issue: whether the justice of the war as a whole and the conduct of the individual soldier can be separated, so that a common soldier on the unjust side may kill as honorably as one on the just side. On the one hand, the disguised Henry's argument with Williams and Bates on the eve of Agincourt stages a debate over who must answer for the cause: the soldiers insist the king bears the reckoning if the war is unjust, while Henry replies that the king cannot answer for each soldier's soul. On the other hand, the play surrounds that scene with material that complicates any comfortable separation, including the cynical clerical justification for the invasion, the threatened sack of Harfleur, the order to kill the prisoners, and the prayer in which Henry himself seems haunted by the dubiousness of his title. I would like you to write the lecture notes I could circulate: a reading of Henry V through the moral equality debate that spells out exactly how the Williams–Bates exchange maps onto Walzer and the revisionist challenge, discusses at least two other scenes that either support or resist the play's being read as endorsing the moral equality of combatants, and concludes by saying what the play contributes to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone do not. Keep the reading faithful in both directions: do not reduce the play to a position paper, and do not distort the philosophy to fit the play; be honest about the places where the two pull apart.
