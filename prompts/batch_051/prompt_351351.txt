I've been rereading Borges's "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote" for a little reading group, and I want someone to help me squeeze the actual philosophy of language out of it rather than just treat it as a clever conceit. The surface lesson everybody takes is that the same string of words can mean something completely different depending on who writes it and when: Menard's chapters are word-for-word identical to Cervantes's, but the narrator says they're far richer, more subtle, and almost anachronistic in Menard's mouth. Fine. But I'm stuck on a deeper question about polysemy and semantic range. If words have a fairly stable range of admissible meanings and context just selects among them, then the two Quixotes can diverge a lot while still being recognizably the same words at some level. But if meaning is purely use — nothing to a word over and above what it does on an occasion — then what's left for the two texts to be identical in? The joke seems to need a stable verbal core to get off the ground, which is exactly what the radical reading says doesn't exist.

So read the story through that lens and be an honest critic in both directions. What does Borges actually dramatize about whether words have meanings, and what does his narrator quietly presuppose that maybe undercuts the whole thing? I don't want a summary and I don't want the usual "meaning is context" truism. I want to know: which specific moments would a use-theorist of language feel vindicated by, where would a semantic minimalist object that Borges has conflated meaning with significance, tone, or felt association, and is there a reading on which the two texts differ in exactly the way two uses of a polysemous word differ without there being any deep fact about meaning that changes? Bonus if you can say what the story adds to the debate that a paper on contextualism can't deliver, since the story is doing something with it's own status as a text too.
