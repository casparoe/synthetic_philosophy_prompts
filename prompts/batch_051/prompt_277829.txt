I'm doing this project for school where we each got assigned an 18th/19th century thinker and have to predict what they'd think about a modern issue that didn't exist yet. I got Jeremy Bentham, which I figured would be easy because he's the utilitarian guy: actions are right when they produce more pleasure than pain, and he even came up with a kind of checklist for adding up the consequences. But I want to make an actual argument, not just say he'd proably be into it because he liked pleasure.

My actual topic is cryonics and the rationality of betting on revival. People pay alot of money, or take out a life insurance policy, to have their body or just their head frozen after legal death, betting that future technology will be able to revive and repair them. Some people say this is a rational expected-value gamble because a tiny chance of centuries more life could still be worth the price, while other people say the odds are basically zero and the money would do more good elsewhere. Bentham feels relevant since he actually had his own dead body preserved as an auto-icon and put on display, which is already somewhat cryonics-adjacent, and his felicific calculus included categories like intensity, duration, certainty or uncertainty, how remote in time the pleasure is, and how likely it is to lead to more pleasure down the line.

Would Bentham think cryonics is a rationally defensible bet if he applied his own calculus to it? Would he sign up himself, and would he see it as morally fine to spend that money on his own tiny chance instead of on living people who could be helped now? Would he care about waking up centuries later into a world where everyone he knew is dead and his skills are obsolete? And would he want the government to regulate cryonics companies, or leave people free to take the gamble? Use the actual machinery of his view—the calculus categories and what you know about his auto-icon and his attitude toward death—not just the generic utilitarian slogan.
