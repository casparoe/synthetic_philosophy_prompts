In your co-taught graduate seminar on classical pragmatism, you and a colleague have spent the term defending rival readings of the pragmatist theory of truth and talking past each other. You hold a Peircean view: truth is the opinion to which an indefinitely continued community of inquirers would ultimately converge if it pursued the method of science in the long run, so that a belief's truth consists in its relation to that ideal end rather than in any present satisfaction. Your colleague holds a Jamesian view: truth is assessed by its practical workings — a belief is true insofar as it proves itself in experience, guides action satisfactorily, and gets confirmed as we use it, so that beliefs become true through verification rather than matching a truth fixed independently of us. The department chair has asked you to produce the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration: a document you can both sign without retracting your positions. Write that document, containing (1) a numbered list of claims about inquiry, verification, experience, and reality that both of you accept; (2) a table of residual disagreements stated in wording each side would accept as its own, explicitly separating merely verbal differences from genuine ones; and (3) the genuine cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of you. For each crux, provide a concrete case or body of evidence that both sides agree bears on it, and state in advance what the Peircean and the Jamesian each predict about it. I expect the cruxes will include whether a once-successful scientific theory that is later abandoned was ever true, whether a religious or ethical belief that works in the believer's life but cannot be settled by scientific inquiry can be true, and whether truth is constituted once and for all by the final opinion or is made piecemeal through each episode of verification.
