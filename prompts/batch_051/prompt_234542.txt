Counterfactual I've been arguing about with a friend: it's 2050, and a machine exists that can produce an atom-for-atom replica of any painting — same pigment chemistry, same isotope ratios, same craquelure pattern, same 17th-century canvas fibres, even the same traces of the artist's DNA in the medium. Every physical test a lab can throw at it comes back saying "original", no matter which canvas you test. The one thing the machine can't copy is causal history: one canvas was worked by the artist's hand in, say, 1657, and the other was assembled last Tuesday. The machine is cheap enough that within a decade it's use is widespread, and provenance records — receipts, gallery labels, old photographs — can still be faked the ordinary way, so chains of custody are now the only thing standing between an original and a crowd of copies. My question: over the next two or three generations, what happens to the value of originals and to the institutions built on them (auction houses, museums, insurers, the courts)? I'm less interested in a yes/no or a story than in the reasoning: walk me through the mechanisms, tell me which effects you think are robust and which are guesses, attach a rough confidence to each link in the chain, and say what assumption about why we value originals in the first place would have to be true for your prediction to be wrong.
