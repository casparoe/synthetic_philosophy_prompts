So I've been reporting on debates about art forgery, and I keep coming back to a weird feature of how we actually treat fakes. A forger can copy a painting so well that experts can't tell them apart by eye — this happens with Han van Meegeren's *Vermeer* and plenty of later cases — and still the works get treated as worthless once the attribution collapses, sometimes literally moved from gallery wall to basement. So it's not that we value the object just for its visual pattern. Fine.

But then I read Walter Benjamin's "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" and got stuck on this passage, where he's trying to pin down what fades under mechanical reproduction:

"Even the most perfect reproduction of a work of art is lacking in one element: its presence in time and space, its unique existence at the place where it happens to be. This unique existence of the work of art determined the history to which it was subject throughout the time of its existence. This includes the changes which it may have suffered in physical condition over the years as well as the various changes in its ownership."

Here's my problem. If the value of an original comes from its unique history and spatiotemporal presence, then a newly discovered "lost original" should carry that aura even before anyone knows its history, just from being the thing that was at the relevant places. But in practice, a painting that spends decades unrecognized in a church storage room gets no special treatment until the provenance is established. And a perfect old copy of a famous picture — a studio work from the 17th century, say — has just as much physical history as the original, but nobody cares. So the practice seems to presuppose that what matters isn't the object's actual history but our ability to know it and document it.

So what's the implicit theory here? Is it really that value lives in the physical object's biographical continuity, or is it that value lives in the certified attribution — in a kind of institutional fact about the object? I'm trying to figure out whether the art world's behavior around fakes and copies is philosophically coherent, or whether it's running on two incompatible commitments at once. If the Benjamin view is right, shouldn't an undocumented original be valuable the moment it's genuine? If the institutional view is right, doesn't that mean a good provenance is doing something almost magical — endowing the very same physical thing with new value without changing any of its visually accessible properties?
