So I've been reading up on Frederick Douglass for a little discussion group I do with some people from work, and I keep tripping over what looks like a pretty sharp U-turn. In the 1840s he seemed to take the line that the US Constitution was a proslavery deal, something he likened to a pact with death and a bargain with hell, and he refused to vote or hold office because doing so would just make you complicit in slavery. Then by the early 1850s he'd broken with Garrison and started saying the opposite: the Constitution, if you just read what's actually on its face, is a liberty document, not a slavery document, and voting can be a legitimate weapon against slavery. He even gave a whole speech in Glasgow in 1860 were he says the real question isn't whether slaveholders helped write it or whether courts used it to prop up slavery, but whether the text itself actually guarantees anyone a right to hold property in another person. To me these two positions sound like they can't both be true. Are they actually in tension, or is there a way to read them as consistent? If he did change his mind, what exactly changed, and why isn't that just him rationalizing a different political strategy? If he didn't change his mind, what did he think he was doing differently in the later period? I'd want the candidate reconciliations and then an honest verdict on which one is least like special pleading.
