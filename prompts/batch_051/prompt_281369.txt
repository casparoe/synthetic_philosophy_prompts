As a retired fluid dynamicist who has lately been spending time in the philosophy of computation, I keep turning over one idea. Suppose that, starting tomorrow, it became an established empirical result—as well confirmed as anything in turbulence—that a large class of far-from-equilibrium systems are computationally irreducible: for any such system, no simulation can determine its state at a future time faster than the system itself would get there, and the shortest adequate description of one of its histories is the history itself. Suppose the class includes turbulent flows, the dynamics of a living cell, and the response of a regional economy to a novel intervention; general relativity, arithmetic, and other exactly solvable or classically tractable systems are deliberately out of scope.

Your task is to predict, with reasoning made explicit, how the sciences of such systems look after thirty years. I am less interested in a story than in the chain of mechanisms. Which lines of research survive, and which quietly die? What does "understanding" a turbulent flow or a cell come to mean once explanation-by-compression is unavailable? Does the ambition to predict give way to cataloguing initial conditions, to intervention and control, to coarse-grained or statistical regularities, or to something else? How do funding agencies, journals, and the prestige hierarchy between theory and experiment reorganize—or fail to reorganize—around the new limits? For each link in your argument, say whether you regard it as robust or speculative, which of your overall predictions you are most and least confident of, and what further assumptions would have to hold for the prediction to go wrong.
