Consider the following asymmetry in how we assign responsibility, and whether anything actually justifies it.

When an individual commits a serious wrong — a murder, an act of fraud, a dispossession — we treat the guilt as attaching to that person alone. We would not call her grandchildren guilty, would not expect them to apologize on their own behalf, and would regard a demand that they pay restitution out of their own earnings as a second injustice rather than as a legitimate extension of her liability.

Yet when the wrongdoer is a nation, we do almost exactly these things a generation or more later. We say the nation is guilty, expect its present government to apologize and to pay reparations, and often hold that citizens born after the events share a national shame or responsibility simply because it is their country. In both cases the living individuals committed no act; the difference is that we reject inherited individual guilt while broadly accepting inherited national responsibility.

Question: is there a defensible difference between the collective case and the individual case, or is the collective practice a convenient fiction we keep only because durable institutions let us collect the debt? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the strongest version of the individual case — for example, a person who not only inherits wealth produced by her ancestor's wrong but continues to enjoy it, or a family firm whose present partners keep the profits of a past generation's fraud. If you attack it, say what should change: should later generations be treated as carrying no responsibility at all, and would that undermine demands for reparations, apologies, or national shame as well?

A useful tool here is Jaspers's postwar analysis of German guilt, which separates criminal guilt (individual, for punishable offenses), political guilt (borne by citizens for the deeds of their state), moral guilt (assessed by conscience for what a person personally did or failed to do), and metaphysical guilt (a solidarity with the wronged that arises from not risking one's life to prevent evil). Notice that on Jaspers's own view a whole people cannot be guilty in the criminal sense; the scheme exists partly to explain what else the phrase national guilt could mean. Use this decomposition, or something like it, to locate exactly where the cross-generational burden is supposed to fall, and ask whether it can be placed in any of those categories without collapsing into the inherited individual guilt we claim to reject.
