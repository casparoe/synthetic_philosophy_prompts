I am a PhD student in comparative political thought, writing a chapter on the classical dispute between Legalist and Confucian views of political order. My focus is Han Feizi's claim that a ruler should secure order not by relying on his own virtue or that of his officials, but by publishing clear standards (fa), holding positional power (shi), and applying the two handles of reward and punishment so that self-interest alone keeps officials and subjects in line. In trying to reconstruct the Confucian objection, I produced the following argument, and now I cannot tell whether it is a genuine refutation or an overreaching inference. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

(1) A legalist order is meant to run on incentives rather than character: the ruler issues public standards and applies the two handles of reward and punishment to officials and subjects, so that conduct serving order is made profitable and conduct subverting it is made costly.

(2) For the two handles to produce order rather than arbitrary compliance, they must be attached to the right behaviors: rewards must fall on conduct that in fact serves order, and punishments on conduct that in fact undermines it.

(3) Attaching the handles correctly requires someone to judge, in advance of any institutional incentive, which behaviors serve order and which subvert it; the handles themselves do not indicate how they should be directed.

(4) The same is true after the standards are issued: every general law must be applied to particular cases by an official who judges whether this case is of the kind the law was meant to reach, and no further standard can remove that judgment, on pain of an infinite regress of rules for applying rules.

(5) A stable disposition to make such judgments well, about both the design and the application of the standards, is not mere rule-following; it is a practical wisdom directed at good order, exercised even where the agent's own immediate incentives do not dictate it.

(6) That disposition is the sort of thing the Confucian tradition means by virtue: the cultivated perception of what is fitting (yi) and a settled commitment to humane and well-ordered relations (ren, li).

(7) Therefore any functioning legalist order presupposes rulers and officials who possess the relevant virtue; legalism has not replaced virtue with incentives but has hidden virtue inside the judgment that selects and applies the incentives.

(8) So the legalist thesis, that a durable order can be secured without reliance on virtue, is false, and by the same reasoning every rule-based political order, including modern constitutional government, turns out to depend on the virtue of the officials who run it.

The final clause of (8) is stronger than I wanted. The argument seems to prove not only that Han Feizi was wrong, but that any legal order is secretly a virtue-regime. I cannot tell whether that broad result is the point or a sign that something has gone wrong earlier. If forced to choose, I would like to keep (2) and (4): I do not see how an order could survive misdirected incentives, and I cannot imagine standards that apply themselves. But I would rather learn what those commitments cost before I accept where the argument leads.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Please give me a diagnosis: either identify the premise or inferential step you reject, or say that the argument is valid and tell me which premise it is least costly to abandon. Then explain what that choice commits a Legalist and a Confucian respectively to, and whether the sweeping result in (8) survives once the repair is made. Keep the diagnosis to no more than 750 words.
