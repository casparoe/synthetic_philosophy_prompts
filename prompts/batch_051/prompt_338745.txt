Defamation law treats false statements of fact as actionable but shields expressions of opinion. Ratings and forecasts—a one-star restaurant review, a credit downgrade, an analyst's price target—sit uneasily between the two: they are plainly evaluative, yet they embed factual claims and can be confuted by later data. Reconstruct what theory of the fact/opinion distinction this legal practice presupposes, and assess whether the theory is coherent. In particular, does protecting a rating or forecast as opinion commit the law to the view that evaluative statements are neither true nor false, or merely to the view that they are not the sort of thing a court can adjudicate? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what the doctrine would have to change if it instead drew the line at verifiability, or at whether the statement implies checkable underlying facts.
