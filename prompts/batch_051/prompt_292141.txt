Suppose a major video-sharing platform adopts the following rule for content that reproduces state-controlled or extremist propaganda for the stated purpose of criticism, journalism, or teaching.

Propaganda re-uploads are removed by default. An upload qualifies for the criticism exception only if it is presented as a "co-extensive critique": (1) the original propaganda footage is broken into segments of at most two minutes, and each segment is paired with substantive criticism specific to that segment — rebuttal, contextualization, or analysis — rather than an introductory warning or a short debunking attached to a long original; (2) the platform serves the criticism and the propaganda as a single unit, so that no user can watch a propaganda segment without also encountering its paired criticism in the same session; and (3) the package is excluded from algorithmic recommendation and is discoverable only through search terms that name the propaganda source or the critic. Clean re-uploads remain removed, and the platform disables downloading of the original footage from within the package, though screen capture remains possible.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral position. Walk through the incentives it creates for critics and journalists, propagandists and their sympathizers, ordinary viewers, and the platform itself. Where does it get gamed first? Consider second-order effects: how the rule changes what "criticism" looks like, how it changes propaganda designed to survive segment-level rebuttal, what it does to the evidentiary value of the primary source, and whether the search-only discoverability rule makes the exception self-defeating. Finally, say which failure modes could be repaired by adjusting the rule, and which would require abandoning its central idea.
