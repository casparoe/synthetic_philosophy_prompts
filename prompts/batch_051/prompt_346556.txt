I'm taking a course on ancient ethics, and something in Aristotle keeps bugging me because it seems to map directly onto an argument I keep having with a friend who designs habit-tracking software. In the Nicomachean Ethics, somewhere in Book VII, Aristotle distinguishes the continent person from the temperate person. The continent person has bad appetites — he genuinely wants the wrong things, or wants them too much — but he follows reason and refuses to act on them. The temperate person is in a different condition: he doesn't have those appetites in the first place, or at least doesn't experience them as powerful temptations. Aristotle seems to think the temperate person is better off, and not merely because restraint is costly. The continent person is still split against himself, while the temperate person's desires have come into line with his judgment about what's good.

My friend's app treats self-control almost entirely as a struggle: streak counters, urge-surfing exercises, pre-commitment locks, "beat yesterday" badges. It never frames the goal as becoming the sort of person who isn't tempted at all, and when I brought up Aristotle he said that's just impractical — you have to work with the user as they are, not as they'd be after some moral transformation. But that's exactly what makes me think the app has a philosophy built into it: it treats effortful resistance as the default model of self-control, and maybe treats inner harmony as either unrealistic or unnecessary. What theory of self-control does this kind of design presuppose? Is it actually committed to that theory, or is it compatible with an Aristotelian picture where continence is at best a stepping stone to temperance? And what would a design aimed at temperance have to change in practice?
