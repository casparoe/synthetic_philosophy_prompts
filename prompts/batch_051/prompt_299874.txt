You are a professional philosopher who works on the methodology of doing good, with particular expertise in the epistemology of causal inference about social interventions and in the debates between effective altruism and systems-change approaches. You are serving as a neutral, good-faith facilitator editing a joint statement for an adversarial collaboration between two camps that have partly been talking past each other. Think carefully and work through the structure before you draft; make the final document precise enough that each side would sign its own wording.

The two parties are:

(A) The marginal-interventions camp. On their view, decisions about how to do good should be made by comparing explicit expected value across candidate actions, where value is cashed out in welfare-relevant outcomes. They privilege interventions with short causal chains, existing rigorous evidence, measurable results, and room for iterative learning. They default to ranking causes by some combination of scale, neglectedness, and tractability, and they are skeptical of structural reform as a primary object because evidence is weak, attribution is difficult, and the risk of wasted effort or unintended harm is high.

(B) The systems-change camp. On their view, the dominant determinants of large-scale suffering are self-stabilizing features of institutions, markets, norms, and distributions of power, so interventions that optimize within the existing system are systematically bounded. They argue that marginal interventions are often absorbed by the system through fungibility, displacement, or reduced pressure for structural change: for example, a program that alleviates a symptom can weaken the constituency for fixing the underlying cause, and money is fungible enough that local gains can be offset elsewhere. They regard the demand for short causal chains and measurement as an epistemic bias in favor of small evaluable effects over large uncertain ones, and they hold that a different methodology is needed, built on leverage-point analysis, historical and institutional comparison, explicit theories of change, and scenario reasoning under deep uncertainty.

They are not denouncing each other. Both care about improving lives at scale and both accept that good intentions and naive do-gooding are insufficient. They disagree about what epistemic discipline should look like when an intervention is hard to evaluate.

Draft the joint statement such a collaboration could produce. It must include the following parts:

1. A short preamble stating the shared problem in terms both parties would accept.

2. A numbered list of agreed claims, phrased so that neither side would object to a single clause. The agreed claims should be substantial and non-vacuous, for example about the reality of systemic constraints, the limits of naive cost-effectiveness analysis, the place of expected-welfare reasoning, the need for feedback and learning, and the inadequacy of pure intention. Avoid the kind of bland consensus that simply hides the disagreement.

3. A disagreements table with the columns: Issue; Marginal-interventions camp; Systems-change camp; Substantive or verbal? Each camp's entry must be written in wording that camp would sign. Include at least these rows: (i) the role of explicit expected-value calculation when causal chains are long; (ii) how to model fungibility, displacement, and the absorption of marginal gains; (iii) whether scale, neglectedness, and tractability can be estimated with enough reliability for structural interventions; (iv) whether the demand for measurable outcomes is a neutral epistemic filter or a bias toward small, evaluable effects; (v) how one should respond to deep uncertainty or cluelessness about long-run and indirect effects.

4. A cruxes section. A crux is a sub-question such that, if one side conceded it, at least one party would substantially update. For each crux, state the crux precisely; describe the evidence or argument both sides agree would bear on it, such as historical comparison, prospective registration of theories of change, natural experiments, structural modeling, case studies, or forecasting; and record each side's prediction in advance. Include at least these two cruxes: whether advocacy and institutional-reform campaigns have ex ante detectable expected value high enough to compete with the best direct-delivery interventions, and whether the largest durable welfare gains in modern history came mainly from structural transformation or from incremental, measurable improvements.

5. A short note identifying any part of the dispute that dissolves once terms are clarified, together with the restatement both sides would accept.

Constraints: Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation; the document should stand on the arguments themselves. Do not write a dramatized dialogue and do not adjudicate who is right. The aim is a constructive text both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than exaggerated or smoothed over. If a formulation could only be signed by one side, revise it or move it into the disagreements table.
