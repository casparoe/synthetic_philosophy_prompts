Take Sweden's switch from left-hand to right-hand traffic on 3 September 1967 as a case study in David Lewis's theory of convention. The salient facts: a 1955 referendum showed a large majority opposed to the change, yet parliament enacted it anyway, and after years of preparing signage, markings, and vehicles the conversion was carried out over a single Sunday. Analyze the episode within Lewis's framework, on which a convention is an arbitrary, self-reproducing regularity that solves a recurring coordination problem and is sustained by common knowledge and mutual expectations rather than primarily by sanctions or commands. Say exactly where driving on a particular side fits this model and where the Swedish case resists it, attending especially to the central planning of the switch, the role of law and enforcement in stabilizing the new equilibrium, and whether the old and new practices are better described as two distinct conventions or one convention whose content changed. Then offer a committed verdict on what the episode shows about the theory—confirmation, anomaly, or something it can handle only with amendments—and say what it implies for the difference between conventions that drift and conventions that are replaced by decision. Indicate which steps in your argument are most and least certain.
