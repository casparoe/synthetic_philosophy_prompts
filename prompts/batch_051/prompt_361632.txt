A friend and I were reading Aristotle's Posterior Analytics Book I Part 3, where he argues that demonstration can't go on to infinity and can't be circular, so there have to be primary immediate premises. He says something like: if you only know the conclusion by demonstration from premises, and those premises are known only by further demonstration, and so on forever, you'd never actually know anything by demonstration. We tried to turn that into a modern epistemic argument and got into a fight about it. Here's the argument in my words:

(1) Every justified belief gets its justification from being supported by another justified belief.
(2) If p gets its justification from being supported by q, then q must already be justified.
(3) So for any justified belief p, there has to be an endless chain p because q, q because r, r because s, and so on with no bottom.
(4) But no belief in an endless chain is actually justified, because justification is always borrowed from higher up the chain, and in an endless chain there's nowhere the borrowing starts.
(5) Therefore, no belief is justified.

My friend says the argument is valid and the conclusion is fine, since Aristotle would just say we know some things non-inferentially. I think step (4) is doing something sneaky, but I can't say exactly what. Is the argument valid? If it isn't, which step fails? And if you were an infinitist who wanted to reject (4), what would the strongest version of that rejection look like—without just asserting that infinite chains can justify?
