I cover AI safety for a general-interest magazine, and before I interview a couple of researchers tomorrow I want to make sure I've got a specific debate straight. I keep hearing people defend the narrow framing of the alignment problem: build systems that do what their creators actualy intend, and leave the question of "the right values" for later. The objections to that framing all start to blur together for me, but I think I've isolated four. (1) The wicked-operator argument: a system that executes the genuine intentions of a bad or deeply misinformed operator still produces terrible outcomes, so intent alignment can't be the notion of safety anyone ultimately wants. (2) The unstable-intent argument: what a person intends changes over time, so "align to intention" just moves the problem to "which version of the person?". (3) The many-principals argument: deployed systems serve a lot of people whose intentions conflict, so intent alignment quietly assumes an answer to whose intentions win. (4) The specification argument: you can't point a system at an intention directly, only at the proxy you can actually write down, so intent alignment inherits the whole problem of proxies missing the real target. I want one more argument that the narrow framing can't be a clean stopping point—genuinely distinct from these four, not a rewording, special case, or combination. Think hard about whether your candidate is really just one of mine in disguise.
