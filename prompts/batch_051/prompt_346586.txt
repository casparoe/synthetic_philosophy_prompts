In a philosophy reading group I attend, two members spent much of the evening on W. K. Clifford's essay "The Ethics of Belief." The passage they kept returning to was this:

"Let us alter the case a little, and suppose that the ship was not unsound after all; that she made her voyage safely, and many others after it. Will that diminish the guilt of her owner? Not one jot. When an action is once done, it is right or wrong for ever; no accidental failure of its good or evil fruits can possibly alter that. The man would not have been innocent, he would only have been not found out. The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him."

Elena took this to express a fully general principle: an epistemic wrong is committed whenever a person forms a belief on insufficient evidence, regardless of the belief's content or later outcome. Marcus called that a misreading of Clifford's own examples, which all involve people whose beliefs put others at risk. For private, self-regarding commitments, he thought the William James line from "The Will to Believe" still stands: some options are genuine only for those who commit to them before the evidence is in, and a blanket prohibition on unproven belief would paralyze practical life. Elena replied that this just relocates the problem, because Jamesian commitments are formed by willing confidence where evidence is lacking, which is precisely what Clifford says is stolen rather than honestly earned. Marcus answered that Clifford cannot mean "believe nothing in advance," since even ordinary agency relies on assumptions that outrun evidence; if Elena retreats to the claim that only felt convictions are governed by the principle, she is drawing a psychological line that Clifford's own argument gives no reason to accept. Elena said their disagreement was exactly about that line: Clifford's rule applies to all beliefs, while practical assumptions qualify at most as tentative hypotheses, not convictions. Marcus objected that the distinction cannot do moral work, since a person who acts on a reckless assumption without explicitly "believing" it seems no less culpable than the shipowner. Elena said his objection actually proves Clifford's point, because culpability attaches to the way the state of mind was formed, not to what we call it. At that point they were going in circles.

They asked me for a verdict, and I am not confident I can separate the strands. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: identify the strongest and weakest claims on each side, say what distinction or commitment each would need to make their overall position coherent, and then state who is more right overall, if either, about the scope of Clifford's principle.
