The debate over the Liar paradox has been running for nearly a century, and, as far as I can tell, nobody in it moves. Everyone starts from the same sentence—one that says of itself that it is false—and everyone agrees that if it is true it is false, and if it is false it is true. Everyone also accepts that the obvious pattern of reasoning behind this, namely that a sentence is true just in case things are as the sentence says, is what produces the trouble, and that some price must be paid. The disagreement is over which price. One family holds that truth is to be defined only relative to a hierarchy of languages, so that no sentence ever predicates truth of itself. A second treats the Liar as a truth-value gap, neither true nor false, and constructs fixed points over partial languages in which truth can coherently apply to its own sentences. A third treats the Liar as a glut, both true and false, and holds that the surrounding logic must tolerate nontrivial contradictions. A fourth treats the extension of the truth predicate as context-sensitive, shifting with the circumstances of evaluation in a way that dissolves the contradiction.

Each of these views, in turn, is met by its rivals with a revenge sentence: a sentence built from the view's own machinery that says, in effect, that it is not in the class the theory assigns it to—not on the right level of the hierarchy, not in the extension of the strengthened truth predicate, not merely false, not true in the present context—which re-derives the contradiction on the view's own terms. Everyone can predict the others' revenge problem in advance; nobody treats their own as a refutation.

My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because it is not a factual disagreement about one thing called truth. It is a disagreement about which of three commitments is non-negotiable: classical logic and bivalence; the unrestricted transparency of truth, the equivalence between a sentence and the claim that it is true holding everywhere; or the full expressibility of natural language, the idea that whatever can be said can be said within the language under study. Each family has already decided which commitment to abandon, and each regards the others' chosen sacrifice as untenable. Diagnose this situation. Is the disagreement substantive, verbal, or a difference of explanatory standards rather than of fact? Is there a hidden formal result—a theorem, a semantic construction, an argument that one sacrifice is strictly cheaper than the others—that could settle it, or is the expectation of convergence the real mistake? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress, if anything would?
