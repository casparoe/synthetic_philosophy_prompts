I am a novelist, and I have been designing the writing system used by a people in my current novel. I want to know whether the script I have devised deserves to be called a phonemic alphabet, or whether that description would be linguistically misleading. Suppose an alphabet is phonemic when it satisfies the following criteria: (1) its letters stand for individual consonants and vowels, rather than for syllables, morphemes, or whole words; (2) the mapping between letters and phonemes is one to one, so that no letter represents more than one phoneme and no phoneme is represented by more than one letter; (3) it writes underlying phonemes and leaves contextually predictable allophones unmarked; and (4) a reader who has learnt the system can pronounce an unfamiliar written word without relying on knowledge of its meaning or its word class.

Now take this borderline case from my draft. In the invented language, [e] and [o] are in complementary distribution—[e] occurs only in open syllables and [o] only in closed syllables—except in a few recent loanwords and one old interjection. My script writes both sounds with a single letter, on the grounds that native speakers do not treat them as distinct and the exceptions are marginal. At the same time, the script marks predictable nasalisation by placing a small diacritic above a vowel letter when that vowel appears before a nasal consonant, while leaving the base vowel letter otherwise unchanged. Does this script qualify as a phonemic alphabet? Work through the criteria one at a time. Where the answer is underdetermined, say whether the difficulty lies in the criteria or in the case, and whether the right response would be to revise the script, the criteria, or both. Please answer in British English.
