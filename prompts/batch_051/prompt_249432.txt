I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. In a study group I participate in, two members got into an argument about the no-free-lunch theorem from machine learning and Hume’s problem of induction. I do not have a stake in the dispute, but I took notes on the back-and-forth and would like a point-by-point adjudication.

M, an ML engineer, opened by saying the no-free-lunch theorem gives a formal version of Hume’s problem of induction. He said the theorem shows that, when averaged over all possible target functions, no learning algorithm is better than any other. So any good performance on real data must come from the algorithm’s inductive bias. He then argued that this bias is the machine-learning equivalent of assuming that the future will resemble the past, and there is no logical justification for that assumption.

S, a philosophy student, pushed back. She said the theorem is a mathematical result about an average over the set of all possible target functions, and that set is not a description of the actual world. In any particular environment with stable regularities, some inductive biases will genuinely work better than others. So the theorem does not show that induction is unjustified in our world. At most, it shows that no learner is universally dominant across all logically possible worlds.

M replied that this misses the point. The reason we pick one inductive bias over another is almost always because it has worked on past problems, but that itself is an induction. If you justify your prior by past performance, you are assuming that the past is a reliable guide. The no-free-lunch theorem helps expose that circularity by making the choice of prior a hidden inductive commitment.

S answered that M is confusing two projects. Hume’s problem, as she understands it, is about whether experience can provide a non-circular reason to believe that the unobserved resembles the observed. The no-free-lunch theorem is not doing epistemological work. It is a weak statement about averages across all possible functions, a scenario with so little structure that no real-world reasoner has ever been in it. The theorem cannot show that our actual inductive practices are circular or unjustified, because it deliberately eliminates the very background structure that makes those practices reasonable.

M came back and said: but the theorem shows that any advantage in one environment is exactly paid for by a disadvantage in another. So if you want to claim your algorithm is better for, say, pharmaceutical data, you owe an account of why the target function belongs to the class of functions your algorithm is biased toward. That account cannot be empirical without already using induction. That is exactly the circle Hume identified.

S replied: no, you are smuggling in a demand for a universal guarantee. We do not need a non-inductive proof that our bias matches the target function. We can have fallible, empirically updated reasons to expect certain structure in particular domains. The no-free-lunch result says nothing about whether those expectations are reasonable in the environments we actually face. It only tells us that if we chose our algorithm at random over a completely unstructured space of targets, we should not expect an advantage.

M then said: that is just restating the problem. How do we know the environments we actually face have the structure our bias assumes? You say we have empirical reasons. Those empirical reasons are inductions. So you have not escaped the circle; you have only moved it to the level of selecting a prior.

S responded: but Hume’s problem, if it is a genuine problem, is a problem for any empirical learning from any data. The no-free-lunch theorem is a specialized result about learning algorithms. It does not add any new philosophical force. If I already accepted that induction cannot be given a non-circular justification, nothing about a theorem over all possible functions would make the situation worse. And if I am a practicing scientist, the theorem also does not tell me to abandon ordinary inductive inferences, because ordinary inferences are not made over all possible functions.

They ended there without resolving anything. Who is right about which specific points, and what are they both missing?
