After a long argument with a friend about whether it's wrong to hope too much, I came away defending a position I want pressure-tested. The position: in moral and political life, hopefulness that runs ahead of what the evidence supports isnt a virtue; it's an epistemic and practical defect. It makes people mistake a nice future for a likely one, defers needed grief or anger, and lets tolerable-but-bad arrangements survive because everyone is waiting for the hoped-for fix. I don't just mean optimism as a prediction—I mean the affective investment in a future you can't justify. What would have to be true for this to be wrong? I want the observations, cases, arguments, or developments that would count against it, sorted from decisive to merely awkward, plus what my view predicts about how people actually act that the hope-as-virtue camp wouldnt predict. I also want an honest answer to this: do I actually risk anything, or have I defined 'too much' so conveniently that no hope could ever count as acceptable? If the latter, is that insulation a fatal problem or just normal for a view about practical reason? What would defenders of my position themselves concede counts against it, as opposed to what critics say? The thing that would move me most is a case where ungrounded hope made someone act better or longer than a sober read of the evidence would have, without the outcome retroactively justifying it.
