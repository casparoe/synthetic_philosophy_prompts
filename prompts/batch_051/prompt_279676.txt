I've spent a fair amount of time on rationalist forums and feel at home with the many-worlds interpretation at a popular level — decoherence, branching, no objective collapse, worlds splitting on interactions — and with related anthropic ideas like self-sampling and updating on one's own existence. Lately, though, I was rereading a thread on quantum suicide and realized I cannot tell where the legitimate part of the reasoning ends. One commenter summarized the thought experiment roughly like this. A person sits in a room with a gun wired to a spin-measuring device. If the particle is measured spin-up, the gun fires; if spin-down, the gun clicks harmlessly. Under many-worlds, both outcomes occur in different branches. In the branches where the gun fires, the person dies and has no further experiences; in the branch where it clicks, he observes himself alive. So from his own first-person point of view the gun will always click, on the first trial and on the ten-thousandth, because there is no branch in which a surviving version of him observes the gun firing. The commenter then drew two conclusions. First, after a long run of clicks, the survivor would have compelling first-person evidence that many-worlds is true, since on any collapse interpretation his continued survival would be astronomically unlikely. Second, the same argument yields quantum immortality: for any lethal event there is always some branch in which it fails to kill him, and since he can only ever find himself in a branch where he survives, he should expect his subjective experience never to include his own death.

I have a nagging worry that this summary moves illicitly between there being a branch in which someone much like me survives and my observing myself survive, and also between the clean quantum-gun setup and an arbitrary lethal event. But I am not confident enough to say where the slide happens. Please treat the commenter's summary as the object of an accuracy audit: go through it and separate what is accurate on a fairly standard understanding of many-worlds, what is an overstatement or conflation, which steps a careful defender of the immortality conclusion would have to defend and with what assumptions about branch weight and personal identity, and which of the commenter's two conclusions survive the corrections. I am especially interested in whether the first-person evidence claim and the quantum-immortality conclusion rest on the same contested step or on different ones.
