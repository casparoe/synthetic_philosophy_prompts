So I had this two-hour argument last night with my friend, who's a molecular biology grad student. She kept saying DNA is literally a code — look, there's a genetic code, codons, a translation apparatus — and I kept pushing back that codes are the kind of thing agents invent and agree on, so calling DNA a code is a metaphor doing more work than it's licensed for. She said I was just being a philosopher about a word, which is maybe fair, but it bugged me enough that I wrote up a short essay afterwards to sort out what I actually think. I'd like comments on it before I show it to her. Please dont suggest readings or a bibliography — just engage with the argument itself.

Here's the essay: People say DNA contains the "code" for an organism. I want to split that into two claims. The thin claim is just that there's a systematic mapping between triplets of nucleotides and amino acids — the codon table, with its redundancies and stop signals — and that cells have machinery (tRNAs, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, ribosomes) that physically realizes the mapping. This part is uncontroversial and I grant it. The thick claim is that DNA is a code the way a message is a code: something that says or instructs or represents, with a sender, a receiver, and content that could in principle be wrong or lie. I think the thin claim is true and does real work in molecular biology; the thick claim is a metaphor that imports a picture of agency and meaning that biology doesn't need. But here's where I got stuck. My friend pointed out that a substitution cipher is also "just a mapping," and the only reason we call it a code is that someone made it and someone uses it. The codon table wasn't made by anyone, but it is used — by the cell's translation machinery. So maybe the real difference isn't semantic at all: it's that human codes are mappings instituted by agreement, while the genetic code is a mapping instituted by physical chemistry (base pairing, enzyme specificity). If that's right, then "is DNA a code?" is a question about what kind of ground a mapping has — conventional or physical — not about whether DNA carries meaning. I'm tempted to conclude that DNA is a code in the thin mapping sense, not in the thick representational sense, and that most of the fight comes from people smuggling the thick sense in through the word "code" and then being impressed by it.

I'd specifically like to know: if you were my friend, where would you push back first? And does the last move — relocating the difference to conventional versus physical grounding — actually do the work I want it to, or does it just move the problem somewhere else? Also tell me if anything in the biology is off, because she will definitely notice.
