I am a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and in our weekly reading group the conversation turned to whether a creature with a finite mind—bounded discrimination, a finite stock of sensory and conceptual primitives, and finite memory—could have only finitely many distinct experiences available to it, and whether an immortal version of such a creature would eventually reach a point after which every further experience is a repetition of one it has already had. One member resisted this conclusion with an analogy. She argued that the inference from finite basic ingredients to a finite space of complex wholes is fallacious: a finite alphabet together with combinatorial rules yields an unlimited number of distinct texts, just as a finite set of basic elements can generate an open-ended family of complex compositions. Since a mind combines its primitives compositionally, she concluded that its experience-space is likewise open-ended and that the worry about eventual exhaustion rests on a mistake.

I am not sure the analogy carries as much as she thinks, but I have also not managed to locate exactly where it gives out. Could you audit it systematically? Please give a table of correspondences between the source domain (alphabet, strings of arbitrary length, grammar, books) and the target domain (sensory or conceptual primitives, represented contents, combinatorial processes, token experiences); identify the disanalogies, separating the ones that matter for her conclusion from the ones that are incidental; spell out any features of the source that the analogy quietly imports into the target on insufficient grounds; and then say whether her conclusion—that the finitude of a mind's basic ingredients does not by itself make its experience-space exhaustible—survives the audit. If the analogy collapses in important respects, I would also like to know whether there is a better analogy for what a finite mind's experience-space is actually like.
