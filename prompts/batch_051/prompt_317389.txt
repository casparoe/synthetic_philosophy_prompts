Having spent my career in the natural sciences and only recently turned to philosophy, I have been rereading Hume on induction with an almost professional pleasure; he treats the habits of the mind rather as one treats an instrument whose calibration one distrusts. That reading has led me to a question I have not seen put quite so directly, and I should value a considered judgement on it.

Consider Goodman's grue puzzle. Take t to be some future time, and say that an object is grue just in case it is examined before t and is green, or it is not examined before t and is blue. If all emeralds observed before t are green, then all observed emeralds are equally grue; the same evidence seems to support both the projection that all emeralds are green and the projection that all emeralds are grue. Yet the two projections diverge for emeralds first examined after t, which the second one predicts will be blue. The familiar response is that green is a projectible predicate while grue is gerrymandered or time-indexed; Goodman then asks what grounds this distinction, and offers the answer that well-entrenched predicates — those that have actually been projected in past inductions — may be projected, while their unprojected rivals may not.

Had Hume encountered this puzzle, what, in your judgement, would he have made of it? Would he regard grue as a fresh difficulty for induction, or rather as a confirmation of his view that reason cannot settle which regularities the mind ought to carry forward? Could his appeal to custom explain why in practice we project green rather than grue, or does grue reveal that custom merely records a historical accident of our vocabulary? And on which points, if any, would he likely have found Goodman's talk of projectibility and entrenchment uncongenial? I recognise that any answer must be reasoned extrapolation rather than established history of philosophy, which is precisely what interests me. I should be grateful if you would write your answer in British English.
