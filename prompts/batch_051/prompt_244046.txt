Last night a friend and I spent a long argument on whether philosophy should be in the business of fixing concepts rather than analysing them. She held the conservative line: a term like 'woman', 'race', or 'knowledge' has a content we can be right or wrong about, and the job of a philosopher is to uncover it. I held the engineering line: some concepts are defective instruments, and the job is to improve them, not merely describe them. The frustrating part was that we kept appealing to different standards. She would reject a proposed revision because it changed the subject; I would accept it because it made our practices go better. She would say a revision must be something ordinary speakers could actually be brought to adopt; I would say it only needs to be the concept we would converge on under reflection. We never sorted out which of those demands are real constraints on an adequate account and which are just our camps' habits.

Before I read further in the fixing-versus-analysing literature, I want the desiderata. Write the list of requirements a theory of conceptual engineering — ameliorative analysis, conceptual ethics, whatever one calls the fixing project — has to meet in order to count as adequate, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. In particular, address these candidate demands and tell me which are genuine desiderata and which are prejudices of one side: (i) the implementation problem, whether a revision that no community would actually adopt can still count as an improvement, or whether uptake is a downstream practical matter; (ii) the continuity problem, how much an engineered concept may change before it is a replacement rather than a revision of the old one, and whether that distinction matters; (iii) the standard problem, by what measure one concept is better than another, and whether that measure must itself be answerable to the same engineering; (iv) the externalism problem, whether the fact that meanings are partly fixed by the world and by expert usage leaves any room for deliberate revision; and (v) the tracking problem, whether an engineered concept must still answer to something independent of our interests, or whether fitness for our purposes is the whole story. Flag any pairs of requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate accounts. Keep any scorecard of existing positions brief — the criteria are what I need.
