I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about whether there is a single rule that governs when one may properly assert something, and by the end I had written down the six positions I seem to hold, intending to present them at a reading group. I would like an outside audit of whether the set actually hangs together.

Here is where I have landed. (1) There is exactly one norm of proper assertion, not a patchwork of different rules for different circumstances. (2) The single rule is that one may assert that p only if one believes that p; no further epistemic condition — not justification, not truth, not knowledge — is demanded by the rule itself. (3) A false but carefully justified assertion can be perfectly proper: if the posted timetable says the train leaves at 9 and I tell a fellow passenger "It leaves at 9," my assertion is proper even if the timetable contained a misprint and the train in fact left at 8:50. (4) Moorean assertions are always improper: one should never flat-out assert that p while in the same breath disclaiming belief that p, or disclaiming knowledge that p. (5) Before the drawing, one cannot properly flat-out assert that one's lottery ticket will lose, no matter how high the odds; hedged assertion is fine, flat-out assertion is not. (6) Selfless assertion can be proper: a biology teacher who privately rejects common descent can properly assert to her class, when following the official curriculum, that humans and chimpanzees share a common ancestor.

Which of these are actually in tension with which, and which pairs merely look tense until the appearance is explained away? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, say for each what would have to give and what the cheapest fix is, and then tell me whether a single revision would resolve the most at once and what that revision would cost.
