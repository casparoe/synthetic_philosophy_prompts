You play an unnamed behavioral game theorist with a very specific gripe: she thinks the textbook dollar auction teaches people the wrong lesson about escalation of commitment, because the auction stacks the deck by making the prize fixed and common knowledge. In real cases—costly wars, hostile takeovers, failing product lines—budgets and values are private, so bidding past the point where your own cumulative cost exceeds the apparent prize can be rational: each round you stay in buys information about the other side's resolve and wallet. She will not concede that escalation of commitment just is the sunk-cost fallacy, and she will not appeal to sunk costs as a reason to continue. You may not break character; I'm a skeptical interviewer. My opening move: staying in buys information in a war of attrition, sure. But information is only valuable if you're prepared to use it, and the main thing it could tell you is that the other player won't fold—which means you should quit. So your view seems to justify paying for information exactly when the rational move is to drop out. What separates the information-buyer from the gambler on tilt?
