Suppose that, on a small island of perhaps five thousand people, a reform movement two generations ago succeeded in installing an explicit, socially enforced rule of conversational scorekeeping: no presupposition may enter the common ground unlicensed. The rule, as actually practiced, is this. If any speaker's utterance carries a presupposition that was not already established in the conversational record, at least one hearer must halt the exchange and challenge it before the conversation may proceed, and a hearer who lets an unauthorized presupposition pass is liable to the same disapproval as the speaker who introduced it. The rule is common knowledge, applies to its own enforcement, and treating it as obvious that some other hearer will issue the challenge itself counts as letting a presupposition pass. Nothing else about the society is stipulated in advance; they still conduct trade, family life, courts of arbitration, religious ritual, and casual small talk.

I want a reasoned prediction of what this community's linguistic practices look like today, two generations in. Which speech acts and registers get selected out or newly invented; how conversational repair and hedging change; whether the community develops substitutes for the functions that silent accommodation normally performs (irony, euphemism, polite fictions, jokes whose presuppositions do the work, coordination on unspoken assumptions); and how conversational labor and the common ground itself reshape under the pressure of a rule like this. I especially want the chain of reasoning run through mechanisms rather than a narrative: the cost of explicit grounding, speakers' incentives to mark presuppositions with hedges instead of asserting them outright, the collective-action problem in enforcement once any hearer may challenge and therefore none must, the risk of regress when a challenge itself presupposes something, and the long-run effect on shared knowledge, trust, and what a group can take for granted. For each link in the chain, please indicate whether you regard it as robust or as dependent on specific assumptions, and identify the empirical fact about natural language or social enforcement whose failure would most overturn that link. This began as a forum discussion about whether accommodation is a bug or a feature of ordinary conversation, and I would rather have the prediction treated as a piece of mechanism-driven world-building than as an exercise in summarizing the pragmatics literature. Please write your answer in British English.
