Take the 2000 Florida recount as a case study for the inefficacy objection to individual voting. I know the basics — Florida went to Bush by 537 votes, the Palm Beach butterfly ballot caused a lot of confusion among Gore voters, the Supreme Court shut down the recount, and the whole thing decided the presidency. I don't need the paradox-of-voting boilerplate. What I want is a careful two-directional analysis. First, run the inefficacy objection over the episode: before the election, what did it say a single eligible Floridian faced — what were the options, what was the probability (from the voter's point of view) that their one vote would be decisive, and what, if anything, did they have reason to do? Then do it again once the 537-vote margin was public: does a near-tie like that refute the objection, or does it just get absorbed as one more small-probability case the expected-value reply already handles? Second, turn it around and use the case to interrogate the objection: does Florida 2000 show that the inefficacy objection smuggles in the idea that the only way an individual contribution counts is by being the deciding vote, so tis asking the wrong question rather than giving a wrong answer? Finish with an honest note on where expected-value, expressive, and complicity accounts of why that vote mattered each fit the facts and where each one strains.
