You are a legal and political philosopher with a specialty in the ethics of private dispute resolution and precedent-based reasoning. Treat the Board's decisions below as the only authority for this exercise; do not appeal to outside statutes or case law. I am not asking what a court would do, only what we should do to stay consistent with ourselves.

I sit on the Rental Practices Review Board of a mid-sized city. Before a landlord may use a standard-form lease, the Board decides whether its arbitration clause is acceptable, acceptable with conditions, or not acceptable. We must now decide on a clause proposed for Elm Park, a 46-unit building owned by a small family company. The clause requires binding arbitration for all disputes "arising out of the tenancy," including claims of housing discrimination and claims of serious disrepair that makes a unit unsafe to live in. Tenants would have 60 days to opt out, without penalty; the landlord would pay the arbitrator's fees and hearing costs; and there is no class-action waiver and no confidentiality provision. Most Elm Park tenants are low-income, and many are recent immigrants.

Our own precedents:
- Juniper Court (2017): arbitration of security-deposit and ordinary repair disputes only; 30-day opt-out; landlord paid all filing fees. Accepted.
- Riverbend (2019): arbitration of security-deposit disputes only; no opt-out; each side paid its own filing and hearing fees. Rejected.
- Fairmount (2021): arbitration of "all disputes," including housing-discrimination and habitability claims; no opt-out; tenants paid half the fees; class arbitration barred. Rejected.
- Calloway (2023): arbitration of repair and deposit disputes; 60-day opt-out; landlord paid filing fees; no confidentiality. Accepted.

I can see at least two principles that would reconcile those four decisions. One says a clause is acceptable when the tenant's consent is meaningful, informed, and affordable. The other says that however fair the consent, claims with a rights-vindicating, public dimension may not be removed wholesale from public adjudication. They pull in opposite directions for Elm Park, because its opt-out and fee provisions look fair, but it sweeps discrimination and serious habitability claims into arbitration.

Please draft the written recommendation I will circulate to the Board. Start by brainstorming the distinct candidate rules that fit our precedents. Then: (1) state what each candidate rule would decide about Elm Park; (2) give a clear bottom line — accept, reject, or accept with specified conditions; (3) identify the closest prior decision and say why it is the closest; (4) be explicit about what principle the Board would be adopting under each main alternative; and (5) describe one future case that would better distinguish the leading candidate rules. Keep the entire recommendation under 900 words. At the end, tell me how confident you are in the bottom line and single out the point in your reasoning you are least sure about.
