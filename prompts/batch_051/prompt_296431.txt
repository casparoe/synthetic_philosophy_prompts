You are an expert moral psychologist with a background in philosophy of emotion. I need your help answering a question that sits upstream of a larger debate: people often argue about whether guilt or shame is the morally preferable emotion, and whether cultures can shift between them, but the debate is hopeless if the two are not cleanly distinguished. Give me a working definition of shame, built to separate it from guilt, embarrassment, and humiliation. I want a definition, not a list of typical features, and I want it tested against the cases below.

Before settling on your definition, briefly brainstorm two or three candidate accounts — for instance, one built on the familiar gloss that guilt tracks the act while shame tracks the self, one built on whether an audience is required, and one built on whether the felt failing must be something the person could control. Say what each account gets wrong. Then state the definition you are running with, and run it through every case. Where it gives no clear verdict, say so; where it contradicts one of my stated intuitions, either persuade me that my labeling was wrong or revise the definition. The deliverable is the definition and its case-by-case performance, not an essay on the history of the concept.

The cases:

1. A senator caught on camera taking bribes stands before a room of reporters with a hot face and a desperate wish that the floor would swallow her. I am confident this is shame.

2. A person who has been quietly cheating on their partner for months, without any discovery, lies awake feeling awful about the wrongness of what they are doing to someone they love. I am confident this is guilt and not shame, or if your definition disagrees, show me why.

3. A survivor of a sexual assault reports feeling "ashamed" of what happened to her, even though she can state clearly that she did nothing wrong and bears no responsibility. Is this shame, internalized stigma, or something else? Give a definite answer.

4. A college student with a severe stutter flushes and wants to vanish every time he has to speak in a seminar. Is this shame or embarrassment?

5. A woman whose brother has been arrested for embezzlement says she feels ashamed by association, although she had no knowledge of or involvement in his crimes. Shame or not?

6. A parent's young child throws a screaming tantrum in a quiet museum. The parent feels partly mortified on the child's behalf and partly urgent that onlookers must be judging her as a bad parent. Is this shame, secondhand embarrassment, or both?

7. An adult who was born decades after a war feels a wave of something hot when the subject of her country's wartime atrocities comes up in conversation. Is this shame, guilt, or neither?

8. A person trips on level ground in a crowd, falls, and spends about three minutes feeling red-faced and conspicuous. I think this is embarrassment rather than shame.

In evaluating the hard cases, make sure to address whether shame requires an audience, whether the defect in question has to be the person's own fault, and whether someone can feel shame about features they merely happen to have or groups they merely happen to belong to.
