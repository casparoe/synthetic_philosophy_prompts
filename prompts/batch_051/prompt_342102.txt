A friend of mine runs a weird little game at his department's fundraising party, and he passed along an argument about it that I've been turning over. The game is a dollar auction: the auctioneer puts up a $20 bill. Bids go in whole dollars. The winner pays their final bid and takes the $20. The twist is that the second-highest bidder also has to pay their final bid, but gets nothing. So if the bidding dies at $7 and $6, the person at $7 nets $13 and the person at $6 just loses six dollars.

His argument, writen out:

(1) Say two people have bid it up to $18 and $19. The person at $18 faces this: quit now and you're out $18 for nothing; or bid $20. If you bid $20 and the other person quits, you break even; if instead they bid $21, you're just in the other person's shoes one round later, with the same choice still available. So bidding $20 dominates quitting.

(2) The same reasoning applies to the person at $19, and then to whoever is second-highest at each round after that.

(3) Therefore, once two people have entered, neither ever has a reason to be the one who quits. Each round, the second-highest bidder should raise rather than swallow the loss.

(4) So the only thing that stops the bidding is someone running out of cash or the auctioneer imposing a cap. The price should run far past the $20 the winner is even bidding for.

(5) So we get two sensible people each taking the locally-sensible step, and ending up in a place neither would have chosen an hour earlier. The only way to win the game is to never enter.

I can't figure out whether this is airtight or whether one of the steps is quietly assuming something about what each bidder believes the other will do, or about how far the auction can run. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what assumption would you have to add to make it go through? And if (5)'s "never enter" is right as advice to a spectator, does it actually tell someone who is already in at $18 what to do, or does it just dodge the question?

Also, if you could write the response in American English, I'd appreciate it.
