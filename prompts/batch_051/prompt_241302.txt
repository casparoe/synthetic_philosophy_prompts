I have recently started working as a literary translation editor at a small press, and for the first time I am using large language models daily to produce first drafts of translated prose, which I then revise. This has sent me back to Hofstadter's well-known critique of machine translation: the argument that even when statistical systems produce fluent output, they are only shuffling tokens without understanding the source, and that this shallowness will inevitably surface in cases involving ambiguity, idiom, cultural connotation, register, and tone. I used to find that argument persuasive. But the systems I now work with are a different order of technology: transformer-based models trained on much of the text on the internet, with far greater capacity, and they often handle exactly those hard cases better than I expected, sometimes producing drafts that read as though someone with a working grasp of the source had written them. I want to know whether Hofstadter's argument survives this development.

Please take on the role of a philosopher of language and mind who knows this critique well. Work from the argument as I have described it rather than looking anything up. Before you commit to a verdict, briefly brainstorm the strongest considerations on each side. Then disentangle the argument's strands: the claim about what understanding requires, the claim about what translation requires, and the claim about the limits of statistical or pattern-based learning. Say precisely which premise the newer systems bear on, and whether that premise can be restored in another form. Finally, give a careful judgment: does the argument still go through roughly unchanged, does it need repair, or does it no longer carry force? Do not let improved output quality settle the question by itself: explicitly address whether fluency and the apparent handling of hard cases amount to evidence of understanding. I am interested in the fate of the argument itself, not in a practical verdict about whether my press should use these tools.
