I am drafting the document for an adversarial collaboration between two members of my department who have spent the past year arguing about the moral equality of combatants. The first, the traditionalist, defends the orthodox view: once a war is underway, combatants on both sides are equally permitted to kill enemy combatants, provided they comply with the rules of jus in bello — the rules governing conduct within war — and this permission does not depend on whether their side's war satisfies jus ad bellum, the justice of going to war. The second, the revisionist, rejects this. On her view, the deep morality of killing in war is individual: a combatant may kill an enemy only if that enemy is liable to be killed, which is a matter of the enemy's own responsibility for an unjust threat and of the necessity and proportionality of killing him. It follows that a combatant fighting for an unjust cause lacks the permission to kill a combatant fighting for a just one, even if he fights cleanly. The revisionist allows that unjust combatants often have excuses — coercion, ignorance, reasonable deference — but insists that an excuse is not a permission.

The document should be a joint statement that both parties could sign without weakening their own positions. It should contain the following.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: propositions about jus ad bellum, jus in bello, the laws of armed conflict, noncombatant immunity, and the distinction between justification and excuse that both camps can affirm. The parties have already stipulated, for purposes of the collaboration, that the laws of armed conflict should continue to apply symmetrically to both sides; their disagreement concerns the underlying morality, not the law.

Second, a disagreements table. Each residual disagreement should be stated twice: once in wording the traditionalist would accept as an accurate statement of his own view, and once in wording the revisionist would accept as an accurate statement of hers. The two columns should be arranged so that the genuine contrast is visible, and the formulations should be neither vacuous nor slanted.

Third, a section identifying the cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer to them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete case or piece of evidence that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts the case will show.

I suspect, though I do not insist, that the real cruxes will concern the epistemic position of the ordinary soldier and what follows from his inability to assess the justice of the war; the question whether the authority of the state, or the existence of a mutually advantageous war convention, can itself create a permission to kill that individual liability alone would not supply; and whether the symmetry the traditionalist wants is best understood as a set of genuine permissions or as a set of standing excuses. The final document should confirm, reject, or replace these candidates.

Throughout, distinguish disagreements that are substantive from disagreements that are merely verbal — for example, disputes about the word permission or the word liability that dissolve once the terms are fixed. Where a disagreement turns out to be verbal, say so and give the common formulation both parties could sign. Do not resolve the debate or declare a winner; the aim is to produce the exact statement the two parties could put their names to before any further testing.
