In preparing a doctoral seminar on John Rawls, I have been working through the charge that his doctrine of public reason effectively drives religious argument out of democratic politics. My understanding of the later Rawls is this. When citizens are arguing about constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice, they ought to be able to explain to one another how the positions they advocate can be supported by public reasons — by political values that reasonable citizens can accept whatever their comprehensive religious or philosophical commitments may be. In the revised essay on public reason, he allows that comprehensive doctrines may be introduced into public political discussion at any point, on the condition that, in due course, public reasons sufficient to support what those doctrines are invoked to support are also presented. But here is what troubles me: if those public reasons must be sufficient on their own, then the accompanying religious argument seems to be doing no justificatory work at all, only providing what is, from the standpoint of the justification of law, decorative or motivational support. That makes the proviso look like a concession with no content, and it leaves the impression that the devout citizen's deepest commitments are, in the end, politically idle even when they are permitted to be mentioned. I would like to know whether anything in Rawls gives the proviso more substantial work than this. On the best reading, does the introduction of religious argument into public deliberation contribute anything to public justification itself, or does Rawls in fact hold the starker position that liberal citizens must treat their theological convictions as giving them no share in the public justification of the laws they support?
