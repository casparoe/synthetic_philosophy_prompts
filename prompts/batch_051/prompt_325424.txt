So I'm taking a philosophy of well-being class this term, and we've spent the last two weeks on the experience machine. I keep thinking the standard setup stacks the deck against the machine, becuase it makes the choice sound like picking from a menu of preselected pleasures, and the usual objections (you'd get bored, it isn't real achievement, you want to actually do things) lean pretty hard on that. I want to think through a variant where that part is fixed.

Suppose the machine has a novelty guarantee. Rather than you choosing experiences from a catalog, the machine builds experiences from a model of your own psychology and is designed so that you will never exhaust the genuinely new experiences it can give you in a finite lifespan. It can simulate real adventure, risk and luck, relationships you have to earn, intellectual discoveries, the slow process of getting good at something, even the feeling of being changed by an experience. It can also simulate you becoming sick of the machine, unplugging, and then living a whole "real" life that rejects the machine—while you are in fact still plugged in the entire time. Whenever anything would start to go stale, it introduces something you couldn't have anticipated.

Here's what I'm trying to figure out. If we treat novelty and variety as genuine components of well-being and not just instrumental to pleasure, does this variant give you a definately stronger reason to plug in than the original? Or does it sharpen the authenticity objection instead—because now the machine is deliberately manufacturing the appearance of authenticity too? I'd like you to work through what a hedonist, a desire-satisfaction theorist, and an objective-list theorist should say about simulated novelty versus actual novelty in this case, and whether any of them should change their verdict on the original machine. Also, does the 'simulated rejection of the machine' bit actually matter? Is there a real difference between wanting authenticity and having a simulated experience of achieving it, and if so, which theory is best placed to capture that difference?
