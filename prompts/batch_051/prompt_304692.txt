I keep coming back to something and I honestly cant tell if its a deep structure or just a cute analogy. In the Greek myths, when the gods swear an oath on the Styx, the binding force is usually described as automatic: break the oath and the Styx itself strikes you — you lose breath, you're cut off from ambrosia and nectar, something like that — as if a circuit closes, not as if someone chooses to enforce a rule. Now that looks to me exactly like a precommitment device in game theory, where you make a promise credible by raising the cost of defection. An oath on the Styx would then be to a plain promise what a penalty clause is to a handshake, or what burning a bridge is to having an intention. But I can't decide whether this is a genuine identity of structure or a loose analogy. If it's genuine, then the binding force of any oath should just be the threat you manage to attach to it, and someone who believed a broken oath would carry no penalty would have no reason to keep one; the parallel also predicts that if the Styx's enforcement could be evaded, the oath would simply stop binding. If it's superficial, then where exactly does the structure come apart? Is it that an oath also does something like calling a power to witness — you've put yourself under its jurisdiction — and not just changed your payoffs? Or that promises generate reasons to the other party that threats and penalty clauses don't? I want to know whether this parallel is doing real work or whether I'm just pattern-matching.
