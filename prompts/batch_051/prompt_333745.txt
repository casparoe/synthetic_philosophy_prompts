Most people would say that punishing someone for violating a rule they had no way to learn is as close to a paradigm of injustice as there is. A regime that kept its rules in a locked room and still fined or imprisoned people for breaking them would not be a legal system at all but a machine for arbitrary coercion. Yet ordinary legal practice sits uneasily alongside this conviction. Systems descended from the common law retain the maxim that ignorance of the law is no excuse, and modern states enforce rules that are public in only the thinnest sense: municipal ordinances no visitor would know to look up, administrative codes running to thousands of pages, obligations that turn on unpublished guidance, and statutes whose practical meaning changes when a court gives an old term a new and retroactive construction. A diligent person can be penalized for breaching such a rule even though no realistic effort would have brought it to their attention. At the same time, we refuse to enforce the cases just one step further out: we condemn ex post facto criminal statutes, we treat genuinely secret rules as tyranny, and a court would generally not enforce a buried term in a contract that one party had no realistic chance to read.

The task is to determine whether the line between the published-but-unfindable rule and the genuinely secret or retrospective rule can be given a principled justification, or whether it is an inconsistency we tolerate for reasons of convenience. What is the strongest principle that permits binding people to rules they could not realistically have known, while still condemning rules that are formally hidden or invented after the fact? Candidates include the incentive to learn the law, the difficulty of proving ignorance, the administrative cost of excusing mistakes, the value of stable expectations, and the idea that publication itself does all the moral work. Take whichever candidate you think is strongest, test it against hard cases—the visitor fined under an obscure local ordinance, the person abroad when a regulation changed, the retroactive judicial interpretation of an old statute, the rule written only in a language the defendant does not read—and then say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or redrawn, and exactly where the line should fall.
