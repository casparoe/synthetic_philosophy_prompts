You are grading an exam in a course on ancient ethics. Below are the question and one student's answer. Grade the answer out of 20 points, with parts (a) through (d) worth 5 points each. Give the numerical grade and then point-by-point comments that justify it: state what each part gets right, identify any errors or imprecisions, and explain the correct reading of Plato's Republic where the answer goes wrong. Question (20 points). At the opening of Republic II, Glaucon constructs a challenge for Socrates about justice. Explain as precisely as you can: (a) the threefold classification of goods Glaucon introduces, including the examples he gives and where Socrates and the many respectively place justice within it; (b) Glaucon's account of the origin of justice as a compromise and the role of the ring of Gyges story within that account; (c) the two lives Glaucon asks us to compare and what the comparison is designed to isolate; and (d) how Glaucon's challenge relates to Thrasymachus's position from Book I.

Student answer. Glaucon distinguishes three kinds of goods: goods chosen for their own sake alone, such as harmless pleasures; goods chosen both for their own sake and for the sake of what comes from them, such as thinking and being healthy; and goods chosen only for their consequences, such as medical treatment and wage-earning. Socrates places justice in the second kind, the finest kind of good; the many, Glaucon says, put justice in the third kind, practiced only for the wages and reputation it brings. Glaucon then asks Socrates to praise justice in its own right, independently of anything that follows from it.

As for the origin of justice, Glaucon reconstructs the common view as follows. Left unrestrained, people find they suffer more from one another's wrongdoing than they gain from committing it, so they agree neither to do nor to suffer injustice; law and the name of justice are the product of this agreement. Justice is thereby an instrumental good, adopted through weakness and mutual fear. The ring of Gyges is introduced to show that anyone who could escape detection would act unjustly. Gyges, a shepherd in the service of the king of Lydia, finds a ring that makes him invisible; with it he seduces the queen, kills the king, and seizes the throne. Glaucon infers that a just man given such a ring would behave no differently from an unjust man, because everyone values justice only for the reputation it brings. This, Glaucon says, proves that no one is just willingly.

To sharpen the challenge further, Glaucon sets two lives in opposition. One is a man who is entirely just in reality but is believed by everyone to be entirely unjust, so that he suffers disgrace and punishment while remaining just. The other is a man who is entirely unjust in reality but is believed by everyone to be entirely just, so that he enjoys honor, office, and wealth. The comparison is meant to isolate the value of justice and injustice themselves by stripping away their reputational consequences: if the unjust life with a good reputation is judged better, then justice is good only for its consequences; if the just life is still judged better despite its ill repute, then justice is good in its own right.

Finally, Glaucon's challenge is best understood as a refined and generalized version of Thrasymachus's thesis. In Book I Thrasymachus maintained that justice is whatever is to the advantage of the more powerful, the rulers, and that they lay down laws to serve their own interest. Glaucon's contract story translates this into a more plausible form: because no one has the power to wrong others with impunity, the many agree to call abstention from wrongdoing just. But the underlying conclusion is the same, namely that a rational person will commit injustice whenever it pays. The real difference is one of posture rather than substance: Thrasymachus asserts the thesis aggressively, whereas Glaucon presents it as the strongest case for injustice merely to force Socrates to answer it, himself requesting that Socrates show the just life to be preferable even when shorn of every reward.
