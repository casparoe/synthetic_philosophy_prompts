I'm putting together a short talk for my town's resilience committee, and I want help prepping for the questions I'll get afterward. I'm not an academic; I got into this by reading the Stoics on my own after a rough hurricane season a few years ago, and I've been turning the idea over ever since. My pitch is going to be this: the committee keeps talking about "resilience" as bouncing back after a disruption, but I want to argue that for a handful of essential functions — water, heat, food, basic medical care, and communications — the town should aim for what I've been calling "30-day autarky." I don't mean producing everything locally or cutting ourselves off from the world. I mean arranging the systems so that if the surrounding region can't reach us for 30 days, those functions keep running, and then the more controversial part: that when we choose between projects, we should prefer the ones that reduce the number of outside things those functions depend on, over the ones that just make us quicker to recover after a failure. My deeper idea, which I know sounds more philosophical than engineering, is a Stoic-sounding one. The Stoics talked about personal self-sufficiency not as owning everything you need, but as shrinking your desires down to what's actually under your control. I think that move is a mistake when applied to individual people — taken too far it becomes a fantasy of not needing anyone — and obviously national autarky is a disaster. But I think at the scale of a town or small city, the move actually makes sense: shrink what essential systems depend on until the dependencies are things the community can genuinely control. So my slogan is something like: resilience is about surviving shocks, self-sufficiency is about shrinking your dependence down to what you can steer.

The audience is seven people: two civil engineers who think in terms of redundant systems and failure modes, an emergency-management guy who's been through real disasters and believes no plan survives contact with the event, a budget person who will ask what every option costs per household compared to just relying on state and FEMA aid, a public-health nurse, a county planner, and the chair, who runs a small hardware store and only trusts arguments made in everyday language. I expect the engineers to be friendly toward backups but suspicious of what looks like a philosophy lecture, the emergency guy to press me on scenarios where the town's own systems fail, and the budget person to be hostile to anything without a dollar figure attached.

Don't write the talk itself. Instead, act as a professional philosopher who works on political theory and applied ethics and has spent time around municipal planning debates. Start by brainstorming what each of those seven people is most likely to grab onto, then think carefully about which objections actually damage my position and which just sound good in a meeting. Then give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way these people would actually say them, with a note on which ones have real force, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and a two-minute reply for each. Also tell me the weakest point in my whole position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss, and the one question I should be hoping nobody asks. Please be specific to this audience — I don't need generic objections to autarky, I need what these seven people will say.
