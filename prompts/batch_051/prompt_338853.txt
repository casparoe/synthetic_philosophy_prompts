In a value theory reading group we have been stuck for weeks on whether 'better than' is transitive, and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on the question itself. One side treats transitivity as a non-negotiable constraint on any relation meant to guide practical reasoning: if A is better than B and B is better than C, then A must be better than C, so any chain of reasoning that denies this should be treated as a reductio of one of its premises. The other side presses spectrum arguments: construct a sequence of alternatives in which each item seems better than its successor by a small margin, yet after enough steps the initial item seems clearly not better than — perhaps worse than — the final one. Both sides, as far as I can tell, accept the same descriptions of the cases and even find the stepwise judgments intuitively compelling; they disagree about which judgments to abandon. My working hypothesis is that this is not an empirical disagreement and cannot be settled by adding further cases: it is a disagreement about which intuitive judgments count as data and about what 'better than' has to do for practical reasoning. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a hidden empirical or formal question underneath?
