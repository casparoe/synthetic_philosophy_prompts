I have been turning over an argument that seems to show that determinism and chaos theory cannot both be understood in the way they usually are, and I cannot locate the precise step at which it goes wrong. It starts from Poincaré’s discussion in *Science and Method*, in the chapter on chance, where he says, in effect, that we call an event chance when a very small cause, one we have failed to notice, produces a sufficiently large effect that we cannot fail to see it. He goes on to observe that if we knew exactly the laws of nature and the state of the universe at an initial moment, we could predict the state of the universe at a later moment; but we can never know the initial state with exactness, and for many systems the smallest error in that initial knowledge makes long-term prediction impossible in practice.

Here is the argument I have been considering:

(1) In a deterministic system, the state at any later time is a function of the state at an initial time and the laws of motion.

(2) If that is so, then the system’s future trajectory is already settled by its present state: there is a unique actual future, even if no finite mind can compute it.

(3) But any finite observer knows the initial conditions only within some nonzero error margin.

(4) In chaotic systems, errors in the initial conditions grow exponentially with time.

(5) Therefore, for any nonzero initial error and any finite prediction horizon, there is some time beyond which the uncertainty has grown to the size of the entire relevant state region.

(6) At that point, talk of “the” individual trajectory is prediction-theoretically empty: the observer can at best assign probabilities over macroscopically different futures.

(7) Yet the butterfly effect and the geometry of strange attractors are standardly presented as discoveries about the dynamics themselves, not as facts about an observer’s computational limitations.

(8) So either unpredictability is an objective feature of deterministic chaotic systems, or the language of chaos theory is systematically misleading and the real phenomenon is merely sensitive dependence plus finite precision.

If I accept the argument, I seem to be forced to say that “chaotic” adds nothing objective beyond “difficult to predict in practice.” If I reject it, I need to explain how a deterministic system can be intrinsically unpredictable without reintroducing objective chance into a deterministic world. My inclination is to suspect a slide somewhere around steps (5) and (6), but I am not sure that suspicion survives scrutiny.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which premise or step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost for how we should understand strange attractors, the butterfly effect, and the sense in which chaotic systems are unpredictable?
