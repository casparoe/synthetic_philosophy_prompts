I cover technology and the ideas embedded in everyday contract law for a magazine, and for an article about whether clicking "I agree" amounts to actually agreeing, I have written down my settled reactions to a series of cases. When a buyer clicks through a sixty-page terms document to purchase a laptop and the arbitration clause is buried in the middle, I do not think they genuinely agreed to arbitration. When a new employee is shown a prominent notice that they are agreeing to mandatory arbitration, but the click is required before they can start the job, I still do not count it as genuine consent; the choice seems too constrained. When a screen lists the surprising terms separately — arbitration, data sharing, automatic renewal — each with its own unchecked box, and the user checks each one, I treat that as real agreement. I also hold a person to a free-trial signup in which the automatic renewal after seven days is printed in plain text directly under the button they clicked, even if they did not read it. I reject the thought that an app update saying continued use after thirty days constitutes acceptance of new terms produces agreement when the user simply keeps using the app. And when the link to the terms of service leads to an error page, a user's check mark next to "I have read and agree" means nothing to me. The case I cannot settle is this: a patient at a hospital kiosk is shown a screen saying that by tapping "I agree" they consent to binding arbitration for malpractice claims, and the only way to be seen by a clinician is to tap. Please tell me what principle, if any, captures these verdicts; which of them it forces me to abandon; and what it predicts about the hospital kiosk. If the request rests on a confused premise, or if no principle worth having can make these verdicts cohere, say so plainly rather than forcing one.
