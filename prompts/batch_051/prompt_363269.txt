I'm an undergrad in a philosophy of science course on Bayesian confirmation, and we just read Clark Glymour's Why I Am Not a Bayesian. The part that stuck with me is where he argues that if evidence e is already known before a new hypothesis h is proposed, then P(e) = 1, so conditionalizing on e gives P(h|e) = P(h), which means old evidence can't confirm a new theory. He uses Mercury's perihelion and general relativity as the obvious counterexample: everyone knew the data for decades, yet it clearly seemed to count for Einstein. My prof says there are like a half dozen fixes—counterfactual priors, Garber-style logical learning, abandoning conditionalization, likelihoodism, and so on—but nobody agrees on which one actually resolves the problem.

I'm thinking of applying to grad school and trying to figure out where a thesis-sized project could genuinely push this forward, not just reorder the literature. Which open question in the old evidence debate is most worth working on, if the goal is a theory of confirmation that scientists could actually use? Which of the standard replies is most upstream, meaning if it worked the others would mostly fall out, and which ones are dead ends that get cited but wouldn't change anything downstream in how we evaluate real scientific episodes? Defend the ranking with criteria like tractability, how many other questions depend on each one, and what kind of payoff there'd be for understanding actual science.
