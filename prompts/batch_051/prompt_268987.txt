I have no background in law or political science, but I have been trying to work out, from my own reactions to concrete cases, what should be decided at which level of government. The passage I keep coming back to is from the Treaty on European Union, Article 5(3):

"Under the principle of subsidiarity, in areas which do not fall within its exclusive competence, the Union shall act only if and in so far as the objectives of the proposed action cannot be sufficiently achieved by the Member States, either at central level or at regional and local level, but can rather, by reason of the scale or effects of the proposed action, be better achieved at Union level."

I can see the general idea, but I cannot tell whether it actually captures my judgments when I run through the cases I feel sure about:

- The EU should set binding limits on lead in tap water; I would not leave this to member states.
- A city or region should be able to cap short-term rentals, even when most of the renters are visitors from other countries.
- The EU should limit carbon dioxide emissions from newly sold cars; a patchwork of national rules would be worse.
- University tuition fees should be set by member states, not the EU, even though students sometimes move across borders.
- The EU should impose cybersecurity requirements on internet-connected devices sold in the single market.
- Rules on police use of facial recognition should be decided by member states, even though the technology also affects criminals who cross borders.

I notice the last two make me uneasy: they both concern surveillance-like technology, and both seem to have effects that do not stop at national borders, yet I have pushed one up and one down. Is there a single principle that gets all six of these right, or nearly right? Please state the principle precisely enough to apply, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate rather than bending it to keep all of them, and tell me what it implies about a case I keep going back and forth on: whether the EU should set noise limits for motorcycles, which mainly disturb people near busy streets but are built for a single European market.
