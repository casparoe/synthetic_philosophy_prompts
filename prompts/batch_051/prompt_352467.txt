I run a philosophy discussion group whose members split roughly between committed longtermists and people who think longtermism is a Pascalian mistake, and we keep getting stuck on a single question: whether the simulation argument should change how fanatical we ought to be about the far future. By "fanaticism about the far future" I mean the view that the far future dominates expected value, so that even a tiny probability of influencing the very long run can justify large near-term sacrifices. The simulation argument, in its familiar trilemma form, says that at least one of three claims must hold: civilizations at our stage almost never survive long enough to run ancestor-simulations; almost none of them that could run such simulations would choose to; or we are almost certainly living in a simulation. I want something for the group that sorts people by what they actually believe when pushed on cases, not by which label they happen to like.

Please design a "which position do you actually hold?" decision tree or sorting quiz on this question: does the simulation argument dampen far-future fanaticism, leave it untouched, or redirect it? Aim for eight to ten leaves. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of labels: each split should be a concrete scenario or commitment on which positions genuinely come apart, and each question should be worded so that a person can answer it without knowing the names of the positions. Good diagnostic splits might include things like: learning you live in a simulation that will be shut down in two hundred years; learning that simulated minds count equally, less, or not at all; learning that the simulators reward or punish certain kinds of behavior; discovering that our far-future forecasts were formed inside the simulation and may not track the base world's regularities; and discovering that actions inside the simulation can or cannot causally affect the base world. The deliverable is a decision tree whose branch labels are those diagnostic questions, with the answer combinations leading to leaves. For each leaf, give the position a memorable name (or mark it unnamed if nobody defends it), state in one sentence what it says about far-future fanaticism, and add one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Along the way, identify unoccupied combinations of answers and say briefly whether they are empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag positions the tree cannot cleanly separate and say what further question would split them. Do not make any question a direct version of "do you think the simulation argument dampens fanaticism?"; the sorting should happen through the narrower commitments. Finally, please separate what is settled about the simulation argument's structure from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic—especially how to count simulated experiences, whether model uncertainty swamps the relevant probabilities, and whether a simulated world gives us new levers on the far future—and do not present a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
