I've been trying to get clear on something in inverse reinforcement learning, and I have a distinction I keep using that I want checked before I lean on it any further.

I call it the difference between behavioral noise and evaluative noise in demonstrations. Behavioral noise: the person has a perfectly stable reward function, but the action you observe is corrupted—motor slips, bad information, sleep deprivation, time pressure, being drunk. Evaluative noise: the reward function itself is the thing that's fluctuating. Their values genuinely change across time and contexts: the person who swore off sugar at breakfast and eats the cake at midnight isn't executing their long-run values badly, they have a different value function winning for an hour.

My central claim is that IRL can handle the first but not the second. Behavioral noise should wash out if you collect enough demonstrations: the errors are unsystematic, so you average them away and recover the latent reward. But evaluative noise doesn't wash out, becuase the thing you're averaging over is the very thing you're trying to infer, and the average of Tuesday-you and Thursday-you may not be a reward function that describes anyone who ever existed. If I'm right, then a chunk of what gets labeled "suboptimal demonstrations" or "human irrationality" is actually just evaluative noise, and fitting a single stationary reward to it is a category error, not an approximation.

I know there's work on IRL from suboptimal demonstrations, and on non-stationary or changing reward functions, and of course there's the whole second-order-desire literature. But I haven't seen anyone carve it exactly this way, or draw the conclusion I want to draw: that if evaluative noise is large, "learn the human's preferences from their behavior" doesn't have a well-defined solution—it's a modeling choice about which of a person's selves to defer to, maybe the one that shows up most, maybe the one they endorse, maybe the one they'd have under full information.

I want an honest assessment, not a cheerleading session. Is the distinction coherent or does it collapse on inspection? Are there existing ideas I'm restating? Is the central claim actually true, or does evaluative noise wash out in a way I'm not seeing? Where does it break? Be candid.
