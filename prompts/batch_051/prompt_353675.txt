Within a single governmental system, three different practices assign value to a human life in three different ways. A transport safety agency will pay up to a limit—a few million dollars, typically—per statistical life saved by a road or rail intervention, a figure typically derived from the small wage premiums workers accept for riskier jobs. A health technology agency scores treatments in QALYs, which counts a year in full health as 1 and a year lived with a disability often as some fraction of 1, so extending the life of a disabled person registers as a smaller benefit than extending the life of a nondisabled person. An emergency department, by contrast, triages the patients in front of it by medical urgency and expected need, and would treat using either the transport agency's price per life or a QALY score to choose between two particular patients as a violation of its professional norms. What theory of value does this pattern presuppose—about what makes a life count, whose valuation governs, and what is being traded in each setting? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit well with, say whether the three are philosophically coherent together or underdetermined by any single theory, and state what would have to change if one theory of life's value governed all three settings. Answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
