Course question. In Part I of Section X ("Of Miracles") of David Hume's An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, Hume writes:

"It being a general maxim, that no objects have any discoverable connexion together, and that all the inferences, which we can draw from one to another, are founded merely on our experience of their constant and regular conjunction; it is evident, that we ought not to make an exception to this maxim in favour of human testimony, whose connexion with any event seems, in itself, as little necessary as any other."

Explain what Hume is claiming about testimony in this passage. Then explain how the passage bears on the contemporary distinction between reductionism and anti-reductionism about testimony. Does the passage commit Hume to one side of that distinction, and if so, to exactly which position?

Student answer. "Hume is claiming that human testimony is not an independent source of justification. Testimony yields justified belief only through the same kind of inductive inference that underlies all factual reasoning: we rely on reports because experience has shown a regular conformity between reports and facts, and the connection between a report and the event it reports is not discoverable a priori. Thus Hume is a reductionist in the contemporary sense: he holds that a testimonial belief is justified only if the hearer has inductive evidence that people generally tell the truth. Anti-reductionists such as Coady reject this, since they regard testimony as a basic source of justification, so that a hearer can be justified in trusting testimony without needing positive reasons for the reliability of testimony in general. The quoted passage supports the reductionist reading because Hume says the connection between testimony and events is not necessary; from this it follows that testimonial evidence is always merely probable, never a proof, and therefore Hume is committed to global reductionism, the view that every testimonial belief must be supported by the hearer's own inductive evidence before it can be justified."
