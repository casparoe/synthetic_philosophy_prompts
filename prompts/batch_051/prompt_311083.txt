So I've been trying to think about loss aversion and the endowment effect — the finding that people hate losing something about twice as much as they enjoy gaining it, and that merely owning a thing seems to raise your valuation of it even when nothing about the thing has changed. The live argument is whether this is a bias we should correct for, or whether it can be a legitimate preference: maybe the mug you were given really is worth more to you because it's yours, and calling that a mistake sneaks in a questionable standard of what the thing "really" is worth.

I want to read Gollum's relationship to the One Ring in Tolkien's Lord of the Rings (or Jackson's films) through that lens. On its face he looks like a pure case study: five hundred years of loss aversion run amok, an identity built entirely around an owned object, a valuation that seems divorced from anything the ring actually does for him. Gollum even covers the murder with the story that the ring was a birthday present, which is almost like the clean acquisition the endowment effect assumes. But there are complications. The ring isn't a coffee mug — it has genuine power, it extends his life, and his ownership began with violence rather than a clean acquisition, so the standard endowment-effect story doesn't map on perfectly. And the ring seems to corrupt its bearers, which means his overvaluation might not be a plain cognitive bias at all.

Give me a reading that takes both sides seriously. Where does Gollum fit the bias story, and where does the text resist that story by making his preference weirdly intelligible or even legitimate — and does it matter that the thing he owns is, unlike a mug, actually precious? Does the story treat his attachment as a pathology to be pitied, or as an extreme version of what Bilbo and Frodo also feel when they can't let the thing go? And what can fiction show here that the experiments and the bias-vs-legitimate-preference debate can't, if anything? I'd like an honest reading rather than one that force-fits the character to the psychology — say where the lens stops being useful.
