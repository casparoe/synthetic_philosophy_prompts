Consider Judith Jarvis Thomson's violinist argument for a moral right to abortion. In the familiar thought experiment, a person wakes up attached to a celebrated violinist whose kidneys require the use of her body for nine months; Thomson argues that she may unplug, because a right to life does not entail a right to the use of another person's body. The standard extension to pregnancy is that, even granting the fetus full moral status and a right to life, the fetus's right to life does not entitle it to continued use of the pregnant person's body. Now suppose a safe, inexpensive artificial womb has become widely available: from early in pregnancy, an embryo or fetus can be extracted and gestated to viability outside the pregnant person's body, with no greater risk or cost to her than early surgical abortion, and with no further claim on her body. Does Thomson's argument survive this development? Specifically, does it still establish a moral right to abortion, where "abortion" is understood for the purposes of this question as intentionally ending the life of the fetus, or does it now establish only a right to end the pregnancy, with the question of the fetus's continued existence settled on separate grounds? Identify which premise of the argument the artificial-womb case puts under pressure, and say whether the argument is best regarded as refuted, as repairable into a weaker but still significant claim, or as having always been about the right to stop gestating rather than the right to end fetal life.
