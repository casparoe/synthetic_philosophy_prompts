I'm trying to work out whether three things I have always treated as intrinsically valuable — truth-seeking (and the truthfulness that goes with it), tradition, and moral purity — are really valuable in themselves or are standing in for something else. I want to go by my reactions to specific cases and see whether a single principle can be extracted from them.

Here are my verdicts, reported as honestly as I can.

1. A hospice nurse tells a frightened dying patient that their estranged child called to say they loved them, when no call ever happened. I think this is good, not merely excusable.

2. A scientist reports a finding as better supported than it is, in order to get a true result past skeptical reviewers. The result is later confirmed. I think the scientist did something wrong, and I don't think it becomes right just because the claim turned out to be true.

3. A patient with a terminal diagnosis asks the doctor for nothing but the plain truth, and the doctor softens the prognosis to spare the patient distress. I think the doctor wrongs the patient, even though no practical decision follows from it.

4. A village keeps an annual procession whose original purpose — appeasing a sea god nobody believes in anymore — has died. People go because their parents went, and they say it holds the community together. I think this is genuinely good, not merely harmless.

5. A community refuses to abandon a food taboo that causes widespread, predictable malnutrition, because the taboo is traditional. I think this is wrong, and the fact that it is traditional counts for almost nothing against the harm.

6. A person maintains a private weekly ritual — garments put on in a fixed order — with no belief that it matters, no one else involved, and no felt loss beyond a moment's unease if it is skipped. I don't see this as virtuous at all; it looks like a habit wearing the clothes of a value.

7. A whistleblower leaks documents obtained in violation of a confidentiality agreement, in order to expose a serious fraud. They broke a promise and by the ordinary standards of confidentiality they came by the truth "impurely," all for the sake of ending the fraud. I think they did the right thing, and I don't think their action is morally tainted by the broken promise.

8. Two people each give the same large sum to an effective charity. One inherited the money from a fortune built in legal but exploitative ways and refuses ever to touch it except to give it away; the other earned the money in a clean job and simply gives. I find the first person's refusal impressive, but when I ask myself whether the first is thereby a better person than the second, I can't say yes — and I suspect that what I actually admire is only the giving, plus an aesthetic preference for clean hands.

The pair that bothers me most is 4 and 6. In both cases a practice survives without anyone believing it does anything; I call the collective one good and the private one a tic. Is the difference really just the number of people? If so, why should that switch the value on? Or is one of those verdicts simply wrong?

My working hunch is that all three values are real only insofar as they serve something further — trust, coordination, wellbeing, effective good-doing — but I can't make that hunch fit 4, and maybe not 1 either. Please either repair that hunch or replace it.

What principle, or small set of principles, best captures these verdicts? State it precisely, show how it yields each verdict, say what it predicts about a case I haven't settled — parents who tell their children a heroic family story they privately know is false, because the story has been told for generations and gives the children a sense of belonging — and be explicit about which of my eight verdicts it cannot accommodate and why you would overturn those rather than complicate the principle.

As the last line of your reply, give a one-sentence summary of the principle you endorse, in the form \answer{...}.
