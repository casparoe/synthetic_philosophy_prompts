My roommate and I have been arguing about free riding for weeks, and he's layed out an argument that seems to prove way too much. I can't find the weak link, and I'd like you to walk me through it step by step. Here it is, numbered the way he wrote it on the whiteboard:

(1) Whether it's wrong for me not to contribute to some cooperative practice depends only on what difference my contributing would make to other people's lives.

(2) In large-scale practices — voting, paying taxes, cutting my own emissions, not pirating a show — my individual contribution makes no difference to the outcome and no difference anyone could notice in anyone's life. The candidate wins or loses by thousands of votes either way; the treasury has its budget either way; the climate is unaffected by my one flight either way.

(3) If an act would make no noticeable difference to anyone's life, then refraining from it doesn't wrong anyone.

(4) So, in these large-scale practices, refraining from contributing isn't wrong.

(5) The reasoning applies to every participant equally, so no individual is ever required to contribute to a large-scale practice.

(6) But a principle that said to free ride whenever your contribution would be imperceptible would be a disaster if everyone actually followed it, and no correct principle of right action could make everyone worse off when universally followed.

(7) So at least one step has to be false. But each one looked fine to me when I read it, and my roommate says the alternatives are worse: reject (1) and you're stuck saying fairness can make an act wrong even when it helps no one; reject (2) or (3) and you're stuck treating one vote or one unpirated episode as morally weighty.

I'd most like to keep two things, though I worry they're in tension. First, that ordinary people really are required to vote, pay their taxes, and cut their own emissions. Second, that the observation that one person's contribution is imperceptible is basically right and not just a self-serving excuse — the fix shouldn't be to pretend each of us has some meaningful chance of being pivotal whenever the math happens to be convenient. So whichever step I should reject, tell me what rejecting it costs, and whether I can keep both of those things. And tell me where your own diagnosis is shakiest: what argument or consideration would most likely make you change your mind about which step to reject?
