I work at the interface of molecular biology and philosophy, and a debate in my group has settled into something of a truce. The molecular biologists speak without embarrassment of DNA as carrying information in a literal sense: the cell reads a gene, a point mutation can be a copying error, a frameshift garbles the message, and the genome is a code in something more than a picturesque way. The philosophers reply that every one of those descriptions has a purely causal paraphrase: certain nucleotide triplets raise the probability that particular amino acids get joined into a growing polypeptide chain, and the impressive stability of the codon table across organisms reflects shared descent rather than semantic content. On their view, the code talk is a high-level redescription of chemistry, and treating the genome as a text is projection rather than discovery. I cannot tell how much of this dispute is empirical, and I would like help turning it into something testable rather than merely verbal.

Please propose the strongest experiment or battery of experiments for deciding between these two readings of the claim that DNA is a code: the semantic reading, on which a sequence has genuine content that the cell in some sense reads, and the deflationary reading, on which it is causal machinery we find it useful to describe in code terms. Be concrete about the manipulation, the measurement, and the result that would favor each side; if the claim decomposes into several sub-claims, test them separately and say which is doing the real work. Then be explicit about what even a decisive-looking outcome would and would not establish — in particular, whether any observation could force the deflationist to concede that something more than causation is present, and whether a positive result would establish that the cell reads DNA in anything like the way a reader reads a text. If some part of the dispute cannot be settled by any experiment, say exactly which part and why.

End with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing a short phrase with your overall verdict, for example \answer{a literal code}, \answer{metaphor only}, \answer{causation plus something real but non-semantic}, or \answer{undecidable as posed}.
