Until recently I would have taken it as obvious that if I am in any mental condition worth naming — being in pain, feeling cold, being annoyed, seeming to see a red wall — I am at least in a position to know that I am in it, and that if I know something I am at least in a position to know that I know it. A minor medical episode made this newly live for me. After a short procedure I spent what felt like a long stretch insisting that I was comfortable and clear-headed while the recovery nurse, evidently correctly, kept telling me I was grimacing and slurring. In hindsight I cannot say whether I was uncomfortable without knowing it, knew it and at once forgot it, or was too cognitively altered for the question to get a grip. The experience pushed me toward the anti-luminosity view, and I now want to know how exposed that view really is.

The position I hold is this: no interesting, non-trivial mental condition is luminous, where a condition C is luminous just in case, whenever C obtains, the subject is in a position to know that it obtains. I accept the corollary that the KK principle fails in full generality: knowing p does not guarantee being in a position to know that one knows p. The argument that moved me is the gradual-change argument. Take an inexact condition such as feeling cold, run a subject stepwise from clearly cold to clearly not cold through a long series of adjacent cases too similar for the subject to tell apart with reliability, and notice that if feeling cold were luminous the subject would know they felt cold at the last cold link in the chain — yet knowledge seems to require a margin for error that the next, nearly indistinguishable link cannot supply. The argument is meant to generalize, and it treats positive introspection in standard epistemic logic as an idealization rather than a description of real agents.

Tell me what would count against this position, understood as a substantive claim about the limits of human self-knowledge rather than a merely logical point. Structure your answer in the following sections, in this order and at roughly the lengths indicated.

- **The position and its stakes.** Restate the claim and its corollary precisely, including what “in a position to know” has to mean for the argument to work, what the view predicts that pro-luminosity or pro-KK rivals do not, and what accepting it commits me to (roughly 150 words).

- **What would decisively count against it.** Which introspective results, experimental findings, formal arguments, or unacceptable consequences would break the view rather than merely complicate it? Engage the main escape routes available to defenders of luminosity or KK — for example, restricting luminosity to phenomenal character or to states known by direct acquaintance — and say how strong each one is (roughly 300 words).

- **What would be awkward but not fatal.** Which findings would force qualifications without overturning the core claim — for instance, domains in which KK seems to survive, or theorizing in economics and computer science that effectively assumes positive introspection (roughly 250 words).

- **What its defenders should themselves concede.** Which considerations should an honest anti-luminosity theorist admit count against the view, even if they ultimately answer them? Keep this distinct from critics’ charges and from what I might hope is true (roughly 200 words).

- **Is the view falsifiable, or has it been insulated?** Say whether the position has been protected by qualifications about trivial conditions, about “being in a position to know,” or about ideal versus real agents to the point of being compatible with everything, and whether that would be a serious defect for a thesis of this kind (roughly 200 words).

- **Bottom line.** Take a stand: given the defeaters canvassed, how confident should I remain in the anti-luminosity view, and which version of it, if any, survives? (roughly 150 words)

For calibration, I would be moved most by a demonstration that the margin-for-error premise can be dropped in the phenomenal domain without giving up on fallible knowledge elsewhere — not by the mere oddity of saying of oneself that one is in pain and does not know it. Please answer in American English.
