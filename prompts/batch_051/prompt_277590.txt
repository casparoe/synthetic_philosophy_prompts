Act as a professional philosopher of religion who specializes in the conceptual structure of eschatological positions. A colleague teaching an advanced undergraduate seminar on the problem of hell wants a handout: a decision tree that sorts students by where they actually stand on the question of what becomes of persons who die unreconciled to God, and whether that outcome can be just. The tree should sort by what a student commits themselves to in answering diagnostic questions, not by which doctrinal label they already like.

Before writing the final tree, briefly sketch the logical space: identify the independent separating claims that do the real work, then build the tree from those claims rather than from a list of names. The tree should be organized around the proportionality debate, so its spine should be the questions determining whether any hell can be just before questions about its population, duration, or final scope. Good candidate claims for branch nodes include: whether a finite life can merit an infinite punishment; whether divine justice is essentially retributive, remedial, or something else; whether punishment requires a continuing subject who suffers it; whether annihilation is a punishment at all or merely a release; whether a creature can persist in settled opposition to God with full knowledge of the alternative; and whether God could be perfectly happy and sovereign if any creature were finally lost. Phrase each branch node as a concrete claim or case a student can answer without already knowing the doctrinal vocabulary.

The leaves must include at least the following positions: retributive eternal conscious torment, annihilationism or conditional immortality, purgatorial universalism, and a bare universalism with no postmortem punishment. Add any further positions the logical space requires, but keep the tree to no more than ten leaves and six to nine branch questions. For each leaf, give the established name if the position has one, and write "unnamed" if it does not; then add a single sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Also mark at least two combinations of answers that no major position occupies, and diagnose each one briefly as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate, and state what further question would separate them.
