A friend of mine has been making the case that a destructive scan-and-copy "upload" of a person's brain is survivable, and she does it by leaning hard on an analogy. She says: think of a pub that's been in the same building for two hundred years. They've replaced the sign, the furniture, the owners, the staff, eventually even the walls, plank by plank. Her claim is that no one thinks it stopped being The Crown and Anchor at any particular moment. So identity can survive total material replacement as long as it happens gradually and the pattern of what the thing does is preserved.

Then she takes a second step. If you could get from the original brain to a fully uploaded version by replacing each neuron one at a time with a chip that does the same job, the "you" at the end is still you, by the pub logic. But if the chips were doing all the work, then a single-step copy of that chip-to-chip arrangement onto the same kind of chips must also be you, since it's the same informational pattern. So: either the gradual path preserves identity and so does the copy, or neither does. I've been pushing back and I dont know how to attack what feels like a bait and switch.

Could you audit the analogy from the inside? What corresponds to what — the pub's name, the owners, the walls, the customers, the legal and social conventions — and where does the mapping to a mind actually break? Which of those disanalogies, if any, matter for the step from gradual replacement to a single-step copy, and does her conclusion survive once they're on the table? A correspondence table would help.
