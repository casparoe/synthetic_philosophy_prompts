I am a philosophy professor whose research is in formal epistemology, and I chair the ethics and risk subcommittee of a regional health authority. We meet monthly to make decisions that commit or withhold substantial public money and affect health, often under uncertainty so deep that our experts and models give incompatible probabilities for the same outcome; our usual instinct is to average them, and I have never seen a defensible basis for doing so. I want to turn what is known about imprecise probabilities — the idea that rational belief is better represented by a set of probability assignments than by a single one — into a standing procedure that our committee can actually run without technical training, within a single two-hour meeting, with the final decision signed off by the authority's director. Please write that procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a refusal or deferral, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than technical judgment. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure and cannot be made mechanical. Then run the procedure on two cases: whether to fund a flood barrier when three climate models assign materially different probabilities to the flood it is meant to prevent, and whether to suspend a widely used drug when two large studies disagree about a rare but serious side effect; say plainly when the procedure returns an answer the committee would rather not accept. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, as in a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
