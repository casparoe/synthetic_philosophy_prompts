Here is an argument I keep reconstructing when I try to make the total-utility framework explicit in a universe with infinitely much value. I am granting, for the sake of argument, additive total consequentialism: value is summed across all welfare-bearers, and one act is better than another if and only if it produces a greater total sum. I am fairly sure the conclusion is wrong, but I want to know the exact step, not a refutation from outside the framework.

(1) An act A is morally better than an act B only if A produces a greater total amount of value than B.

(2) Suppose the universe contains infinitely much positive value. Then total value is an infinite quantity T.

(3) A finite act — saving one drowning child, rather than walking past — changes total value by only some finite amount d. The total is T + d in the one case and T in the other.

(4) Since d is finite, T and T + d can be put into one-to-one correspondence: each unit of value in T can be paired with a distinct unit of value in T + d, and conversely. So the two totals have the same magnitude; neither is greater than the other.

(5) Therefore saving the child does not produce a greater total value than walking past.

(6) So, by (1), saving the child is not better than walking past; all finite acts are morally on a par.

Question: Is this argument valid? If not, what is the first step that fails, and what is the correct version of that step? If you think step (4) is the culprit, explain exactly what goes wrong in the inference from one-to-one correspondence to equality of magnitude, and whether an additive total consequentialist can consistently hold that T + d is greater than T although both are infinite — and, if so, what formal representation of total value makes that possible. If you think an earlier step fails, say which and why. I want the diagnosis to be precise enough to distinguish a genuinely impossible implication from an artifact of treating an infinite total as a bare cardinal.
