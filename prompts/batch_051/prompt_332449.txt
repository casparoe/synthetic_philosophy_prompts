I've been thinking about gauge theories—electromagnetism in terms of potentials, Yang-Mills, the standard model. The view is this: the gauge-dependent parts of the formalism are pure descriptive surplus. The vector potential, the connection, a choice of gauge—none of it is physically real. Only gauge-invariant quantities (field strengths, Wilson loops, holonomies, scattering amplitudes) pick out real features of the world. The gauge symmetry is just a symptom of redundancy in our description, same spirit as coordinate freedom in general relativity. I know this is basically the mainstream line, or close to it, which is exactly why I want it stress-tested.

Tell me what would count against this. Not just "you need to fix a gauge to do calculations"—that feels like an annoyance, not an objection, unless someone can turn it into one. I want the stuff that should make me downgrade the view from "obviously right" to "one interpretation among several" or abandon it outright. Things I half-remember and can't decide about: the Aharonov-Bohm effect, where the loop integral of the potential shows up in the phase; the fact that gauge-invariant observables often have to be nonlocal; anomalies; ghost fields; the way quantization seems to make the gauge structure do real work; maybe the fact that gauge fixing isn't always unique. Take whatever subset of those you think matters, add whatever I'm missing, and for each say whether a committed surplus-structure person would grant that it counts against them or explain it away.

Then rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. If the view ends up mostly insulated, say so, and say whether the insulation is a feature or a bug.
