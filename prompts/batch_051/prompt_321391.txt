You are marking a final exam in an upper-level course on moral philosophy. The question the student was asked to answer was:

Assess the claim that the distinction between killing and letting die has no intrinsic moral significance, so that when it matters morally it does so only through factors that tend to accompany it, such as intention, special obligations, or the cost of intervening. Explain what the claim must mean if it is to be interesting, present the strongest argument for it and the strongest reply, and say what paired-case arguments of the bathtub variety do and do not establish.

The student answered:

The distinction between killing and letting die is the difference between bringing a death about through one's actions and allowing a death to occur that one could have prevented. The claim that the distinction has no intrinsic moral significance means that the bare difference between the two provides, by itself, no reason for treating one case differently from the other; if the distinction matters, it is only because it usually accompanies something that does the actual moral work.

The strongest argument for this claim is the paired-case argument. Consider two men, each of whom will inherit a fortune only if his nephew dies, and each of whom has decided to drown the nephew in his bath on Sunday. The first finds the child playing in the water, holds his head under, and kills him. The second, with the same motive and plan, finds the child already underwater and simply stands by. In both cases the boy dies from drowning, and in both cases the man wanted and intended exactly that outcome. Our judgment of the two men is the same: neither is less culpable than the other, and no plausible moral view would treat the man who stood by as better merely because he did not move his body. Since the men have the same motives, intentions, opportunities, and outcomes, the only remaining difference between them is a difference in bodily movement, and a bare difference in bodily movement cannot be intrinsically significant. The killing/letting-die distinction therefore carries no intrinsic moral weight.

This does not mean the distinction never matters. A lifeguard who lets a swimmer drown is just as culpable as a lifeguard who actively drowns a swimmer, but this is fully explained by the duty attached to the role, not by the doing/allowing distinction itself. The same goes for cases of withdrawing versus withholding life-sustaining care: here too, what seems to matter is never the bare difference between acting and refraining but a further factor—the clinician's intention, the professional role, or the costs of continued care. The distinction therefore matters only derivatively, through these companions, and treating it as morally weighty in its own right is a confusion.
