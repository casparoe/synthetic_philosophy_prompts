A colleague of mine works on moral personhood from a broadly relational, Ubuntu-inspired standpoint. Rather than asking her to state the theory outright, I have been collecting her verdicts on particular cases, all of them answers to the question whether the being in question is a full moral person — by which I mean a member of the moral community in the sense that carries full moral standing. Her verdicts so far:

1. A healthy adult member of a small community, active in reciprocal relations of care and obligation: a full moral person.
2. A healthy adult from a distant, culturally unrelated society with which she has had no contact: a full moral person.
3. A human newborn: not yet a full moral person, but a human being with substantial moral status and a claim to be raised into personhood.
4. A human adult in a persistent vegetative state with no measurable awareness: no longer a full moral person, though a human being still owed respect.
5. A human adult with a significant intellectual disability who needs daily support but shows clear affection, simple communication, and participation in family routines: a full moral person.
6. A chimpanzee raised in a household, using a limited set of signs and strongly bonded to its caregivers: not a full moral person, despite the relationships.
7. A corporation that the law treats as a person for some purposes: not a full moral person; legal personhood is a status of another kind.
8. A fully competent adult who, after a normal upbringing, spent thirty years as a solitary hermit with no human contact: still a full moral person.

Next week she will be asked about a new case. An adult human of normal biological endowment was abandoned in infancy and grew up without human society or language. After being brought into care around age twenty, the person tests as broadly normal cognitively and affectively for an adult, learns signs quickly, and seems capable of forming attachments, but has never entered into human relationships or a moral community. I want to know what she will say about this case before she says it.

Predict her verdict. In doing so, lay out the candidate rules of personhood that fit all eight of her earlier verdicts, identify which of those rules diverge on this new case, and say what each predicts. Then give your best guess about which rule she is actually running and why. Finally, specify one further case, distinct from the new one, that would most cleanly settle which of the remaining candidate rules she holds.
