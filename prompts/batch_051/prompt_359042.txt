Modern democracies run their franchises as a patchwork. A citizen who left decades ago and has no continuing presence in the country can typically still vote in national elections. A non-citizen permanent resident who has lived, worked, and paid taxes in the country for decades typically cannot vote in national elections, even though in many places that same resident can vote in municipal ones. A sixteen-year-old who works and pays income tax cannot vote anywhere. A visitor or recent arrival cannot vote even when the state's decisions plainly affect their interests, and incarcerated citizens are disenfranchised in some places and enfranchised in others with no obvious principle behind the difference. I take this to be the boundary problem: who belongs to the demos? Does this patchwork presuppose any single account of membership in the demos—all-affected-interests, all-subjected-to-law, citizenship, stakeholding, residency, or something else—or is it an unstable compound of several? Reconstruct the conception the practice best rationalizes, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely permits, identify which rival views it quietly rules out, and say what would have to change in franchise law if that conception were replaced by each of the main rivals.
