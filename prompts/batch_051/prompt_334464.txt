As a journalist who covers science and its conceptual foundations, I recently sat in on a working group about classification in the sciences, and an evolutionary biologist offered the following argument for the claim that a good scientific taxonomy is not merely useful but must be tracking structure that is really out there in the world. I have reconstructed his reasoning as precisely as I can, and I am left with a conclusion I find almost impossible to accept and a chain of steps I cannot fault.

(1) If a single domain can be legitimately partitioned into kinds in more than one way — the same organisms sorted by evolutionary ancestry, by ecological role, or by genomic similarity, with none of these being the uniquely correct taxonomy — then the correctness of a partition is relative to the classifier's purpose. (2) If the correctness of a partition is relative to purpose, then there is no purpose-independent respect in which one partition fits the structure of the world better than another; to call one partition better is simply to say it serves a chosen goal better. (3) But there is such a purpose-independent respect. A taxonomy that groups whales with fish is not merely inconvenient for certain projects; it fails to record something about how those organisms are related that a whales-with-mammals taxonomy records, and this failure would remain even if someone's immediate purposes made the whales-with-fish grouping handy. (4) From (1) through (3), then, we must reject the multiplicity described in (1). If we want to keep (3), the structure of the world must settle a single best partition for any given domain, and the cross-cutting taxonomies used by the special sciences are at best convenient distortions. (5) But that conclusion is flatly at odds with how the sciences operate: genetics, morphology, and ecology cross-classify the same organisms without imputing error, and chemistry groups the same substances in different ways for synthesis, for toxicology, and for biological function. The argument seems to prove too much.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I am most unwilling to surrender (3), and my instinct is that nature can have a real structure without that structure being a single filing system, but I know that instinct is not a diagnosis. Please identify the step or premise that should be rejected, and say what rejecting it costs.
