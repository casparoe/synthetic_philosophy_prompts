I've been rereading the Pearl material after the old causality sequence posts, and I've built an argument that feels like it proves too much, and I can't find the flaw. I'll number it so you can point at a step.

(1) The do-calculus is complete: whenever a causal query is identifiable from observational data plus a known causal diagram, there's a finite derivation converting P(y | do(x)) into a do-free expression. That's the ladder-climbing story — sometimes rung-2 quantities collapse into rung-1 data.

(2) So, once I have a graph, the only remaining problem is algebra. Randomization doesn't contribute causal information that observational data lack; it just hands me a graph where the treatment has no parents, i.e. it draws the diagram for me.

(3) And when I don't have a graph, I can learn it from data. PC, FCI and similar discovery algorithms recover the skeleton and the essential orientations from observational data under assumptions like faithfulness and acyclicity, and with enough data they're supposed converge to the true structure.

(4) Therefore, in principle, randomized trials are just a convenience for people who can't wait for more observational data and better structure-learning. A big enough dataset plus a decent discovery algorithm should make trials strictly unecessary.

(5) But that conclusion is absurd. It would mean the FDA's insistence on randomized evidence is a concession to our statistical weakness rather than a real epistemic requirement, and that unmeasured confounding is never an in-principle obstacle, only a practical one.

(6) So something in (1)–(4) has to be false, or one of the steps is invalid. Where does this go wrong, and what do I give up by rejecting that step? I'd most like to keep (1), since the completeness theorem seems solid to me, but I'm afraid that forces me onto harder choices elsewhere.

When you answer, tell me how confident you actually are in the diagnosis and single out the specific point you're least sure about.
