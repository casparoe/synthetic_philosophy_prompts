I work as an appraisal officer for a district council in England. Among my duties is writing the community-impact section of assessments for decisions that come before the planning and assets committees: whether to sell a community building for redevelopment, to close or merge a school or library, to approve a road scheme that severs a residential area, or to allow a high-street change that displaces the places where people regularly meet. I need a decision procedure my team can reuse, not a one-off opinion: a sequence of questions to ask in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure stops with a recommendation to proceed (with or without mitigation), and where it stops because the choice is one of values and must be handed to elected members rather than settled by an officer's checklist.

The conceptual difficulty is that belonging is not just a preference. A community can be a source of continuity, mutual recognition, informal mutual aid, low-friction repeated contact and shared identity—goods that are relational and cannot simply be recreated by providing alternative services in another building. But "community" is also used loosely, sometimes meaning only a demographic area or an imagined group with little actual interaction; treating every use of the word as equally weighty would make any change impossible. The procedure therefore needs to distinguish a functioning community from an aggregate, to separate losses that can be offset (space, services, activities) from losses of the relational good itself, and to say when compensation or replacement can reasonably stand in for what is lost.

Each assessment should be completable in about a day by a generalist officer without specialist social-science training; the procedure must be usable across roughly thirty cases a year; and the final sign-off rests with elected members, so the procedure should tell us clearly what is a screening or factual matter, what is a judgement call, and what is a properly political values choice.

Please write the procedure, then run it on at least three cases:

(a) A proposal to demolish a declining working men's club—average age of members in their seventies, daily use by a small core, much of it for mutual checking-in and lifts—to build forty affordable flats, with the club offered nothing guaranteed but a possible future room in a nearby community hall.

(b) A controversial proposal to close a small village primary school and merge it into a new central school a few miles away; the school is a focal point for parents and children, but the village keeps its pub, hall and shop, and the council offers a minibus and a weekly stay-and-play session in the village hall.

(c) A proposal to pedestrianise a town-centre street in a way that would end the free drop-in use of a library foyer where a loose but long-standing group of mostly elderly and disabled residents gathers daily; the pedestrianisation is expected to benefit many other residents and traders.

Use at least one case to show the procedure giving a result a council would be reluctant to accept; do not soften the procedure so that it always tells the authority what it wants. After the worked cases, say plainly what the procedure cannot settle and where it would need revision after use.

Write in British English. Begin your reply with the procedure itself, with no preface or commentary and without restating the question; then give the worked applications and the note on the procedure's limits.
