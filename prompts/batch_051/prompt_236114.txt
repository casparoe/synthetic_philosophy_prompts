My friend and I just got back from a three-hour argument about the axiom of choice, and we want to turn it into something useful. I’m on the side that the axiom of choice is obviously true—if you have a bunch of nonempty boxes, you can pick one thing from each box. My friend thinks it’s not that innocent: it lets you prove Banach-Tarski-type stuff and that the reals can be well-ordered, so calling it “obvious” is smuggling in finitary intuitions. We both read Russell’s Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy, chapter XII, “Selections and the Multiplicative Axiom,” but we can’t agree on what his passage is doing. He introduces the multiplicative axiom as the claim that any collection of nonempty, mutually exclusive classes has a selector class containing exactly one term from each—then treats it as less self-evident than the other axioms, even though it looks like a simple generalization of ordinary choosing. That’s as much as we can agree to paraphrase, but I want the document to be about the concept, not Russell exegesis.

Write the joint statement the two of us could actually sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section and then a disagreements table. Include: claims about where the axiom is actually used in ordinary proofs (as opposed to where it just feels used); whether the “picking” metaphor is doing hidden work; which infinities each side thinks are acceptable; and the precise formulation of where we part ways. For each disagreement, give the crux—what mathematical result, argument, or conceptual analysis would move at least one side—and each side’s prediction written down in advance. Where a disagreement is just about words, say so and dissolve it. Dont try to settle the argument; just make it exact enough that we could hand it to someone else and theyd know what the fight is really about.
