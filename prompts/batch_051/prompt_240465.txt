I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about a disciplinary case on which I serve as an appeals board member for a professional association. A working group badly mishandled a public project, and the draft decision recommends expelling the most junior member who compiled the final report, even though the failures were plainly distributed across the group and he neither designed nor authorized them. My friend insisted that the word scapegoat is only a metaphor here: the board is not claiming blame is literally transferred to him, only that consequences have to land somewhere and he is the one being singled out. I am not convinced the ritual case and the organizational case are so different.

The argument kept returning to the structural parallel I cannot decide about. In a scapegoat ritual, a community's accumulated guilt or impurity is laid on a victim who is not causally responsible for it; the victim is then expelled or destroyed, and the rite treats the community as cleansed. In an organization, a collective failure occurs, a member who is not its principal author is made to bear the blame and is removed, and the institution afterward treats the matter as closed. In both, something is transferred from a group to an individual, and the individual's removal is supposed to do something restorative for the group.

My question is less about the case itself than about the parallel. Is this a real identity of structure, or is the organizational case merely borrowing an old metaphor? I would like the mapping laid out precisely, including where the cases match and where they come apart. Specifically: is the transfer of guilt in the ritual case the same kind of relation as the transfer of blame or responsibility in the organizational case, or are these distinct relations hidden under one word? What would have to be true of collective responsibility for the parallel to license saying that an organization literally expels its own guilt when it fires its scapegoat? And does the parallel predict anything that turns out false—for instance, that removing the scapegoat actually restores the organization rather than merely allowing it to declare itself restored?
