ok so I just spent an hour and a half arguing with my friend marco about harsanyi's aggregation theorem and now im way less sure of my own talk, which is a problem because I give it tuesday. the thesis im defending is that the theorem isnt a proof of utilitarianism, but it does something almost worse: it forces anyone who accepts the von Neumann-Morgenstern expected-utility axioms both for individuals and for a social evaluator, plus pareto indifference, to either sign up for evaluating options by a weighted sum of individual utilities or reject something further upstream. my claim is that the real fight is entirely in the inputs — what those vNM utilities represent, whether you can calibrate scales across people (so, interpersonal comparisons), and whether its even coherent to make the social evaluator satisfy vNM at all. so the familiar objection that you just cant add utilities across people misses the point: by the time youve accepted pareto indifference and vNM for the evaluator, the adding is already forced. thats the talk.

marco's counter was that the whole thing is circular. the weights are arbitrary until you bring in the impartial observer (equal probability of being anyone), and once you bring that in youve already assumed utilitarianism, so the theorem is theater. i pushed back that a representation theorem can still be informative if it relocates the moral content somewhere you can fight about it precisely, but now i keep turning it over.
