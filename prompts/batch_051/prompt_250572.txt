I'm a clinical psych PhD student, and I've been going in circles with my advisor about a word everyone in our lab uses but nobody can define: rumination. The standard clinical gloss is something like repetitive, passive, negatively-valenced self-focused thinking, but that seems to me to capture too much and too little at once — it makes a structured journaling habit sound like pathology, and it doesn't explain the faintly pejorative edge the word has in ordinary use. What I actually want is a working defintion that gets the folk contrast right: the difference between dwelling on yourself in a stuck, harmful way and examining yourself in a way that's productive or at least neutral.

So here are the cases I want the definition to handle, roughly ordered from clear rumination to clearly not: (1) lying awake at 3 a.m. replaying an argument, cycling through the same four sentences without getting anywhere; (2) brooding over a mistake at work for days after you've already apologized and fixed it; (3) endlessly generating interpretations of an ambiguous text from a friend; (4) a scientist going over a failed experiment again and again until the confound is found; (5) a therapist and client tracing a relationship pattern across sessions until the client changes how they act; (6) ten minutes of journalling each night, writing down what went wrong and one thing to try tomorrow; (7) a monk doing analytic meditation on thoughts arising and passing, without distress; (8) a mindfulness exercise where you notice a thought and label it "thinking" without following it.

I suspect the word is doing two jobs at once — one descriptive and one condemnatory — and that some of these cases move between the two definitions. I'm especially curious whether the negative valence or the repetitiveness is doing the real work. If you agree about the two jobs, give me both definitions and say which cases shift. Whatever you settle on, run it against every case, tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise or defend the surprising verdicts. And flag the verdict you're least secure about; I'm less worried about matching the DSM than about getting the ordinary contrast right.
