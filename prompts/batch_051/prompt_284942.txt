I am a PhD candidate in philosophy of art working on the aesthetics of agency in digital games. For a graduate seminar session on games as art, I would like to close with a poem that does real philosophical work, and I would like you to write it for me.

Please act as an expert philosopher of aesthetics with a solid command of Schiller's Aesthetic Education and of contemporary debates about whether games are art because of what they let players do. Think carefully before composing. In particular, before you draft anything, privately sketch how each structural feature of the poem can carry a step of the argument, and only then write the poem itself.

The occasion pairs a passage from Letter XIV of Schiller's Letters on the Aesthetic Education of Man with a discussion of agency-centered accounts of game aesthetics. The passage I have in mind is the one in which Schiller distinguishes the play drive from the sensuous drive and the form drive: the play drive is neither passive compulsion nor bare formal constraint, but a third thing that suspends both, taking living form as its object, and it is in play, on this account, that the human being is fully realized. I will not quote the passage directly, and I do not want the poem to quote it either. Instead, the poem should enact the passage's logic.

Write a sonnet. The octave should build the naive view of a videogame as a made object of screens, fiction, sound, and rule-bound challenges. The volta should land at the moment the reader sees that the game's aesthetic object is not those components but the shape of agency itself: the opening, tightening, and resolution of possible action inside the game's constraints. The closing couplet should say, with exactness rather than mere names, what Schiller's play drive lets us see about games that neither a formalist reduction to mechanics nor a narratological reduction to story can capture.

Specialists in Schiller and in the philosophy of games should both recognize the poem as right; a reader who knows neither should still find it a good sonnet.
