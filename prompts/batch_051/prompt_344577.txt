Below is a passage from Arthur Schopenhauer's essay "The Vanity of Existence," in Studies in Pessimism (translated by T. Bailey Saunders, 1913):

"The whole foundation on which our existence rests is the present—the ever-fleeting present. It lies, then, in the very nature of our existence to take the form of constant motion, and to offer no possibility of our ever attaining the rest for which we are always striving. We are like a man running downhill, who cannot keep on his legs unless he runs on, and will inevitably fall if he stops; or, again, like a pole balanced on the tip of one's finger; or like a planet, which would fall into its sun the moment it ceased to hurry forward on its way. Unrest is the mark of existence."

The passage seems to run several lines of thought together, and I want to know which are doing the real work. Below are five reconstructions of what the passage argues. Rank them from most to least faithful to what the text actually establishes, and score each out of ten for how much warrant the passage gives it. Give one sentence per item for its rank, naming what it captures and what it overreaches. Then tell me: which reconstruction commits Schopenhauer to a claim only if we import his doctrine of the will-to-live from outside this passage, and which could stand if one kept the claims about time and motion but dropped that doctrine entirely?

(a) Because the present constantly vanishes, no state we reach can be a final good, so striving is all there is to a human life.
(b) Existence is essentially restless: it sustains itself only by moving, as in the runner, pole, and planet similes, and rest would be its collapse.
(c) We are constitutively aimed at rest, yet the nature of existence makes rest unattainable, so existence is self-frustrating by structure rather than by bad luck.
(d) Unrest is not a contingent affliction of unhappy lives but the mark of existence itself, so no happiness consisting in lasting satisfaction is possible.
(e) The passage is a compressed statement of the will-to-live doctrine: time is the medium in which a blind, endless becoming reveals its own failure to reach an end.
