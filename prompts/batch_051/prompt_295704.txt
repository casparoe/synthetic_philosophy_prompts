I have been working through the pragmatist theory of truth and find myself drawn to the version associated with Peirce rather than the version associated with James. James's emphasis on beliefs that prove satisfying or expedient strikes me as making truth too dependent on the needs and temperament of the individual believer. Peirce's account, by contrast, seems objective: to say that a proposition is true is not to say it mirrors a world standing wholly apart from inquiry, but that investigation, pursued far enough by a community of inquirers, would settle on that proposition and would not be moved off it by further evidence or criticism. I understand this as a counterfactual about the ideal limit of inquiry, not as the claim that truth is whatever people currently agree on, and not as the crude verificationist claim that nothing is true until someone has actually checked. The view appeals to me because it ties truth to the practices of testing, exposure to anomaly, and correction that I already trust, while avoiding both a mysterious correspondence relation and an individualist relativism.

But there is a class of cases I cannot make the view absorb, and they matter to me. Call them lost facts. Take the question whether an odd or even number of leaves fell in a particular uninhabited valley on a particular autumn night in the ninth century, or the question what an anonymous traveler muttered under his breath on a road three thousand years ago. No record was made, and no trace remains; the evidence is gone. Yet it seems to me plainly true that there is a fact of the matter in each case: the leaf-count was odd or it was even, and the traveler said one definite thing rather than another. These are not speculative or exotic claims; they are just ordinary facts that happen to be unrecoverable.

The Peircean account appears to leave only two ways of handling them, and both strike me as hard to accept. It can say that, because no possible inquiry would converge on an answer, the propositions are neither true nor false; but that makes truth about the past depend on the future investigative capacities of inquirers, which seems to get the direction of dependence backwards. Or it can say that inquiry could in principle recover the answer if it were only pushed far enough; but that seems like a refusal to take seriously how completely the traces have vanished. I suspect the right response may be to debunk my own intuition: perhaps the sense that these lost facts are determinate is a residue of the correspondence picture I claim to have abandoned, and the pragmatist should teach me to treat it as a merely verbal doubt. But that suspicion sits badly with me, because the oddness or evenness of the leaves does not feel like a philosopher's contrivance; it feels like a fact I am simply not in a position to know.

I want a negotiated verdict, not a vote for one side. Present the strongest case for the Peircean account's treatment of lost facts and the strongest case for taking the intuition at face value. Then tell me which response is best, and, more importantly, say what would justify each of the available moves: debunking the intuition and dropping it; amending the theory so that truth can outrun any possible settlement; or showing that the convergence view, read correctly, does not require an actually recoverable answer and is compatible with my conviction. If you think some version of the theory can be kept, be explicit about what it gives up.
