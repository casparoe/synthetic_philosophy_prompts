Here's an asymmetry I can't get to sit right. A city passes an ordinance making it a $200 fine to stand in a public square and scream an ugly insult at a passing stranger. A lot of people—including people who think the screamer behaved badly—call the fine a free-speech outrage: even a small state fine for speech is coercive, it leaves a record, it's decided by officials with their own incentives, and the person can't realistically fight it. Now change only the mechanism. Someone films the same insult, an online mob identifies the man, and he loses his job, gets deplatformed from the sites he uses for work, and his landlord declines to renew his lease; the total cost lands around $50,000. None of the features that supposedly made the fine objectionable are present—no finding of fact, no appeal, no proportionality limit, no end date—and no one with authority ever had to say he'd done anything wrong. Yet a big slice of the same people who opposed the $200 fine call this a private group exercising its speech and association rights, or just "consequences." So a $200 state fine is tyranny but a $50,000 shunning is the marketplace of ideas working as intented. What, if anything, justifies treating a larger private sanction as less troubling than a smaller formal one for the same act? Is the load-bearing difference the state's monopoly on legitimate force, due process, the fact that no one has a right to a job, the thought that private sanctions are just people talking and therefore can't chill speech, or is it just an inconsistency we live with? Pick whichever distinction you think is doing the most work, test it against nearby cases—a small shop refusing service, a union blacklist, a congregation shunning a member, a neighborhood that collectively keys a car—and say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
