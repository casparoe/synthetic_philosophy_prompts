I have been defending a view about the limits of possible experience, and I want an honest map of what would count against it. The view is that with sufficiently precise neural interfaces—say, patterned microstimulation of early visual cortex, or direct modulation of the channels that implement colour opponency—a human being could in principle have a genuinely novel colour experience: a hue that is not a mixture, interpolation, saturation variant, or memory distortion of any hue she has previously encountered. I mean a new way for a surface or scene to look, standing to familiar hues roughly as a newly discovered primary would stand to red, yellow, green, and blue. I am not claiming present technology can produce this, only that there is no in-principle barrier set by human neurobiology or by the nature of colour experience itself.

Several considerations push the other way. Opponent-process models of hue coding suggest a closed set of elementary hue mechanisms. Colour similarity space appears to have a stable geometry into which every reportable hue fits. The disputed reports of forbidden reddish-green and bluish-yellow experiences from image-stabilization work are widely taken to be artifacts, afterimages, or unstable transitional states rather than stable novel qualia. And representationalist or functionalist accounts of consciousness may entail that any experience a subject can recognize and report must already belong to a kind within her conceptual or discriminatory repertoire. Some of these may be decisive; others may count only against the most incautious formulation of the view.

Play it straight. What would have to be true for this position to be false, and which of those things are we already in sight of? Distinguish evidence that would be fatal from evidence that would be merely awkward; identify the objections a committed defender should concede are genuine risks rather than caricatures; and rank the defeaters from the one that should move me most to the one I could reasonably set aside. Say what the view commits me to that a closed-repertoire rival would not, and which of those commitments could in principle be tested. Finally, say whether the view, as formulated, risks anything at all, or whether the words novelty and in principle have been stretched far enough to insulate it from refutation—and if it has been insulated, whether that is a reason to reject it or simply the normal cost of a modal claim about experience.
