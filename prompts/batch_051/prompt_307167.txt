I've been stuck for a month on the Islamic version of the Euthyphro problem, and I want something more constructive than another defense of one side. Quick background so we're on the same page: the Mu'tazila held that reason can know that some acts are wrong before any revelation arrives, so God commands the good because it is good, and God can't command something unjust—God's justice is real, not just whatever God ends up willing. The Ash'arites held the opposite: nothing is good or evil or obligatory until God commands or forbids it, so if God had commanded lying, lying would have been obligatory, and no standard outside God's will binds or limits Him. Both sides still insist God is just and truthful, which is where the trouble starts. (Some Mu'tazila pushed this to the aslah doctrine—that God does, and in some versions must do, what is most beneficial for His creatures—which their opponents read as making God subject to a human-ish welfare calculus.)

Fix the purpose as: a theory good enough to tell a reasonable believer what to say about a command that looks unjust, and whether 'God is just' is still a claim with content rather than a tautology. Now the actual task: write the full specification that a theory of divine command and morality has to meet before its allowed to settle this debate. I don't care which side wins; I want the desiderata first.

Structure it in exactly this order:

1. Fixed points (about 200 words): the claims no serious participant in the debate would give up, with why each is a fixed point. I'd expect at least: God is just and truthful; God isn't under an outside law; divine commands genuinely obligate creatures; and some commands look insane to ordinary reason while others look reasonable.
2. Distinctions that can't be collapsed (about 250 words). I have candidates—value vs obligation, what makes something right vs how we know it's right, a standard binding God vs one binding us, God's being good vs our judging Him good—but I want you to add any others the debate needs.
3. Requirements list (about 500 words): each requirement plus the case or argument that forces it, and mark each one negotiable or nonnegotiable. The hard cases must include Abraham being commanded to kill his son, the hypothetical command to lie, a ruling God later changes, and a genuinely new case scripture doesnt mention.
4. Scorecard (about 200 words): the simple Mu'tazilite view and the simple Ash'arite view graded against the list (satisfied / stretched / violates); a table is fine.
5. The one exclusionary requirement (about 150 words): the single requirement that does the most to exclude both simple readings, and why.
