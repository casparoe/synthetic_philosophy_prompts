I've been toying with an idea I call the accommodation budget. The standard story as I understand it: speakers presuppose stuff, hearers accommodate if it's unproblematic, and the common ground quietly updates; occasionally someone balks and says wait, that's not established. My addition is that accommodation isn't free and isn't all-or-nothing. Every time a hearer quietly lets in a presupposition that wasn't already shared, it draws down a finite, per-conversation budget of tolerance — the bigger the gap between what's presupposed and what the hearer actually took for granted, the bigger the withdrawal. A single loaded question (have you stopped cheating on the exam?) overdraws the account at once, and you get the balking response. What I actually want checked is the accumulation claim: a string of individually tiny presuppositions can quietly drain the budget, so a perfectly ordinary question that would have been accommodated at minute one gets challenged at minute forty, not because the question is worse but because the hearer is out of patience. I think this explains why people snap at innocuous questions late in a tense meeting. Is this a real mechanism or just a metaphor with a bank account attached? Does it already exist under some name in the presupposition/scorekeeping literature (I know the Lewis-style picture and the basics, not much beyond)? Where does it break — give me a concrete case where the cumulative-budget prediction comes out wrong or predicts conversational behavior nobody actually exhibits. And keep the settled part separate from the genuinely contested part; don't present a contested view as if it's the consensus.
