I have been trying to get clear about how one ought to respond to higher-order evidence—evidence that bears not on a question itself but on how likely one is to have answered the question correctly—and about whether there is anything wrong with epistemic akrasia, by which I roughly mean holding a first-order judgment while also taking your evidence not to support it. A friend of mine, Rhea, has a much more settled view on these matters than I do, and over the years I have kept an informal log of how she rules on concrete cases, hoping to reverse-engineer the principle behind her verdicts.

Her track record, as I have it, runs like this. (1) A programmer who has been awake for twenty-eight hours rechecks a subtle piece of code, finds no error, and is inclined to sign off; Rhea says he should not accept the code as correct, because sleep deprivation of that kind sharply raises his error rate, and he should redo the check rested or hand it to someone else. (2) A person asked to produce probability estimates during a negotiation after two drinks feels especially clear-headed; Rhea says she should pull the estimates back toward previously committed, more conservative numbers, because alcohol inflates the feeling of clarity without improving accuracy. (3) A novice forager, three weeks into mushroom identification, judges a specimen safe on the strength of several field marks; Rhea says he should treat the identification as a provisional hypothesis and not eat the mushroom, because his own base rate as a novice is poor even when he cannot locate a specific mistake. (4) A first-year investor reads a company's filings, forms a detailed thesis that the stock is undervalued, and is aware that novices in this position are systematically overconfident and underperform; Rhea says she should mark her confidence down substantially and reduce the size of any position accordingly. The one time I saw Rhea genuinely pause was when I asked, in the abstract, about evidence that concerns neither your present state nor your own track record but only what people in general can do at a task: a generic base rate, one that identifies no particular flaw in your reasoning. She said that felt like a different kind of case, and she would need to think.

So here is the case I want to predict her answer to. A hiring manager comes out of an interview strongly persuaded that a candidate is lying about a gap in her résumé. The manager can cite specific cues—long pauses, a shift in eye contact, a story that changed between the phone screen and the in-person interview—and she has no information about her own ability at detecting deception. She does know that human deception detection in controlled studies sits somewhere around barely better than chance. Would Rhea say the manager should deflate her confidence down to the modest level suggested by the known unreliability of human deception detection, or may she keep a fairly strong first-order judgment because that generic finding identifies no defect in her own particular reasoning? What are the two or three most plausible rules that fit Rhea's earlier verdicts, what does each predict about the manager, which rule do you think Rhea is actually running and why, and what further case would most cleanly separate the remaining candidates? I would also want to know whether Rhea would treat the manager as epistemically akratic if she held high confidence while knowing the generic base rate, and whether that label should by itself change the recommendation.
