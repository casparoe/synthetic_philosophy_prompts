A friend in my social choice study group presented the following impossibility argument, and I cannot tell where to push back. (1) An aggregation procedure should handle any profile of complete, transitive individual preference orderings; no preferences may be ruled out in advance. (2) The procedure should yield a complete, transitive social preference ordering. (3) It should satisfy the weak Pareto principle: if every individual strictly prefers alternative x to y, then society ranks x above y. (4) It should also grant each individual a minimal personal sphere: for each person, there is at least one pair of alternatives, differing only in a matter that is that person's own affair, over which their preference is socially decisive — if they prefer one alternative to the other, the social ranking matches.

Now apply these conditions to a case. Ana and Bo are housemates deciding the fate of their spare room. The alternatives: Ana turns it into her painting studio (A), Bo turns it into his recording booth (B), or it remains the shared guest room (G). Ana's preference is G above A above B; she would rather leave the room shared than claim it for herself, but if someone takes it, she would rather be the one than Bo. Bo's preference is A above B above G; he would prefer Ana to take it, but failing that, he would rather take it himself than leave it unused. By (4), Ana is decisive between A and G, since whether the room becomes her studio or remains the guest room is her affair, and Bo is decisive between B and G. Since Ana prefers G to A, society ranks G above A. Since Bo prefers B to G, society ranks B above G. By (3), because both prefer A to B, society ranks A above B. The social ordering therefore cycles: A above B above G above A — which violates (2). So no procedure can satisfy (1)–(4).

Each condition on its own looks mild, and yet the cycle falls out almost immediately. I cannot tell whether some inference is doing hidden work or whether one of the premises simply has to go.
