I've been reading Hume's economic essays for a class — the ones about money, interest, and the balance of trade — and I keep turning over how much of his position rests on the thought that money is not itself wealth, just the medium that keeps commerce moving, and that the amount of money in a country will drift toward whatever level matches its commerce and industry. The move everyone remembers is that a trade surplus can't pile up money forever: the inflow raises domestic prices, exports get less competitive, and the surplus reverses itself; likewise, a country that loses money just ends up with lower prices and then recovers. But he also had a subtler point that a slow, continuos increase in the money supply can temporarily prod people into more activity before prices adjust.

What I can't stop wondering about is how he'd react to bitcoin. On the one hand, it looks like a clean test of his account: no government backing, a supply capped at 21 million, and almost nobody using it to actually buy stuff — money whose only value comes from people coordinating on it. On the other hand, that's also sort of true of money in his theory; he didn't think gold had much of a point apart from the fact that everyone accepted it. Would he call bitcoin a speculative token rather than money? Would he have liked the fixed supply, or would he have thought it was the exact wrong feature, since deflation would strangle the very activity he thought money existed to facilitate? I get that we can't know for sure; I'm asking for a principled extrapolation.
