i want a villanelle about generic statements — the 'ducks lay eggs' kind, where a generalization stays true even though plenty of ducks dont satisfy it. The two refrains should be a generic claim and one stubborn counterexample to it, something like 'ducks lay eggs' and 'the drake does not.' Let the body lines pile on more exceptions — ducklings, spent hens, the entire male half of the species — while the generic refrain keeps coming back unbothered, and in the last two lines they should end up side by side with neither one winning. The form should do the actual work: a line that repeats against a current of exceptions and finally meets them in the closing couplet is doing what a generic does, holding the generalization and its exceptions together without collapsing into statistics. Dont resolve it into a synthesis or a shrug — the point is that the two coexist. It should be right enough that a philosopher of language recognizes the phenomenon and doesnt wince: 'ducks lay eggs' is fine even though only some ducks (the laying hens) do it, while 'ducks are female' is false even though every egg-layer is one of those females. If the poem has to take a stand on why generics work like this — a striking-property account, a normic-default story, whatever — either let the poem show that stand or add a short afternote flagging where you leaned on a contested reading.
