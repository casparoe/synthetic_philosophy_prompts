Im teaching Shirley Jackson's The Lottery next week and I want to frame the whole discussion around the scapegoat mechanism — roughly the Girardian version: a community loads its diffuse guilt or its internal tensions onto a substitutable victim, then kills or expells them, and that somehow restores the group's equilibrium, as long as nobody involved experiences it as murder. I know the stock reading, small town ritual sacrifice shows how ordinary people do violence, but the story keeps resisting it for me. Tessie isnt an outsider and isnt marked out as a wrongdoer, the village seems basically fine — no famine, no war, no visible crisis the ritual is managing — and yet the machinery still runs. So what Im actually asking is: does The Lottery fit the scapegoat and ritual substitution model, or does it complicate it in ways that matter? Where does the story show the transfer of guilt actually doing work, and where does it show a ritual that has outlived whatever guilt or rationale it was built to handle? I especially want you to take the randomness of the victim seriously: the lottery picks her, nothing marks her out. What does that do to the claim that the victim is a substitute, and to the idea that killing her restores anything at all? Give me a reading that treats the story and the theory as equally serious, and be honest about the places where Jackson refuses to let the clean philosophical account stand. And no bibliography or reading list — just the reading.
