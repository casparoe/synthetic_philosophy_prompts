In my philosophy class, we have been studying David Hume's An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals. Two of his claims about justice seem to pull in opposite directions, and I hope you can help me understand whether they can be reconciled. I will paraphrase each argument rather than quote it exactly.

First, when Hume explains why we approve of justice, he argues that justice is an artificial virtue. He claims that what makes justice valuable is not some immediate natural sentiment, but the fact that a general practice of honest dealing is necessary for society to survive and flourish. Without it, he argues, human cooperation on any large scale would break down, so the social usefulness of justice is ultimately the basis of our approval of it.

Second, near the end of the book, Hume introduces an imaginary person, a shrewd and prudent cheat. This person notices that, although the general practice of justice benefits everyone, he might be able to break a promise or take an unfair advantage in a particular case without being found out. Since his single violation would probably do no noticeable damage to society, the cheat reasons that, on Hume's own principles, there seems to be no decisive reason not to violate the rules when he can do so safely. Hume responds that the cheat, even if he avoids punishment and preserves his reputation, will lose something important: he gives up the calm satisfaction of being able to reflect on his own character without uneasiness.

My question is whether these two lines of thought can be held together without special pleading. If the moral value of justice comes from its usefulness to society as a whole, how can an individual be blamed for a private violation whose public consequences are negligible? The cheat is not attacking the general practice of justice; he is asking why he should conform to it in a particular case. Is Hume's appeal to inner peace a satisfying answer to that challenge, or does it secretly introduce a different kind of justification for justice than the one he originally offered? Which ways of reconciling the two arguments are most honest, and which amount to special pleading?
