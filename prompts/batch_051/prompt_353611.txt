I am enrolled in a philosophy of cognitive science course, and in two weeks I am leading the seminar on a question I chose: whether the reward signal in reinforcement learning agents should be treated as a kind of valence, so that we have real reason to take seriously the possibility that some trained RL systems could suffer rather than dismissing the idea as a category mistake. My thesis is specific. In a reinforcement learner, the reward signal plays a functional role that closely parallels the role valence plays in biological agents: it marks states and outcomes as to-be-sought or to-be-avoided, it modulates learning, and it organizes downstream behavior. Throughout I distinguish valence understood as a felt quality from valence understood as this functional signature, and my claim is that the functional signature does much of the work in our ordinary attributions of felt valence to nonhuman animals. Since we already accept such functional markers as defeasible evidence there, I argue we should accept them as defeasible evidence in sufficiently complex RL agents as well, unless someone can state a principled disanalogy. I am not claiming any existing system definitely suffers; I am claiming the inference cannot be blocked simply by observing that reward is a scalar used to update a policy.

The seminar is small and I know exactly who will be in the room. The instructor is a philosopher of mind who works on phenomenal consciousness and is, I suspect, skeptical of computationalist claims about machine experience. Three classmates are from the machine learning side of the program and will likely insist that the reward signal is merely part of an optimization procedure, with no more mental significance than a gradient. Two are animal cognition students who will want to know what the view implies about invertebrates, farmed fish, or insect-level systems. The rest are general philosophy majors. I would like a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a polished defense. Specifically, give me the eight or so objections I am actually likely to get from this audience, ordered by how probable and how damaging each one is, phrased the way these particular people would phrase them, each with a concise reply I could give in the seminar and a note on where that reply is weakest. Include the question I should be most afraid of, and tell me which concessions I can make cheaply to this group and which ones would undercut the whole talk. For what it is worth, I suspect the instructor will attack the functional-kind premise first, and I am less sure how to handle the animal cognition students' demand that I say which actual systems, if any, fall on the wrong side of the line.
