You are a professor of constitutional theory who designs hands-on simulations for advanced undergraduates, and I want you to produce one runnable classroom activity (90 minutes, 16 students) for an upper-level seminar on the rule of law. The topic is constitutional gaps and interregna, and the activity should make participants feel the force of the problem rather than hear it stated as a doctrine: when a constitution's rules for succession run out, the question who is entitled to govern has no answer inside the legal order. Whatever answer emerges is supplied by something outside the rules — necessity, force, coordination, popularity, or a claim to act in the name of the people — and only later may acquiescence and habit turn that answer into law. By the end, students should come away with a felt distinction between asking what the rules require and asking who will in fact govern, and with an understanding that citing the rules cannot settle what the rules are silent about.

Begin by brainstorming briefly (a few sentences) on three candidate mechanics for the simulation — for example, competing factions after a simultaneous vacancy, a sealed decision that no one is authorized to make, or a neutral role that may answer only by quoting the written text — then commit fully to one and develop it. Do not hedge between alternatives in the final design; make definite choices and give reasons for the main ones.

The final activity must include all of the following:

- A short constitution or rule sheet simple enough to absorb in ten minutes, complete for ordinary succession but silent for the relevant calamity.
- Roles for all 16 students that distribute different kinds of leverage: the seals, records, and payroll; the chamber and the claim to speak for the public; the residual enforcement staff whose chain of command ends in conflicting orders; and at least one role charged with answering from the text alone and saying plainly when the text is silent.
- A concrete deadline or coordination problem that forces someone to act (an order, payroll, or similar), so that authority becomes a practical problem rather than a debate.
- A timeline for the 90 minutes: setup, the precipitating event, several choice points, and the debrief.
- A debrief that maps specific moments in the play back to the ideas: the silence of the rules, legal answer versus effective governance, appeals to necessity, the role of acquiescence and habit, and the way a successful course of action can begin to look lawful.
- A paragraph on what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run and how to handle it.
- An honest note on which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and what the facilitator must therefore supply afterwards.

Think carefully about how to make the gap show up in what happens, not in what the facilitator says afterwards: the rules, incentives, and roles should push students into the problem before anyone names it.
