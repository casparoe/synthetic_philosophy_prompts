Two friends of mine argued through most of a dinner about whether someone who currently eats beef should switch to chicken on animal-welfare grounds, and neither gave ground. I stayed out of it, but I'd like a disinterested verdict on the exchange as it actually happened.

A started: "A steer yields a few hundred kilos of meat, while a broiler chicken yields about two. Replace beef with chicken meal-for-meal and you're deciding the lives of roughly a hundred times as many farmed animals, each one living in a crowded, fast-growth shed. What more is there to say?"

B replied: "That only counts the animal on the plate. Chickens convert feed to meat much more efficiently than cattle, so a kilo of beef has several times more corn and soy behind it than a kilo of chicken. Harvesting those crops kills field animals. If wild deaths count at all, the crop footprint of a beef meal could outweigh the suffering of the chicken you'd have eaten instead."

A came back: "You're assuming every cow eats a full feedlot ration. A large share of a beef animal's life is spent on pasture eating grass, not feedlot corn, so your crop arithmetic overstates. And harvest-death estimates are wildly uncertain, whereas a broiler's six weeks in a shed is a known cost. You're letting a speculative wild-animal number outweigh a certain, very large number of farmed lives."

B answered: "Uncertainty isn't a reason to set crop deaths to zero — it's a reason to use expected values and wide priors. Feedlot finishing is standard, the acreage is real, and wild animals vastly outnumber farmed animals. Unless your best estimate is that harvest mortality is negligible, the expected wild-animal cost of the extra feed behind beef is large relative to the single chicken slaughtered for the alternative meal. And uncertainty about quality of life cuts both ways, so you can't lean on 'known' suffering while dismissing the wild side as 'speculative.'"

Adjudicate the four moves one by one: rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Give the answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion — the first four items rating the moves in order, and the fifth stating which argument or consideration would most change your overall verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
