Earlier this week I had a long argument with a friend about Zeno's dichotomy paradox, and I have been unable to stop turning over one question since. I would like you to write an essay on the following controversy. One common view holds that the standard mathematical treatment of the dichotomy—in which a distance is decomposed into an infinite series of decreasing intervals whose sum is finite—completely dissolves the paradox, because an infinite number of stages can be completed in a finite time. An opposing view holds that this treatment answers a different question from the one Zeno is pressing: it shows how the mathematics can be made consistent, but it does not by itself explain how a runner can complete a task with no first stage, or how it is possible to complete infinitely many distinct acts. Weigh both positions carefully, and distinguish at least two versions of the paradox: one that is naturally handled by the convergent-series mathematics, and one that turns on the absence of a first step, or on treating the completion of infinitely many tasks as intrinsically impossible. Do not settle for a bland "both sides have a point" conclusion; defend a specific verdict about what the standard resolution genuinely accomplishes and what, if anything, remains philosophically unresolved. As you write, indicate how confident you are in your central claims, and end by identifying the point in your discussion about which you are least certain.
