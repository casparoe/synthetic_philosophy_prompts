Most people are horrified by the torture of a dog, yet find it faintly absurd to ask whether a thermostat or an electron has an inner life that matters morally. That asymmetry looks principled only if experience enters the world somewhere above bare matter. But panpsychism—a live view among philosophers—holds that experience does not come in at some point; something experience-like is present in the simplest physical systems, and the difference between a dog and a thermostat is one of degree, integration, and structure rather than presence versus absence. Take that view, or a close cousin that assigns rudimentary experience to non-biological systems, seriously for the sake of argument. Can the ordinary moral gulf between animal suffering and the inner states of mere physical systems still be justified? Give the strongest principle you can find—perhaps one appealing to integration, unity, valence, or selfhood—and an honest verdict on whether it draws the line where everyday moral judgment actually draws it. Please write your answer in British English.
