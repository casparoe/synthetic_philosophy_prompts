Two people in my discussion group have been arguing for nearly a year about whether it is legitimate to take another person's word about a moral matter in something like the way we take a trusted mechanic's word about a failing brake line. One holds that when someone whose judgment you have good reason to trust tells you that an action you are considering is wrong, that fact alone can be a strong reason—even a sufficient reason—to conclude that it is wrong, and it is no defect if you cannot yet see what makes it wrong. The other holds that moral matters are different in kind: a moral conclusion accepted chiefly on someone else's say-so is second-hand in a way a factual conclusion accepted on expert testimony is not, and a person who acts on it without understanding for herself what makes the action wrong has missed something central to acting well. Each thinks the other keeps moving the goalposts, and each thinks the other's fairest-sounding formulation hides a concession.

They have agreed, in good faith, to produce a single document that both of them can sign: a record of what they have established in common, where precisely they still differ, which sub-questions would actually move one of them, and what each predicts about the considerations both accept as bearing on those sub-questions. I have been asked to help draft it, and I want the draft to be one neither side could call slanted and neither side could call empty. Please write that document.

Treat the following as part of the task. First, produce a numbered list of agreed claims, written precisely enough that neither side feels the wording already decides the matter against them. Second, produce the disagreement section—ideally as a table—stating the contested point as exactly as possible, with each side's position written in wording that side would accept as a fair statement of its own view. Third, identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side; for each, specify a concrete case or consideration that both agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. The cases they have used to pressure one another include a trusted friend with a long record of moral soundness, an inexperienced parent deciding how much to defer to older relatives on how to raise a child, and a person who after years of deferring can state her moral conclusions but cannot explain their grounds. Use these cases or improve on them. Fourth, say whether any part of their dispute is merely verbal—for example, whether one side is really talking about evidence and the other about the decision itself—and if so, which part and why.
