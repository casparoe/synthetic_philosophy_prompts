I play alot of abstract strategy games, and I keep running into a move in reasoning that I don't have a word for. When people argue about whether the second player can force a win, somebody will say: if the second player had a winning strategy, then the first player could just make a harmless throwaway move on turn one and then copy whatever the second player's strategy would tell them to do. I've seen this used for Hex (the connection game on a rhombus of hexagons) and tic-tac-toe, where people act like the extra stone can't possibly hurt the first player, so the second player can't have a winning strategy. But I've also seen people reach for the same style of argument in games where it seems to just break. In chess there's that endgame situation where you'd rather it was the other player's turn, because any move you make only makes your own position worse. There an extra move isn't free at all, and "copying the second player's strategy" doesn't even get off the ground, since the position you'd need is gone once you move. With hidden information or randomness it feels even sketchier.

So what I'm after is: does this assumption have a name, and is it one single principle or a couple of different ones that people blur together? More than a label, I want to know exactly what a game has to be like for the argument to go through — what makes an extra move automatically harmless in Hex but not in chess — and what the cleanest counterexamples are where the exact same words fail. I've tried poking around game theory stuff but it's mostly payoff matrices and minimax, not this.

Basically, is "the first player can always just copy the second player" a known, named rule with actual conditions, or is it just a loose heuristic that happens to work in a few games?
