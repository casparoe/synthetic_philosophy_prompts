I've been involved in life-extension debates for a few years, mostly in online forums and a local healthspan meetup, and I have no formal background in philosophy. I've noticed that what longevity advocates dismiss as "deathism" is not one position: some people treat death as positively good, some treat it as bad but prefer it to the world a life-extension treatment might create, and some seem to be reconciling themselves to a fact they can't change. I'd like an instrument that separates these positions by what people say about concrete cases, not by which label they'll accept.

Please write a "which kind of opponent of life extension are you?" sorting quiz for me to use with the forum and meetup crowd, answerable by people with no academic training. It should have ten to twelve questions, each built around a concrete scenario: a pill that stops aging but leaves you as likely to die in a car crash as anyone else; a therapy that doesn't extend lifespan but compresses late-life decline into a few weeks; a treatment that keeps you biologically thirty-five for two hundred years but gradually replaces most of your memories every few decades; a public health program that rolls a working treatment out to everyone; a choice between an excellent eighty-five years and a flat, mild three hundred; whether you would want your children to receive it; and similar cases of your own devising. Each question should offer two or three options, and none should simply ask whether death gives life meaning or whether aging is natural.

The scoring key should map patterns of answers onto at least the following five positions: (a) death is good or necessary because a worthwhile life requires limits and an ending; (b) death is bad, but extending lives is worse on practical or social grounds; (c) a much longer life would not be the same person's life, so a normal lifespan doesn't deprive the original person of the extra years; (d) death isn't the real issue at all—the issue is how the final years go; and (e) a sour-grapes or adaptive-preference position that rejects extension chiefly because it can't be had. Add any further positions the answer patterns genuinely force, and give every position a short name plus one sentence on the commitment it is most uncomfortable to defend. Build the key from the logical space of answers first and attach labels second; note any combinations of answers that correspond to no coherent position and say briefly whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored; and flag any two positions the quiz as written cannot reliably tell apart. Please write the whole thing in American English.
