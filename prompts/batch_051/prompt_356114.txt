Construct a thought experiment for a seminar on the divergence between ex ante preferences over rules and equilibrium behavior inside a rule. The case should be built around a commitment race: each party would rather lock in an aggressive course of action first, yet both would prefer, before learning who gets to move first, that the locking-in technology did not exist.

Set up the following ordinary two-party encounter, with two symmetric parties and a single interaction. Before the interaction, each party may pay a small positive cost to make an irrevocable, publicly observed commitment to a hard-line action. If neither commits, an established convention supplies each with a moderate payoff. If exactly one commits, the committing party, net of the cost, receives a payoff above the moderate one, while the party committed against receives a payoff below it. If both commit, the two commitments collide and each receives less than the party committed against receives in the one-sided case. Use this timing protocol: a fair coin selects which party moves first; the first mover may commit or decline; then, observing that choice, the second mover may commit or decline.

The case must jointly satisfy the following three specifications.

(1) Ex ante, before the timing coin is tossed, each party's expected payoff is strictly higher in a world where neither has the technology than in a world where both have it.

(2) Holding the interaction fixed, each party would strictly rather occupy the role of the sole committing party than the role of the party committed against.

(3) The unique subgame-perfect equilibrium has exactly one party committing, the other declining, and the declining party ends up below the moderate no-technology payoff.

Write out explicit numerical payoffs, the cost, and the backward-induction argument showing that all three specifications hold at once. Then identify the one structural feature of the payoff table that creates the wedge between ex ante preference and ex post race behavior. Explain what happens to that wedge if commitment is made costless. Finally, give the strongest objection a contractarian-institutionalist would raise — that two rational symmetric parties could simply negotiate past such a destructive race — and the best reply available to someone defending the case as a genuine instance of the phenomenon.
