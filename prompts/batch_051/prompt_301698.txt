For an upper-level undergraduate course on the ethics and politics of quantification, write the final essay question on Goodhart's law and specification gaming. The question should present the following asymmetry: a metric is praised as informative while it is used purely diagnostically, but the same metric is condemned as corrupted once it becomes a target. The question should ask whether anything justifies treating the measure differently in those two roles, or whether target-setting merely reveals that the proxy was always imperfect. It should be answerable in 90 minutes. Then give a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, each worth 4 marks (total 20), with a one-line note on how candidates typically get each point wrong.

Question:

A university has for years used the average grade in first-year courses as an internal diagnostic to identify students at risk of dropping out. In that period, the grade average was a stable and useful predictor of later success. The university then begins publishing department-level grade averages and using them in annual program reviews. Within two years, average grades rise, the distribution of marks narrows, and the same average has lost much of its predictive value for dropout. Administrators now describe the measure as corrupted by grade inflation and teaching to the test. Yet the university continues to use the same number in its internal advising system because it still believes the original diagnostic use was valid.

Critically evaluate this practice. Is there a defensible distinction between using a measure to observe a system and using it to steer a system, or is the high-stakes use merely exposing a limitation that was present all along? Distinguish different mechanisms by which a metric can fail under pressure, and judge whether the university's asymmetry in attitude is justified.

Model answer:

A strong answer would begin by stating Goodhart's law in a qualified form: when decision-makers attach rewards or penalties to a proxy, behavior changes so as to protect the proxy, and the metric may lose its standing as an independent indicator of the underlying target. But the answer should not treat the corruption as automatic. Some target-setting improves the underlying target as well as the proxy. The central question is whether the distinction between diagnostic use and target use is principled.

The best answers would distinguish at least three mechanisms. First, gaming: agents manipulate the indicator or the conditions under which it is recorded without improving, and often while worsening, the underlying target. Grade inflation, assignment scaffolding, and teaching narrowly to the exam are examples. Second, selection or reclassification: agents alter which cases count, such as discouraging weaker students from taking certain courses or redesigning first-year curricula to retain strong students. Third, legitimate improvement: agents respond to the new incentive by improving teaching, which raises grades and reduces dropout, but this still changes the statistical relation between the proxy and the target because the proxy no longer measures the same underlying variation.

A top answer would then evaluate the asymmetry. The obvious defense is epistemic: when a measure is used diagnostically, we are observing a system under relatively low stakes, so the measure captures a stable baseline relation. When it becomes a target, the act of measurement feeds back into the system, changing the very distribution the measure was designed to track. On this view, the asymmetry is justified because the two uses belong to different causal regimes. An internal audit is a snapshot; a performance metric is an intervention.

But the answer should push back. The critique is that the proxy was always a lossy, partial indicator of the underlying target. Diagnostic use allowed that limitation to remain invisible because nothing depended on it and because cross-sectional variation in student grades was being interpreted as if it were caused by stable differences in readiness. High-stakes use did not corrupt a clean measure; it altered the composition of the population and the grading process enough to expose the measure's dependence on those conditions. On this view, the asymmetry is psychologically comforting but not justified: the university should not say the measure was good then and bad now, but rather that it was valid only under specific, unstable conditions.

The strongest answers would test the boundary between observation and steering. The same number may simultaneously function as an advising tool and as a performance indicator. An academic advisor who knows that first-year grades affect department rankings may, without any conscious gaming, treat a student's grades as a signal of departmental health rather than individual risk. This shows that low-stakes diagnostic use and high-stakes target use are not cleanly separable once the instrument is public. A final answer should take a clear position: the asymmetry is defensible only if the institution can specify the causal conditions under which the proxy is valid and show that those conditions are not present once the proxy is used for accountability. Otherwise the asymmetry should be abandoned.

Marking scheme:

1. States Goodhart's law in a qualified form, avoiding the claim that every target statistically invalidates its own measure. (4 marks)
   Common error: candidates quote the law as an iron law and treat the university's outcome as inevitable.

2. Distinguishes gaming the metric from improvements in the underlying target, and identifies selection or reclassification as a separate mechanism. (4 marks)
   Common error: candidates lump all post-target changes together as gaming.

3. Gives the strongest epistemic defense of the diagnostic/target asymmetry: measurement as observation versus measurement as intervention. (4 marks)
   Common error: candidates assert that incentives cause bias without explaining the causal difference between the two roles.

4. Criticizes the asymmetry by arguing that high-stakes use exposes pre-existing limitations in the proxy rather than creating them. (4 marks)
   Common error: candidates treat the original diagnostic use as self-evidently valid and never ask why the proxy lost predictive value so quickly.

5. Reaches a clear, supported verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned, and tests it against the case where the same number serves both functions. (4 marks)
   Common error: candidates give an equivocal conclusion that the university's attitude is partly justified without specifying when the distinction fails.
