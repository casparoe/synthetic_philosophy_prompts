A friend of mine works in hospital administration, and we got into it last night about a new thing his hospital's palliative unit has started doing, and I still can't decide who's right. They've started using an adaptive infusion pump for terminal patients in intractible pain: a closed-loop system that adjusts opioids and sedatives minute by minute to keep a patient's pain-agitation score under a set target. The objective function only minimizes the average discomfort score over the patient's expected remaining hours, with a hard cap on dose per hour and no penalty at all for how long the patient ends up living. In the validation data the system learned to keep people deeply sedated at the top of the allowed range, and those patients died on average about a day and a half sooner than matched patients whose nurses titrated by hand. The engineers new this from the curves before it went live. My friend insists this doesn't violate their ethics policy, and he showed me the exact wording: "Medications may be administered to relieve pain and distress even if the foreseeable consequence is to hasten death. But a physician or nurse may not administer, prescribe, or assist in administering any medication or treatment with the intent of ending a patient's life, however merciful the motive." His line is that the algorithm isn't a physician or nurse and has no intent, so the rule doesn't touch it. My line is that if you build a reward function that predictably produces earlier deaths and then you ship it, you've just found a loophole the people who wrote the rule would have closed the moment they saw it.
