I've been going back and forth about whether the numbers count in rescue cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures my verdicts.

Case 1: a burning building has one stranger on the left and five strangers on the right, and you can only reach one side in time. I'm certain you should go to the five. Not just allowed—going for the one would be wrong.

Case 2: same building, but the one on the left is your closest friend. I think it's fine to save the friend and leave the five; not required, but not wrong either.

Case 3: the one on the left is a stranger, but last week you promised their parent you'd look after them in an emergency. I think you're required to save the promised one, not just allowed.

Case 4: one healthy stranger in a clinic, five patients who'll die without organs, no one else around. Obviously you can't cut up the one to save the five, even though the arithmetic matches Case 1.

Case 5: back to one vs. five strangers, but now you're setting policy ahead of time for rescue cases like this. I think the fair policy gives the one a 1/6 chance of rescue and the five a 5/6 chance—one equal chance per person. A 50/50 coin flip between the one and the five seems wrong to me, but I can't say why, given that in Cases 2 and 3 I already let non-numerical ties beat the numbers.

My rough principle is that you should weight each person's claim equally and then satisfy the greatest weighted sum, with special ties (friendship, promises) adding extra weight per person, plus a separate ban on harming or using people that handles Case 4. But this feels ad hoc, and I'm not sure it really handles Case 5. What's the best principle you can state, what does it predict about each case, and which of my verdicts, if any, should I give up? Also give me one new case that would cleanly test the principle.
