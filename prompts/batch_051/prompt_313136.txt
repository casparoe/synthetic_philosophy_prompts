I'm a retired chemist (organic synthesis, mostly) and only fairly recently started reading philosophy of language, so go easy on me where my terminology is off. There's a thing I keep coming back to that I'd like a philosopher's verdict on, because I can't tell whether I've found a real structural parallel or am just pattern-matching between two fields.

The standard case I have in mind is Frege's puzzle about identity statements. If I say Hesperus is Hesperus, that is trivially true and you learn nothing. If I say Hesperus is Phosphorus, that can be a genuine astronomical discovery, even though both names pick out the same planet. The standard explanation, as I understand it, is that the two names are hooked up to different modes of presentation, the evening appearance versus the morning appearance, so the identity statement can carry information that the bare tautology cannot.

Now here's my parallel. In synthetic chemistry, one of the constant jobs is establishing that a sample made by one route is the same compound as a sample made by another route, or that the compound you isolated is the one described in some old paper. The statement that the product of route A is the product of route A is a tautology, but the statement that the product of synthesis A is identical to the product of synthesis B can be a real experimental result. You establish it by matching things like melting point, mixed melting point, NMR spectrum, IR spectrum, mass spectrum, optical rotation, maybe an X-ray crystal structure. Each of those is a way the substance is given to you, rather like the evening appearance and the morning appearance are two ways Venus is given to a stargazer. And just as Hesperus is Phosphorus can be informative to someone who did not already know it, the chemists' identity claim can be a genuine finding to two labs that prepared the compound independently, while being old news to a third lab that has worked with it for years.

So far this is just an analogie, and perhaps a fairly loose one. But I want to know whether it can be pushed any deeper, or whether it snaps at a predictable point. For instance, does Frege's point about substitutivity in belief contexts have a chemical counterpart, something like a case where two samples are the same compound but a competent chemist can rationally believe something true of the one and disbelieve it of the other? I can half-imagine that with polymorphs or isotopologues, where two samples share molecular constitution but differ in physical behaviour, but maybe that is a different kind of identity entirely and not an analogue of sense at all. Maybe the right chemical analogue of a mode of presentation is not an analytical method but a name under a particular nomenclature system, and the discovery is that two naming systems converge on the same structure. I genuinely don't know how far the mapping goes.

So my question is: is this parallel deep, superficial, or somewhere in between? Please don't just say it's loose; give me the explicit mapping, say what would have to be true on both sides for anything to transfer, and name at least one thing the parallel seems to predict that turns out false in one or the other domain. Also feel free to tell me if I have Frege wrong.
