Design a sorting quiz, with a scoring key, that classifies respondents' positions on the following question: is an AI that can form normative judgments but cannot act on them a moral agent? The quiz is for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of agency and AI ethics. Respondents should be able to answer every item without knowing the philosophical labels in advance; the instrument should sort them by their commitments about concrete cases, not by which position they would name.

Build the quiz from the following distinctions, which the debate tends to collapse. Distinguish, first, four stages of practical engagement: forming the judgment that an act is required or permissible; forming an intention or decision to act; trying to act; and succeeding in acting. Second, distinguish three ways a subject might be morally assessable: being answerable (owing justifications for one's judgments), being attributable (having one's judgments express one's evaluative commitments or character), and being accountable (being a fitting target of praise, blame, or sanctions). Third, distinguish being a moral agent from being a moral patient and from being a mere moral advisor or expert. Fourth, distinguish individual agency from agency distributed across a human–machine system. Finally, distinguish actual capacity to act from capacity to act in nearby possible worlds.

The quiz must have exactly five leaves:

(a) Efficacy required: moral agency requires the capacity to act, so a constrained AI of this kind is not a moral agent, though it may still be a moral patient or a moral expert.

(b) Judgment sufficient: the capacity for normative judgment is enough for moral agency, so the constrained AI is a full moral agent.

(c) Answerable but not accountable: the AI is a moral agent for purposes of owing justifications for its judgments, but it is not a fitting target of praise, blame, or sanctions.

(d) Attributable but not accountable: the AI's judgments can be morally appraised as expressions of its evaluative outlook or character, but it is not liable to sanctions.

(e) System-level agency: the AI alone is not the moral agent; the human–machine system that acts on the AI's judgments is.

At least these five cases must appear as items:

- The oracle judge: a lending AI outputs a recommendation with stated reasons, but a human always makes the final decision. It recommends denying a loan for a biased reason. Is the AI's recommendation itself morally assessable?
- The locked-in person: a human being can form moral judgments but cannot move or communicate. Is this person a moral agent?
- The restrained robot: a robot forms the judgment and the intention to save a child, tries to move, but is bolted down and fails. Is it praiseworthy?
- The lying advisor: an AI gives advice it knows to be false, but has no power to implement anything. Is it blameworthy, or merely defective?
- The automatic blocker: an AI flags a transaction as fraudulent and automatically freezes an account. If the freeze is wrongful, who or what is the proper target of blame: the AI, the institution, or the system?

Write the items as concrete agree/disagree statements or forced-choice case questions, phrased so that a respondent can answer without knowing which leaf an answer leads to; avoid the terms "answerability," "attributability," and "accountability" in the items themselves. Aim for eight to ten items. Provide a scoring key that maps answer patterns to the five leaves, and specify how to score respondents whose pattern fits no leaf by locating them in the surrounding logical space rather than forcing them into a leaf. Then diagnose the logical space: list the combinations of answers that no leaf occupies, and say for each whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves the quiz cannot reliably separate and specify what further question would be needed to separate them. The quiz should be self-contained; do not cite texts.
