I have been thinking, in an amateur way, about a single question: how one should decide which level of government—a town council, a regional government, a national government, or a body such as the European Union—should be allowed to decide what. I have kept a notebook of judgements on particular cases, and I would like to know whether there is one coherent principle behind them, or perhaps a small set of candidate principles, and whether I should revise any of the judgements. Although the cases below are written generically, I want the principle to be able to travel across systems as different as the American states, the German Länder, the Swiss cantons and the European Union, rather than rest on one constitution.

The first six cases are settled views of mine; the seventh is the one I return to and cannot settle.

1. A region in a federal country wants its state schools to teach mainly in the region's minority language, and to let local history occupy a larger place in the curriculum. The national government forbids this in the name of uniform standards. I think the region should be allowed to go ahead, even though pupils may find it harder to move elsewhere for work later.

2. A poorer region relaxes its factory emission limits to attract manufacturing jobs. A large part of the resulting air pollution drifts into three neighbouring regions, which have no say in the decision. I think the national government should be able to stop the relaxation.

3. A town of old low-rise stone houses refuses permission for any new building above four storeys. The rule keeps house prices in the town very high; teachers, nurses and young families from surrounding areas can no longer afford to move in, and local employers struggle to fill jobs. I think, nevertheless, that the town should be left to keep the rule.

4. One region wants to set the age of criminal responsibility at eight, far below the national norm, arguing that local people know their own circumstances best. I think the central government should be able to block this.

5. A member state objects to a common EU standard for food labels, mainly because its own shoppers are used to a familiar national label. The common standard would make cross-border sale considerably easier. I think the EU standard should apply.

6. Some member states argue that the EU should require every country to finance its hospitals through the same model, since duplication is wasteful. I think the EU should leave each country free to organise its own healthcare.

7. A small, prosperous state within a larger union—think of a Swiss canton or a small EU member state—cuts its corporate tax rate sharply to attract company headquarters from its neighbours. The neighbouring states then feel forced to cut their own rates to compete. Part of me says this small state is only using the same local freedom I protected in cases 1 and 3; another part says it is making its neighbours worse off through no fault of their own. I go back and forth on whether there should be a common floor on such rates.

Some of these verdicts seem hard to reconcile on paper. In cases 1 and 3 I allow a local majority to impose real costs on people outside the area—narrower opportunities in one case, expensive housing in the other—whereas in cases 2, 5 and 6 I accept that a higher level may override a lower one, and in case 5 I accept even a supranational body overriding a member state. I would like to know whether there is a principle that actually sorts these cases, or whether some of my verdicts have to go.

The word people usually offer me here is 'subsidiarity', but I have never seen a version precise enough to settle case 7. Please do not simply repeat the word or give me a slogan; I want the tests, not the label.

Please set out the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best fits what I have written, and say exactly which clause is doing the work in each numbered case. If no single principle can deliver all seven without becoming a patchwork of ad hoc exceptions, say so plainly, and tell me which of my verdicts each candidate principle would force me to revise. Then test the principle or principles on a further case I have deliberately left out: a regional government wants to exempt itself from a national minimum-wage law for workers under twenty-one in high-unemployment rural areas, again on the ground that local conditions are special. What would each candidate principle say, and what in my other verdicts would support its answer?
