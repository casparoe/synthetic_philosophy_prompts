I've been taking notes for a term on a professor's off-the-cuff rulings about disagreement cases. She insists there is a coherent principle behind her views but has refused to state it outright. I want you to use my notes to predict her next verdict and to reconstruct, as precisely as you can, the principle she seems to be running.

Her rulings so far:

1. The dinner bill. Two friends of equal arithmetic ability each independently compute what they owe on a shared restaurant bill. One arrives at $57.30 per person; the other at $61.00. She says both should move to roughly the midpoint and should hold only modest confidence in the resulting number.

2. The shared dataset. Two social scientists with substantively identical training are given the same public dataset and the same codebook and are asked for the probability that a policy improved outcomes. One reports 0.75; the other reports 0.25. She says both should move a great deal, perhaps nearly to 0.50, and that neither should simply retain their original confidence. She adds that the only reason not to settle at exactly 0.50 would be some independent, articulable reason to think one researcher chose a better model.

3. The credential gap. A parent who has read two review articles disagrees with a specialist about a treatment's risk. She says the parent should defer almost entirely, because the two parties are not similarly competent on the question.

4. The unequal evidence. You and a colleague disagree about what was actually decided at a meeting. You attended; the colleague did not and is relying on a written summary. She says you need not conciliate at all, because the colleague lacks evidence you possess, so the two of you are not genuine epistemic peers.

5. The philosophical stalemate. Two able graduate students have spent a semester exchanging complete arguments on a disputed question in moral philosophy. Each can state the other's argument accurately; neither can find a specific error in it; and each nonetheless continues to find their own conclusion more plausible. She says each may retain most of their confidence, and the disagreement should move them only slightly.

Now the new case. You and a mathematical peer of equal training have each worked independently on whether a certain combinatorial proposition is true. You arrive at opposite conclusions, and each of you has a proof. You exchange proofs. Each understands the other's proof, has checked it line by line, and can find no error in it; each still believes their own conclusion is correct and that the other is somehow making an unnoticed mistake. Neither has any independent evidence that the other is generally more error-prone.

What do you predict the professor will say about the proof case: should the parties conciliate substantially, and if so, how far? More importantly: which rules fit her five prior rulings, which one best explains the whole record, and how does that rule apply to the proof case? If her prior verdicts genuinely underdetermine the prediction, tell me what principle she would be adopting with each possible answer. Finally, propose the single best further case to run by her that would decide between the remaining candidate rules.
