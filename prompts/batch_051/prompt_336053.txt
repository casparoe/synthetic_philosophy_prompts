In Daniel Dennett’s hierarchy of kinds of minds, adaptive behavior is produced by a nested series of generate-and-test processes: Darwinian creatures get their competence from natural selection over genotypes; Skinnerian creatures add selection over emitted behaviors by reinforcement; Popperian creatures preselect candidate behaviors in an inner environment before acting; Gregorian creatures import and manipulate selected artifacts from the wider culture as tools for thinking. Write a decision-tree diagnostic for classifying an observed agent—biological, artificial, or hypothetical—into one of these four classes, using only behavioral or design evidence, not the labels themselves. The diagnostic questions must be concrete enough that someone could answer them without already knowing Dennett’s terms. Include the full logical space of combinations (e.g., Popperian without Skinnerian, Gregorian without Popperian, Skinnerian without Darwinian), indicate which cells are occupied, and for each unoccupied cell give a brief diagnosis: incoherent, impossible given the mechanics, merely unexplored, or cryptically equivalent to an occupied cell. End with leaf labels and one sentence on each leaf’s most awkward commitment. The intended audience is an advanced undergraduate philosophy of cognitive science seminar, and the tree should have at most ten questions and eight leaves.
