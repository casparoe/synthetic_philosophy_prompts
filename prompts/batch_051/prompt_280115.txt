I am trying to work out what I actually think about informal social punishment — cancellation, deplatforming, public shaming, and the loss of work or standing that follows — as distinct from punishment by the state. I can report my reactions to a series of cases, but I do not yet have the principle behind them.

Here are the cases with my verdicts.

(1) A senior journalist is sacked after an internal investigation upholds complaints that he spent years bullying junior staff. This seems to me not only permissible but right: the dismissal protects the people still in the newsroom.

(2) A nurse posted a poorly judged but not hateful political message on a private account eight years ago. An anonymous account unearths it, the hospital is inundated with demands for her dismissal, and she is eventually let go. This seems wrong — the response is wildly out of proportion to the original act.

(3) A streaming platform removes a decade-old comedy special after clips circulate and employees complain. The material was deliberately offensive when it was made, and watching it is optional. I am torn: I doubt the removal does much good, but I resist calling it unjust.

(4) A university professor publishes a carefully argued piece defending a controversial but not hateful position; students petition to have him removed from a curriculum committee. This seems wrong: ordinary academic disagreement is being treated as misconduct.

(5) A public figure who has made similar demeaning remarks over many years, ignoring repeated criticism, is dropped by sponsors. I find this acceptable. But an old post written by someone at nineteen resurfaces and costs a person their job; I think that is wrong, even though the content of the old post is worse than anything the public figure said in one go.

(6) A restaurant owner is secretly recorded making racist remarks at a private dinner. His staff walk out, suppliers stop delivering, and the business collapses. I do not think anyone acted beyond their rights, but the outcome — a person losing a livelihood over a private remark — unsettles me in a way that the journalist case does not.

The nearest I can get to a principle is this: informal punishment is justified when it is a proportionate response to continuing, public misconduct and thereby protects particular people; it is unjust when it is retroactive, when it fixes on one old lapse, or when it is aimed at someone with little power. But this does not fit my reactions to the comedian or the restaurant owner, and it may simply beg the question by building in the distinctions I am unsure about.

Is there a single defensible principle that captures most of these verdicts? State it precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why, and tell me what it implies about a further case: a psychiatric nurse with an unblemished record and no patient complaints is revealed to have expressed, under a pseudonym in a members-only forum, private political views that most colleagues would find abhorrent. Should the nurse be dismissed, and what does the principle that survives your analysis say?
