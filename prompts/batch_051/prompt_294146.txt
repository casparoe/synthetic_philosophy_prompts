I'm teaching a graduate seminar on intertemporal choice, and a student has now spent two weeks of office hours on the same thought experiment, so I'd like to have it worked through carefully rather than batted around informally.

Imagine a free, permanent, entirely voluntary medical intervention offered to every adult. It sets a person's pure time preference to exactly zero: from the moment of treatment, they cease to weight their own future well-being less merely because it lies further in the future. The intervention changes nothing else. It does not alter factual beliefs about the future, risk attitudes, life expectancy, preferences over goods, strength of will, or concern for other people; it targets only the discount applied to their own future well-being for the bare passage of time. Thus a treated person would, except for uncertainty about survival and about the world turning out as expected, be exactly indifferent between an hour of well-being tomorrow and an hour of the same well-being forty years from now. Suppose the intervention is cheap, safe, and socially unremarkable, but as it happens only about twenty percent of adults choose it in the first ten years.

I want a reasoned prediction of how the next thirty years play out, concentrating on mechanisms rather than a narrative. Address at least: saving, borrowing, and the pricing of credit by the treated and the untreated; addiction, smoking, and procrastination; education, career choice, and physical risk-taking; long-term health and environmental behavior; and how markets, employers, and political institutions respond to a minority with no pure discounting of their own future. One specific question to address head-on: it is often useful to distinguish pure time preference from present bias, the special motivational pull of the immediate present. Does zeroing pure time preference also eliminate present bias, or can present bias survive as a distinct phenomenon, and with what behavioral consequences? For each link in your argument, distinguish what is robust from what is a guess, and say what would have to be different for the prediction to fail.
