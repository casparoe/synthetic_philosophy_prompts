I'm drafting the take-home final for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of love and emotion. The course has worked through the appraisal/bestowal debate via Frankfurt on love as a bestowal of value, Velleman on love as a response to the beloved's rational personhood rather than to contingent excellences, and Kolodny on love as warranted by the history of a relationship rather than by present qualities or an act of will. Every year strong students compress this into a dichotomy between love answering to the beloved's properties and love being groundless, and I want a question that makes that compression fail.

Please write me three things: the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly 800–1,000 words, and a marking scheme. The exam question should be for a 72-hour take-home, capped at 1,500 words, worth 30 marks, and it should center on an invented scenario of your choosing in which the three positions come apart — for instance, a long marriage where one partner's admired qualities have been lost to illness or radically transformed while the person and the shared history remain recognizably intact. The question should require students to state how Frankfurt's bestowal view, Velleman's personhood-appraisal view, and Kolodny's relationship view would each assess the case, explain why their verdicts diverge, and then defend the verdict they regard as least revisionary of ordinary judgments about fidelity and grief, while naming the main cost of that verdict. The scenario must be specific and not identical to the stock dementia example from lecture, and the question should be unanswerable by reciting a summary of the three views.

The model answer should serve as a worked example: show the moves a top student would make, paraphrase each philosopher's position rather than quoting or citing, and make the argumentative crux visible instead of merely summarizing the readings. The marking scheme should specify six must-hit points worth 5 marks each, and for each point a one-line note on the particular misunderstanding, conflation, or omission that typically costs students those marks. I care more about whether students can taxonomize the views, extract their divergent practical verdicts from a single case, and argue for one against the others than about whether they reproduce lecture formulations.
