I read the essay once, but I was going pretty fast, so afterwards I went to the summary in our course reader to check whether I'd got the structure right. The reader summarises Mill's argument roughly like this: Mill is applying his utilitarianism to the relations between the sexes, and his central move is to reject appeals to nature. Since we have only ever observed women under institutions that have trained, restricted, and rewarded them from birth, we have no real data about what women are 'naturally' like, and what gets called women's nature is mostly custom in disguise. The reader then says Mill concludes from this that there are no natural psychological differences between men and women, and that any argument from nature to how society should be arranged commits the is–ought fallacy. On this telling, the entire case for women's subordination rests on the claim that it is natural, so Mill holds that the subordination is unjustified and should be replaced by full equality between the sexes.

Something about the final stretch — where it runs from 'we can't know women's nature' to 'there are no natural differences' to 'the is–ought fallacy' — feels too quick to me, but I can't tell whether the problem is with Mill or with the summariser, and I don't want to push back on a reading just because the book is a bit polemical.
