I'm a novelist, and I'm drafting a book set in a small town in a half-emptied county; the town keeps trying to keep people from drifting apart after the cannery closed, and I've invented a bylaw I cant quite tell whether it would hold together or quietly rot. The bylaw: every adult has to log eight "belonging hours" a month in a town app—going to the assembly, helping a neighbor with a ride or a repair, coaching a kids' team, clearing snow off the common paths, sitting with a housebound person. Hours are self-reported and confirmed by the person you helped, or by a rotating council witness if you did the thing alone. You can buy yourself out at forty dollars per unlogged hour into a common pot, deliberately steep. At year's end the pot gets split among the households that logged the most hours per resident, and anyone who falls under the minimum two months running loses their vote in the town assembly and their plot in the community garden until they catch up. Newcomers need a sponsoring household, and if a newcomer falls short, the sponsor loses their own year-end share.

I'm less interested in whether this is a good idea in the abstract than in how it would actually behave against its own goal of keeping people attached to one another. Treat it as a mechanism: what does it push busy parents, retirees, teenagers, newcomers, the well-off, and the people who already do the invisible work to do? Where does it get gamed first—fake confirmations, logrolling, paying a neighbor's buy-out to wreck their share, inventing chores for each other, cliques of witnesses? What happens to the introverts and the people working two jobs, and does the town end up with belonging or just a market in the appearance of it? Are the obvious patches (auditing, cheaper buy-out, exemptions) enough, or do they just move the failure around? Walk me through it the way a suspicious game designer would.
