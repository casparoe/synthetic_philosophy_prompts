Please make a careful argument for the following thesis: neither Kant's universalizability test nor its later relatives—Hare's universal prescriptivism and Singer's generalization argument—succeeds as a substantive criterion of moral rightness. On every reading of 'What if everyone did that?' that keeps the test purely formal, the test either returns no verdict on ordinary wrong actions or returns a verdict only because some independent normative premise has already fixed how the action is to be described, which features of the universalized world are to count as unacceptable, or whose interests the universalization must track. Work through the contrast between Kant's two versions of the test (contradiction in conception and contradiction in will) and then through the different machinery in Hare (universalizable prescriptions) and Singer (generalizing the consequences of everyone's performing the act), showing in each case where the suppressed premise enters. Then answer the three strongest objections. First, address the claim that a maxim such as 'I may make a lying promise whenever it benefits me' fails the contradiction-in-conception test without any extra moral premise, because mutual reliance on promising could not survive the maxim's universalization. Second, explain why Hare's appeal to the prescriptivity of 'ought' does not escape the argument, even though it lets him reject the person who says 'I ought to cheat, but no one else in my exact circumstances may.' Third, explain why your criticism of Singer is not simply assuming that his generalization argument is a disguised rule-utilitarianism. Conclude by stating, as precisely as you can, the one extra premise that the strongest surviving version of the test still needs—and why, once that premise is added, what remains is no longer the purely logical test its defenders present.
