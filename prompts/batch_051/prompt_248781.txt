I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about the Mary's room argument. You know the setup: Mary grows up in a black-and-white room, learns every physical fact about color and color vision, and then leaves the room and sees red for the first time — the puzzle being whether she learns something new. Here's a variant I want to run by you, because I want the piece to be a bit more in the weeds than the usual version. What if, instead of walking out into full color, Mary is released into a lab where the only colored things are a series of evenly spaced patches that ramp from the exact gray of her room's walls up to a fully saturated red. She already knows all the physical facts about each patch — its reflectance curve, its place in the sequence, everything. The steps are made small enough that she cannot tell patch n from patch n+1, even though she can easily tell the first patch from the last. Now here's what's bugging me. The knowledge argument seems to need a moment where a new fact — what red is like — shows up. In the original, that moment is obvious: she sees red. But in the ramp version, when exactly does she get the fact? Is there a first patch in the sequence that gives it to her, and if adjacent patches are below her discrimination threshold, how could that patch be factually different from the one before it? Or does the argument actually depend on a sharp jump from no-red-experience to obvious-red-experience, and the gradual version exposes that as a hidden assumption? Could a defender just say the new fact arrives whenever the first reddish experience occurs, even if Mary herself cant say which patch that was — and if they say that, have they made the argument depend on an unobservable, maybe vague, boundary in a way that's a problem for them? I'm not looking for a the-thought-experiment-is-broken answer; I want to know wether the core of the argument survives this change, and if so what it has to say about vague boundaries between having and lacking a phenomenal property.
