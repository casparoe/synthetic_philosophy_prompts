I run a fortnightly philosophy discussion group for undergraduates — usually around fourteen students, mostly second- and third-years, a mix of philosophy majors and science students — and we have ninety minutes. A few weeks ago a friend and I got into an argument about coffee: he insisted his taste had not changed at all and that only his standards had improved, and I found I could not say what would settle the matter. That led me to Daniel Dennett's essay "Quining Qualia" and the Chase and Sanborn case, and I would like to build a session around it.

Please design a runnable classroom activity for this group that lets participants feel the force of Dennett's claim that qualia, as usually conceived, are an idle or incoherent posit. I want them to come away seeing that the dispute between "my experience changed" and "my standards changed" may not be a substantive empirical dispute at all, and to arrive at that realization through what happens in the activity rather than through anything I say afterwards. Do not begin with a lecture, and do not introduce the words qualia or quining until the debrief.

The activity should be built around a version of the Chase and Sanborn scenario: two tasters of the same familiar beverage both change their verdict about it, but one insists that his experience is unchanged while his standards have shifted, and the other insists that his standards are unchanged while his experience has shifted. The rest of the group should be tasked with adjudicating the dispute using the full range of available evidence — the tasters' sincere first-person reports, their discrimination behavior, memory of past tastings, comparisons with each other and with their own past performance — and should gradually discover that every piece of evidence they propose can be absorbed by either story. Ideally they construct this discovery themselves.

Please provide the following:

1. Setup and materials: the roles, the two tasters' scripts, the tasting materials if any, the rules for questioning and for proposing tests, and any constraints on what evidence teams may use.
2. A ninety-minute timeline, divided into stages, with the debrief at the end.
3. The debrief: how to map specific moments in the activity onto Dennett's argument — the way each proposed test gets reinterpreted, the moment participants begin to suspect the dispute is empty, the difference between the plain fact that experience has a first-person character and qualia as a theoretical posit, and how the case bears on the features qualia are traditionally taken to have (being private, ineffable, intrinsic, and directly or immediately apprehensible).
4. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle each failure mode — for instance, participants may conclude the tasters are lying or introspectively incompetent, or they may jump too quickly to the claim that there is no fact of the matter.
5. An honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce and what the facilitator must supply: in particular, what is lost because the first-person standpoint is represented by scripted confederates rather than by the participants themselves, and how to close that gap.

The activity should not require anyone to have read the essay in advance, and it should not require internet access or outside sources.
