In many firms, employment contracts expressly forbid employees from discussing their salaries, and even where such clauses are legally unenforceable, workers are socialized into treating an unprompted disclosure of one's pay as a failure of professional judgment. The practice is ordinarily defended in quiet, practical terms: differential pay is needed to reward performance, but making the differences visible would provoke envy, resentment, and intractable conflict, so secrecy is said to protect morale. Yet the same organizations describe themselves as meritocratic and often encourage employees to question whether decisions are fair, and in some jurisdictions labor law explicitly protects the right of employees to discuss wages — which suggests the practice embeds a contestable view rather than a neutral administrative choice.

What theory of envy and of fairness does the practice of pay secrecy presuppose? I am not primarily asking whether the practice is good or bad policy, but what philosophical commitments it quietly rests on.

In your answer, use exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **The implicit theory reconstructed** (about 40%): Spell out the picture of envy, of interpersonal pay comparisons, and of the relation between fairness and the visibility of rewards that salary secrecy appears to commit an employer to. Consider what the practice assumes a salary comparison is: a dependable pointer to unfairness, a trigger for a destructive passion, a private matter, or something with several of these characters at once. Consider also what it assumes about who should get to draw conclusions from pay information, and who should not.

2. **Strict presupposition vs. mere fit** (about 25%): Separate what the practice strictly presupposes — what must be true for it to make sense at all — from what it merely fits well with. In particular, say whether pay secrecy actually requires the view that envy is a vice to be suppressed, or whether it is also compatible with deliberately egalitarian, paternalistic, and efficiency-based justifications. Identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.

3. **Consistency checks** (about 15%): Test whether the practice's implicit commitments hang together. For instance, does hiding pay differences sit consistently with the same organization's professed meritocracy, its formal grievance procedures, or its willingness to publicize other comparative rankings such as sales leaderboards, awards, and promotion lists? If there is a tension, locate it precisely.

4. **The Rawlsian alternative and what would change** (about 20%): Rawls argued that envy proper is ordinarily a vice, but that it can be excusable rather than merely condemnable when the inequalities that provoke it are large, unjust, and wound the self-respect of the less advantaged. On that alternative view, envy functions partly as a signal of possible injustice, not just as a corrosive emotion to be managed. Suppose an organization adopted this alternative view. What would it have to change about pay secrecy — would it have to publish all salaries, or only the principles and ranges behind them, or merely change how it hears and responds to expressions of resentment? Say what the practical difference actually is.
