I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about where legal limits on speech should be drawn, and I came away realizing that I had defended a package of positions I have never had to state all at once. Before I have to defend that package again, I want an audit of whether it hangs together. What I seem committed to is the following.

1. The centerpiece is Mill's harm principle: the only legitimate basis for legal restrictions on speech is the prevention of harm to others. Falsity, hatefulness, indecency, and offensiveness are never, by themselves, sufficient grounds.

2. Offense is not harm in the relevant sense. A society committed to pluralism must tolerate speech that disgusts or demeans, so long as the injury is to the hearer's sensibilities rather than to some further interest.

3. Targeted harassment is different. A sustained campaign of messages directed at one person can be restricted even when no individual message is threatening, because the campaign as a whole can deprive that person of the capacity to participate in work, politics, and public life.

4. The state may also restrict speech when there is strong evidence that it substantially and foreseeably increases the risk of physical harm to third parties, including when the causal chain runs through other people's choices. The clearest example is deliberately false public-health guidance during an epidemic.

5. But I do not accept that speech may be restricted simply because the audience might form false beliefs and act badly on them. Adults are presumed competent to evaluate what they hear, and the mere fact that some listeners may be persuaded is not a sufficient legal ground.

6. I reject paternalistic speech restrictions outright. The fact that a person may make a poor decision in response to what she chooses to hear does not license the state to silence the speaker for that person's benefit.

7. Whether speech is regulable depends on its context and targeting as much as on its content; the same sentence can be protected in one setting and regulable in another. For that reason, I do not think there should be a distinct legal category of hate speech as such. Bigoted speech is regulable only when, in its actual context, it produces one of the harms described above, such as an intimidating campaign, not because it demeans a group in the abstract.

I want to know which of these pairs are actually in tension and which only appear to be, and I want the genuine tensions ranked by severity. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be. Then tell me which single revision would leave the rest of the package most intact. I am looking for an audit, not a lecture, and I do not know in advance which of these commitments is the culprit.
