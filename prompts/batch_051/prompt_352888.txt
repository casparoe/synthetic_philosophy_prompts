I'm on my school's academic honor counsel and we got handed a weird case. The school's statement of academic values opens with this: "Curiosity is a virtue, and knowledge is to be pursued for its own sake, not only as a means to grades, credit, or later advantage." It gets quoted at assemblies all the time, but nobody ever defined what kind of pursuit actually counts.

Here's the case. A junior named Marcus has basically stopped doing the assigned work for most of his classes. Instead he's spent eight months keeping what he calls an Atlas: he records, to the tenth of a second, the exact time every classroom door in the building opens and closes each day, plus the pitch of the bells and the delay between the end of first period and the start of second. He has notebooks full of these numbers but hasn't analyzed them or drawn any conclusions from them. He says it's pure knowledge for its own sake, and that the values statement protects (or even encourages) what he's doing. Some teachers want to fail him for academic neglect; others think he's ironically the only student actually taking the statement seriously. How should we interpret the quoted line: read literally, as the adults who wrote it probably meant it, and as a reasonable student would read it? Does his project count as the kind of knowledge-for-its-own-sake the line is talking about? And if those readings point in different directions, which one should the council base its decision on?
