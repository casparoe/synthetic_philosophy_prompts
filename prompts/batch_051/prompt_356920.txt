I recently found myself in an argument about whether appeals to culture and values ever explain economic outcomes or just gesture at a residual after material incentives are accounted for, and I picked up a general-audience economic history book on the subject. Its capsule treatment of Weber's Protestant ethic thesis struck me as suspiciously tidy, but I could not locate exactly where it goes wrong. The book's account, in my paraphrase, runs as follows.

Weber set out to explain why modern capitalism — the systematic, calculating pursuit of profit as an end in itself, with profits reinvested — arose in northern Europe. His answer was psychological: Calvinist predestination made believers anxious about their salvation, and since good works could not purchase election, they threw themselves into disciplined worldly labor and treated success as evidence that they were among the elect. Work thereby became a calling rather than a necessary evil, and the resulting ethos of frugality, methodical self-control, and reinvestment instead of consumption supplied the motivational engine for capitalism. Weber supported this by showing that Protestants were overrepresented among business owners and in technical and commercial education. Later economic historians dismantled the thesis: capitalism already flourished in Catholic Italy and the Low Countries before the Reformation, and many successful early capitalists were not especially devout. The book concludes that the Protestant ethic episode is the standard cautionary tale about explaining economic development through values, since such explanations are unfalsifiable and can always be adjusted to fit the evidence.

I am inclined to think the book is roughly right, since its conclusion flatters my prior that culture is usually invoked as a residual explanation; but I also know Weber explicitly denied being a simple idealist, and I would rather not have my prior confirmed by a caricature. Please go through this account piece by piece and tell me which claims a careful Weber scholar would accept, which are overstatements or conflations of distinct claims, and whether the book's concluding lesson about values-as-causes actually follows from the historical material it reports. Be specific about where the summary jumps from Weber's actual argument to something stronger.
