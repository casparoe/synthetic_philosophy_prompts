I keep noticing this move in arguments about historical figures, and I can't tell wether it's a named thing or just sloppy reasoning. The pattern goes like this. With Jefferson: when someone brings up that he held slaves, the defence is that he was a man of his time—everyone in his class did it, so it's unfair to judge him by today's standards. But those same people will happily praise him for being radically ahead of his time, say for writing about liberty and equality while most of his contemporaries were still totally fine with inherited rank. Same with Churchill: the bad stuff (racial views, the Bengal famine) gets the 'he was a man of his time' treatment, while the good stuff (seeing what Hitler was before most of his contemporaries did) gets the 'he rose above his age' treatment.

So the same person is being treated as a product of his era for his vices and as someone who transcended his era for his virtues. Is there an established name for this exact asymmetry? If there is, I want to know what distinguishes it from plain double standards, special pleading, and the often-reasonable point that we shouldn't judge past people by our norms. I'm not asking for just a label—I want the concept carved out enough that I can tell when someone is doing this versus when they're making a fair point about historical context. Maybe it's not actually incoherent, because a person can be typical in one respect and exceptional in another; if the move is fine in some cases and not in others, I want to know where the line is.
