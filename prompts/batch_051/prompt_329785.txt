I host a small philosophy podcast, and my next guest defends a reconstructed Benthamite position on the legal regulation of sexual conduct. She states the position in a deliberately strong form. The only legitimate ground for punishing, or otherwise coercively restricting, a sexual practice is that it causes pain to someone other than the participants. The disgust, religious offence, or sense of unnaturalness felt by outsiders carries no independent weight. Sexual conduct between adults acting with free and informed consent, which harms no third party, lies outside the proper scope of the criminal law. Harm that the participants do to themselves may be met with persuasion, education, or the removal of enticements, but never with criminal punishment. And the inherited sexual prohibitions we still feel drawn to defend are, in her view, expressions of what she, following Bentham, calls the principle of asceticism — a hostility to pleasure that gets rationalized after the fact with talk of purity and corruption.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond inviting her to restate that view, and present them in the order I would ask them. Do not ask questions that merely request exposition of Bentham's texts or manuscripts; the pressure should be on the philosophical structure of the position. Cover each of the following, and for each, state the answer she is most likely to give and include a follow-up keyed to that answer.

First, the problem of third-party pain. If a majority or a determined minority genuinely suffers at the thought that certain consensual acts occur in private, why does that suffering not enter the utilitarian calculation? She will probably answer that such suffering is prejudice rather than pain, and that counting it would let every majority veto any liberty simply by feeling outrage. The follow-up should force her to state a test distinguishing prejudice from genuine pain that does not already presuppose her conclusion.

Second, the line between persuasion and coercion. Ask what the position says about an adult who, fully informed and undeceived, consents to sexual arrangements that will predictably destroy his health, career, marriage, or self-respect. She will most likely answer that even severe self-regarding harm licenses only persuasion. The follow-up should ask whether, when utility and the anti-paternalist line conflict, she follows utility or follows the line — and whether the line is doing more work than utility is.

Third, publicity versus privacy. Bentham is famous for the Panopticon and for treating publicity and inspection as guarantees against the abuse of power. Ask how a Benthamite can combine that enthusiasm for visibility with a defense of a private sexual domain shielded from public judgment. She will probably answer that inspection is for institutions and office-holders, not for consensual private dealings. The follow-up should press whether the distinction between public power and private life is itself a utilitarian one, or whether she has quietly imported a principle of rights or natural liberty that her official theory denies.

Fourth, what remains of the family. Once chastity and the sexual monopoly of marriage carry no intrinsic moral weight, does utility still recommend durable, exclusive, child-rearing unions? If she says it does, ask what separates that recommendation from the traditional sexual morality she rejected; if she says it does not, ask how she handles the consequences for children and for the security of caregiving. State her likely answer and key the follow-up to it.

Fifth, what could count against the view. End with a question about what social evidence would lead her to support criminalizing a practice she now regards as outside the law's reach — suppose, for instance, that a systematic natural experiment showed that decriminalizing a practice reliably increased aggregate misery. Use her answer to ask whether utility is really doing the work or whether the liberal conclusion was fixed in advance.

Each question should be answerable from the commitments of the position itself, without recourse to archival scholarship, and the sequence as a whole should leave a listener wanting to hear how she gets out of each corner.
