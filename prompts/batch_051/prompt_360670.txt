After thirty years in a spectroscopy lab I got used to the idea that clean clasification is mostly a convenience, so when I retired and started reading philosophy of mind I expected to find it either too tidy or too vague. Instead I got stuck on an older computational argument about concepts. The old Copycat project (Hofstadter and colleagues) worked in a toy world of letter-string analogies — if abc changes to abd, what should ijk change to? The puzzles look trivial, but the argument behind the project wasn't: any system with a fixed set of symbolic rules, the claim went, will fail at human-like analogy because the relevant concepts have to slip under pressure from the rest of the problem. 'Successor' can slide toward 'predecessor,' a group of letters that looks like a run in one frame can become three unrelated items in another, and the alphabet itself can flip between a line and a loop. Copycat's whole architecture was built around that idea: many small stochastic processes (codelets) proposing, supporting, and dismantling candidate construals, so that conceptual organization stays fluid.

I've spent a few months reading around this, and now a real-world development has me spooked. Transformer language models trained on ordinary text solve these letter-string analogy problems quite well, with no codelets, no explicit slippage mechanism, and no re-perception of the input — unless you count billions of scalar weights as having learned something equivalent to slippage. I can read this two ways. One, the original argument was a claim about an inner mechanism and has been undercut: fluid concepts simply aren't necessary for the task. Two, the argument was about what a satisfying computational explanation of analogy has to look like, and passing the puzzle is not the same as doing it the way the argument cared about. Which premise is actually carrying the argument, and does the transformer result bear on it? I keep flip-flopping and would appreciate a diagnostic answer: does the argument survive, does it need repair, or is it dead?
