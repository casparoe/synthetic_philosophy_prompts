A few weeks ago, at a dinner after a workshop on normativity, I watched two colleagues argue about a case, and I have been unable to resolve the argument myself. The case concerned a visiting researcher from a country without a tipping practice, who had spent a month in a city where sit-down restaurant servers are paid a sub-minimum "tipped wage" on the standing assumption that gratuities make up most of their income. The hosts had explained this to him more than once. After a full-service dinner, he left no tip, observing that he found the arrangement ethically dubious and that a gratuity, by definition, cannot be owed.

The first colleague, Ana, a moral philosopher, said the visitor had acted wrongly. The price on the menu was not, she argued, the real price: it was kept artificially low by a convention whose implied term is that diners pay the rest directly to the server. One may not accept service under that pricing scheme and then declare oneself out of the practice once the bill arrives. Conventions generate obligations when people reasonably rely on them, and the visitor was a participant, not a spectator; after a month he knew perfectly well what leaving no tip would communicate to a server who depends on it. The second colleague, Ben, a social ontologist, replied that Ana had thereby shown that the convention itself was doing no moral work. If the pay structure is exploitative, he said, the wrongdoer is the employer who built it, not the diner who declined to subsidize it. Nothing was presented to the visitor as a term of service, and "participation" cannot conscript a person into local norms; otherwise a traveler would be bound to bribe officials in a country where bribery is the accepted way of getting things done. There is, Ben insisted, no free-standing duty to follow local conventions.

Ana answered that bribery is disanalogous because it is wrong on antecedent grounds and is not constitutive of a transaction the visitor has chosen to enter. A better test, she said, is queueing: if a visitor joins a country where people form orderly lines and then pushes to the front, he wrongs the people behind him even though he will never see them again, and the wrong is best explained by the reasonable expectations a shared practice creates for newcomers as well as locals. Ben replied that the queueing case does its work through ordinary harm, since the pusher seizes a place others had a legitimate claim to and imposes concrete delay. Consider, he said, jaywalking: in one city everyone crosses against the light, and in another nobody does; is a visitor morally required to jaywalk in the first city and refrain in the second merely because each is the local convention? Surely not. Where safety and law leave the matter weak, the convention is morally inert, which suggests conventions never add to the balance of reasons themselves. Ana then narrowed her claim: grant that conventions have no force "of their own"; still, the visitor knew what his act would convey and allowed the server to serve him under a false expectation. At minimum, he owed her an explicit declaration before ordering that he rejected the tipping convention, so that she could decide whether to serve him on those terms. Ben replied that this concession gives away the question: the duty would be one of non-deception, not one of obedience to a convention. And the visitor, by simply withholding the tip, announced his rejection plainly enough; no deception occurred. A visitor, Ben concluded, has no general duty to inventory and announce which local customs he refuses.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher working on the moral status of social conventions and social ontology, and think through this exchange slowly and carefully. Begin by listing the distinct claims in play, then go through the exchange move by move in the order it occurred, identifying for each move whether it is clearly right, clearly wrong, or partially right, and briefly justify each rating. Then answer three questions separately: (a) did the visitor have an obligation to tip in this case, and if so, what kind of obligation was it, and exactly what grounded it; (b) can "follow the local conventions" ever be a free-standing moral duty, or is it always shorthand for underlying considerations such as harm, reliance, promise, deception, or exploitation; (c) what did both Ana and Ben overlook — perhaps the difference between conventions that constitute a practice, conventions that coordinate behavior, and conventions that express respect, or the way the visitor's status as a temporary guest might matter. Conclude by saying who is more right overall, even if neither of them has the whole truth, and do not hide behind "it depends" without saying exactly what it depends on.
