Adopt the role of a social epistemologist who works on information cascades and rational herding. I am a philosophy professor putting together an exercise for a graduate seminar, and I want a technically careful ranking rather than a survey or a reading list. Please work through the problem step by step, and before settling on your final ranking, spend a preliminary paragraph brainstorming the mechanism by which each intervention is likely to operate in the model described below.

The setting is the canonical sequential binary-choice model of observational learning. A faculty is deciding, one member at a time in a fixed and publicly known order, whether to adopt or reject a new pedagogical method. There is a single relevant state of the world—the method is either genuinely effective or not—and before voting, each member receives a private clue about that state with accuracy p, where 1/2 < p < 1. Each member observes the complete public history of earlier votes but not the clues that produced them, and each member’s only goal is to vote for the option that is actually best. In this environment, once the public tally becomes sufficiently lopsided, a later voter can rationally follow the crowd against her own clue, and the group can lock into an informational cascade that persists whether or not the early majority was correct; call a cascade *wrong* when the group locks onto the option that does not match the relevant state.

A dean is considering six interventions to reduce the expected harm caused specifically by wrong cascades. Rank them into four tiers, from most effective to least effective or actively counterproductive: (1) Secret ballots: no vote is revealed until everyone has voted, after which the full tally is announced. (2) Signal transparency: each voter must publicly state, along with her vote, the clue she privately received. (3) Interim public signal: after the second vote, the dean releases a public report containing one further clue of the same accuracy p. (4) Contrarian pay: the seventh voter—and only the seventh—receives a bonus large enough to ensure she votes according to her own clue even when the current majority points the other way, and everyone knows this. (5) Margin-only history: later voters see only whether adoption or rejection currently leads and by how many votes, but not the order in which the earlier votes were cast. (6) Conformity bonus: each voter is rewarded if her own vote ends up matching the final majority decision. For each intervention, explain in a sentence or two why it lands in its tier, identify whether it works primarily by changing the information available, altering the sequence, or reshaping incentives, and note any major side-effect, such as suppressing valuable information aggregation or strengthening conformist motivation. Finally, state which structural feature of the sequential-choice environment makes wrong cascades possible in the first place, and explain whether your top-tier intervention removes that feature or merely blunts its operation.
