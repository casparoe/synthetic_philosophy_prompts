I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about whether species are real units of nature or just labels that taxonomists impose for their own convenience. The argument kept circling back to a concrete case that now strikes me as philosophically stranger than I had thought: conservation law. The U.S. Endangered Species Act directs agencies to list and protect species, but it also extends the same protections to subspecies and to distinct population segments, and listing decisions routinely treat these categories as though they pick out objective divisions already present in nature. Yet the same system quietly tracks the shifting fortunes of taxonomy. A population can gain or lose legal protection when systematists decide to split or lump a species, or when a subspecies is demoted, even though the organisms and their circumstances have not changed at all. Hybrids, too, are handled unevenly — sometimes treated as contamination of a protected type, sometimes as part of the protected entity — and the law never quite says what kind of thing is being protected.

I want to understand what metaphysics of species this practice presupposes. Does it strictly presuppose that species are real, discoverable, uniquely bounded entities — something like individuals, or lineages, or natural kinds — or is the practice compatible with several rival views rather than requiring any one of them? What does its willingness to treat subspecies and distinct population segments as protectable units do to that question? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, where do its parts presuppose inconsistent things, and what would have to change in the statute, in listing practice, and in recovery planning if we replaced the implicit metaphysics with a thoroughgoing species pluralism, or with the view that species are conveniences rather than units of the world?
