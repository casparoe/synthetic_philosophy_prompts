Last week I listened to two friends, Mara and Dan, argue for nearly an hour about whether physics could have a final theory and whether we could know if we had found one. I could not settle it, and I would like a careful adjudication of what each of them actually said.

Mara began: the idea is just the fantasy late nineteenth-century physics is famous for. The confident view then was that physics was nearly complete, with only minor clouds and more precise measurements left; within a generation, relativity and quantum mechanics overturned the picture. Any present-day claim that we are near a final theory should be discounted for the same inductive reason.

Dan replied that this is too crude an induction. Premature declarations of completeness are distinguishable from a mature final theory. The premature ones were made in the face of known anomalies and unexplained loose ends. A genuine candidate would be constrained from the inside: it would predict that further inquiry reaches a scale or mathematical structure at which the theory is self-consistent and unique, and it would reproduce everything we can currently measure.

Mara said that "everything we can currently measure" is exactly the problem. A theory is only ever confirmed in the regimes we have probed. We cannot test it at energies we cannot produce or in conditions we cannot arrange, so no amount of success rules out a surprise in the next regime. We would always be one collider, one future cosmological observation, or one new experimental technique away from discovering a deeper layer.

Dan answered that she was setting an impossible standard. We never know empirical claims with certainty, yet we rightly treat some of them as established within their domains. A proposed final theory could earn the same standing by a different route: universal scope, a uniqueness theorem, and the apparent exhaustion of the regimes testable in principle could make the hypothesis that it is final better confirmed than any rival.

Mara said the uniqueness step smuggles in the conclusion. A theory can be unique among theories of a certain mathematical kind, but that does not show the world is built from that mathematical kind, and it does not rule out future mathematics describing a deeper layer. And "exhaustion of testable regimes" is not something one can observe from the outside; it is something one would be living through, with no guarantee that the list of testable regimes is closed.

They kept circling around this. Please adjudicate the exchange as it happened: identify who is right about which specific claim, flag where either side overreaches or shifts the issue, say what, if anything, both are missing, and then say who is more right overall. Please give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
