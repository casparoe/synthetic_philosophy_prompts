I am a student in a philosophy of mind course in which we have been contrasting higher-order theories of consciousness with first-order representationalism. The higher-order claim under discussion is that a mental state is conscious exactly when its subject is aware of being in that state: a separate higher-order representation takes the first-order state as its object, and that higher-order representation need not itself be conscious. The first-order rival says that a state is conscious in virtue of its own representational properties and the access it has to downstream cognition, with no distinct higher-order state required.

I want to know whether this disagreement is empirically decidable. Take the familiar case of a briefly masked stimulus that a subject classifies above chance and that primes subsequent responses, even though the subject reports no conscious percept. The higher-order theorist explains this by saying the first-order representation exists but is not the object of any higher-order representation. The first-order theorist explains it by saying the first-order representation is present but too weak, unstable, or unavailable to count as conscious. Because both theories can absorb the same surface results, the disagreement seems to concern something unobserved: a missing second state versus a missing first-order property.

Could you help me design the strongest empirical test one could plausibly run to separate these two readings? The design should try to hold first-order informational strength roughly constant while independently manipulating the presence or quality of higher-order, metacognitive representation — through confidence judgments, post-decision wagering, dual-task interference, transient neural stimulation, or whatever manipulation you judge most appropriate. For your proposed design, specify which pattern of results would support each theory, whether the theories genuinely make different predictions rather than merely accommodating the data afterwards, and what a positive result would still leave unsettled. I would especially like an honest account of what no experiment can settle: whether the higher-order state is the cause of consciousness or only a reliable correlate, whether the operationalization of "higher-order representation" really captures the theory's notion, and whether any test that depends on a subject's report is unavoidably biased toward the higher-order picture because the act of reporting itself introduces a further higher-order state. In short, I want the best empirical adjudication this debate can have, together with a clear statement of what even that cannot decide.
