Two people at the rationalist meetup I go to got into it last month about the Fermi paradox, and I've been turning it over every since. They weren't doing the usual "aliens exist because the universe is big" or "the government is hiding them" stuff. They were specifically arguing about whether the quiet sky is even evidence that technological intelligence is rare, once you try to do the anthropic update properly. I got asked to referee and couldn't, so I'm writing up the exchange from memory and looking for a move-by-move verdict.

Here's the exchange as close as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Sam: The usual line is "we see no aliens, so technological intelligence must be rare or short-lived." But that inference has a selection effect in it. If technological intelligence is rare, there's roughly one civilization worth of observers. If it's common, there are astronomically more observers. The common hypothesis gets a huge compensating boost just from making our existence more likely, and that boost can cancel the fact that a common, expansionist galaxy should look crowded and loud. So an empty sky doesn't licence the standard inference to rarity.

(2) Dana: That's SIA-style reasoning, and it double-counts. The data aren't "observers exist somewhere." The data are "we exist and we see no one else." Our existence is already in the data with probability one; we don't update on it twice. The only part of the data that differs between the hypotheses is the empty sky. A common, expansionist galaxy predicts we'd see other civilizations; a rare one predicts silence. The likelihood ratio clearly favors rare. You're smuggling in a prior that rewards hypotheses for predicting more copies of an observer, which is exactly what plain Bayesianism should *not* do.

(3) Sam: You're the one sweeping selection under the rug. If you sample uniformly from all observers, then under rare every observer looks out and sees an empty sky. Under common, almost all observers see a colonized galaxy, and observers like us—early, before contact, seeing nothing—are a tiny slice. So your own random-observer reasoning says a common universe makes our actual sky extremely unlikely, which is what drives the ordinary update. SIA adds the other factor, the weight for there being many observers at all, and that softens the push. Calling that double-counting is just announcing that you don't like SIA; it isn't an argument.

(4) Dana: But notice what happens once you let in the number of observers. The strength of the empty-sky update now depends on which reference class you choose: observers, civilizations, species, Earth-like planets, years of observation. Each choice gives a different answer, and you haven't given a principled rule for picking one. That makes the correction a free parameter. The plain likelihood—where our civilization is the observation platform and the data are "we see nothing"—doesn't have that problem.

They went in circles after that. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification each. Then say what the real distinction they're fighting about is, which of them is more right overall, and what the posterior over "technological intelligence is common" should do under the anthropic assumption you think is most defensible.
