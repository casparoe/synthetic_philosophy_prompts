I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of language. After a long argument with a friend about whether two-dimensional semantics genuinely explains necessary a posteriori truths or merely re-describes them, I have become convinced that a good final exam question must force students past the familiar examples. I would like you to produce one such exam question, a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks.

The exam question should introduce a fictional term, call it 'glorp', that a community introduces by stipulating that it is to refer to whatever actually causes a widely observed syndrome S. Two features of the case matter: determining whether a subject has S requires a laborious empirical test, and the unique cause, compound Q, is discovered only many years after the term enters the language. The question should ask students to assess, using the distinction between primary and secondary intensions, whether the sentence 'Glorp is Q' is knowable a priori, and to identify the most serious difficulty the case raises for the two-dimensional framework. It must not use 'water', 'Hesperus', or any other stock example, and it should not be answerable by reciting the definitions of primary and secondary intension.

The model answer should make the following moves. It should distinguish the trivial truth that whatever actually causes S is whatever actually causes S, if a unique such thing exists, from the substantive identity claim involving Q. It should identify the two empirical gaps on which 'Glorp is Q' depends: first, that the reference-fixing description is uniquely satisfied, and second, that Q is the thing that satisfies it. It should explain why the primary intensions of 'glorp' and 'Q' can come apart when different candidates are considered as actual, even though the secondary intension treats 'Glorp is Q' as necessary. And it should state at least one genuine limitation the case exposes, such as the framework's sensitivity to which reference-fixing description is associated with the term, or the possibility that the primary intension collapses into a notational record of apriority intuitions rather than an explanation of them.

The marking scheme should name the specific misunderstandings that cost marks. In particular, it should penalize the inference from the fact that the reference was fixed by a description to the conclusion that 'Glorp is Q' is a priori; it should penalize confounding primary with secondary intension and treating epistemic possibility as ordinary ignorance about the actual world with reference held fixed; and it should separate an A answer, which keeps the semantic and epistemic roles of the reference-fixing description clearly apart and examines both empirical gaps and the limitation, from a B answer, which deploys the terminology correctly but applies it mechanically without pressing on those gaps or the limitation.
