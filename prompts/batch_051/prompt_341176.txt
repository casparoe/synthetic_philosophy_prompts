I've been reading Nietzsche lately and I keep noticing a move he makes that I don't have a name for, and I'd like to know if the field has one. He'll tell a story about how some moral ideal got started—humility, pity, turning the other cheek—among people who were weak and resentful, and then he seems to treat that origin story as a reason to stop respecting the ideal. For example, he describes slave morality as the revenge of the powerless: the weak couldn't beat the strong, so they redefined strength, pride, and power as evil and made their own meekness into "goodness." He isn't just idly wondering where the ideal came from; the origin seems meant to discredit it. Now, I know that showing where an idea came from is usually called the genetic fallacy and is considered a bad reason to reject a belief. But Nietzsche seems to be doing something more specific: the origin isn't just low, the ideal is supposed to still carry the resentment inside it, like a weapon aimed at the strong, so the origin keeps showing up in how the ideal works.

Is there an established name for this move—using an origin story to debunk or downgrade a moral ideal rather than just a factual belief? How exactly is it different from the plain genetic fallacy, and from other nearby things like debunking explanations? And what has to be true for it to count as criticism rather than just narration—does it work against some ideals but not others?
