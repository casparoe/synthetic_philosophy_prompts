I'm taking a course on early Christianity and my professor keeps presenting divine impassibility as almost overdetermined: the tradition had to absorb the Greek idea of apatheia, because of what ancient thinkers assumed about a perfect being, and so the mainstream settled on a God who cannot be affected by creatures. Which got me idly wondering weather the opposite settlement was ever a live option, and I figure the best way to test that is to run the counterfactual. So: suppose that by around the late second century the emerging Christian mainstream goes the other way, and declares that God is passible in his own nature. Not just that the incarnate Word suffers in his human nature, but that every pain, grief, and humiliation of every creature is an actual modification of God's own life. Stipulate that this is held together, however awkwardly, with omnipotence, omniscience, and God's final blessedness—so we are not automatically pushed into a finite or evolving God. Nothing else about the political or social situation changes.

I want a reasoned prediction of how the next five centuries of theology and practice play out: what happens to the problem of evil, to the doctrine of the atonement, to asceticism and martyrdom, to liturgy and popular devotion, and to apologetics against pagan philosophers who prized apatheia. Which of these effects are forced by the doctrine almost regardless of other factors, and which depend on whether the view sinks down from learned theology into ordinary piety? And are there internal pressures—pastoral, intellectual, or institutional—that would push the tradition back toward impassibility over time? I care more about the causal story than the particular dates, so please be explicit about which links in your reasoning are robust and which are guesses.
