I spent my working life in the natural sciences, and only since retiring have I been able to read philosophy in earnest. A difficulty now strikes me as fundamental, since it concerns the kind of inferential step I formerly took for granted. As I understand Carroll's tortoise, no supply of additional premises can force an inference: if one grants that P and grants that if P then Q, yet declines to accept Q, then offering the further premise that P together with "if P then Q" entails Q merely provides a third premise to decline, and so on without end. I draw the lesson that an inference rule such as modus ponens cannot be treated as, and therefore cannot be justified by, another premise; consequently, any demand for a non-circular justification of deduction seems impossible to satisfy. And yet, when I actually move from premises to conclusion, I do not experience myself as exercising a convention I might decline. I find myself answerable to a constraint that is already there, such that ignoring it would be an error. I am not asking which side should prevail. I want to know what would have to be true for each side to give way—for the intuition that deduction is binding to be relinquished, or for the demand that this bindingness be justified to be relinquished—and whether there is a sufficiently precise sense of "justification" or "binding" on which the regress and the intuition can both be retained.
