Last night a friend and I argued for hours about forgeries, and he left me half convinced of a view I still cannot bring myself to accept. I want help deciding which of us should yield.

He defended a strict perceptualism about painting. On his account, all of a painting's aesthetic value resides in its perceptible surface: the arrangement of pigment, color, line, texture, and how those features look to an adequately prepared viewer under adequate viewing conditions. It follows that two canvases stipulated to be indistinguishable to any possible act of looking cannot differ as artworks; a flawless forgery is, artistically, a duplicate of the original. The extra value we place on the original, he granted, is real but belongs to a different genus of value: documentary, biographical, monetary. To treat it as aesthetic is a category mistake. The doctrine is attractive precisely because it is simple, unified, and gives a clean decision procedure: if you could not tell the objects apart by looking, do not rank them differently as works of art.

I found this compelling. But two cases keep troubling me. First, when a canvas I have long admired turns out to be a pastiche by a later hand rather than the painter to whom it was attributed, nothing visible changes, yet the experience changes: passages that once read as daring now read as imitative, and what felt like originality now feels like mimicry. I take this alteration to track something aesthetic, not merely a shift in market value or in my social standing as a collector. Second, my friend pressed me with a harder case: if a copyist made a reproduction so exact that no viewer could distinguish it from the original, and the original were then destroyed, the aesthetic value would survive intact in the copy. I resisted. The loss of the original felt like an aesthetic loss, not merely an archival or sentimental one, and I am unwilling to treat that resistance as an error to be explained away.

Please present the strongest version of the perceptualist theory and what it must say about these cases. Then present the strongest version of my intuition, including why it deserves to be taken as evidence rather than dismissed as provenance snobbery or as a rationalization. Finally, offer a negotiated verdict: should the intuition be debunked and dropped, should the theory be amended so that aesthetic value is partly constituted by a work's origin and history, or are the two positions compatible because they concern different kinds of value after all? For whichever resolution you defend, make explicit what would justify it and what it would quietly give up. Keep the reply to 800 words or fewer.
