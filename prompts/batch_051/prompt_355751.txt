Take Truman's decision to drop atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945 and analyze it through the lens of the dirty hands problem in politics. I don't need the historical play-by-play — I've got the basics: the bombs killed well over 100,000 people, most of them civilians, and the stated rationale was that ending the war quickly would avoid a much costlier invasion of the Japanese home islands. What I want is the ethics worked hard.

Spend the first part keeping the mapping tight. In the dirty hands framework, who exactly is the player here — Truman the person, Truman the office-holder, or the collective (the administration, the US as a wartime nation)? What were the options as the framework would frame them — was there a clean alternative on the table in the relevant sense, and does acting wrongly require that a cleaner option was really available, or just believed available? And what counts in this episode as the wrong being done — the mass killing of civilians, the use of a weapon of a new indiscriminate kind, the lack of a demonstration or warning, or something else?

Then run it the other way. Here's the wrinkle I keep tripping over: the framework seems to require that the agent sees the act as wrong, hence the dirty part — a cost in the form of guilt or a moral remainder that sticks to the agent. Truman seems to have framed the decision afterwards as straightforward and, by most accounts, didn't express much lingering guilt. Does that mean the case never really lands in the dirty hands category — that it's just a tragic consequentialist calculation, or even self-deception about the options — or does it expose a hole in the framework: that the moral remainder can live somewhere other than the agent's conscience, maybe in the institution, in the public, or in the historical record itself? Put differently: if a leader does something the framework counts as dirty but feels clean about it, is the framework's verdict stronger because the agent fails to take on the guilt they should, or weaker because it can no longer say what the dirtiness consists in?

Finally, say what you think the episode does to the theory: is Truman a standard dirty hands case, an anomaly the framework has to stretch to cover, or a case it should admit it was never build for?
