Here's a thing I can't ever quite square. We say thinking for yourself and questioning authority are virtues, and we stuff the curriculum with lessons about spotting fallacies and resisting groupthink. But the moment someone actually follows through in a direction we don't like—your cousin does his own research and becomes a flat earther, or a guy reads every study he can find and concludes the vaccines did more harm than good—we stop calling it critical thinking and start calling him a crank or a dupe. We never say, well, at least he's thinking for himself. We say he's doing something wrong. Yet if you compare what he's doing to what we praise, it looks like the same activity: doubting a consensus, distrusting the experts, refusing to defer. The only difference that jumps out is the place he ends up.

So here's the question. Is there a principle that draws the line somewhere other than 'the conclusion I happen to like'—a principle that explains why one person's doubt counts as critical thinking and the other person's doubt counts as a failure of it? Find the strongest such principle you can. Then stress-test it on a person who uses exactly the moves from a critical thinking textbook, as carefully as anyone, and still lands on something absurd or repugnant. Does your principle say we should treat that person's doubt as virtuous or as blameworthy? And then be honest: is the asymmetry—praising the doubt that stays on the reservation, punishing the doubt that wanders off—something to keep, something to revise, or its just tribalism with a nicer name?
