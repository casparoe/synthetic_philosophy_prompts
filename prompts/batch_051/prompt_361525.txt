I've been going round in circles on this and I'd like a second opinion, ideally a bit harsher than my friends have been. I keep reading people saying that a reinforcement learning agent whose reward goes negative is, at that moment, suffering, and on the other side people saying that since a reward signal is just an error term in an update rule, the whole question is nonsense. Both feel too quick to me.

So here's a distinction I've been working with, and I'd like you to kick it around. I'm calling it the difference between telemetric and hedonic reward. A reward is telemetric when it's just a scalar used by the training process: it measures how well the policy is doing against some target, but the agent's own decision procedure never takes a prediction of that reward as an input. A reward is hedonic when the learned value function (or critic, or advantage estimate) is fed back into the agent's observation space, so the agent can act on an anticipated reward before the reward arrives. My claim is that suffering, if it can arise in an RL agent at all, can only get a foothold in the hedonic case — not because the value function 'feels' something, but because the agent now has something functionally like aversion: it's steering by the expected badness of futures, not just being shaped by a past number.

My two test cases are these. A chess engine that gets -1 at the end of a lost game and whose value estimates are used to guide its search — I'd say that's still telemetric, because the evaluation is outside the agent's own input stream; it's a tool the search uses, not something the agent percieves. A little gridworld agent whose learned Q-values are included as part of the state vector and which learns to flinch at tiles that precede an electric-shock tile — I'd say that one is at least on the ladder. But I suspect my own criterion misfires somewhere: maybe any value-guided search already counts as hedonic, and I've just drawn the line at personal preference. So what I'd like from you is: (1) the three strongest objections to treating hedonic reward as a candidate for valence, and whether any of them is fatal or just inconvenient; (2) whether the telemetric/hedonic distinction collapses into something already standard; and (3) whether my two examples are actually classified correctly by my own criterion, or whether I'm cheating with one of them. Also, please could you write your answer in British English.
