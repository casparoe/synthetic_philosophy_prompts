Act as a professional philosopher of social science who is also fluent in experimental and observational design. Think through the problem carefully, beginning with a brief brainstorming pass in which you list several candidate tests and note what each would fail to settle, then develop and defend the strongest single design.

On the campus where I work, a pedestrian norm has grown up over the years: people keep to the right in corridors and stairwells, even in buildings far from any road, with no posted rule and no obvious punishment for walking left. A sociologist colleague calls this a textbook Lewis convention. He means, roughly, that right-walking is an arbitrary, self-sustaining solution to a recurrent coordination problem: nearly everyone conforms, expects nearly everyone else to conform, prefers to conform provided nearly everyone else does, and would have settled on walking left just as happily if that had been the salient alternative. A psychologist replies that this attributes too much cognition to people: she thinks right-walking is mostly an unthinking habit imported from road traffic, and that the talk of common knowledge and alternative possible conventions is untestable philosophizing. I want to know how much of the sociologist's stronger claim can be given genuine empirical content.

Design the strongest concrete test — observational, experimental, comparative, or some combination — of whether right-walking on this campus is a Lewis convention in something close to the full sense, rather than (a) a mere behavioral regularity, (b) a rule maintained by informal sanctions, or (c) an unreflective individual habit. Specify exactly what you would measure or manipulate, what pattern of results would support the Lewis account, and what pattern would count against it. Then sort the components of the Lewis package (the role of expectations, the preference for conformity, the availability of a genuine alternative such as walking left, the common-knowledge condition) into those your test can operationalize and those it cannot. Finally, be explicit about what a positive result would and would not show: which aspects of convention get translated into observation, and which remain a matter of interpretation. Aim for a design realistic enough that a small research group could run it without elaborate equipment.
