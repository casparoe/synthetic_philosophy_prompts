Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether signing up for cryonics is rational when treated as a bet on eventual revival, and we ended exactly where we began. We agree, in broad terms, that the probability of a frozen body ever being revived is vanishingly small and that the payoff if revival occurred would be enormous. I maintain that the decision should be settled by expected value and that most people's refusal is status-quo bias dressed up as prudence. He maintains that probabilities this small are decision-theoretically inert — that letting an astronomical payoff do real work in a tiny-probability calculation amounts to a mugging — and that declining the contract is simply reasonable. The strange part is that neither of us changed any factual belief, yet neither of us could name a principle the other was violating. I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a disagreement about the probability, about the legitimacy of expected-value reasoning with extreme probabilities, about whether the revived person would be me in the identity-relevant sense, or about something prior — a difference in who bears the burden of proof? And once the kind of disagreement is diagnosed, what would count as progress?
