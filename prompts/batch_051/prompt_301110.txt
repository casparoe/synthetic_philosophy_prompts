I teach a seminar on language and social reality, and yesterday a student came to office hours with an argument he says he got from a friend in linguistics. He wouldn't write the slur out, so he just called it S and it's neutral counterpart N. The argument, as he gave it:

(1) S and N pick out exactly the same people; their extension is identical.
(2) What a word literally says is just its extension—the set of things it labels.
(3) So S and N have the same literal meaning.
(4) But the offensiveness of S can't be part of its extension; an extension is just a set of people, and sets aren't offensive.
(5) So the offensiveness of S is not part of its literal meaning.
(6) Therefore anyone who says the meaning of S itself is derogatory is confusing semantics with pragmatics. The word itself is a harmless label, and the harm lives entirely in how it's used.

I'm pretty sure this is too clean, but I couldn't give him a sharp diagnosis on the spot, and I'd rather not just say "the literature on slurs disagrees." So: is the argument valid as written? If not, which step fails, and under what reading of "literal meaning" does it fail? If the weak link is (2), tell me whether there's a weaker version of it that still gets you to (6), or whether the conclusion just stands or falls with an implausible premise.
