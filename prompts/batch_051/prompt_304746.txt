Consider the classic tension between the value of a statistical life and the rule of rescue. A health authority has a fixed annual budget of $100 million that can be spent either on road safety, which saves one anonymous life per $1 million with no upper bound, or on rescues. Each rescue saves one identified patient who presents for treatment and would die within a month without it, and each rescue costs $10 million. In the standard one-shot version, the authority chooses between spending $10 million on a patient who has already presented and spending the same sum on road safety, which would save ten anonymous lives. Cost-effectiveness analysis recommends road safety; the rule of rescue licenses prioritizing the identified patient. Now vary the case in one respect: the authority knows at the start of the year that twelve such patients will present, one each month. At each presentation it must decide irrevocably whether to spend $10 million on that patient or leave the money for road safety; a patient who is refused dies. Give a precise decision rule for (a) a committed QALY-maximizer who treats each saved life as equally valuable, (b) a pure rule-of-rescue agent who saves each identified patient who presents for as long as the budget allows, and (c) a pluralist who assigns substantial but defeasible weight to identified lives over statistical lives. Does the sequential variant show that identifiability was doing genuine justificatory work in the original one-shot case, or that the one-shot case was under-described? Which position survives the variant with the least special pleading?
