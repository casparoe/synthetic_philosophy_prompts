Right, before I teach the Hart–Fuller debate again I want to stop having the same argument with myself about what an account of legal validity is even supposed to do. So here's what I'd like you to do rather than summarize the debate: write the desiderata for an adequate theory of legal validity — what any view has to get right about what makes a norm a law as opposed to a moral command, a social custom, or a standing threat backed by force. For each requirement, give the case that forces it and say why it's a genuine constraint rather than just one side's table-thumping.

Work through at least these cases, and add others if they force new requirements:

(a) The old problem where a later court has to decide whether to treat an odious but procedurally impeccable statute from a prior regime as law — the grudge-informer sort of case.

(b) A statute that satisfies every written constitutional requirement but has been consistently ignored by officials for forty years, with an informal convention among judges that it just won't be applied. Is it still law, and if so, in what sense?

(c) A legal system whose highest validity test explicitly instructs courts to strike down legislation the court finds grossly unjust — or that violates some abstract constitutional guarantee like dignity or equal protection. Is morality now a criterion of validity, or merely a license for judicial discretion within a still-positivist framework? What feature of the system decides that?

(d) A system where the actual test for legal validity is secret: officials apply it, but nobody outside a small judicial committee can state it. Is public knowability a requirement on validity, or on something else, and does it matter which one it is?

Then tell me which single requirement most cleanly seperates a Hart-style positivist from a Fuller-style natural lawyer, and whether any pair of requirements on your list can't be jointly satisfied. Score the two camps against the list only briefly — the list itself is the deliverable. And flag the one requirement a positivist is most tempted to declare negotiable, and whether letting them negotiate that away breaks anything else on the list.
