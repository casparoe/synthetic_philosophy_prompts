I am taking an aesthetics seminar with a unit on games as art, and I keep noticing something in my own play that I cannot find a term for, though it comes up obliquely in discussion. Two examples will make it clearer than a definition. In a tactical role-playing game, I reached a position where two moves were almost exactly equal in their odds of success, and the real difference was that each would push me into a different temperament over the next several turns: one patient and reactive, the other aggressive and brittle. I saved the game and spent some time not deciding, but attending to the choice itself—the way the two options had been built to be equally viable yet expressively opposed, so that each one made the other legible as an alternative. In a dialogue-heavy game, two available lines led to the same event but gave the scene different emotional framings, one casting it as a betrayal and the other as a disappointment; the game seemed to be presenting two readings of one incident simultaneously, and the interesting object was the fork between them rather than either branch.

This is not the pleasure of a difficult decision, since the appeal was not in the choosing or in what the choice revealed about me; it was in the architecture of the possible actions themselves. It also strikes me as narrower than what my professor calls, broadly, the aesthetics of agency, which seems to cover the experienced quality of acting under a game's constraints. The thing I am trying to isolate concerns the possibility space considered almost statically, apart from which option I take and even apart from whether the options change the outcome. Nor is it simply good design or balance, because the beauty seemed to live in how the alternatives defined each other, not in their equilibrium or their consequences.

My question is whether this narrower phenomenon already has a name in the literature on games and aesthetics, and if it does, where its boundaries lie. If it does not, I would like a term proposed with a definition precise enough to sort cases: when does a mere menu of choices become an instance of this, and when is it just a well-tuned decision? How should it be distinguished from the broader aesthetics of agency and from plain evaluative language about good game design? I am not satisfied with a bare label; I need the carving, the edge cases, and the contrast classes. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence—closest established concept and how well it fits, or a verdict that nothing established fits—then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
