I teach a seminar on language and social action, and this term I had to sit on a university panel in which a student argued that a threatening message he sent could not have been a threat because he "meant it only as a joke." The hearing forced me to admit that I do not actually have a worked-out view of when someone's words count as performing an act of a certain kind, rather than merely meaning something. Since then I have been accumulating cases and my own immediate verdicts on them, and I have reached the uncomfortable point where I can no longer state the principle that generates my verdicts; I suspect at least one of them must go. Here are the cases. (1) A wedding is performed by an officiant whose license had lapsed a week earlier; the couple, the officiant, and all the guests believe everything is valid, the vows are exchanged, the papers are signed. My verdict: the couple is simply not married — a background institutional condition was missing, even though no one knew it. (2) I borrow money from a colleague and say, plainly and seriously, "I promise to repay you by Friday," though I have already decided not to pay. My verdict: I did promise; my colleague can rightly say I broke a promise, not that no promise was made. (3) At a farewell party a colleague says with theatrical pomp, while everyone is laughing, "I solemnly promise to buy drinks for all of you next week." My verdict: no promise was made; no one could later collect. (4) I whisper to my sleeping partner, "I promise I'll take you to the airport tomorrow." My verdict: I did not promise, because a promise must be received and my partner could not receive it. (5) A pedestrian sees a cyclist approaching a pothole and shouts, "Look out!" but the cyclist is wearing noise-canceling headphones and hears nothing. My verdict: the pedestrian still warned the cyclist; the warning took place even though it did no good. (6) A company's automated customer-service system sends an email reading, "We sincerely apologize for the inconvenience." My verdict: no apology has taken place, though something apology-like was displayed. (7) A department chair says to an assistant, "Would you mind closing the window?" in a context where both know the assistant cannot decline. My verdict: the chair ordered the assistant to close the window, despite the interrogative form. I am most uneasy about the pair (4) and (5), which look hard to reconcile under any single uptake requirement, and about (2) and (3), which differ despite both speakers lacking something. My provisional principle was something like: the words perform act A when the speaker's intention to perform A could have been recognized by an addressee in the circumstances. But I already see that this principle says the couple in (1) is married and that the chair in (7) merely asked a question, so it cannot be right as stated. What I would like from you is the best account you can give of these verdicts: one principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely, with the case or clause that earns each element of it; an honest statement of which of my seven verdicts it cannot accommodate and why; and how the winning principle applies to at least one further case of your own construction that you expect to strain it.
