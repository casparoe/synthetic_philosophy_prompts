I'm putting together a handout for a third-year politics seminar on the boundary problem — the question of who should actually count as part of 'the people' when a democracy claims to govern in their name. I want a decision tree or flowchart that takes a reader from their gut answers about a set of hard cases to a named (or at least labelled) position on membership in the demos.

The angle I care about: the first split should be about what does the grounding work — being affected by decisions, being subject to the laws/coercion, or contributing to the joint enterprise — and then the tree should push hard on cases where those three come apart, because that's where I think students actually learn it. The leaves that have to show up are roughly: all-affected interests (in a version that's willing to include future people), all-subjected (with its usual 'the law applies to you' test), a contribution/stakeholder view, a 'who decides the boundary is itself a democratic decision, all the way down' view, and a demos-as-a-done-deal view where membership is just a historically given community. If the logical space throws up another leaf you can't name because nobody defends it, say so and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

The branch questions need to be things a student can answer without already knowing which position they're being sorted into — concrete cases or claims they actually have an intuition about, never 'do you accept the all-subjected principle?'. Cases I'd like used (you don't have to use all of them, but they're the right sort): a non-citizen who's lived and worked here for twenty years, a citizen who left thirty years ago with no remaining ties, a sixteen-year-old who pays tax, a future person who'll be bound by a climate law, a long-term resident with advanced dementia, and someone abroad whose livelihood swings on our trade policy. Also flag any two leaves the tree can't genuinely pull apart — I dont want students thinking they've got a clean diagnosis when the positions overlap on every case the tree can pose.

Aim for about two pages, maybe 12–15 nodes, and keep it readable for people who've never seen the phrase 'boundary problem' before, so don't lean on the literature; the diagnostics have to do the work.
