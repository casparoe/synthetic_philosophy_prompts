I keep going back and forth on whether the inflection/derivation split actually carves morphology at a real joint, or whether it's just a cluster of tendencies that linguists reified because it makes grammars easier to write. By a real joint I mean: a good theory of morphology will do better treating the split as categorical (with some messy edges) than treating morphology as one space where inflectional and derivational are just two ends of a bunch of independent scales. Give me your actual credence in that claim — a number, not "it depends" — and decompose the reasoning. I want terms at least for: (a) whether the classic criteria (obligatoriness, productivity, category change, semantic transparency, position relative to the root) genuinely cluster, instead of coming apart once you look at language after language; (b) whether the notorious borderline cases — English -ing, participles read as adjectives, diminutives, the English possessive marker that attaches to a whole phrase — are edge phenomena or the general case; (c) granting the split is real in languages we know, whether it's likely to be universal or at least a robust cross-linguistic tendency, rather than parochial to certain families. Give a rough probability for each term with a one-line justification, say how you're combining them, then tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to and what evidence (cross-linguistic data, a formal result, a psycholinguistic finding) would move it most.
