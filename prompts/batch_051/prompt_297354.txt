I run a forecasting team at a regional grocery chain. Every couple of weeks we have to pick between predictive models for demand, waste, and store staffing — gradient boosting, ARIMA, a deep net a vendor swears by, stacked ensembles, whatever. Stakes are real but not existential: a bad model costs us maybe tens of thousnds in spoilage or mistimed labor, and our supply-chain director signs off on each pick. Nobody on the team, me included, has any formal epistemology background. I keep seeing claims float around about no-free-lunch theorems and the problem of induction, but I can't turn them into something we actually do on a Tuesday afternoon. So: write me a reusable decision procedure — a checklist or a flowchart in words — for choosing among candidate predictive models, grounded in what those theorems and the problem of induction actually commit you to. It has to be something we can run in about three hours with the materials we have. For each step, say what question we're asking, what counts as a good enough answer to move on, where the procedure stops with a 'no,' and where it has to hand the decision to a human because it's genuinely a judgment call. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) an off-the-shelf deep net that beat our linear model on last quarter's holdout for produce demand; (2) a 400-parameter ensemble that slightly beats a six-parameter model with domain-informed features in backtesting; (3) a vendor model whose assumptions we can't inspect. I especially want you to be honest about the places where 'it won on the holdout' is itself just an inductive leap we're making. And if the procedure tells us to pick the boring model we don't want to pick, don't soften it. Close by saying where the whole procedure will misfire or go quiet even though a decision still has to get made.
