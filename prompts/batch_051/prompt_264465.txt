Here's a prompt I keep reusing in my intro course and I'd like to know how far it actually still works. In Libet's version, subjects watch a rapidly rotating clock, decide to flex their wrist whenever they feel like it, and later report where the clock hand was when they first felt the urge. EEG records a readiness potential, and it starts rising maybe 300 to 500 milliseconds before the reported urge time, so the standard reading is that the brain has already begun the movement before the person becomes conscious of deciding. Libet himself softened this into a story about consciousness having a veto over already-begun actions, but plenty of people took the stronger lesson: the conscious will is a late-arriving spectator.

Now suppose a more recent development: the readiness potential may be an artifact of they way spontaneous movements are selected. On this model, the relevant brain activity is slow noise drifting around, and a movement fires whenever the noise happens to cross a threshold. From the outside you then see a slow negative drift in the averaged EEG leading up to the movement, but the drift is not a pre-conscious decision; it is just what threshold crossing looks like when you line the trials up backwards from movement onset. The reported time of the urge might itself be the brain's best post hoc guess about a clock position, not a readout of an internal decision moment. Does Libet's argument survive that changed assumption? Which premise actually takes the hit, and is there a repaired version that still says something worth worrying about, or does the whole thing collapse into a point about noisy brains making up their minds?
