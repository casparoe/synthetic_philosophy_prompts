I keep returning to the procreation asymmetry, in roughly Benatar's form, because it does several things at once with one principle. It lets me say that the fact a possible child would live a life dominated by suffering is a weighty reason not to bring her into existence, while denying that the fact a possible child would live a life of joy is a parallel reason to bring her into existence. If pleasures, when present, count in favor of a life; pains, when present, count against it; the absence of pain in an unrealized life counts in favor of non-existence; and the absence of pleasure in an unrealized life does not count against non-existence, then I get a person-affecting ethics of procreation that never treats each merely happy possible person as a missed benefit, and I am not pushed toward the conclusion that we are obligated to keep adding lives until the next one is only just worth living. That economy is the main reason I find the view compelling.

The problem is that the asymmetry will not stay confined to the creation-versus-non-existence comparison where it is most at home. I was thinking about a same-number, different-people case: a couple undergoing IVF must choose exactly one of three embryos to implant, and all non-welfare consequences are equal. Embryo A would develop into a child with a rich, flourishing life; embryo C into a child with a merely tolerable life; embryo B into a child with a life of severe, sustained pain. I have no doubt that choosing B is wrong, and I have no doubt that choosing A is at least permissible. But I also cannot bring myself to regard the choice between A and C as indifferent. It seems to me that A's additional prospective good is doing real justificatory work: choosing A over C is better, and it is better because of the quality of the life A would have, not merely because A is farther from B. The asymmetry, read as a general theory of how the welfare of possible people generates reasons, denies this. It counts B's future pain as a reason against choosing B and gives no corresponding role to A's future joy as a reason for choosing A. According to it, once B is off the table, A and C are on a par.

I want to know whether the theory or the intuition should yield, or whether there is a way to keep both. Give me the strongest version of each position. What would justify concluding that the A-versus-C intuition is an artifact of the contrast with B — a salience or contrast effect to be explained away rather than honored? What would justify instead holding that the asymmetry is a truth only about the comparison between existence and non-existence, and that choices among possible lives must be governed by a different principle which the asymmetry has to be supplemented by? Is there a reading of 'better' or of 'a reason' on which both the asymmetry and the intuition survive? And if the asymmetry were revised so that A's future joy counts in selection cases, say explicitly how the revised view would treat the original one-embryo-or-none case that the asymmetry was introduced to handle.
