I've been trying to work out what makes an oath bind beyond an ordinary promise, and my gut keeps giving different answers on different cases. I'd like you to find the principle behind my judgments, or tell me which judgments to abandon.

Here are my verdicts.

1. An atheist witness in court swears on the Bible to tell the truth, then lies to protect a friend. I think this is fully perjury; that the Bible means nothing to her doesn't weaken the oath.
2. A friend says, "I swear on my mother's grave I'll pay you back," though his mother is alive and he doesn't mean it, then never pays. I think the swearing adds nothing; he broke an ordinary promise, and the oath was theater.
3. A teenager swears to his dead grandmother, alone in his room, that he'll finish the novel she wanted him to write. No one else knows. A year later he gives up. I think he broke something real, but a private thing: a failure of self, not a betrayal of another person.
4. A woman swears a loyalty oath to a group after being threatened with being cut off from her family if she refuses. I don't think the oath binds her at all.
5. A mayor takes the oath of office with her fingers crossed and the private thought that it doesn't count, then later violates it. I think the private reservation changes nothing; the oath binds fully.
6. A soldier who swore to obey orders is ordered to do something he regards as a war crime and refuses. I think he does right; an oath can't bind someone to do wrong.
7. A gambler who doesn't believe in the Greek gods swears by the river Styx that he'll pay a debt, then doesn't. I think the Styx adds nothing, but he's still bound to pay.
8. In the old stories, the gods themselves swear by the Styx, and a god who breaks such an oath is punished. I accept within the story that the oath really binds the god, and that the god's private skepticism wouldn't matter.

Cases 1 and 2 feel different to me though they look alike, and 7 and 8 seem to pull in opposite directions about whether the thing sworn by has to be real. I can't say why in either case.

I also keep going back and forth on this one: a parent swears to their young child, "I swear on my own life I'll be at your play," then misses it because something merely inconvenient came up. Is that a serious wrong, or just a small promise broken in a cruel way?

Is there one principle that delivers these verdicts, or close to them? Please state it precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and whether I should overturn them, and say what it predicts for the parent and child. I want a committed answer: pick a definite position and defend it, rather than laying out the options for me to choose from.

Return your answer as a JSON object with these fields:

- "candidates": the main alternatives you considered, each with a one-sentence statement and the case that most damages it.
- "verdicts": an object mapping each case ("1" through "8", plus "further") to your principle's verdict, with "matches_mine": true or false for each.
- "overturned": a list of my verdicts you think I should abandon, each with a reason.
- "tension": the hardest pair of cases for your principle and how you resolve it.
