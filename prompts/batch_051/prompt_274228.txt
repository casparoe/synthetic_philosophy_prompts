I am a philosopher of mind, and last term a distributed-systems engineer sat in on my graduate seminar on the unity of consciousness. During the split-brain sessions he kept insisting that the debate was, in his terms, straightforwardly a partitioning problem: the corpus callosum is the interconnect, each hemisphere is a node with local state, complete commissurotomy is a permanent network partition, and the classical dispute over whether the patient is one subject or two is just the familiar question of whether one has a single cluster with degraded consistency or two independent systems. I found the analogue suggestive and probably too glib, and I want a disciplined version of it.

I am fluent in the philosophical literature but only a careful reader of distributed-systems material, so write with the precision an engineer would demand rather than popularizing. Restate each of the main positions in the unity-of-consciousness debate as it bears on split-brain cases in the vocabulary of distributed systems: nodes and processes, shared state and message passing, network partitions, split-brain, quorum and majority, leader election, fencing, replication, conflict resolution, and consistency models. The positions I have in mind are roughly these: the patient remains one subject with severely disunified consciousness; each functioning hemisphere is a separate subject of consciousness, at least under testing; there is no determinate fact about how many subjects there are; and the number of subjects depends on the degree and kind of functional integration rather than on an all-or-nothing split. Keep the translation honest: do not import distributed-systems assumptions and then call them translation.

Then give me the residue report. For each position, say what survives cleanly, what had to be distorted to fit, and what has no expression in the target vocabulary at all, in particular the first-person or phenomenal aspects of consciousness and anything about personhood and responsibility. End by telling me whether the contrast between one cluster with degraded consistency and two independent systems is a faithful rendering of the one-subject versus two-subjects contrast, and whether the residue is a defect of the distributed-systems vocabulary or a sign that the philosophical debate was less precise than it looked.
