At a reading group last month we were working through the chapter on taxation in Mill’s Principles of Political Economy, and two people got into it about one passage. Mill says, almost in passing, that inheritances and legacies over a certain amount are highly proper objects of taxation; that the revenue from them should be as large as it can be made without prompting evasion through gifts made during life or concealment of property; and that graduation—taxing larger amounts at higher rates—is just and expedient for inheritance duties, even though he thinks graduation in general taxation is objectionable.

Diane said this shows Mill is actually more radical than people give him credit for. His real target is unearned advantage: someone who works and saves may deserve their wealth, but the heir didn't do anything. So a heavy inheritance tax isn't punishment, it's a way of making the next generation start more nearly equal. The proviso about evasion is just him being a practical legislator, not a utopian.

Marcus said that's a face-saving reading. Mill explicitly says the tax should stop at the point where it would create evasion through gifts inter vivos. But a gift to your kid at 25 equalizes starting points exactly as much—or as little—as a bequest at 45. If the principle is equal opportunity, then the tax should follow the transfer, not the event of death. Leaving lifetime gifts untouched is a giant loophole, and Mill names it himself.

Diane came back that Marcus was treating a limit of administration as a moral limit. There's a continuum from ordinary parental support—paying for piano lessons, a car, help with a first apartment—to disguised estate planning. Any system has to draw a line somewhere based on what's feasible to observe and enforce. Death is public and verifiable, so an inheritance tax is much harder to evade than a general gift tax. Mill is saying: given that line, get as much revenue as you can before evasion actually becomes widespread.

Marcus said that just moves the goalposts. If ordinary parental support is fine, then the original problem was never inheritance as such, but the size of the advantage. And if a parent can transfer a fortune during life, the recipient's starting point is already fixed before any death tax arrives. So Mill's proviso doesn't just qualify his principle—it reveals that the principle can't really be equality of opportunity, because he's willing to tolerate massive unequal starting points whenever equality would be inconvenient to enforce.

Diane replied that this is demanding a purity no actual policy can have. Mill's goal can be to reduce dynastic transfer while still accepting that the state cannot perfectly police the family. A second-best tax is not the same as no principle at all.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Please explain who's right about what at each step, and say who is more right overall.
