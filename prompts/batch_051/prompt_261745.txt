I've been reading around in philosophy of language and there's a debate I keep coming back to that seems completly stuck. The Millian side says the semantic content of a name just is its referent, so an empty name like "Sherlock Holmes" contributes nothing to the proposition expressed; sentences like "Sherlock Holmes lives on Baker Street" come out false, or maybe truth-valueless. The other side says that can't be right, since we all know that in the stories Holmes lives on Baker Street, we say "Holmes does not exist" and mean something true, and it seems absurd to say we've been talking about nothing when we argue about whether Holmes was a detective. There are fixes on offer — Fregean senses, pretense theory, treating fictional characters as abstract artifacts — but every argument has been on the table for decades and nobody changes there mind. My running hypothesis is that this isn't one debate at all: the Millians are doing semantics for ordinary assertions, while much of the opposition is doing the logic of fiction, and the phrase "true in the fiction" is being used to paper over the difference. I'm less interested in who's right about "Holmes" than in what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is that diagnosis right? If it is, what exactly are the two questions, and does the reframing actually dissolve the dispute or just relocate it to something like "what does 'according to the stories' mean"? And if there's a real disagreement left over under there, what would count as progress on it?
