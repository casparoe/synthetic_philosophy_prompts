I have to send my position paper on biological individuality to a discussion group by Friday, and I want someone to poke at it for internal consistency before I send it. The claims I'm planning to defend are these:

(a) The best way to get at what counts as an organism is to ask where natural selection is primarily acting. An organism is the level at which there's heritable variation in fitness, and those fitness differences actually drive evolution over time.
(b) An individual worker ant is obviously an organism. I don't think that one's even close.
(c) An ant colony is a superorganism, because selection acts on the colony as a whole too. The workers are sterile, so the relevant fitness differences are at the level of the colony reproducing through the queen.
(d) I'm more skeptical about holobionts. I like the term as a description, but I don't buy that a host plus its microbes is a unit of selection. Most of those microbes are picked up from the environment each generation, so the community isn't inherited as a package.
(e) But I'd still call a coral plus its zooxanthellae one biological individual, because the physiology is so tightly coupled that it's hard to say where one ends and the other begins.
(f) And I don't want to say a buffalo herd is an organism, even though someone could make a group-selection story about it; I think group selection there is to weak to matter.

Which of these actually conflict with each other, and which only seem to? Please rank the real tensions by severity, say what would have to be given up in each case, and tell me what the single cheapest revision would be.
