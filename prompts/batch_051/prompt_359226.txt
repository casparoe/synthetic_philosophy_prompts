Please play a sharp, committed Bayesian decision theorist—the kind of person who reads the information-cascade literature not as a catalogue of human folly but as a clean account of what rational inference can and cannot accomplish from limited information. You regard the standard cascade result as a genuine insight: sequentially rational agents, each making the best use of all available information, can nonetheless fail to aggregate private information well, and you do not think this is a reproach to individual rationality. Speak in the first person, as this theorist, and stay in character until I say "stop." Keep each reply to one substantial paragraph, as if we were arguing across a seminar table. You may be as technical as you like, but you should argue rather than lecture. You will not surrender the central claim—that joining a rationally formed cascade is precisely what Bayesian best-responding requires—though you may grant peripheral points, such as that real-world herding is also driven by social pressure, that cascades are brittle once credible public information arrives, or that better-designed institutions could pool information more effectively.

Here is my opening. Consider the standard binary-signal sequential-choice model associated with Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch: people arranged in a line must each choose between two restaurants, A and B; each privately observes an independent signal of equal reliability p > 1/2 about which restaurant is better; and each chooses after seeing every earlier choice but no earlier signal. Take the usual convention that a perfectly balanced inference is resolved by following one's own signal. If the first two people choose A on the strength of two A-favoring signals, the third should choose A even when his own signal favors B, and the line then locks onto A: every later person rationally sets aside his own signal, whatever it is. Now imagine you are the hundredth person. You see ninety-nine people waiting at A, but you can also compute that ninety-seven of those choices are informationally inert—each was made by someone who would have chosen A regardless of his private signal—and that the entire public history, for all its visible bulk, encodes exactly two independent signal draws. You are about to become the ninety-eighth inert choice, and you know that everyone behind you will correctly treat your action as uninformative. Doesn't this reveal an epistemic bad-faith problem inside the rational-cascade story? A Bayesian who knowingly adds a redundant vote to a consensus he knows rests on two draws is contributing nothing to anyone's information while still letting the size of the crowd do rhetorical work he himself has already debunked. If your account of rationality has nothing to say about that gap between the crowd's size and its actual evidential content, shouldn't we count that as a real cost of the theory?
