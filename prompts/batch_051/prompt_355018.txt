You're a philosopher of language who works on indexicals, and I want you to think carefully — maybe sketch the possible positions before committing. In my own course I teach the indexical/non-indexical line this way. A meaningful unit counts as an indexical iff it meets four conditions:

(a) its referent shifts with the context of utterance;
(b) its standing meaning is just a rule mapping a feature of the context onto a referent — the Kaplan-style character/content story, where the character stays constant and the content is whatever the rule picks out;
(c) the rule is token-reflexive, meaning it has to mention the utterance token itself or some event it's part of, rather than merely describing the situation from outside;
(d) you can't swap it for a non-indexical definite description without losing its role in action and inference — the old Perry point that the thought expressed first-personally is what gets you running, while the same thought expressed as a description of the person who happens to be you may not move you at all.

Now consider a language, Brel, spoken on a small island. Brel has no personal pronouns at all, and no demonstrative words either: no I, no you, no this or that in the lexicon. It has plenty of proper names, common nouns, and descriptions. Instead, it has an obligatory manual channel: whenever speakers refer to anyone present, including themsevles, they point at the person while speaking, and everyone treats an utterance as referentially unresolved without some suitable indication — a point, or direct gaze at the addressee when the hands are full. The community insists the points are not part of the language. They describe them as just showing who, not as words; children aren't drilled on points the way they are on vocabulary, and the points are never written down.

Does Brel have indexicals? Go through my four criteria honestly. If your answer is something like depends on whether the pointing channel is part of the language, then say whether the criteria themselves pull it in or leave it out, and whether the language/gesture boundary is doing more work than it should. I'm especially interested in whether this case shows that one of the four conditions is misdescribed — for instance, whether (b) quietly requires the rule to be lexically encoded, and whether that requirement is really part of indexicality or just a fact about the languages we're used to.

Don't give me a survey of existing views or a reading list; I want your own run at it. Where the criteria undertermine the verdict, say so and say how confident you are in each subjudgment. If you think the premise is confused — that the whole question of whether a language lacks indexicals is ill-posed, or that some of my conditions are doubles — tell me that directly instead of humoring me.
