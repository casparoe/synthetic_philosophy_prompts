Retired geologist here, mostly I study moraines, but lately I've fallen pretty hard into philosophy of interpretation — statutory construction, original intent, all that. Our town has a genuinely interesting mess over a statue and I want your best analysis, not just a vibe.

The town's Public Monuments Policy, passed in 2014 after an ugly fight about a Confederate memorial, contains this clause:

"No monument or street name commemorating a person shall be removed, relocated, or renamed unless the Council finds in writing that either: (a) the commemorated person's public conduct was, in a central and enduring way, directed toward the subordination of a group on the basis of race, religion, or national origin; or (b) the continued display of the commemoration causes substantial and ongoing distress to a group of town residents, such that a reasonable resident could not regard the display as one the town merely tolerates rather than endorses."

The current fight is not about the Confederate memorial. It's about a statue of a nineteenth-century botonist in the town park. Everyone agrees she was, by the standards of the clause, blameless — her journals are ordinary taxonomy and plant collecting, no subordination of anyone, and nobody argues otherwise. What has happened is that, for the past three years, a far-right group with no local members has held a torchlight march around her statue every autumn. Photos from the event show up in their recruitment material, and the statue has become shorthand for them online. Residents now say they cross to the other side of the park to avoid it, and the town is spending a lot on barriers and police overtime each year just to keep the march contained. But the harm is coming from what the group does at the site, not from the woman's public life. The drafters, as the 2014 fight shows, were clearly thinking about monuments to people with a record of racial subordination, and about the pain of seeing such a figure honored. A blameless botanist whose plinth happens to get adopted as a stage by people the town detests is not a case they imagined.

So my question is: on the words of the clause, does condition (b) reach this case or not? Is the distress "caused" by the continued display, or by the marchers, with the display as mere occasion? Work through what a textualist, a purposivist, someone asking what the 2014 drafters would have said if asked, and a reasonable-reader approach would each say, and tell me which approach ought to win and why. If (b) doesn't reach it, can you state a principled amendment that would allow removing this statue without making every monument hostage to anyone mean enough to hold an ugly rally next to it?

Hard limit: 700 words, no more. I'd rather have the arguments compressed than padded.
