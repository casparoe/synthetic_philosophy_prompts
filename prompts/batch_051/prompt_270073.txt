Write a dialogue between two philosophers of science arguing about whether the expression 'closer to the truth' can be given a principled meaning. One of them, A, defends the Popperian content view: a theory is more truthlike to the degree that it entails lots of true claims and few false claims, where a theory's content is just its deductive consequences. The other, B, defends a similarity view: truthlikeness is distance from the actual world in a space of possible worlds, so two theories can be compared even when both are false. Make them equally sharp, with no strawmen, and give each one the best version of their usual moves. B should press A on the old language-dependence worry (a verdict shouldn't flip just because you rewrite the theory in a different language) and on the problem that, on a strict consequence reading, false theories don't trade truth content against falsity content the way A needs them to. A should push back hard, not cave, and should argue that B's account only gets its neat comparisons by smuggling in a metric over possible worlds—a choice about which ways worlds can differ matter more then others—that isn't given by the physics. At one point B should use a concrete toy astronomy example: one theory gets the shapes of the orbits right but the periods wrong, and a rival gets the periods right but the shapes wrong. At the end, let each character admit, concretely, one comparison the other person's framework handles better than their own.
