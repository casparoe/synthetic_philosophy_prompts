I run a medical-ethics reading group, and two of my regulars have been fighting for a year about what death actually is. One holds the whole-brain view: a human being is dead when all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem, have irreversibly ceased, so a brain-dead body on a ventilator is a warm corpse being artificially ventilated. The other holds the circulatory-respiratory view: a human being is dead only when circulation and respiration have irreversibly stopped, and "brain death" is a legal fiction we adopted so we could take organs from a body that is, by the real criterion, still alive. They've agreed to do an adversarial collaboration, and I'm supposed to draft the document.

Write the joint statement they should produce. I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both can actually sign, not just "death is important"; (2) the exact remaining disagreements in neutral wording, with each side able to accept the formulation of its own view; (3) a table of cruxes, where each crux is a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of them, and for each crux a concrete case or piece of evidence they both agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction. If part of their dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why.

Two cruxes I already suspect, though you can add or replace them: whether the unity or integration of a living human organism is a function of the brain or of the circulation, and whether "irreversible" should be judged relative to currently available techniques or to a stronger standard. Test the cruxes against at least these cases: someone awake and talking on ECMO with no native heartbeat; a brain-dead woman kept on a ventilator to bring a pregnancy to term; and a person with a continuous-flow left ventricular assist device who has no pulse but is conscious.

And seperate what's settled (clinically, conceptually, or legally) from what's genuinely contested among people who know this literature; don't present a contested view as the consensus.
