For a working group on moral psychology and political disagreement, I've been asked to write down my actual commitments so the group can probe whether they fit together. Here is the profile I produced.

1. Moral Foundations Theory is roughly right as a descriptive account: moral judgment is driven by a small set of innate, domain-specific intuitions — care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and sanctity/degradation — with culture and individual experience setting how heavily each one is weighted.

2. The main difference between self-described liberals and conservatives is a difference in weighting: liberals lean mostly on care and fairness, whereas conservatives draw on all five foundations much more evenly.

3. This descriptive asymmetry is by itself normatively inert. Facts about which intuitions move which people settle nothing, without further premises, about who is morally correct.

4. Normatively, I keep coming back to the view that moral progress consists substantially in widening the circle of moral concern to all sentient beings and reducing their suffering; in that respect I take the liberal emphasis to be closer to correct than the conservative one.

5. But I also believe that loyalty, authority, and sanctity often track genuine goods — stable communities, traditions worth transmitting, protections against degradation — that a purely care-and-fairness morality would miss or underweight.

6. The cross-cultural evidence that Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic participants are outliers in how they weight these foundations seems to me a strong reason not to treat that kind of psychology as the standard for human morality as such.

7. I also accept a social-intuitionist picture: most of what we call moral reasoning is post hoc rationalization of fast intuitive judgments, which is why moral argument so rarely changes minds.

Take the list as a whole. Tell me which pairs of commitments pull against each other and which pairs only seem to; rank the genuine tensions from most to least severe, and for each say what has to give and what the cheapest repair would be. Then identify the single change across the profile that would do the most to make it cohere. I don't need each item defended one by one; I need to know where the set actually breaks.
