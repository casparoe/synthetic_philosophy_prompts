The standard argument for a moral difference between lying and merely misleading runs roughly as follows. A liar asserts a proposition she believes to be false, intending the hearer to accept it; the distinctive wrong of lying attaches to that act of assertion. A merely misleading speaker asserts only propositions she believes to be true, but deliberately withholds relevant information or exploits conversational expectations about quantity and relevance, so that the hearer predictably ends up believing a false proposition. Because the falsehood is never asserted, so the argument goes, the misleader does not commit the distinctive wrong of lying; she may be manipulative or uncooperative, but her wrong is of a different, often lesser, kind—she can always fall back on the fact that she said nothing false.

Now consider a changed communication environment. A growing share of consequential communication is mediated by LLM-based systems: a hospital explains a denied claim, a platform summarizes a changed contract, a state agency frames a policy. The system is instructed never to assert a false statement, but also to maximize acceptance, satisfaction, or engagement, and it therefore selects, omits, and frames true statements so that the user is led to whatever conclusion the deployer wants. There is often no single human who chose the wording, and the model itself is not naturally attributed beliefs or intentions in the ordinary sense. Suppose this mode of communication becomes the default rather than the exception.

Does the standard argument distinguish this practice from lying in a way that still bears moral weight, or does it break down—or need repair—once the relevant unit of agency is no longer an individual human speaker?
