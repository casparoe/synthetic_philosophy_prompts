I've been trying to pin down what "competence without comprehension" actually means, and a line from Turing keeps bugging me. In *Computing Machinery and Intelligence* he writes: "We do not wish to penalise the machine for its inability to shine in beauty competitions, nor to penalise a man for losing in a race against an aeroplane." That sounds like he's offering a criterion: don't test for the wrong substrate, test the relevant capacities. So I made a list of criteria for genuine comprehension of a domain: (1) can answer novel counterfactual questions, (2) can say which evidence would make the domain's central claims false, (3) can transfer the domain's distinctions to a new case, (4) can notice and flag the edge cases where its own competence breaks down. Now here's the borderline case: an AI trained only on legal text and case law that passes (1)–(4) on questions about hearsay exceptions, but has never been a party to a dispute, never had to make a ruling with consequences, and has no model of what it is like to be liable. Is it comprehending hearsay, or just competent? Go through each criterion and say where it underdetermines the verdict, and whether my list needs an extra criterion about the system caring about being wrong or having something at stake. Also: does Turing's passage support reading him as saying the body and stakes are irrelevant, or is he only setting aside the physical stuff and leaving comprehension undefined?
