In my criminal law class we keep insisting that mens rea is what seperates a crime from an accident, and that it's monstrous to punish someone who did everything a reasonable person could have done. Then we get to strict liability and the same system seems perfectly comfortable convicting people with no guilty state at all — the bartender who checked an ID so good the cops said no one could spot it, the 19-year-old who genuinely and reasonably believed his partner was 18, the factory official over a discharge he had been assured was compliant. And nobody in the room blinks. I keep going back and forth on whether the rule against punishing without fault is actually our principle, or just a default we quietly drop whenever proving mens rea would be too costly. Is there a defensible line between the strict-liability offenses we should keep and the ones that just punish bad luck? If you defend the practice, you have to say why we shouldn't make every careful mistake a strict-liability crime; if you attack it, you have to say why the fake-ID case doesn't still bother you less than the assured-the-discharge-was-compliant case, when both are faultless in the same lay sense. What buys the difference, if anything?
