In constitutional theory, a familiar argument defends entrenched constitutions and judicial review by appealing to the idea of a Ulysses contract. Ulysses, expecting to be overwhelmed by the Sirens' song, orders his crew to bind him to the mast and to ignore his later commands until the ship is safely past; his later, temporarily deranged self is thereby prevented from acting on a passion he knows in advance would be ruinous. The constitution, on this analogy, is a society's way of binding itself before temptation arrives: a founding moment, presumed more sober than ordinary politics, entrenches rights and procedures, and courts are assigned the task of holding later majorities to that earlier decision even when they insist on being untied. The intended conclusion is that judicial review is not undemocratic but a form of democratic self-government extended across time. I suspect the analogy is doing more of the argumentative work than it lets on, and I want it audited systematically. First, give the correspondence table, specifying what is supposed to map onto Ulysses, the ropes, the mast, the crew, the Sirens' song, and the later pleas to be released. Second, identify where the mapping breaks down, and separate the disanalogies that are fatal for the intended use from those that are merely ornamental. In particular, it is worth asking whether the people at the founding moment are the same agent as the people bound later; whether the crew is bound or merely instructed; whether anyone is authorized to untie the ropes, and under what conditions; and whether the earlier decision is reliably more rational than the later one it overrides. Third, state whether the conclusion the analogy is typically used to support — that enforcing constitutional limits against current majorities is legitimately democratic — survives the audit, or whether the analogy was carrying it all along.
