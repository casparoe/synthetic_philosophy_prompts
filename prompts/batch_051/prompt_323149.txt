I'm a high school student who writes a fair amount of code and knows git reasonably well — branches, merges, commits, authorship, blame, remotes, and so on. I recently read about a debate in the philosophy of science and I'd like to think it through using the vocabulary I actually have.

The debate concerns whether scientific discoveries are, in some important sense, inevitable. The standard evidence is that major discoveries have often been made more than once, independently and almost simultaneously: Newton and Leibniz both developed calculus in the late seventeenth century, and Darwin and Wallace both arrived at natural selection in the mid-nineteenth century. From this, some people conclude that individual discoverers matter less than we normally suppose: by the time a field has accumulated certain prior results, tools, and unanswered questions, the next move is nearly determined, and if one person had not made it, someone else would soon have. Others resist this conclusion: they hold that simultaneous discovery does not cancel what particular individuals contributed, and that priority — who got there first and how — still matters both for historical understanding and for credit.

Please act as a professional philosopher of science who also genuinely understands version control, and restate this debate in the vocabulary of git. Map researchers to developers, discoveries to commits, simultaneous independent discovery to two branches independently making equivalent commits, and the ripeness or inevitability idea to something about the state of a repository making certain commits natural or nearly forced. Be systematic and keep the translation honest: do not stretch terms until they quietly decide the debate for you.

Then give a residue report. Which claims on each side translate cleanly into git's model of concurrent work and authorship? Which translate only with distortion? Which have no counterpart in version control at all, and is that because the git vocabulary is too thin, or because those parts of the debate were doing work that facts about who committed what and when cannot capture? Wherever possible, connect the residue to what each side actually wants to claim, rather than to a straw version of it. I'd like you to begin by brainstorming several possible translations before settling on the ones you use, and to think carefully about the difference between a metaphor that illuminates an argument and a metaphor that replaces it.
