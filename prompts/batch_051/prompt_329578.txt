For some years now I have followed the dispute in applied animal ethics, and in the adjacent effective-altruist literature, over the so-called least-harm diet, and I am struck by how little it moves. The question is not whether animal suffering matters — all parties agree that unnecessary animal death and suffering are bad — but which diet actually minimises it once the deaths involved in producing plant foods are counted. Arable agriculture kills field animals at scale: rodents, ground-nesting birds, amphibians, and, on some estimates, very many invertebrates, killed during ploughing, harvesting, and pest control. One side argues that, if one is going to eat animal products at all, pasture-raised ruminants are morally preferable to chicken or eggs. A steer is a single large death spread over a great many meals and need not be fed combine-harvested grain, whereas broiler chickens and laying hens are small animals raised and killed in enormous numbers, and they must be fed grain whose production generates field deaths of its own. The other side replies that this is arithmetic with the parameters chosen in advance: monogastric animals convert feed into edible calories far more efficiently than ruminants do; pasture systems have their own less visible deaths, including haymaking, drought or winter starvation, predator control, and the displacement of wild animals from land given over to livestock; and the comparison as a whole turns on contestable judgements about invertebrate sentience, about whether many small deaths can be aggregated against one large one, and about how to weigh wild animal suffering on both sides of the ledger. The curious feature is that the participants are mostly consequentialists who accept the same broad framework, cite the same studies, and yet have not moved one another in the better part of two decades.

I would like a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on the diet. What kind of dispute is this, exactly? Is it a hidden empirical dispute, in which the deciding facts are simply too badly measured for either side to be refuted? Is it a substantive moral disagreement about the structure of harm — for instance, about whether deaths of different kinds and sizes are commensurable on a single scale, or whether foreseen but indirect deaths count differently from direct ones? Is it a disagreement about standards of evidence, such as which estimates count as the baseline and who bears the burden of proof? Or is the question of the least-harm diet itself ill-posed, because expected-value reasoning over these uncertainties is swamped rather than merely imprecise? My own suspicion is that the empirical talk is partly a proxy for a deeper dispute about the commensurability of harms, but I offer that only as a hypothesis. Whatever your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress given it — a particular sort of empirical study, a clarified weighting principle, a dominance argument, a change of question — or, if you think progress should not be expected, say why. Please write your answer in British English.
