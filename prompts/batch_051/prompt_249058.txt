I'm prepping a one-paragraph "philosophical profile" for a seminar on introspection and I'd like a consistency check before I have to defend it. The rough package Ive ended up with is:

(1) First-person reports about current conscious experience should get a default authority: if someone sincerely says they seem to be in pain, that's better evidence about their experience than any outside behavioral or neural measure.

(2) I'm pretty convinced by William James's point in The Principles of Psychology that introspective attention tends to alter or destroy the very state it tries to inspect, like trying to catch a spinning top to see how it moves. So a lot of what we call introspecting is actually retrieving or reconstructing.

(3) I also think introspection is bad at access to mental causes: experimental subjects' explanations of why they formed a judgment or choice are often confabulations, systematically off from what the manipulations show.

(4) But I don't think that refutes the project of disciplined introspection; I suspect trained introspectors (in phenomenology, for instance, or some meditative traditions) can get reasonably stable and cross-checkable reports.

(5) And I still want to keep a strong privileged-access thesis for current phenomenology: some beliefs about what my present experience is like are not just well-justified, they're infallible or unrevisable in normal circumstances.

So the question is whether this hangs together. Are (2) and (3) just different points that don't conflict, or do they jointly undercut the authority I grant in (1) and (5)? Is (4) a fix or is it just ignoring the James point? And is (5) in tension with (2) or only with a stronger version of (2) I'm not actually committed to? Don't just flag everything: rank the tensions by severity, tell me which pairs are merely apparent and what dissolves them, and say what the cheapest single revision would be to clean up the worst problem.
