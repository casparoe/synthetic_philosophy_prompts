You're a philosopher who knows Sartre's Being and Nothingness well, and I want you to construct a thought experiment to spec. Think carefully about the specs before you commit: brainstorm two or three candidate scenes, tell me in a sentence why the one you picked avoids the obvious traps, then write it out.

The case is for a teaching handout on bad faith, so it has to force someone to use the facticity/transcendence machinery rather than just rerun the famous examples. Quick reminder of the machinery: for Sartre a human being is always both facticity — the brute givens you find yourself with, like your body, your past, your situation — and transcendence, the freedom to take those givens and give them a meaning by projecting beyond them. Bad faith is trying to collapse that double structure and pass as only one of the two sides. The most famous example, the waiter who plays at being a waiter, runs in one direction: the person shrinks into their facticity and tries to make themselves into a fixed thing, a role, a label. I want the other direction.

Construct a scene in which someone is in bad faith by fleeing their facticity into pure transcendence: they treat their own past, habits, and commitments as if those are not really theirs, or as if they place no weight on what the person chooses next. The character should be sympathetic and ordinary — a real human, not a caricature — and the scene should be one paragraph, set somewhere mundane like a friendship, a workplace, or a family conversation. Nobody in the scene talks philosophy or has read Sartre, and the character knows the facts of thier past perfectly well; the self-deception is not about what happened but about what it means for who they now are. The bad faith shouldn't hinge on the character literally announcing that they're radically free and so can't make promises, unless you can make that not sound like a cartoon.

After the scene, do the walkthrough. State the person's project, the exact bit of facticity being fled, why this counts as bad faith for Sartre rather than mere optimism, forgetfulness, or weakness of will, and then isolate the single feature of the case that is doing the work. Remove or swap that feature and say whether the case stops being bad faith. Finish with the strongest objection from someone who says this is just a healthy refusal to let your past define you, and give the Sartrean reply that respects the objection but shows it's aimed at the wrong target.
