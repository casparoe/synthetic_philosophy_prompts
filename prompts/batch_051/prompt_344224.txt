I'm putting together this year's exam for my third-year undergraduate philosophy of science course, and I need one essay question on no-free-lunch theorems and the problem of induction. The students have already been through Hume's problem, Goodman's grue riddle, and a couple of lectures on the formal no-free-lunch results (the supervised-learning version and a bit of the optimisation one). So they all know the slogan "no algorithm is better than any other on average," and most of them will just parrott it. I want a question that makes that useless as an answer.

Here's what I need: the question itself, a model answer, and a marking scheme. It should be answerable in about 90 minutes, essay format, and it should not ask for a bibliography or citations. The marking scheme should have five must-hit points totalling 20 marks, and for each point a one-line note on how students typically get it wrong. The question should seperate the students who can state the theorem from the ones who can say what it actually licenses. In particular, I want it to force them to distinguish the theorem's uniform expectation over all possible target functions from the further assumption that the real distribution of problems we face is uniform (or unknown), because they blur those together every term. And make sure the question demands at least one move you can't make just by summarizing the theorem.
