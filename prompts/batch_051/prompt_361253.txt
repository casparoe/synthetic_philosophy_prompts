I've been trying to recover my own considered view about when a system literally has beliefs, as opposed to when it's merely useful to adopt the intentional stance toward it. My pre-theoretic verdicts, case by case:

– A bimetallic-strip thermostat: no beliefs, and I would not even call its set point a desire.
– A honeybee that has learned the location of a rewarding flower patch and communicates it to nestmates: no beliefs, though it is carrying around something like usable information.
– A Roomba that builds a map of a room and plans a route back to its dock before the battery fails: no beliefs, but I do want to say it has a goal.
– A rat that watches food being buried and later digs it up from the remembered location after a delay: beliefs and desires, unproblematically.
– A self-driving car that brakes at a crosswalk because its occupancy model represents a child as possibly occluded by a van, though no child is visible: yes, I want to say it believes a child may be there.
– AlphaZero: no beliefs, though I cannot help saying that it expects a position to be winning; I would call that as-if.
– A large language model that answers factual questions correctly and gives accurate causal explanations of its answers: no beliefs.
– A language-model-based agent with persistent memory and a planner, which writes down what it expects to happen next, then changes course when that expectation is contradicted: here I keep going back and forth.

Is there a single principle that captures all of these, or nearly all of them? If so, state it precisely and say what it predicts about a weather-forecasting system that maintains an explicit probabilistic model of the atmosphere and revises it against sensor data, but whose only behavior is to emit forecasts. If no single principle works, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and which replacement principle is the least bad overall.
