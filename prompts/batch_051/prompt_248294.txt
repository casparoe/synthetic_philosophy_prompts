I am the managing editor of a small press that publishes literary fiction and essays in translation, chiefly from Japanese, Arabic, Portuguese, and German into English. Our translators and copy editors keep relitigating the same question at the worst possible moment: what to do with a word that has no tidy English equivalent. Some leave the original in italics with a gloss, some domesticate it to the nearest English word, some paraphrase around it, and the disputes now surface at copyediting, when changes are expensive. I would like a reusable decision procedure that a translator with no background in philosophy of language or translation theory can run on any contested term in under fifteen minutes, before the manuscript reaches copyediting. The procedure should say, in order: what questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, when to stop and leave the term untranslated with a gloss at first use, when to translate it with the nearest equivalent, when to paraphrase, and when the decision must be escalated to me or to the author because it is a judgment call rather than a mechanical step.

Part of my difficulty is that "untranslatable" is doing too much work at once. Almost no word has an exact one-to-one equivalent across languages, so if that were the standard we would have to leave half the dictionary in italics. I want the procedure to distinguish the ordinary absence of a perfect match from the residue that actually matters in this particular text—semantic range, register, connotation, sound, the way a term organizes a whole work—and to take seriously the ethical stakes of the choice, including the worry that domesticating everything flattens difference while exoticizing everything turns the source language into a costume. Please ground the procedure in what is defensible about untranslatability, but present it as an operational checklist rather than a theory summary. Then run it on three cases: (1) Portuguese saudade used once in a novel as the narrator's one-word description of a character's mood; (2) Japanese mono no aware in an essay that uses the term as its organizing concept; and (3) a term in a translated memoir whose nearest English equivalent would impose a religious or moral category the original does not carry—pick a concrete example and defend your choice. In at least one of the three, the procedure should return an answer my marketing department will dislike, since our house style favors keeping flavor words untranslated; tell me where the procedure overrules that preference and why.
