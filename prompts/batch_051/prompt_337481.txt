I find myself drawn to a thoroughgoing extended-mind view of epistemic assessment. It has a functionalist engine: it identifies understanding not with any particular inner state but with a system's reliably generating correct, well-explained answers, and it allows that profile to be realized across a person and their tools. This unifies reliance on a calculator, a lab group's division of labor, and reliance on a language model under one principle, and it yields a crisp verdict: if the person-plus-tool system performs that way, understanding is present, however distributed. But I also have an intuition I cannot shake: a student who can produce flawless answers only by prompting an AI, and who could neither restate what the answer means nor catch a subtle error in it, does not understand the material, even though the system they form with the AI performs perfectly. I am not looking for a verdict handed down from one side. Set out as forcefully as you can the case that the intuition is a residue of an individualist picture of the mind, and the case that the extended view has been stretched beyond its proper reach. Then say which you would give up, what would justify giving it up, and whether there is instead a reading of 'understanding' on which both the system-level claim and the individual-level claim are true.
