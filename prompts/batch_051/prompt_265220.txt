My great-grandparents wrote letters in a regional language that had fallen out of the family by the time I was born, and I recently began working through those letters with a dictionary. What keeps puzzling me is how differently we describe that disappearance and the passage from Latin to Italian. Latin is standardly called a dead language, yet its slow transformation into Italian is treated as ordinary historical change: no generation set down one language and took up another, and nobody thinks of Italian speakers as having done anything to Latin. The loss of my great-grandparents' language, by contrast, is described as a loss, and in comparable cases people speak of language death, cultural erasure, and sometimes injustice, even though the distance between the earlier speech and the later speech is no greater in kind than the distance between Latin and Italian.

I want to know whether anything can justify this asymmetry. Give me the strongest principle you can find for treating slow internal drift and rapid displacement differently, then test it against the hard intermediate cases: a language displaced over three centuries by state pressure, and a community that drops its language within one generation without coercion. Should we keep the asymmetry, abandon it, or reverse it? Please answer in American English.
