Our school's student government has a constitution whose entire amendment clause reads: "Any provision of this constitution may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the entire membership." The student government elects a constitution commissioner to rule on procedural and interpretive disputes, and I have been collecting the commissioner's past rulings because of a dispute unfolding now. The rulings most relevant to the dispute are these:

1. A proposal to amend only the name clause—an ordinary substantive change—received 71% of the membership and was upheld, with the commissioner rejecting the argument that some provisions are too fundamental to be changed through the ordinary amendment process.
2. A proposal to add a rule that amendments to the meeting-schedule clause need only a simple majority received 85% of the membership and was upheld.
3. A proposal to add a rule that amendments to the name clause need a three-quarters vote received 79% of the membership and was upheld; the commissioner said the proposal cleared all the thresholds that could plausibly apply.
4. A proposal to amend the amendment clause itself by replacing "two-thirds" with "three-quarters" received 84% of the membership and was upheld. Since that ruling, the general amendment threshold has been treated as three-quarters.

A group is now proposing to amend the amendment clause again, this time replacing "three-quarters" with "a simple majority." The proposal received 61% of the membership. The commissioner has not yet ruled.

I don't only want a prediction of how the commissioner will rule. I want it worked out the way someone would reconstruct a body's rule from its precedents: what distinct principles could fit the four past rulings, what each principle would say about this new proposal, which ruling the past record most naturally commits the commissioner to, and what further case would most sharply separate the leading candidate principles.
