I am a PhD student in moral psychology, working on the norms of gratitude: in particular, when gratitude is owed for a benefit, and how much the benefactor, luck, and the past should matter to that judgment. I want to determine which features of a situation are doing the real normative work when we say that someone ought to be grateful. Below are six candidate conditions that have been proposed, individually or in combination, as necessary for owed gratitude. Please sort them into tiers according to how defensible each is as a necessary condition, and write a few sentences for each explaining its placement. Do not place everything in a single tier; use at least two and no more than three tiers, and rank the candidates within each tier where you can.

(a) The benefactor acted with the intention of benefiting the recipient.
(b) The benefactor bore some cost, effort, or risk in providing the benefit.
(c) The benefit is one the recipient has reason to value, not merely one the benefactor believed would be good for the recipient.
(d) The benefit would not have occurred, or would not have occurred as well, had the benefactor not acted.
(e) The recipient accepted the benefit, or at least did not reject it when rejection was feasible.
(f) The benefactor and the recipient stand in some relationship — personal, institutional, or historical — that gives the benefit meaning beyond its material value.

Weigh each candidate against the hard cases: failed attempts to help, harmful or unwanted gifts, benefits that arrive by luck or accident, overbearing interventions that impose costs, and benefits that reach the recipient from the past without any living benefactor. For each candidate, say explicitly which of these cases it handles and which it cannot. Then conclude by identifying the combination of surviving conditions that you take to get closest to the best account of owed gratitude, and indicate whether any candidate should be rejected outright rather than merely placed in a lower tier.
