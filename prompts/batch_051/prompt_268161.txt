I have been asked to take over a colleague's final-year political theory seminar for a week, and the assigned topic is "Order and harmony as political values." The students have just read Mill's On Liberty, and they tend to treat liberal pluralism as the standing position: a good society is one that tolerates different experiments in living, and any departure from that looks to them like authoritarianism. I want the hour to make them take seriously a rival picture, in which harmony is a political achievement procured through social form and ordered difference rather than through non-interference. To anchor the session I have chosen a passage from the Analects of Confucius, 1.12, in James Legge's translation (it is spoken by the disciple Youzi, not the Master himself):

"The philosopher You said, "In practicing the rules of propriety, a natural ease is to be prized. In the ways prescribed by the ancient kings, this is the excellent quality, and in things small and great we follow them. Yet it is not to be observed in all cases. If one, knowing how such ease should be prized, manifests it, without regulating it by the rules of propriety, this likewise is not to be done.""

I have deliberately not chosen the better-known saying about the superior man harmonising but not conforming, because I want the students to see that ritual order, not a maxim about character, is doing the philosophical work here. Please do not explain the passage to me. I need a plan for a fifty-minute seminar: what has to be in place before the passage can work (in particular, what they need to grasp about li, and whether I should gloss it as "ritual propriety" or avoid that gloss), the order in which the ideas should be introduced so that the contrast with Mill's pluralism arrives at the right moment rather than as a straw man, and the one carrying example that should run through the session and what it must have and lack. I had considered an orchestra or a family meal, but I suspect the orchestra smuggles in a conductor and the family meal presupposes too much intimacy; tell me whether either can be made to work or what would do better. I am also expecting three misreadings: that "natural ease" means informality or individual spontaneity; that harmony is just consensus or the avoidance of conflict; and that the passage is only about etiquette. Indicate where in the sequence each misreading is likely to form and how to head it off without flatly denying it. Finally, say what I should openly leave out at undergraduate level, and give me one question to ask at the end that would show whether a student has understood harmony as ordered difference rather than as sameness, peace at any price, or personal style.
