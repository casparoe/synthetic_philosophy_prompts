I retired from geochemistry a few years ago and have been reading my way through the philosophy of forgiveness; I keep coming back to the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission as a test case. Take the TRC's central arrangement — amnesty in exchange for full disclosure by perpetrators, victims testifying, and almost everyone framing the whole thing as forgiveness rather than punishment — and analyze it through the lens of philosophical theories of forgiveness. I know the standard story about the TRC, so don't summarize it; what I want is the careful mapping. In particular: in what sense, if any, was the TRC an exercise in forgiveness, given that forgiving is usually the victim's own change of heart and not something a state can do on someone else's behalf? The view that forgiveness must be earned through repentance and apology seems to imply that the TRC asked victims to give up something they shouldn't have been asked to give up, while the view that forgiveness can be unconditional fits the rhetoric about no future without forgiving but has trouble explaining why a commission got to broker it. Does the episode support one side, refute the other, or show that both sides were aimed at the wrong thing — maybe a kind of political forgiveness that works alongside amnesty rather than replacing justice? And what does it do to the claim that forgiveness can be obligatory? I want where the theory explains the TRC, where it has to be stretched, and where it goes quiet.
