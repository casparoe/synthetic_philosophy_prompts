I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I have been reading around the foundations of mathematics, and I keep running into a debate that seems to have been going on for over a century without either side moving. In day-to-day programming, infinity is always treated as potential: a loop that can run arbitrarily many times, a limit that is approached, a floating-point sentinel for overflow—never a completed object one can actually point to. But set theory talks about actually infinite totalities and compares their sizes. Cantor's diagonal argument is standardly taken to show that the real numbers are strictly larger than the natural numbers, even though both are infinite, and more generally that the power set of any set is strictly larger than the set itself. Hilbert's hotel is often introduced to make actual infinity vivid: a hotel with countably many occupied rooms can make room for one new guest, or countably many new guests, without becoming any larger.

Then the finitist side says this is exactly the problem. Hilbert's hotel does not exhibit a coherent fact; it exhibits the fact that "completed infinite set" smuggles in a notion of size and equality that behaves like nothing finite, and that the only legitimate notion of infinity is potential—a possibility of going on, never a finished totality. Against the diagonal argument, the finitist will say that it is a method for producing a real outside any proposed enumeration, and that nothing in the construction requires or warrants the claim that an uncountable completed totality exists. The Cantorian replies that rejecting actual infinity leaves mathematics without a home for the continuum, for ordinary real analysis, and for the standard semantics of quantification. At that point, in my experience, the exchange simply restarts.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really a dispute inside mathematics. The two sides appear to accept all the same finite computations, and they agree on the conditional claims: if such-and-such formal system is admitted, then such-and-such results follow. Instead, the disagreement seems to be about what mathematics is for—whether it is a discipline of reliable finite procedures or a description of an abstract realm—and about what should count as a reason to believe that an object exists. If I am wrong, I want to know what genuine mathematical or formal question is actually being fought over. So: what kind of disagreement is this? Is it substantive, verbal, or two different questions going under one name? And given your diagnosis, what, if anything, would count as progress—a formal result, a distinction, a reframing—or should no one expect the two sides to move?
