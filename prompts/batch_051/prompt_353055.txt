I am a PhD candidate in history and philosophy of science, and for a chapter on how attribution errors become stable in scientific nomenclature I am compiling examples of Stigler's law of eponymy. I understand the law as the systematic tendency for named discoveries, laws, constants, and regularities to be named after someone other than the person who first discovered or formulated them; Stephen Stigler, who gave the law its name, himself credited the underlying observation to Robert K. Merton, which makes the law its own first instance.

So far my catalogue contains these cases:

1. The Gaussian distribution (the normal distribution): first derived by Abraham de Moivre as a limiting case of the binomial distribution and given a central treatment by Pierre-Simon Laplace, well before Gauss's work led to the standard name.
2. Benford's law: noticed by Simon Newcomb in the late nineteenth century, but named for Frank Benford, who rediscovered and publicized it in the 1930s.
3. The Pythagorean theorem: the relation was known and used in Old Babylonian and other pre-Pythagorean mathematical traditions, so the Greek eponym reflects later legend rather than first discovery.
4. Avogadro's number (or constant): Avogadro formulated the gas law that motivates the constant but never computed its magnitude; first estimates were obtained by Josef Loschmidt, and the early precision measurements came from Jean Perrin.

I know these are among the standard cases, and I do not want a fifth case that is only a variant of them. In particular: do not give me another named mathematical constant whose eponym arose from a priority dispute, do not give me the self-instance of Stigler's law, and do not give me Halley's comet, which is a standard case of popularization rather than discovery. By "new" I mean an instance whose attribution dynamics differ in kind from the four cases above, for example one from outside mathematics and physics, or one where the name was attached through honorific naming by a later textbook writer, a naming committee, translation lag, or some institutional route rather than through a priority dispute.

For your proposed case, make clear what is named, who is named, who had the result first or more fully, and how the name actually came to be attached.

Return only a single JSON object with the following fields:

- "eponym": the name by which the phenomenon, law, constant, unit, or object is known.
- "field": the relevant scientific discipline or tradition.
- "named_person": the person to whom the eponym attributes the discovery.
- "earlier_source": the person or tradition that had the result first or more fully.
- "attribution_mechanism": one of "priority_dispute", "honorific_naming", "textbook_canonization", "translation_lag", "popularization", "institutional_naming", or "other".
- "evidence_quality": one of "strong", "contested", or "weak", followed by a one-sentence explanation.
- "why_it_is_new": one or two sentences on how this case differs in structure from the four listed cases.
- "relation_to_stiglers_law": one or two sentences on whether the case supports the law, qualifies it, or suggests the conditions under which eponyms track actual priority.
