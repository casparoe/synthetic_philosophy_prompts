The standard rule-of-law argument against never-enforced statutes runs roughly as follows. A statute that remains on the books while officials never apply it can no longer guide conduct, yet it stays available as a license for arbitrary or selective enforcement; the longer it lies dormant, the more it undermines notice, equality, and accountability — the problem traditionally labeled desuetude. That argument assumed non-enforcement is a stable condition: a dormant statute simply sleeps, and reviving one was uncommon and costly. That assumption may no longer hold. Machine-readable public data, automated cameras, and algorithmic targeting now make it cheap for a municipality to resurrect a decades-dormant ordinance and enforce it selectively against particular streets, individuals, or groups. Does the classical argument against unenforced laws survive this change? If it survives, say which premise the development bears on and whether the argument becomes stronger or weaker. If it does not survive in its original form, say whether what remains should still be called desuetude or is better redescribed as a different failure, such as defective notice or algorithmic selectivity. I am asking about the fate of the argument, not for an enforcement policy.
