Over the holidays a friend of mine who did parliamentary debate in college kept insisting that if you took argumentation seriously as a sport—rankings, salaries, training camps, a televised circuit—you'd end up with measurably better public reasoning, the way chess is supposed to make kids better at math or whatever. I found this somewhere between plausible and insane. I'm not asking wether it would be good as a spectator thing; I want a prediction about the actual ecology of the thing if it existed.

So here's the counterfactual. Starting next year, a well-funded organization launches a professional Argumentation League in the English-speaking world. It works like this: two-person teams draw a live policy or philosophical question 90 minutes before the match; they can use any notes but no internet; they must defend the side they drew. Panels of trained judges score them on explicit criteria—validity, charitable handling of the opposing case, responsiveness, refusal to equivocate—and the scoring rubric explicitly penalizes rhetorical polish, emotional appeal, and crowd-pleasing. The league is televised, players have ELO-style rankings, there are transfer fees and sponsors, and within a decade top players are earning tennis-level money. Schools and universities start treating debate the way they treat football, with feeder academies and scholarships. By year 25 it's genuinely mass culture, not a niche hobby.

Predict how this plays out, and be specific about mechanisms. Does the quality of everyday public argument—news, politics, social media—actually improve, stay the same, or get worse? Do the incentives of a professional sport (optimizing to a rubric, playing to judges, meta-gaming) corrupt or sharpen the reasoning the sport is supposed to train? What happens to the boundary between "sport argumentation" and real argument, and does anything transfer across it? I also want your confidence at each step: which predictions are robust features of any such system, and which depend on details like who the judges are, whether audiences reward flash anyway, or how the feeder pipeline is built. If the whole setup rests on a bad assumption—for instance that you can score "validity" cleanly enough to build a sport on—say so and work from there rather than just going along with the hypothetical.
