I am a high school student taking two courses that have unexpectedly begun to talk to each other, and I want to know whether the conversation is real or an artifact of my own vocabulary. In philosophy we have been discussing Searle's Chinese Room argument; in computer science we have been told repeatedly that passing a test suite is not the same as being correct. I can state the parallel precisely, but I cannot tell whether it is deep or superficial.

On the Searle side, as I understand it: a person who knows no Chinese sits in a room and follows an English rulebook for manipulating symbols, producing Chinese responses indistinguishable from those of a native speaker. Searle holds that neither the person nor the room as a whole understands Chinese, even though the system passes the behavioral test. So passing the Turing test cannot by itself establish understanding; syntactic manipulation alone is not sufficient for semantics.

On the computer science side: a program that passes every black-box test in a suite may still be wrong. One student writes a shortest-path function that actually computes shortest paths; another hardcodes the expected outputs for exactly the test inputs, or consults a lookup table from input to output. Both pass the same tests. Only the first implements the function described in the specification. Behavioral equivalence to a correct program is not the same as being a correct program, because correctness also depends on what the program does internally.

The parallel I notice is that Searle's room is the hardcoded program. It passes the behavioral tests — the Chinese input-output pairs — while its internal procedure, moving symbols according to formal syntactic rules, fails to be the kind of procedure that counts as understanding. Just as my computer science teacher would say that tests cannot turn a lookup table into an algorithm, Searle seems to be saying that the Turing test cannot turn symbol manipulation into understanding.

Yet I can see a possible break. In computer science, the hardcoded program and the real algorithm are compared against an intended specification supplied from outside, and we can inspect the source code of both to locate the difference. In the Chinese Room case, there is no external specification of understanding that the rulebook fails to meet; the claim is supposed to be that no syntactic procedure, however elaborate, could meet it. Does the analogy fail at precisely the load-bearing point? Or does it expose that Searle is assuming, without argument, that understanding is like implementing the intended algorithm — an internal matter — rather than like passing an indefinitely long battery of tests?

I would like a verdict: is this a deep structural analogy, a loose analogy, or a confusion? In giving it, please (a) state exactly what would have to be true on each side for results to transfer between them, (b) say whether Searle's argument, reconstructed in test-suite terms, gains or loses force, and (c) identify one specific thing the parallel would lead one to predict about minds or programs that is false. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
