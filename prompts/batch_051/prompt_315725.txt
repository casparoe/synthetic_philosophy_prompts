I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and Hempel's raven paradox has been nagging at me. The argument is hard to resist. It stands on two principles I cannot easily reject. First, a generalization is confirmed, at least somewhat, by each of its positive instances: finding a black raven is evidence that all ravens are black. Second, logically equivalent generalizations are confirmed by exactly the same evidence, since they say the same thing in different notation. But "all ravens are black" is equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens." So by the second principle whatever confirms one confirms the other, and by the first principle a white shoe — a non-black thing that is not a raven — confirms the latter. Therefore a white shoe confirms that all ravens are black.

In programming terms, this feels even more compelling. To me `raven(x) -> black(x)` and `!black(x) -> !raven(x)` are the same condition; the second is just the contrapositive. A white shoe is a perfectly valid positive case of the second. And yet, if I were writing property-based tests for an invariant about raven coloration, I would never generate white shoes to probe it. The logic says the two forms are one property, so the test should be just as relevant; my testing instincts say the opposite.

Here is the intuition I cannot shake: not that the white shoe is weak evidence, but that it is no evidence at all. Inspecting the objects in my apartment feels irrelevant to ornithology — a red herring, almost a category mistake. I don't want a verdict that just picks a side. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that gives, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that gives, and whether there is a reading of "confirms" or "evidence" on which both survive — and what would justify each of those moves.
