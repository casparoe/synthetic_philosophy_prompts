I have been defending the claim that thinking about one's own thinking — monitoring and evaluating a thought process while it runs — is always helpful to thought, never a net hindrance. A colleague pressed me to make the argument explicit, and here is what I produced.

(1) A thought process deserves our trust only if it is sensitive to correction: there must be some mechanism by which it would be revised when it was going wrong.
(2) For a thought process to be sensitive to correction, the thinker has to monitor how the process is going and adjust it when it departs from good reasoning; monitoring and adjusting one's own thinking simply is metacognition.
(3) So any trustworthy thought process is one the thinker monitors and adjusts; such processes already involve metacognition.
(4) If a process already involves metacognition, then engaging in metacognition is not an extra interference added from outside but an internal constituent of what makes the process trustworthy.
(5) A constituent of what makes a process trustworthy cannot be what makes it go worse.
(6) Therefore metacognition never makes a trustworthy thought process worse; it is always helpful to it.

I am unsure whether the real fault is in (2), because correction can come from outside the thinker — colleagues, instruments, the world pushing back — and the thinker can respond to that pushback without standing above their own process and monitoring it. But if I weaken (2) to allow external correction, the argument no longer seems to establish that the thinker herself ever metacognizes. Is (2) the step that fails, or does the argument fail later, perhaps in (4) or (5)? Say exactly which step is false or invalid, state the precise reading of its terms under which it fails, and say whether the conclusion could still be true for reasons this argument does not provide.
