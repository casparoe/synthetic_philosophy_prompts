You are a decision theorist working on the foundations of choice under uncertainty. I am preparing a short position statement on the Ellsberg paradox and ambiguity aversion for a reading group, and before I circulate it I want an audit of whether the views I list below actually hang together. Please think carefully and reconstruct, for each commitment, what exactly it rules in and rules out. Then work through the commitments pair by pair, distinguishing genuine tensions from merely apparent ones. For each genuine tension, rank it by severity, say what would have to give to resolve it, and what the cheapest fix would cost. For each apparent tension, say exactly why it dissolves. End by saying which single revision would do the most to restore consistency.

My commitments are these:

(1) I accept Savage's sure-thing principle as a constraint on rational preference: where two acts agree on an event, their ranking is determined by where they differ, so changing their common payoff on that event should not reverse the ranking.

(2) In the classic one-urn Ellsberg problem — an urn with 30 red balls and 60 balls that are black or yellow in unknown proportion — my preferences are the standard ambiguity-averse ones: I prefer a bet that pays on red to a bet that pays on black, and I prefer a bet that pays on black-or-yellow to a bet that pays on red-or-yellow.

(3) In the two-urn Ellsberg problem — one urn with a known 50/50 red-black mix, another with an unknown red-black proportion — I prefer a bet on the known urn to the corresponding bet on the unknown urn for each color. I regard this as the same underlying phenomenon as (2), not a second, distinct effect.

(4) I accept dynamic consistency and a consequentialist approach to choice in decision trees: at a later node, absent new information, I evaluate the remaining subtree exactly as I would have evaluated that same subtree from the start, and I follow through on plans that were optimal ex ante.

(5) I accept the reduction of compound lotteries for objective risk: a two-stage lottery in which the probabilities at each stage are objectively given is equivalent to its reduced one-stage form.

(6) I take the preferences in (2) and (3) to be rational responses to missing information rather than a bias to be corrected, and I am not willing to secure them by giving up dominance or statewise monotonicity.

(7) I want the difference between the known urn and the unknown urn to be accounted for in purely informational terms — what further sampling would or would not tell me — rather than by a metaphysically heavy distinction between two species of probability.

Please go through these and tell me which commitments pull against which, how badly, and which revisions are cheapest.
