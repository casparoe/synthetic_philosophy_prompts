"Political competence" is the load-bearing notion in arguments over epistocracy — proposals to restrict or weight the franchise by knowledge or ability — and in democratic replies to them. I want a working definition of "politically competent" as a property of an individual voter.

One thing makes the term especially contested: it is unclear what the competence is for — advancing the voter's own interests, advancing the common good, approximating the judgment of a just or impartial observer, or something else. Make that commitment explicit.

Define "politically competent" so that it classifies the following cases. Some strike me as clear, others as genuinely borderline; I am less interested in whether you share my impressions than in the definition doing real classificatory work.

(1) A voter who has followed the campaign, can accurately state the major candidates' positions on the policies they care about, and has a roughly correct understanding of how those policies would work.
(2) A voter who cannot say what office is being contested or what its powers are, and chooses by name recognition.
(3) A single-issue voter who knows their issue in detail but is ignorant of nearly everything else on the ballot.
(4) A well-informed partisan whose factual beliefs are mostly accurate but whose interpretations of ambiguous evidence reliably favor their own side.
(5) A voter who holds a false empirical belief — say, that violent crime is rising in their city when it has been falling for a decade — acquired from sources they reasonably trust.
(6) A low-information voter who votes by party label as a heuristic that has, over several elections, tracked their interests reliably.
(7) A recent immigrant with limited fluency in the majority language who has carefully followed the incumbent's record in their local community.
(8) A politically engaged 16-year-old who scores above the adult median on a civics-knowledge test.
(9) An adult with a cognitive disability that impairs numerical and probabilistic reasoning but whose values and preferences are clear and stable.
(10) An economist who understands the incidence of a tax proposal cold but knows little about the candidates' other commitments or the institutional constraints on implementing it.

Propose a definition, run it through each case, and say where it gives the wrong verdict or no verdict. Then revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. Finally, say which of your classifications is least secure and why.
