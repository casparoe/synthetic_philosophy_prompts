My friend and I just spent an hour going in circles about Buridan's ass, and I'm not even sure whose got the better view anymore. She thinks the ass is a fake problem: an agent with complete preferences can be indifferent, and indifference plus the need to act licenses picking either option, so the ass only starves because it refuses to pick. I kept pushing that some ties are broken by reasons that aren't already hiding in the agent's preferences—a promise, a role, a commitment—and that if you describe the case so those are absent, you've just stipulated a malformed agent, not dissolved the problem. The usual versions of the ass are too thin to settle it, so we ended up agreeing that what we actually need is a better case.

So build me one: a single ordinary-life scenario (no demons, no perfect predictors, no science fiction, no coin flips or randomizers, and no unstated differences in what the agent cares about) in which two options are a genuine preference-tie—the agent's desires and values are exactly on a par between them—but the agent nevertheless has a decisive reason to choose one option rather than the other. I want the case set up so that a pure preference-based theory of choice (roughly: practical reasons are just the agent's desires or preferences, and a tie is a tie) must say "either option is rationally fine," while a reasons-based theory (roughly: some reasons are normative and can require things the agent doesn't currently want) must say "option A is the one to choose." The scenario has to generate that split; don't just stipulate it. Name your two theories, state the two options and what's held fixed, walk through why each theory gives its verdict, then tell me which single feature of the case does the work, what happens if you remove it, and how a preference-theorist would attack the case (especially the familiar move of redescribing the "reason" as a preference all along). It's for a paper section on whether rational choice requires complete preferences and whether reasons reduce to preferences, so don't lean on a bare moral duty unless you can build it in so the preference-theorist can't just absorb it.
