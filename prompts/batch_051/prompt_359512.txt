I'm taking a graduate seminar on twentieth-century moral philosophy, and we spent the last two meetings reconstructing G. E. M. Anscombe's objections to Oxford's awarding of an honorary degree to Harry S. Truman. The position is forceful, but I keep losing track of exactly which claim is doing the work. As I understand her, the argument runs:

(1) Deliberately killing the innocent as a means to one's ends is murder.
(2) In authorizing the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, Truman deliberately killed innocent non-combatants as a means to the end of forcing Japan's surrender.
(3) Therefore, Truman committed murder.
(4) A university ought not confer an honorary degree on someone who has committed murder, because to confer such a degree is to honor what the person has done.
(5) Therefore, Oxford ought not to have conferred the degree on Truman.

I suspect the move from (2) to (3) is too smooth, but I cannot tell whether the difficulty lies in the intended-versus-foreseen distinction, in the idea that one can kill by authorizing the act of another, or somewhere else entirely. Is the argument valid as numbered? If not, which exact step fails, and on what reading of that step? If it is valid, which single premise should an opponent attack, and how?
