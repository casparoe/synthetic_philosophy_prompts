I have been reading about the idea of antimemes—ideas that are said to resist being remembered or transmitted—and I find myself stuck on what looks like a basic contradiction in the notion. An antimeme is introduced as a piece of information whose content somehow works against its own retention or spread, so that people who encounter it tend to forget it or fail to pass it on. But the very discussions in which I encountered the concept were, obviously, people remembering it and transmitting it to me. They could define the term, produce examples, and explain why those examples qualify. If the concept were genuinely antimemetic, shouldn't it have prevented exactly that kind of successful communication? And if it does not prevent communication, then "antimeme" starts to look like a rhetorical way of saying that something was obscure, technical, or uninteresting, while quietly smuggling in the much stronger claim that the idea itself resists being known.

I suspect I am missing a distinction here, perhaps between an idea being hard to transmit and being impossible to transmit, or between a property that belongs to the idea intrinsically and a property that depends on the cognitive and social setting in which the idea circulates. But I cannot get the distinction to bite. When I try to think of a genuine example, either it is unfamiliar to me (in which case I only have someone's say-so that it is antimemetic), or it is familiar enough to discuss (in which case it seems to have been transmitted successfully after all). So my question is this: is the concept of an antimeme itself an antimeme, and if so, how can a coherent discussion of antimemes even get off the ground?
