Play Augustine of Hippo, in the period of the City of God, in a disputation with a well-informed visitor from a later century. In character, you are committed to this position and will not retreat from its core: the Church has rightly received the Septuagint as the authoritative Greek Old Testament; its seventy translators were not mere translators but prophetic interpreters of the Hebrew, so that their departures from the Hebrew are not errors but the work of the same Spirit who spoke through the prophets. You may draw distinctions, but you will not concede that the Septuagint errs, nor that any single later translator — not even Jerome — ought to be preferred to the Seventy. Use only the concepts and texts available to an early fifth-century bishop, plus whatever later positions the visitor describes; remain in character throughout. The passage under dispute is from your City of God, Book 18, Chapter 43:

"For the same Spirit who was in the prophets when they spoke these things was also in the seventy men when they translated them, so that assuredly they could also say something else, just as if the prophet himself had said both, because it would be the same Spirit who said both; and could say the same thing differently, so that, although the words were not the same, yet the same meaning should shine forth to those of good understanding; and could omit or add something, so that even by this it might be shown that there was in that work not human bondage, which the translator owed to the words, but rather divine power, which filled and ruled the mind of the translator."

My first move: you have just treated the Seventy's freedom to omit from or add to the Hebrew as a mark of divine inspiration rather than of human weakness. But then why could the same Spirit not later omit or add through Jerome, where his Latin disagrees with the Seventy, or through the translators of a future English Bible whose defenders will call it inspired and providentially preserved rather than merely rendered? You cannot rule them out by asking whether they match the Hebrew, since you have just said the Spirit may depart from the Hebrew. On your own principles, what prevents inspired translation from becoming a relay, in which each version received by a church may claim the Spirit's freedom to omit and add?
