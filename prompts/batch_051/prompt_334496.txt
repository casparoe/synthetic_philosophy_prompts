A friend and I have spent the past few months arguing about what makers of historical films and biographical fiction owe to the real people they depict, and we have converged on a plan: we want to turn the argument into an adversarial-collaboration document that each of us could sign without feeling that our view had been thinned out.

Here is where we each stand. I hold a fidelity view. I do not think a dramatized life must stick to every documented fact, and I accept composite characters, elision, and a reasonable imaginative filling-in of private scenes. But I do think a dramatizer can wrong the dead themselves by materially misrepresenting their words, deeds, or character—especially where the misrepresentation is damaging to how they would reasonably wish to be remembered—and that this duty does not reduce entirely to what audiences believe. She holds a dramatic-license view. She does not think anything goes: she agrees that a self-presented documentary that fabricates evidence is deceptive, and that makers can be answerable for foreseeable effects on living people, institutions, or public memory. But she denies that the dead have interests left to injure and thinks the relevant obligations run to the work and its audience, not to the historical person.

Please draft the document we should have produced, in two parts.

First, a numbered list of claims we could both sign. It should cover the obvious common ground—about labeled drama versus documentary, about fabrication with no artistic purpose, and about whether some portrayals are irresponsible independent of our disagreement—but it should also include the strongest shared formulations we could honestly accept about where the line is hardest to draw.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should state one residual disagreement, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own view; identify the crux the disagreement actually turns on; describe a concrete case or observation that both sides would accept as bearing on that crux; and record each side's prediction about it in advance.

I suspect, but do not insist, that the real cruxes are whether a reputation can be a good that survives a person, whether the label of fiction does the moral work my friend thinks it does, and whether there is a stable difference between altering undocumented private moments and contradicting well-documented public acts. If you find that some of our months-long dispute was verbal, say which part and why; if some of it is genuine but not resolvable by the cases we care about, say that too.

This should be a constructive document, not a dramatized exchange or an adjudication of who is right. The point is to find the formulations that survive contact with the other side. Avoid mere slogans on both sides, and do not make my view stronger or hers weaker than we have each stated.
