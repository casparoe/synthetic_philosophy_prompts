Two members of my reading group met last week to discuss Timothy Williamson’s 2000 book Knowledge and Its Limits, and they reached a standstill over his anti-luminosity argument. Neither could persuade the other, and as the only neutral bystander I was asked to referee. I could not. Here is the dispute as fairly as I can reconstruct it.

The background, as our group summarized it: Williamson defines a condition as luminous just in case, whenever it obtains, the subject is in a position to know that it obtains. He argues that no mental condition is luminous. His strategy is to consider a person who feels cold at dawn and gradually warms up until, at noon, they no longer feel cold. If feeling cold were luminous, then at every moment during the transition when they still felt cold, they would be in a position to know they felt cold. But, Williamson claims, knowing that one feels cold requires a margin for error: if one knows one feels cold at a given moment, then one would also feel cold at a very nearby moment. Applying this margin-for-error principle repeatedly across the series would force the person still to feel cold at noon, which is false. Therefore feeling cold is not luminous, and the same structure is supposed to generalize to any mental condition.

Dana read this as a direct refutation of the KK principle—the principle that if a person knows a proposition, then they are in a position to know that they know it. Her reasoning was that if no mental condition is luminous, then a person can know that they feel cold without being in a position to know that they know it. Otherwise, being in a position to know that one feels cold whenever one feels cold would just be luminosity for coldness, which Williamson has ruled out. So, she concluded, anti-luminosity is really an argument against the second-order access that KK demands.

Tom said this conclusion runs faster than the argument permits. Anti-luminosity, he argued, only denies a universal link from a condition’s obtaining to one’s being in a position to know that it obtains. It does not deny a link from actual first-order knowledge to the knowability of that knowledge. A proposition can be knowable on some occasions without being luminous, since luminosity concerns every occasion on which the proposition is true. Thus, Tom said, Dana has mistaken a point about the distribution of a condition for a point about the structure of knowledge itself.

Dana came back by asking what the margin-for-error premise would imply for second-order knowledge. If one knows that one knows that one feels cold at a moment, by the same kind of margin-for-error reasoning one must also know that one feels cold at the next moment; but then the failure of luminosity for coldness places sharp limits on how much second-order knowledge one can have across a gradual transition. That, she said, is all the damage to KK that she needed: internalists cannot insist that first-order introspective knowledge always brings with it further knowledge of that knowledge.

Please adjudicate this point by point. Which specific claims does each get right or wrong, and is Tom correct that anti-luminosity leaves KK essentially untouched, or is Dana correct that it undermines or significantly constrains the principle?
