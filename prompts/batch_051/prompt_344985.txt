I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long piece about the expanding social demand for transparency — explainable algorithms, open-plan accountability, the insistence that people state their intentions rather than leave them to be inferred. In my reporting I keep running into a philosophical argument that has clearly stalled. One camp treats legibility as a default moral requirement: if others cannot understand why you act as you do, you fail to treat them as agents, and unreadability shades into manipulation by omission. The opposing camp treats opacity as sometimes permitted, sometimes positively owed: requiring others to make themselves legible can force them to flatten themselves into whatever categories the interpreter already possesses, and people can have a claim to remain unread. The puzzle is that both sides justify themselves by appeal to respect for persons — one says respect requires rendering yourself accountable, the other says respect requires not demanding that someone become readable for your comfort — yet neither side moves. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict on who is right. Is this a verbal dispute about the word legibility, a hidden empirical question about whether transparency actually improves trust and coordination, an unargued difference about where the burden of interpretation lies, or something else? My own hunch is that the two camps are assigning the burden of legibility to different parties — the person acting versus the person interpreting — but I'd rather have that tested than defended. Please act as a professional philosopher working in ethics and social epistemology. Begin by brainstorming the possible sources of the impasse before committing, and then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress — a distinction, a study, a reframing — or whether the question is ill-posed.
