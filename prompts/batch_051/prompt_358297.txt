I am attracted to a deflationary account of existential emptiness: the felt void is an affective state with causes—disrupted routines, isolation, cognitive fatigue, a mismatch between effort and reward—and no revelatory content. The theory has real systematic virtues: it is measurable, it treats the experience as variable across persons and cultures, and it explains what would otherwise be an ineffable negativity by redescribing it as the mind’s way of signalling depleted or unintegrated activity. But I also have a firm intuition, when I actually undergo it, that the void is not merely an internal symptom. It presents itself as disclosing something about the human situation: that there is a real absence at the center of my projects, or that no available description of the world tells me how to go on. Pascal in the Pensées treats our inability to remain quietly at rest with ourselves as a symptom of flight from our true condition, not simply as a passing mood. Which should yield? Give me the strongest version of the case for treating the intuition as an unreliable artifact of affect, the strongest version of the case for letting it count as a genuine disclosure, and a negotiated verdict: could the deflationary theory be amended to preserve both, or must one side be given up?
