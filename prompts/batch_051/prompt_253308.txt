So I got into a debate with my roommate after a probability lecture, and I keep coming back to this asymmetry in how we treat ignorance, and I genuinely can't tell weather it's principled or just comfortable habit. Here's the thing. If you hand someone an opaque coin and say it might be biased in either direction, almost everyone is happy assigning 1/2 to heads — no reason to favor one side, so split the difference. Same for a six-sided die you're told is loaded but not which way: 1/6 each, obviously. But as soon as the unknown is continuous, the principle of indifference turns into a cautionary tale. Bertrand's paradox: ask what's the probability that a random chord of a circle is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle, and "no reason to prefer any possibility over any other" gives 1/3 if you select two endpoints uniformly on the circumference, 1/2 if you select a radius then a uniformly random point along it, and 1/4 if you select a midpoint uniformly in the disk. Same words, same rule, no stable answer.

I want to know whether anything actually justifies the asymmetry between these cases. We keep using indifference-style reasoning for finite, symmetric-looking setups (coins, dice, cards) and treat the continuous case as an ill-posed problem rather than a refutation of the principle. So: give me the strongest defense you can of the claim that finite equiprobability is in better standing than continuous equiprobability, then try to break it. Because I can re-partition the finite cases too — "lands the same as the previous toss" versus "lands different", or "lands on 1" versus "lands on 2 through 6" — and if I apply the principle mechanically to those partitions I get different numbers. What exactly does the coin have that the chord lacks, if anything? And if it lacks it, does that mean we should drop even the coin-case indifference, or is there some restricted principle that survives both? I'm a stats major, not a philosopher, so I'm very ready to be told I'm missing something obvious.
