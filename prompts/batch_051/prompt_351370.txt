I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've spent the last week chewing on the old statue-and-lump-of-clay problem for a short piece about why our everyday category of "a thing" goes strange the moment you press it. A mate who did some metaphysics walked me through an argument that seems to show statues aren't really there, and now I can't see the trick. I'll set it out the way he gave it.

(1) Suppose the statue exists at noon on Tuesday, after the sculptor has shaped her lump of clay into a horse. Then the statue is either identical with the lump of clay or distinct from it. (2) It can't be distinct: two distinct material objects can't be composed of exactly the same parts, arranged in exactly the same way, in exactly the same place at the same time. (3) It can't be identical either, because the lump existed on Monday and would survive being squashed back into a ball on Wednesday, whereas the statue did not exist on Monday and would not survive the squashing; and if x is identical with y, then whatever is true of x is true of y. (4) So the statue does not exist after all — "statue" is just a useful way of talking about clay arranged horse-wise. (5) And there's nothing special about statues: by the same reasoning, a table is just wood arranged table-wise, and tables, chairs, mountains and ordinary objects generally are fictions; reality contains only the underlying stuff. That conclusion seems to prove far too much: shaping a lump of clay can't be a way of making the horse unreal, and I don't fancy denying my desk exists just to keep the argument tidy. But everytime I think I've caught the slip, it closes up again.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want the diagnosis precise: why the tempting step looked like common sense, what the main candidate repairs are (coincident objects, a different reading of "same thing", denying that the statue exists, or whatever else you think is doing real work), and which is honest rather than special pleading. I'd also like to know whether rejecting step (2) just lands you in a worse problem: if the statue and the lump are allowed to be distinct while sharing every part and every physical property, what makes it true that one of them is a statue and the other is a lump? I'd most like to keep that the statue genuinely exists and that there aren't two distinct objects sitting on the same pedestal at the same time — but if you think that pair is exactly what I can't have, say which one costs less to give up.
