Two people in my research group spent most of an evening arguing about a proposal we had read, and neither budged. The proposal was to build a software agent in a simulated environment, give it an internal "distress" signal activated by damage to its virtual body, wire that signal into avoidance and learning, and let the agent report its distress in ordinary language. The authors ask whether the agent ought to be treated, provisionally, as a moral patient.

Maya said the question was confused from the start. (1) The distress signal is just a variable. A ten-line script that prints "that hurts" when its virtual body is struck obviously doesn't suffer, so the mere presence of the signal plus the outputs gives no reason at all to think the agent has experiences.

Omar replied (2) that this tests a serious proposal against a deliberately degraded mock-up. The proposal's point is that the agent's whole behavioral economy — avoidance, learning, trade-offs, lasting stress effects — is organized around the signal. At some level of structural fidelity, the better explanation of that organized behavior is that the agent has something relevantly like pain.

Maya answered (3) that "some level" is carrying the entire argument. Either he names the threshold at which a causal role becomes an experience, or he concedes that every pain-flag in every video game qualifies. Since no one can name that threshold, she said, the concept of moral status does no real work for digital systems.

Omar said (4) she cannot name the threshold for humans either. She treats other humans as moral patients on the strength of structural similarity plus behavior, not because she has a crisp cutoff in mind; no one has direct access to another's pain. The digital case is weaker evidence, but it is not zero evidence, and where there is a nontrivial chance that a system suffers, precaution favors treating borderline systems as patients until a defensible criterion rules them out.

Maya replied (5) that this precautionary principle is unbounded: any program that says "I suffer" would get a hearing, the claim could never be checked or retired, and precaution aimed at avoiding the error of denying a real sufferer ends up exploding the opposite error of granting status to systems that feel nothing. A sensible policy has to manage both errors, not just one.

Omar answered (6) that the two errors are not symmetric. Failing a real sufferer is far worse than being overcautious toward a non-sufferer, since the second error costs only unearned consideration. And unless Maya can supply a substrate-neutral property that the simulated agent lacks but a human brain has, her refusal to extend consideration to silicon looks like the very parochialism she would reject elsewhere.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Please go through the exchange move by move: for each numbered move, say what is right, what is overstated, and what a stronger version of that speaker's point would need to add. Then say who is closer to a defensible view, and what a defensible criterion of moral status for digital minds should say about thresholds, the precautionary asymmetry, and the risk of over-attribution.
