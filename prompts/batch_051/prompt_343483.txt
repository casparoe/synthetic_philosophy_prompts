For a paper I am writing on whether "terrorism" is a neutral descriptive category or primarily a delegitimizing label, I would like a careful case analysis of the African National Congress's turn to armed struggle in the early 1960s — specifically the Umkhonto we Sizwe sabotage campaign launched after the Sharpeville massacre and the banning of the ANC. I know the history reasonably well; what I need is the conceptual mapping. Please analyze the episode through the lens of competing definitions of terrorism, testing the sabotage campaign against each of the following candidate criteria: (a) the use or threat of violence in pursuit of political ends; (b) the deliberate targeting of noncombatants; (c) the non-state status of the actor; (d) a communicative aim of spreading fear beyond the immediate targets; and (e) how the violence is framed — as war, crime, or terror — by both its perpetrators and its opponents. For each criterion, say how the campaign fares given what is commonly known about it: the choice of infrastructure targets such as power lines and government installations, the stated aim of avoiding casualties, and the defense offered at the Rivonia trial, which distinguished sabotage from terrorism. Then state clearly which definitions, if any, the episode satisfies.

Now run the analysis in the other direction. The fact that different definitions yield different verdicts on the same well-documented episode should tell us something about the concept of terrorism itself — whether the disagreements are substantive, merely verbal, or downstream of the label's political function. Give particular weight to the institutional afterlife of the episode: the United States kept Mandela and other ANC leaders on its terrorism watch list until Congress removed them in 2008, long after the early sabotage campaign and years after Mandela had become an international symbol of legitimate resistance. Does that belated reclassification support the view that "terrorism" functions to delegitimize whatever violence the classifier disapproves of, or can a principled definitional view explain it? End with an honest account of where the definitions go silent or beg the question, rather than forcing the episode to settle more than it can.
