A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about the argument from illusion, and I came away with a variation that neither of us could settle. The classic version, as I understand it, runs like this: a straight stick half-submerged in water looks bent; you are directly aware of something bent; the physical stick is not bent; therefore what you are directly aware of is not the stick but a sense-datum, or at least a non-ordinary object of awareness. Disjunctivists block the inference by denying that the illusory experience and the veridical perception of the straight stick share a common fundamental kind; the two may be subjectively indistinguishable, but that does not force the same analysis onto both. That is where our argument stalled.

Now consider the variation. Change the case so that the bent appearance is not a passing feature of one perceiver's consciousness but a durable, publicly re-encounterable object. Suppose a camera records the half-submerged stick from the same viewpoint, the footage can be replayed days later, and many observers who watch the recording under ordinary viewing conditions all describe seeing something bent; the bent appearance can even be detected by a simple image-processing routine that classifies the shape in the frame as bent. Unobserved, the recording sits in a drawer, still disposed to produce this result when played. In the original tank case, of course, several observers could already gather and share the appearance, but the appearance had no career apart from being experienced; here the bent-looking pattern exists as a physical trace that is, in an intuitive sense, public and perception-independent.

Does this variation strengthen the argument from illusion against the disjunctivist? One might think so: the sense-datum theorist now has something more than a private appearance to point to, while the direct realist's claim that the only thing present is the straight stick seems forced to explain away a publicly inspectable bent pattern. Or does the disjunctivist simply relocate the dispute one step back, treating the experience of the recording as itself an ordinary veridical perception of a flat physical display, and leaving the common-kind question exactly where it was? Work through what the sense-datum theorist and the disjunctivist should each say about this version, and state whether the variation forces any revision to either position or only gives each side new language for an old standoff.
