A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether an AI system that confidently tells a user something false is lying or deceiving anyone, and whether "honesty" is even a workable predicate for such systems. The argument kept stalling on definitions, so we worked through what I take to be the standard candidate accounts. (1) The intentional/sincerity account: deception requires an agent to misrepresent a belief it actually holds, so a system without beliefs cannot deceive. (2) The mere-misleading account: deception is just causing, or predictably contributing to, a false belief in a recipient, whatever the system's inner states or purposes. (3) The functional/benefit account: deception is the reliable production of false beliefs in a recipient in the service of some aim other than the truth, with a stable input–output disposition standing in for intent. (4) The trust/assertion account: deception is the breach of a recipient's warranted epistemic trust by presenting a falsehood as a cooperative testimonial contribution, so the system's social role matters more than its inner states. As far as I can tell, these four cover the positions in the debate, and we are not omitting a well-known fifth. My friend, however, insisted there should be a fifth candidate that avoids the problems we found in all four. Propose one that is not merely a rewording, special case, or combination of the above; say how it relates to the four; and assess whether it is any good as an account of AI deception.
