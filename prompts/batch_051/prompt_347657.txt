I cover the intersection of neuroscience and the philosophy of mind for a general-interest magazine, and in that capacity I occasionally serve as a guest examiner for an upper-level seminar called "The Neuroscience of Volition." For a short in-class response, I set the following question:

"Benjamin Libet's clock-timing experiment (1983) is widely thought to have shown that the conscious intention to move arises too late to cause the movement. Does the experiment in fact show that conscious intentions are epiphenomenal — that they play no causal role in producing voluntary action? Answer with close attention to the structure of the inference, not only to the empirical results."

A student submitted this answer:

"The relevant experiment is Libet's clock paradigm. Participants fitted with scalp EEG were asked to flex a wrist whenever they felt like it, while watching a spot sweep around a clock face; after each movement, they reported where the spot had been when they first became aware of the urge or intention to move. Libet found that a slow negative potential — the readiness potential, or Bereitschaftspotential — began over the supplementary motor area roughly 550 ms before muscle activity, whereas the reported time of conscious intention (W) averaged only about 200 ms before movement. The standard reading is therefore that the brain begins preparing the action roughly a third of a second before the person is aware of deciding to act; this, on Libet's own account, is why conscious will is an illusion generated after the fact, a retrospective confabulation rather than a cause.

Libet wished to preserve some role for consciousness, and he proposed that free will survives as a veto: we cannot consciously initiate the movement, but we can consciously block or permit it in the final 100–200 ms before it executes. Yet this concession is hard to square with his timing data, because the gap shows that the conscious intention is epiphenomenal with respect to the movement. Once neural preparation is underway, whatever the subject reports about an intention comes too late to matter. The experiment therefore supports a strong conclusion: because the brain's preparation precedes awareness, voluntary actions are initiated unconsciously, and rejecting that conclusion requires denying the phenomenon itself rather than rejecting the inference."
