I'm a student in an archaeological theory course, and I've been struck by a pattern in how excavation reports and museum catalogues classify artifacts. An object that can be assigned an obvious practical use—a flint scraper, a storage jar, a grinding stone—is recorded under economic or domestic categories. But when an object resists that treatment, the default label seems to be "ritual" or "ceremonial." A carefully polished stone ball with no visible wear, or an unusually shaped object found alone in a pit, gets described as having had symbolic or religious significance. The underlying inference appears to be: if we can't tell what a thing was for, it must have been for something non-functional, perhaps sacred. My seminar classmates and I have been debating whether this is just a filing convention or a substantive assumption about how meaning attaches to objects.

I would like to know what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes about the relation between material objects and the beliefs, meanings, and institutions of past people. Does it require a sharp divide between the functional and the symbolic, where symbolic meaning is assigned only when functional interpretation fails? Does that divide survive scrutiny, or is the practice merely a tidy but unjustified way of sorting finds? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of material culture behind this practice, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say whether the practice is coherent. Finally, if archaeologists instead worked with the assumption that every artifact is at once practical and meaning-bearing, what would have to change in how finds are classified, interpreted, and written about?
