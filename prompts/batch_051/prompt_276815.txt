Had a long argument with an old friend last night about privacy, and hes convinced its value is entirely downstream: losing privacy matters only because the information can be used to embarrass, blackmail, discriminate, manipulate you, etc. He kept saying that if you're the kind of person who wouldn't be hurt by any of that, you lose nothing. I kept pushing the line that being watched matters even when the watcher never does anything with what they learn. I'm trying to write a short essay about this and would like comments on the idea.

My working claim is that privacy is partly constitutive of having a self you can try things out and revise without an audience: being observed changes how you think and act, so the value isn't just avoiding downstream harms, it's being able to let your guard down. I've been leaning on this passage from Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis's The Right to Privacy (1890):

"The intensity and complexity of life, attendant upon advancing civilization, have rendered necessary some retreat from the world, and man, under the refining influence of culture, has become more sensitive to publicity, so that solitude and privacy have become more essential to the individual; but modern enterprise and invention have, through invasions upon his privacy, subjected him to mental pain and distress, far greater than could be inflicted by mere bodily injury."

The essay notes so far:
- Distinguish "privacy as refuge/retreat" from "privacy as control over information." The quote seems to run both together: retreat from the world vs. protection against mental pain from publicity.
- Argue my friend's view captures the second but not the first. The first is about the state of not having to perform, even if no pain or reputation loss ever follows.
- Worry: Warren and Brandeis are mostly complaining about newspapers causing distress, so maybe my autonomy/self-constitution reading is anachronistic.
- Maybe the 1890 technological panic is doing the real work, not the principle.

I can't tell whether my friend is right that im laundering a Victorian complaint about gossip into a fancier theory, or whether the quote actually supports the stronger claim. Comments on whether the distinction is real, whether the passage supports it, and where the essay is most likely to be wrong would be really helpful.
