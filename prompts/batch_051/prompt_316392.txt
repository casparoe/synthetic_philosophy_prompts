I have recently moved into a quality-assurance position where I must decide which measurement results to trust, and a senior engineer has presented me with an argument that I find difficult to accept but have not been able to fault. She reasons as follows.

(1) A measurement result is trustworthy only if the instrument that produced it has been calibrated against a standard.
(2) That standard is itself trustworthy only if it has been calibrated against some further standard.
(3) Therefore, a measurement result is trustworthy only if there is an actually completed infinite chain of calibrations, each standard vouched for by a higher one.
(4) No actual practice completes such a chain: every real chain terminates somewhere, in a physical artifact, a stipulated definition, or an institutional convention.
(5) Therefore no measurement result — including the one on the certificate in front of me — is trustworthy.
(6) But measurement results are sometimes trustworthy; the entire practice of measurement depends on this.

I would most like to keep (6), and I also find (1) hard to give up in a laboratory setting. But I cannot see which step to reject, or whether the argument is valid and the conclusion must simply be lived with. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? And if the regress in (3) is somehow not vicious, what general principle distinguishes a regress that undermines a chain of reasoning or practice from one that can be harmlessly accepted?
