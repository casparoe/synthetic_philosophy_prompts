I teach a graduate moral philosophy seminar, and we just read the last chapter of Sidgwick's *The Methods of Ethics*. He says, in effect, that he can't see why the dictates of rational self-love and rational benevolence should always coincide, and that when they pull apart, practical reason seems to give discordant verdicts, leaving the "cosmos" of duty a "chaos" — I'm paraphrasing because I don't have the text in front of me. I have some verdicts about cases where self-interest and the general good conflict, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them, and whether that principle dissolves the dualism or just relabels it.

(1) If someone sacrifices a small benefit to themselves to prevent a much larger loss to someone else, I think they're rational, not merely admirable. (2) If someone forgoes a modest pleasure now to get a larger personal pleasure later, I think that's rational too. (3) If someone chooses their own large gain over a slightly greater gain to a stranger, I don't think they're rationally required to choose the stranger, but I do think they've failed to respond to a reason that's genuinely there. (4) If someone asks, "What ought I to do, all things considered, when my own good and the general good conflict?", I want to say there's no all-things-considered verdict — but that feels unstable.

Is there one coherent account of practical reason that delivers all four of my verdicts, and if not, which would you overturn and why? Please also say what the resulting account says about a fifth case I haven't listed: a person who is indifferent to both their own long-term well-being and the welfare of everyone around them.
