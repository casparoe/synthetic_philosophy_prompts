An asymmetry I've never been able to settle. Suppose I get you to sign a contract by arranging the room so your sleep-deprived and distracted, with the signature line as the default; we'd call that manipulation and say the choice isn't really yours. Now suppose I get you to sign by offering terms so good that, given your preferences, refusing would be irrational; we'd call that a free choice. Both are deliberate causal interventions on your decision, and in both cases I can predict and control what you'll do. From a Woodward-style interventionist point of view — X causes Y just in case there's a possible intervention on X that changes Y while other things are held fixed — the two cases have the same causal structure. So is the manipulation/persuasion distinction tracking anything real, or is its just folk psychology we refuse to give up? Give me the strongest principle you can find that draws the line, then test it against the hard cases: a huge incentive offered to someone in desperate circumstances, an honest argument that knowingly exploits the listener's biases, withholding a fact that would have changed their mind. Tell me whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or dropped. And say whether interventionism actually helps here or just redescribes the problem — especially since interventions are themselves partly causal notions, so leaning on it might be circular.
