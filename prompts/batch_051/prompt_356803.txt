Take the Burr–Hamilton duel and run it through the honor/dignity/victimhood-culture framework. I know the basic facts — 1804, a sitting VP kills a former treasury secretary, Hamilton maybe wasted or mis-aimed his shot, Burr was ruined after — but I want the careful mapping before the verdict. Who in the episode is operating in which register: Burr, Hamilton, the seconds, the press, the publics they answerded to. What the honor code says each was obligated to do once the insults went public, and where a dignity code would have demanded a different move (a public retraction, a suit, walking away). And is there any victimhood-culture reading of the thing, or does that category mostly attach not to the actors but to how we read them now? Then be honest about where the framework runs out: the manic newspaper war, the politics around the 1800 election, dueling already being illegal and on the decline up north, Hamilton's own history with duels. Does the episode vindicate honor culture, indict it, or show the framework is doing descriptive anthropology under a normative cover? And nail down the one bit everyone argues about: Hamilton showing up and then probably wasting his shot — how do the honor-frame and the dignity-frame read that act differently, and which reading do the surviving facts actually support?
