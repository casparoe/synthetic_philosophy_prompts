I've been trying to think through what justifies my ordinary beliefs, and I've built an argument that seems to force a conclusion I can't accept. Each step looks unavoidable on its own, so I need to know which one to reject and what I'd have to give up with it.

(1) A belief of mine is rationally held only if it is justified by something; it cannot just be a brute fact about me.
(2) If a belief is justified only by another belief, then that second belief needs justification too, and the same demand recurs.
(3) This cannot go on forever, because an endless chain of supporting beliefs gives no belief a real foothold; and it cannot run in a circle, because two beliefs propping each other up do not touch the world.
(4) So every justified belief must be grounded in something I directly encounter that is not itself a belief and is its own evidence: a raw given that needs no further backing.
(5) But once such items are allowed, I see no way to exclude anything else a person finds immediately and self-evidently present: a vivid recovered memory, a gut certainty, a voice heard in prayer, an overpowering political conviction.
(6) So the same move that grounds my belief that this page is white also grounds the person who is convinced the radiator is speaking to him; the given cannot be challenged, because it is defined as the sort of thing that lies beyond questioning.
(7) An account on which plain perception and plain delusion are justified in exactly the same way cannot be right. It proves too much, because it empties justification of the very work it was supposed to do: sorting the well-founded from the merely felt.
(8) But the only alternative I can see is to deny that my belief about the page is justified the way I thought, which seems to throw me back into the endless chain or the circle that step (3) already rejected.

I would most like to keep the thought behind step (4): that my seeing the page as white directly justifies my belief. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost?
