After I retired from drug development I started reading philosophy of science, and there is a puzzle from my working life that I now suspect I only ever maneged around, never actually solved. A friend from my old lab has been pushing an argument about Simpson's paradox and I can't find the catch, even though the conclusion is clearly too strong.

Here is his argument, roughly as he puts it.

(1) Simpson's paradox shows that an association can hold in every subgroup yet reverse when you pool the subgroups.

(2) For any association you see in aggregate data, there is some way of subdividing the observations—by age, sex, baseline severity, region, time of day, whatever—such that the association weakens, vanishes, or flips. We just never check every possible subdivision.

(3) If whether an association holds depends only on how you carve the data, then the association isn't a fact about the world; it's an artifact of the carving.

(4) But the claim that a drug works rests on an aggregate association in a randomized trial: the treated arm did better than the placebo arm overall.

(5) Randomization doesnt escape (2). There will be some subgroup of treated and control patients—defined after the fact, maybe not even measured—inside which the drug looks useless or harmful. So the trial result is exactly the sort of thing that (3) calls an artifact.

(6) Therefore either randomized trials never tell us anything and a frightening amount of medicine rests on an artifact, or one of (1) through (5) is false.

(7) But (1) is just arithmetic and (2) is hard to deny in practice. So the argument seems to force us to either give up (3) or accept the first option in (6); either way causal inference from data starts to look shaky.

He says the real upshot is that causal inference from data is built on sand. I would rather keep the belief that a well-run trial tells us something about the world, but I don't want to just assert that and hope. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I had to choose, I'd sooner give up (3) than give up on trials, but I can't see what principle then separates the subgroupings we are allowed to ignore from the ones that genuinely threaten a result.
