I have been trying to repair a gap I see in the Principal Principle, and I have arrived at something I call the Disregard Criterion. The Principal Principle says that, once you know the objective chance of an outcome, your credence should match it — unless your evidence is what David Lewis called 'inadmissible'. But which evidence is inadmissible has always seemed to me under-described, so here is my proposal. A piece of evidence E is admissible for an outcome A at a time t just in case a rational agent who already knew the full chance theory and the history up to t, but not how A in fact turns out, would leave her fair odds on A unchanged if she were handed E. Put differently: admissible evidence is evidence you are entitled to ignore. I try to earn the word 'entitled': if E would move her odds, then E contains information about A that the chance does not, and an agent trading on E could systematically take money from someone still pricing A at the chance — which I take to show that ignoring E is not only allowed but required for the deferential stance to survive contact with the market.

I suspect I may have merely restated the Principal Principle in fancier words, and I want to know whether that suspicion is right. So: is the Disregard Criterion a genuine explication of admissibility, a trivial reformulation, or a strengthening? Does it get the standard hard cases right — a crystal ball, a forecaster whose private evidence beats the chance, facts learned after the chance was fixed — and where does it break most badly? Have I unwittingly reinvented a position someone already holds, and if so which one?
