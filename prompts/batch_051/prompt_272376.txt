In the first of his Orations, the fourth-century Gregory of Nazianzus defends his brief flight from ordination by drawing on the prophet Jonah. In one portion of that defense he writes: I was not going to submit to be pressed into the ranks, when I was neither ready for ordination nor prepared for facing the surge of life in the world. But what is far graver, I feared the holy ground itself, lest I should be like those who put on sacred vestments before they are purified, and so, in the words of Scripture, be found a man without a wedding garment.

I want to use this passage as a test case for two conceptions of obligation under a doctrine of predestination. On the first conception, a person's role in salvation is wholly assigned by God's eternal decree, so the only real question is whether one has been appointed to a function; hesitation about one's own fitness is at best a diagnostic sign and at worst a failure of trust. On the second, divine predestination works through, rather than around, an agent's own discernment about present unfitness; the agent can be obliged to refuse a call, not because the call is ultimately revocable, but because God's ordination of the end includes the moral means, including the agent's refusal when the agent is not yet the kind of person who should accept.

Analyze this as a real historical episode in which Gregory is the player and his flight is the option. What, if anything, does the passage show about the difference between Augustine's mature account of predestination and a more Arminian reading—not by asking which side Gregory would join, but by asking whether either side can even describe his refusal as a coherent act of obedience rather than a symptom of resistance? Then reverse the direction: if we take Gregory's refusal seriously as an obligation one can have, what constraints does that put on any account of predestination that wants to preserve real responsibility for the refusing agent?
