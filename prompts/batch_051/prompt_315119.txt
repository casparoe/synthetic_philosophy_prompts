I'm on the Memory and Reconciliation Board in a midsize city that's about twenty-five years out of a civil conflict. The peace settlement set up a public, searchable online Registry of Collaboration: around nine thousand names, drawn from victims' testimony, of people alleged to have informed, denounced neighbours, or helped with forced sales during the war. Most entries were never tested in any court — a lot rest on a single uncorroborated statement — and there was no real appeal before a name went up. The enabling law says the Board has to review the Registry at the twenty-five-year mark and vote to keep it public, seal it, or change it's terms. That review is happening now.

The victims' groups want it kept public and probably expanded; they say the dead and disappeared are at least owed the truth, and sealing the names would be a second injury. The other side — families of people named, some parishes, a few local historians — says a permanent searchable list has become a pillory: children get refused jobs and flats because of what a parent allegedly did, and the Registry now gets used less for truth-telling than for electoral dirt. They're pushing to close the public database and move everything to a request-only archive. A third option on the table is to keep the public database but clearly mark the uncorroborated entries and allow each named person an attached response.

Draft the Board's decision memo. I want a clear recommendation — keep, seal, or something in between — with the reasoning, including an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side rather than a strawman.
