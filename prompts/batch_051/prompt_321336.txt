I've recently gotten into philosophy, and Morgan's canon keeps coming up as if it's obviously right. In his Introduction to Comparative Psychology, near the end of the chapter on other minds, Morgan says something like this (I'm paraphrasing, not quoting): don't ascribe a higher psychological process to an animal if a lower one will do, but also don't deny the higher one when the behavior shows features the lower processes can't account for. My question: restate Morgan's canon in the vocabulary I actually use—null model, alternative models, overfitting, parsimony, maybe causal inference—as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the canon translate cleanly into how a behavioral ecologist thinks about modeling, which parts have to be fudged, and which have no counterpart at all. In particular, is the canon just a warning against fitting a rich cognitive model to sparse behavioral data, or is there something leftover—something about how we should stand toward other minds—that my statistical vocabulary can't express? And if there is a residue, is that my framework's defect or a sign the philosophers have smuggled in something extra?
