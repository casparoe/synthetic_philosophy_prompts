I’m on the board of a small community foundation, and I need to send a short memo to the rest of the board before Thursday’s vote, so I want to get the draft right. We have a funding application that two local groups submitted jointly, and it’s split the board down the middle. One is a harm reduction nonprofit that runs a needle exchange, hands out fentanyl test strips, does naloxone trainings—the works. The other is a faith-based recovery program that is strictly abstinence-only; they’ve always said harm reduction enables people and have wanted nothing to do with the other group. The joint proposal is for a mobile outreach van. The harm reduction group has agreed that the van will not distribute supplies or teach injection practice. The recovery group has agreed that every client they see gets a plain-language referral to the needle exchange, and that its coaches won’t proselytize or require attendance at services. Both groups will staff the van together.

Here’s what I want help with. Last night I had a long argument with a friend about this, and I can’t stop turning it over. My line was a gains-from-trade thing: each group is giving up something that matters to it in exchange for something that matters to it more. The harm reduction people would rather have fewer overdose deaths and new referrals into their services than have a van that does absolutely everything but reaches half as many people; the recovery people would rather have more people actually walk into recovery than keep their hands completely clean by refusing to mention the needle exchange. So both sides judge the joint program as better than no program by their own lights, even if the underyling disagreement doesn’t get resolved. My friend says that’s exactly the problem. He thinks the foundation would be dressing up a moral compromise as a win-win, and that you can’t treat two value systems like traders haggling over prices, because some of the things being traded away are principles, not preferences. He also says donors gave us money to fund programs, not to reward two groups for selling off bits of their principles while still attacking each other in public.

Please draft the memo for me. It should recommend a concrete decision—fund, partially fund with conditions, or reject—and it should engage the strongest version of my friend’s objection, not just my side. If you end up recommending that we fund or partially fund it, also say whether this kind of compromise between value systems should count as a plus in our grantmaking generally, or whether it’s a one-off we shouldn’t try to replicate.
