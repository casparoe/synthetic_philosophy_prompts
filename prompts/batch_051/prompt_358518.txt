Before I read any more arguments for or against minimum wages and rent control, I want the full specification of what a theory of price controls is supposed to get right. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of when a price floor in a low-wage labor market or a price ceiling in a rental housing market is justified would have to meet, where "adequate" means good enough to guide a city-level decision about whether to enact, strengthen, keep, or repeal an ordinance in a high-cost city with a tight rental market, a service-heavy low-wage labor market, steady in-migration, and a mix of controlled and uncontrolled units. The deliverable is the list of criteria and their motivating cases, not an evaluation of existing positions; put the weight on the requirements.

For each requirement: state it as a precise constraint on a theory, give the case, mechanism, or distinction that makes it a requirement, and mark it either non-negotiable or a preference specific to one camp. Do not produce a generic list such as "be plausible" or "fit the evidence"; each requirement should be specific enough that a theory could fail it while passing another.

The list must respect the following distinctions rather than averaging over them: short-run versus long-run supply responses in both labor and housing; incidence on parties who never complete a transaction (the would-be renter who is rationed out, the worker whose hours fall, the person who stops searching or never starts); incumbent tenants versus newcomers and incumbent workers versus new entrants; the difference between defending a control in an ideal market, in a market with one specified failure such as monopsony or search frictions, and in the actual market the city faces; and the non-price rationing mechanism that replaces price when a binding control creates excess demand (queueing, discrimination, favoritism, quality degradation, side payments). If a familiar rationale for minimum wages or rent control turns out to depend on collapsing one of these distinctions, the list should expose it. Also say whether labor floors and housing ceilings should be governed by one unified theory of price controls or by two domain-specific theories, and what a defensible answer on that point would commit one to.

Then say which subsets of the requirements are jointly unsatisfiable, and where exactly the conflict bites. I want that part sharp: do not just say there are trade-offs, but identify the specific combination of requirements a theory could not honor at once and why.

Identify the single non-negotiable requirement that does the most to eliminate popular positions, and say which positions it eliminates and which it spares.

Close with a compact table or short paragraphs scoring each of these camps against the non-negotiable requirements only: the presumptive anti-control view that treats any binding control as a distortion unless a specific market failure is diagnosed; the frictions-and-monopsony view that defends modest minima and limited rent stabilization as corrections; the distributive-commitment view that treats the control as a transfer to workers or tenants that need not pass an efficiency test; and the supply-first view that says the only durable answer is expanding supply and the control is at best a transitional palliative. For each camp, say which non-negotiable requirement it fails, or satisfies only by stipulation or by defining the problem away.
