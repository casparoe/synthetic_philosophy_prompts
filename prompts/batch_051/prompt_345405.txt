I've been teaching a unit on Moore's paradox — the oddity of asserting something while, at the same time, also asserting that you don't believe the thing you just said (the classic rain case). I tried to pin down when something counts as a genuine case of the paradox, and ended up with this checklist: (1) one person asserts, in their own voice and in the present tense, both some proposition p and that they don't believe p; (2) they're being sincere, not acting, joking, being ironical, translating, or quoting someone else; (3) the two conjuncts are put forward as a single overall statement, not as seperate claims made at different times.

Now here's the case that's bugging me. A senior engineer at a small company is asked whether the server migration can be finished by Friday. She says: "It can be finished by Friday — I accept that, and I'm making all my decisions on that basis — but I don't believe it can be finished by Friday." She's not being cute or sarcastic. She's read the plan, sees no flaw in it, and is acting on it, but she insists the full-blown belief just isn't there: her professional acceptance and her private belief have come apart. Is her utterance a Moorean absurdity by my criteria? Go through each criterion, say where it underdetermines the answer, and tell me wether the case shows I need to add or revise a criterion (for instance, something about whether asserting p expresses outright belief or merely acceptance).
