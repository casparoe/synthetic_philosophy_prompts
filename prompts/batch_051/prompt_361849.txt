Over the last few months my lab group has been arguing about wether bees feel pain, and the more we argue the less sure I am about what would even settle it. One camp says it is a straightforwardly empirical question: run the right experiments and you can find out whether there is something it is like to be a bee, just as you can find out whether bees can see ultraviolet. One postdoc keeps insisting that unlimited associative learning — roughly, the capacity to learn arbitrary, compound associations and deploy them flexibly rather than just falling back on fixed reflexes — is the decisive marker, but I don't see why passing that sort of test shows felt pain rather than just impressive learning. The other camp (which I drift towards when I'm tired) says no experiment can ever show more than nociception, avoidance learning, motivational trade-offs, and the like, and that the step from those observations to felt pain is a conceptual leap. I'm supposed to give the lab meeting next month, and I want to work out exactly where the line is between what a test can show and what it merely assumes.

So here is what I'd like you to do. Treat the claim 'bees feel pain' as a live empirical hypothesis. Design the strongest realistic battery of tests a lab could run in, say, a year, using only publicly observable behaviour, physiology, and learning. For each component, say precisely what result would count for the claim and what would count against it. Then be honest about what a clean positive result would and would not settle: would we have shown that bees feel pain, or only that they satisfy the operational definition of pain we built into the design? Is there a way to design the battery so it does not simply smuggle in the answer by picking markers that already presuppose bee-consciousness? And if some residue is in principle untestable, tell me where you'd put the residue and why.
