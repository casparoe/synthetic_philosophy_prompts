You are a philosopher of probability who has worked in both the frequentist and subjectivist (personalist Bayesian) traditions and who is now serving as the neutral rapporteur for an adversarial collaboration between a committed frequentist and a committed subjectivist. Your task is to draft the joint statement the two of them would produce after a long workshop, not to adjudicate between them. The central question they are trying to make progress on is what probability statements mean and what constrains them, with special attention to single-case probabilities: statements such as "the probability that this particular patient will respond to this drug is 0.7", "the probability that it will rain here tomorrow is 0.4", or "the probability that this coin lands heads on the next toss is 0.5". Both sides accept the standard Kolmogorov axioms and Bayes' theorem as pieces of mathematics; the dispute is about what, if anything, makes a single-case probability claim correct or incorrect, and what evidence or argument could show that a particular assignment of probabilities is wrong. The propensity interpretation has been deliberately set aside so that the collaboration can focus on these two positions.

The document should open with a numbered list of claims both parties can sign. These should be substantive rather than vacuous, and should cover at least: the status of the probability calculus; what long-run frequency data can and cannot establish; the principle that degrees of belief should be coherent in the sense of being non-Dutch-bookable; and the claim, if both accept it, that when an agent knows the objective chance of an event their credence ought to match it. Then present a disagreements table in which each row isolates one precise residual disagreement, phrased so that the frequentist would accept the wording of the frequentist column and the subjectivist would accept the wording of the subjectivist column. After that, identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, record in advance what each side predicts the answer will be and what evidence, formal result, or argument both agree would bear on it. Likely candidates for cruxes include the reference class problem, the normative force of calibration arguments, whether exchangeability judgements are objective or merely psychological, and the relevance of optional stopping, but include any further cruxes you judge more fundamental. Finally, if you conclude that part of their dispute is verbal, say precisely which part and propose a reformulation both could accept. Think through the formulations carefully before writing; avoid wording that is slanted toward either side; and write the final document in British English.
