I'm a philosophy of technology PhD student, and I sit on the committee that reviews the behaviour of the AI writing assistant our university is piloting for undergraduates. Every fortnight we get a batch of proposed guardrails: the assistant should refuse to write a first-person account of a suicide attempt for a creative writing class, or should correct a student who cites a hallucinated source, or should gently steer a student asking for a study plan toward a subject the model thinks suits them better. The committee has no framework, so each meeting collapses into 'students are adults' versus 'the tool shouldn't let them hurt themselves'; none of us are ethicists and we have an hour, so it has to be usable by non-philosophers.

I want you to turn the standard paternalism debate into a decision procedure we could actually run on these proposals, not an essay. Ground it in the usual soft/hard paternalism distinction and the rough idea that overriding someone is only justified when their decision is substantially non-autonomous or the stakes are high and irreversible, but don't let the word 'substantially' do the work. The procedure should seperate (a) refusing a request from (b) correcting the user and (c) trying to change the user's goals, since I suspect the justification differs across the three. For each, give the questions in order, what counts as a pass or fail, where the proposal gets rejected, and where the committee should stop and treat it as a values call rather than a policy judgement. Then run it on three proposals: (i) refusing all requests that could be read as instructions for self-harm, (ii) overriding a student's request to write a job application that exaggerates their experience, and (iii) an assistant steering a student who wants to study engineering toward the humanities because it thinks they seem happier there. Include at least one case where the procedure pushes us to approve something the committee would rather reject, and end with a short note on what the procedure cannot settle.
