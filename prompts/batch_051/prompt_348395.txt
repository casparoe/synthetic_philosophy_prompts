It has become routine to say that an AI system should be legible, not merely correct: a user should be able to see why the system did what it did, form accurate expectations about when it will fail, and trace the decision to something they can hold to account. I am trying to turn this demand into a working definition, and I keep finding that candidate definitions fail on one or another of the following cases. Propose a definition of legibility for AI systems, run it against each case below, say explicitly where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. If the definition should be audience-relative—one thing for a developer with full internal access, another for an affected user—build that relativity into the definition rather than treating it as a patch.

Cases:
(1) A linear model with five documented and independently validated features; the team operating it can state, in advance and correctly, the conditions under which its predictions become unreliable.
(2) A deep network exposed only through an API, with no explanations and no access to internals; the only way to learn its behavior on new inputs is to sample it.
(3) The same network, but with weights and activations completely available for inspection—though no one can summarize how it reaches any particular output and no one can anticipate its next serious error.
(4) A model that supplements every output with a fluent explanation citing plausible features and examples; the explanation persuades users, but it is generated after the decision and there is evidence that it may not track the actual basis of the decision.
(5) A short rule of thumb that users confidently believe explains a model—and that the vendor encourages—but that causes users to be systematically wrong about when the model will fail.
(6) A forecaster that is perfectly calibrated, assigning probabilities that match observed frequencies in the long run, even though no one can explain why it assigns 0.73 to this particular case.
(7) A decision tree with a million nodes: any single path can be checked by hand, but the tree as a whole is too large for anyone to hold in mind.
(8) A radiologist who detects a lesion with high accuracy but cannot articulate the features she is using, and whose judgments are no more inspectable than a black-box model's.

I suspect that legibility is being asked to do at least two jobs at once: one concerns a faithful relation between an explanation and the actual basis of the decision, and the other concerns what a particular audience can grasp and act on. If you agree, give me the two senses and say which of the above cases move between them; if you disagree, say why the term can be unified without loss.
