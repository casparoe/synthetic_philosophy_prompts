A friend in my philosophy of mind course spent an evening trying to convince me that self-deception is impossible in the strict sense, and weeks later I still don't see how to answer him. His argument was roughly this. (1) To deceive someone about p, the deceiver must take p to be false (or at least strongly suspect that it is) and must intentionally bring it about that the other person comes to believe p. (2) In self-deception, deceiver and deceived are the same person. (3) So if I deceive myself about p, I must take p to be false while intentionally bringing myself to believe p. (4) But if I already take p to be false, then, if the project succeeds, I end up simultaneously taking p to be false and believing p; that is not a coherent state for a single subject of belief, however conflicted a person can be. (5) And if I am the one intentionally producing the new belief, I know it was manufactured to satisfy a wish rather than to track evidence, and that knowledge will stop the belief from ever genuinely taking hold. (6) Therefore self-deception is impossible. (7) Yet we plainly attribute self-deception to real people — about their health, their relationships, their own motives — and we usually mean something more than metaphor. So one of the moving parts has to give.

I want a committed diagnosis rather than a survey. Please tell me which premise or step I should reject, or whether the argument is valid and the real task is to decide which costly premise to abandon. Explain why the vulnerable step looked unavoidable, and spell out what I have to accept about belief or about deception to keep self-deception as a real phenomenon. If you think the cleanest move is to deny that self-deception really is deception of oneself — that it is something like motivated, biased information-processing instead — then defend that move and say what the word “deception” is still doing there. I would most like to keep two things: that people really are self-deceived in at least some ordinary cases, and that belief is not simply adopted at will; I have no strong attachment to the demand that self-deception work exactly like deceiving another person.
