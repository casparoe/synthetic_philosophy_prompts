I'm putting together a module on interpretations of probability, and I keep getting stuck on a tweak to Bertrand's paradox that I'd rather you actually work through than summarize. In the standard version you take a random chord of a circle and ask for the probability it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. The three usual ways of making "random chord" precise — two random endpoints on the circumference; a random radius with the chord perpendicular to it at a randomly chosen point along it; and a random midpoint in the disk — give 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 respectively. Here's the variation. Keep the circle and keep those same three sampling methods, but change the comparison length to the circle's radius, which is, not-accidentaly, the side length of an inscribed regular hexagon. Work through each method for this new threshold and give me the numbers. Then tell me whether this version is more or less embarrassing for the principle of indifference than the original: do the three answers move closer together, are there non-degenerate thresholds where they agree exactly, and if they do agree somewhere, is that a point in favor of the principle of indifference or just a coincidence? Finally, do something I always forget to ask: say which step in your reasoning a critic should be most suspicious of, and what result or argument would change your mind about whether this variant still poses the same problem for the principle of indifference.
