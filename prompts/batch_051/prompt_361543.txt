I am a high school student writing a short paper for my school's philosophy club, and I have ended up defending an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept. I need help finding where the argument goes wrong.

The argument runs as follows:

1. Morgan's canon instructs comparative psychologists not to attribute a higher psychological faculty to an animal when a lower faculty suffices to explain the behavior.
2. The rationale for the canon is parsimony and suspicion of extravagant explanation, not any claim that animals are a special metaphysical case. The same rationale therefore applies when we explain human behavior.
3. Sound principles should be applied uniformly. If we exempt humans, we must point to a relevant difference; otherwise the rule is special pleading.
4. So, applied to humans, the canon instructs us not to attribute higher faculties such as deliberate, conscious reasoning when lower faculties such as habit, association, or unconscious processing suffice to explain the behavior.
5. For much ordinary human behavior, lower faculties do suffice: routine actions, social scripts, and many quick judgments can be explained by habit and automatic processing.
6. Therefore the canon requires us to withhold attributions of deliberate, conscious reasoning from humans across much of ordinary life.
7. That is unacceptable. It would make everyday explanations of human action systematically false, and it denies something we know from our own case, namely that at least some of our behavior is guided by conscious deliberation.
8. So at least one of those steps must be false.

I would most like to keep Morgan's canon as a working rule for animal psychology, and I would also like to keep the ordinary conviction that we sometimes act from conscious reasoning. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Is there a principled way to state the canon that blocks the move from animals to humans?
