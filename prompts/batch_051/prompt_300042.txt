The rule, roughly: if a quantity can take any value in an interval and that's all I know, I split the interval evenly, so equal-sized subintervals are equally likely.

My calls:

1. A coin has an unknown bias. Probability of heads on the next toss: 1/2.
2. A square's side length is between 0 and 1 m, otherwise unknown. Probability the side is under 1/2 m: 1/2.
3. Same square. Probability the area is under 1/4 m²: 1/2, since that's just the side being under 1/2.
4. But if the only thing I'd been told is that the area is between 0 and 1 m², then probability the area is under 1/4 m²: 1/4. That feels right too, and that's what bothers me.
5. The chord one I keep going back and forth on: a random chord of a circle, is it longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? My gut says 1/2, then I hear 1/3, then I hear 1/4, and I stop knowing what I think.

Is there a single principle that delivers all of these, or close? If not, which call (or which part of the rule) should go? And what does your preferred principle say about a sphere whose radius is known only to be between 0 and 1 m—what's the probability its volume is under 1/8 m³?
