I've been in a reading group on classical American pragmatism, and one gloss of Peirce's pragmatic maxim keeps coming up — a few of us have started calling it the cash-value reading. On this reading, the maxim amounts to the claim that the whole meaning of a concept consists in the conceivable experiential consequences of its object's being as the concept says it is; the meaning of an idea, on this gloss, is like the value of a check or a bond, which just is the stream of payments it entitles the holder to. The analogy is being used to license a strong conclusion: a metaphysical dispute with no conceivable experiential payoff, such as a dispute about whether the world is fundamentally material or fundamentally mental in a way no possible observation could distinguish, is empty — like a check drawn on a bank that does not exist.

I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher specializing in Peirce, and to audit this analogy rather than rehearse the standard textbook contrast between Peirce and James. Start by brainstorming the mapping before committing to it: what plays the role of the check, the bank, the teller, the redemption event, the currency, the payment schedule, the maturity date, the creditworthiness of the issuer, and the possibility of forgery? Then give the correspondence table explicitly. After that, identify the places where the analogy breaks, paying special attention to features of financial instruments it quietly imports — for instance, that a note's value is fixed by an issuer's promise, that its consequences can be enumerated in advance like contractually scheduled payments, that value is fungible and measurable on one scale, and that a note's fate is settled at a determinate maturity. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion (that disputes with no cashable difference are empty) from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion survives once the analogy is no longer doing the work, and if it does not, whether a better analogy is available — one drawn, say, from laboratory instructions, recipes, or mathematical definitions.
