A lawyer I know is working on a drafting exercise about what makes a ground of discrimination suspect in constitutional law, and she has produced a scheme that replaces open-ended judicial judgment with a formulaic one. In her draft, a country's new anti-discrimination statute creates a standing Classification Board to which any citizen may petition to have a trait recognized as a protected ground. The Board scores each petition on four published criteria: the historical disadvantage of the group, measured in part through documented patterns of exclusion; the group's current political powerlessness, measured through its underrepresentation in elected office relative to its population share; the immutability of the trait, understood as how costly it is to change or to conceal; and the trait's general relevance to the categories of decision the statute regulates, such as hiring, housing, and the provision of services. The composite score fixes the intensity of scrutiny applied to decisions that turn on that trait: the highest scores attract the most demanding review, middling scores an intermediate review, and low scores a deferential reasonableness check. Designations are reviewed every seven years and may be regraded or revoked, the total number of recognized grounds is capped, and the Board is financed by fees levied on respondents in the cases it processes rather than by general taxation.

I have been asked to stress-test the scheme, and I would like you to do that for me. Walk through how this institution would actually behave rather than how it is meant to behave. Who would petition, and how would the framing of a claim shape the score a group receives, given the latitude over group boundaries, comparison populations, the evidentiary record for historical disadvantage, and the measurement of political powerlessness? Where would the system first be gamed, and by whom: groups seeking designation, employers and landlords seeking to avoid elevated scrutiny, political parties, or the Board itself? Then give a committed judgment on which of these pathologies are fatal, which are merely annoying, and whether any can be fixed by adjusting the criteria, the cap, the funding mechanism, or the seven-year review without thereby restoring the very open-ended judgment the formula was meant to eliminate. Please write your answer as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and take definite positions rather than laying out alternatives for me to choose among.
