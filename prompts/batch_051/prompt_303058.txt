I'm taking a class where we've been reading a back-and-forth about whether human dignity is the real foundation of rights or whether it's just a fuzzy word that does no independent work; I think the skeptical line is the one associated with Macklin, who said that in biomedical ethics dignity just collapses into respect for autonomy and could be dropped without loss. I keep trying to test this against actual legal practices, and one example got stuck in my head. Years ago a French town wanted to let a local bar host dwarf-tossing events, where a performer of short stature gets thrown by customers onto an air mattress; he had signed a contract, was being paid, and argued he freely chose the job. The mayor banned it anyway and France's highest administrative court agreed, explicitly on the ground that the activity was contrary to human dignity even though the person had consented. Something similar shows up in laws against selling a kidney even when the seller is competent, well informed, and desperately poor, and in some countries' restrictions on certain invasive body modifications that people request for themselves.

So here's what I'm trying to work out. What theory of dignity does a legal system have to presuppose for consent to stop being decisive in cases like these? Is the implicit view that dignity is inalienable, so there are ways of treating a person — as an object to be thrown, as a priced spare part, as raw material to be altered — that are wrong regardless of what the person chooses, because that person has a kind of value they can't waive or sell? That sounds pretty Kantian to me. Or does the practice only strictly presuppose something weaker, like the state being allowed to protect people's long-term interests and public order, with dignity-talk doing no real work? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, and say what would have to change in these policies if we adopted the view that dignity is a useless concept that adds nothing beyond respect for autonomy and informed consent. And do the dwarf-tossing ban, the organ-sale ban, and the body-modification restrictions all presuppose the same thing, or do they quietly rely on different theories?
