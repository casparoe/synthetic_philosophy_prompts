I've been going back and forth on something and keep hitting the same wall. Lately I've been reading about the "hedonistic imperative" — the view that we should use genetic engineering and other biotech to phase out suffering itself, not just treat pain after it shows up but re-engineer the biology of sentient life so that the worst states possible are mild gradients of well-being rather than agony. And it struck me that Brave New World is, on the face of it, a novel-length dystopian warning about exactly that project. But I can't tell whether the novel actually engages with the strong version of the view or just with a cheaper version — social stability bought by conditioning and a pleasure drug.

So here's what I'd like: read Brave New World through the lens of the hedonistic imperative, and go in both directions. Specifically: does the World State actually try to abolish suffering in the hedonistic-imperative sense, or is it doing something importantly different — managing people for stability rather than eliminating suffering for its own sake? What would the view make of soma: a stopgap that the biotech project would eventually render unnecessary, or a hint that the novel and the imperative converge on the same endpoint? Is John's suffering supposed to be evidence that suffering has some value the imperative misses, or is he just a badly calibrated creature colliding with a world he was never designed for? And where does the novel resist the lens — for instance, does it mostly worry about flatness and the loss of struggle rather than about pain, and does that miss the imperative's aim of replacing pain with rich gradients of well-being rather than uniform contentment? I'd rather have an honest mapping that says where the novel distorts the view than one where the book neatly "proves" or "refutes" it.
