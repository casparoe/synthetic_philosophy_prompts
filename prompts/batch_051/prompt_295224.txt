I live in a small housing co-op, and we’re revising our health and safety rules. One member strips antique furniture in his third-floor flat and keeps about sixty litres of solvent and thinners in a cupboard on the landing. It has never leaked or caused an injury, but if it caught fire the stairwell would be impassable. The co-op wants to ban storage above, say, five litres, and he says no actual harm has come to anyone and the co-op is overreaching.

The rule we keep coming back to is Mill’s in On Liberty, Chapter IV: 'As soon as any part of a person’s conduct affects prejudicially the interests of others, society has jurisdiction over it, and the question whether the general welfare will or will not be promoted by interfering with it, becomes open to discussion.' Mill plainly did not have modern household chemical storage in mind, and he offers 'prejudicial effect on interests' as the test, not risk of effect.

What I want to work out is whether, on the best reading of that passage, merely imposing a serious fire risk counts as affecting prejudicially the interests of others, even before any injury. How would you decide this if you were applying the words themselves, the purpose of the harm principle as Mill states it, and what Mill would probably have said if asked about unforeseen dangerous activities whose harm is probabilistic? If those three approaches diverge, which should a defender of the harm principle follow here, and does the fact the risk was imposed without neighbourly consent matter more than the fact it hasn’t materialised?
