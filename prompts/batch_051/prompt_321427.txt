Over the past year I've become mildly obsessed with a disconnect in how my city handles infrastructure, and I'd like a professional philosopher of technology to help me reconstruct the implicit theory behind it. The city's official plans say they want to reduce car dependence and improve equity, but in practice they keep building things that do the opposite. A main avenue got rebuilt with underpasses that are too low for city buses, supposedly because of drainage and cost, while a parallel project spent a lot of money on adaptive traffic signals that give priority to cars from a wealthy subdivision during rush hour. There are also park benches with armrests in the middle, which everyone knows are there to stop homeless people from sleeping, but the city describes them as just a design for durability and maintenance. When I pressed a friend who works in the transportation department, he said I was overreading it: a bridge clearance is just a bridge clearance, data-driven signal timing is just optimization, and benches are benches. It made me wonder what view of technology, politics, and responsibility a pattern like this actually presupposes. Is the city committed to the idea that artifacts and technical systems are neutral tools whose political effects come only from how people use them, or can a practice like this count as a kind of distributive politics operating through design? I want you to think carefully and start by brainstorming several distinct theories before settling on one. Then tell me: what theory of technology and value does this pattern strictly presuppose, what is it merely compatible with, and what would have to change in the city's decision procedure if it adopted the view that design choices can embed specific politics?
