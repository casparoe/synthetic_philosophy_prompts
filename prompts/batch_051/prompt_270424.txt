Please design a 75-minute classroom activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of action, with about eighteen students who have already had two weeks on intentional action, that lets them discover the Knobe effect in their own judgments and then force-test the usual explanations for it. The activity should be runnable with only printed one-page materials and a board: exact wording for a small set of vignettes, a rating instrument, a procedure for pooling and displaying responses, small-group tasks, and a timed debrief. Arrange the sequence so that students produce the asymmetry before anyone names it: they should first record judgments about a harmful side effect and a helpful side effect whose structures are matched as closely as possible, with demand characteristics suppressed through blind conditions, prediction tasks, or a staged reveal. They should see the split in their own response data, articulate what explains it, and then test at least three candidate explanations against new vignettes they modify themselves: an account on which the agent's standing indifference or evaluative stance drives the judgment, an account on which norm violation drives it, and an account on which moral judgment itself drives the attribution of intentionality. The debrief should map what happened in the room onto the Knobe effect and onto the live dispute about whether the effect exposes something deep about the folk concept of intentional action or is a more deflationary artifact of how we report morally laden side effects. Give exact timings, instructions the facilitator should read aloud, what to write on the board, how to handle a student who announces the effect early, and a fallback if the expected asymmetry does not appear. I want a committed design rather than a survey of options: settle on the vignettes, say why you wrote them as you did, and decide in advance how you will frame the debrief, including which of the explanations the activity as designed best supports and which it merely leaves open. Close with an honest note on what a single live session cannot reproduce about the experimental research—sampling, counterbalancing, control conditions, statistical power, and the effect as it appears across large datasets—and what the facilitator must therefore supply. Do not include a bibliography or suggest readings.
