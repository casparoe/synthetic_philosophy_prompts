I am a PhD student in political philosophy working on family ethics, and in a seminar we have been discussing proposals to license parents. One version keeps coming up, and I find it much harder to evaluate than the usual in-principle disputes. The proposal is this: any adult who wishes to become a legal parent, whether through birth, adoption, or surrogacy, would first have to obtain a parental license. The license would be free and would carry no educational or income requirements. To receive it, an applicant would have to complete a short standardized course in child development and child safety, pass a written exam (with unlimited free retakes, an oral alternative, translator services, and disability accommodations), pass a criminal-background check limited to offenses against children or other dependents, and undergo a single home visit by a social worker. The license would be permanent once granted but revocable through the ordinary child-welfare system in cases of later neglect or abuse. Enforcement is the unusual part: giving birth without a license would not by itself be grounds for removing the child. Instead, the family would be enrolled in a mandatory in-home support-and-monitoring program for the child's first two years, and the home visit could be appealed to an independent board. The stated rationale in our readings was that children have an interest in keeping their future options open, and that grossly incompetent parenting can foreclose those options before the child has any say; the license is meant as a mild screen rather than a eugenic filter.

I understand the familiar objections — that it is illiberal, that it will discriminate in practice, that it burdens procreative autonomy, that people will game it. But I am less interested in whether the proposal is acceptable in principle than in how it would actually behave if enacted in a wealthy liberal democracy. Could you walk through who would likely pass and fail, how applicants would try to game or route around the requirements, what the mandatory home-visiting provision would do in practice, where social workers' and appeal boards' discretion would be exercised, and what second-order effects would appear (for example on adoption, surrogacy, abortion, undocumented residents, and family courts)? Please treat the scheme as a mechanism with incentives rather than as a moral symbol. End with a one-sentence summary of what you judge to be its single most consequential likely failure point, on a line beginning "Most consequential failure point:".
