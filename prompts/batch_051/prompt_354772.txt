I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and I am preparing an interactive piece that lets readers discover which theory of rights they actually hold by looking at their reactions to hard cases rather than at the label they would choose for themselves. Please act as a political philosopher with a talent for taxonomy, and think through the design carefully before giving me the finished instrument.

I want a quiz of eight to ten concrete fictional scenarios, each with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key. The scenarios should be self-contained and should not require any legal, historical, or scholarly background. The instrument must sort readers into at least these positions:

- rights as trumps, where a right ordinarily defeats aggregative welfare considerations but is not necessarily absolute;
- rights as side constraints, where rights are agent-relative restrictions on what a person may do, so that one may not violate a right merely to minimize the total number of similar violations by others;
- rights as threshold constraints, where constraints hold until enough is at stake and then give way;
- rights as instruments, where rights earn their keep by tracking interests or producing good social consequences and may be revised or overridden when they stop serving that function;
- a deflationary position on which the concept of a right is dispensable, because everything that needs saying can be said with duties, interests, permissions, and powers.

If you think there are further positions the instrument genuinely separates, include them; do not pad the tree with merely verbal variants.

The diagnostic questions should be answerable without knowing any philosophical vocabulary. They should force choices along at least these fault lines: whether a right against some treatment can be overridden to prevent a larger number of comparable violations; whether it matters who does the violating; whether rights primarily protect agency and status or track welfare and interests; and whether a right's justification rests on the good consequences of having it or on something about the person that does not reduce to aggregate consequences. Include at least one scenario about bodily integrity, one about free expression or association, and one that puts those two kinds of rights in conflict.

Before drafting the quiz, build the tree from the logical space: list the main diagnostic questions, the answer combinations they allow, and locate the named positions within that space. Flag answer combinations that no plausible position occupies, and say briefly why—because the position would be incoherent, practically unappealing, or merely underexplored. Also flag positions the instrument cannot cleanly separate; for example, if trumps and threshold constraints come apart only in catastrophic-choice cases, say how the instrument handles that instead of papering over the problem.

The scoring key should map patterns of answers onto positions rather than single questions. The tone should be journalistically clean and usable by an educated reader who has never taken a philosophy course: no jargon before it is earned, and no theorist name-dropping except where a descriptive label in the scoring key needs one. Start by brainstorming candidate cases and testing whether they genuinely separate the positions before settling on the final quiz.
