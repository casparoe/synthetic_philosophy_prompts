You are a professional philosopher specialising in formal epistemology and the ethics of risk, with a working knowledge of AI safety. I want you to design a teaching instrument for an advanced, mixed seminar of graduate students in philosophy, computer science, and public policy: a "which position on safety guarantees do you actually hold?" sorting quiz. Please think carefully about the diagnostic power of each question before writing it; if a planned question would not separate positions, replace it.

The quiz should sort respondents on this question:

When, if ever, is a safety case adequate for deploying a widely capable AI system in open-ended settings, given that no finite test suite can enumerate every possible failure mode?

Build the instrument from the following two cross-cutting axes rather than from a list of familiar labels. Make this scaffolding explicit in the scoring section.

1. **Guarantee shape.** What epistemic form must the assurance take? Distinguish at least:

   (a) a formal worst-case guarantee over all environments the system could face;  
   (b) a high-probability guarantee over a specified deployment distribution, with explicit monitoring for distribution shift;  
   (c) an expected-loss guarantee that trades failure costs against benefits under an explicit cost function;  
   (d) no formal statistical guarantee: staged deployment, audit, kill switches, and rollback are required instead.

2. **Tail-risk posture.** How should a respondent treat a sufficiently low-probability catastrophe? Distinguish at least:

   (a) lexically disqualifying: no amount of expected benefit redeems it;  
   (b) capped: deployment is permissible only if the estimated probability stays below a chosen threshold;  
   (c) continuously tradeable: a sufficiently large expected benefit can justify it;  
   (d) containment-sensitive: permissible only if the failure is local, contained, and reversible.

On the basis of these axes, construct a 4 × 4 matrix. Indicate which cells you regard as live positions (at least eight), give each a short, neutral name, and write one sentence on its most awkward commitment. For the cells you mark empty, say briefly whether they are empty because the combination is incoherent, unstable in practice, or merely undefended.

Then write nine concrete deployment scenarios. Each scenario should describe a proposed system, a proposed safety case, and four possible decisions or conditions a respondent could endorse. The options must be concrete choices about the case, not self-descriptions; they should not use the labels of the positions or the terms "worst-case", "average-case", "tail-risk absolutist", or any near paraphrase. Write them so that a respondent can answer from their practical judgements without knowing which position each option codes for.

Finally, provide a scoring key mapping patterns of answers to the live positions. If any two live positions cannot be separated by your nine scenarios, do not paper over this: identify the pair, explain which additional question would separate them, and state why you left it out.

The nine scenarios must jointly exercise both axes and discriminate the live positions as sharply as possible. Do not ask for citations, reading lists, or knowledge of the technical alignment literature; everything needed should be in the prompt. Write the final instrument in British English.
