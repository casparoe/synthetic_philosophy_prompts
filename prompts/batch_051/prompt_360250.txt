I recently read a general history of early modern philosophy that summarised Descartes on animals as follows:

"Descartes held that animals are pure machines: a dog's yelp is no more a sign of pain than the creaking of a wheel. His argument was simple — animals do not use language, language is the only outward sign of thought, so animals have no thought; and since feeling is one mode of thought, animals have no feelings either. His doctrine became the warrant for vivisection, since it allowed experimenters to dismiss every cry of a vivisected dog as mere clockwork. It survives today only as a curiosity, because once naturalists documented genuine problem-solving in animals it became impossible to believe that such complex behaviour could arise without any inner mental state."

I suspect this is partly a caricature, but I cannot tell which parts are unfair and which are just Descartes's view stated more bluntly than he would have liked. Would you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me which claims a careful scholar of Descartes would accept, which they would qualify, and which they would reject as distortions — and whether the closing inference, that documented animal problem-solving refuted the doctrine, actually follows from the accurate parts? I am not asking for citations or a reading list. What would help me is the conceptual equipment to audit this sort of summary myself: the difference between thought and sensation in Descartes's system, the role language plays as evidence for a rational soul rather than as the whole argument, and how much of the moral conclusion about vivisection actually follows from the metaphysics.
