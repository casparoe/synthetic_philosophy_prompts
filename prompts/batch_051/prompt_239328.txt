I follow a policy when reading arguments outside mathematics that invoke Gödel's incompleteness theorems — claims, for instance, that artificial intelligence is irreducibly limited, that the human mind cannot be mechanized, or that some legal or scientific framework is bound to contain unprovable truths. Unless the argument shows that its target can be represented as a formal system that is consistent, effectively specified, and strong enough for the theorem's arithmetic, and unless it explains how the syntactic limitation then transfers to the nonmathematical subject, I treat the Gödel appeal as decoration and set that step aside. The policy has saved me from much hand-waving, but I suspect it may also screen out legitimate analogical lessons. Is it defensible as stated? If not, what would a defensible version look like, and how would I distinguish, from the inside, applying that version from shielding myself against a style of argument I dislike?
