I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I have been following a dispute that recurs beneath public arguments about language: whether a word's earlier meaning has any authority over how it should be used now. One side holds that it does, at least sometimes — that a term can drift away from the meaning it carried when it was coined or borrowed or introduced as a term of art, and that this drift can be a loss rather than a mere change. The other side holds that it does not — that a word's meaning is fixed by the living usage of its present speakers, and that the move from an older meaning to a claim about proper current use is a version of the etymological fallacy. I would like to see what a year of rigorous, good-faith argument between these two camps would produce, and I want it written up as the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration.

Please draft that document. The two parties may be called the Original-Meaning Camp and the Usage-Sovereignty Camp. The document should have the following parts, in order.

First, a numbered section of Agreed Claims. The claims should be substantive rather than diplomatic: they should identify, at a level of detail both sides can accept, what is fixed by current usage, why ordinary speakers do not need to know a word's history in order to use it correctly, and which appeals to etymology neither side defends. But every claim in the section must be one both camps could sign without amendment.

Second, a Disagreements table. State each side's position in wording that side would accept as a fair statement of its own view, not the other side's caricature. Include a column indicating whether each disagreement is substantive or partly verbal, and say which word or distinction the two sides would need to align on before that disagreement could be settled.

Third, a Cruxes section. A crux is a sub-question such that an answer in one direction would force at least one side to revise something it now asserts. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side would predict or take that case to show.

Throughout, the document should deal explicitly with three distinctions I suspect are doing more work than either camp usually acknowledges. The first is that original meaning is ambiguous among at least three things: the earliest attested sense of a word, the reconstructed source meaning in an ancestor language from which the word descends, and the sense the word had when it was introduced into a specialized practice such as law, medicine, or science. The second is the difference between a word's history as a cause of present usage and as a reason for present usage; a person can grant the first while denying the second. The third is a difference among the objects to which this authority is supposed to attach: a word's extension, the features and distinctions it is used to mark, and its evaluative force. The two camps may partly be asking about different objects, and the document should say where that is so.

The statement should also work with the cases the two camps actually fight about, for instance beg the question, where a name for a logical failure has also acquired an everyday sense of raising a question; decimate, whose earlier one-in-ten sense sits alongside a broader sense of large-scale destruction; and atom, a word introduced for what was presumed indivisible and now used of something that turned out to be divisible. Use these or sharper cases wherever they expose a crux.

Where part of the dispute is verbal, say exactly which part and why; where part is substantive, do not dilute it into a compromise. The test of the finished document is that each side can read it and say: that is my view stated fairly; those are the claims I endorse; and those are the observations that would tell against me.
