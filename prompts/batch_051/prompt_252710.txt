I have been trying to work out what I actually think about naked statistical evidence, and my reactions on cases refuse to line up. In the blue bus case, a pedestrian is struck by a bus, and the only evidence linking the Blue Bus Company to the accident is that it runs 80 per cent of the buses on that route; I do not think the pedestrian should win against the company, even though this makes the company more likely than not responsible on the balance of probabilities. In the gatecrasher case, 1,000 people attended a rodeo, 499 paid, and the organisers sue one attendee selected at random; I would not make that person pay, although it is slightly likelier than not that she entered without paying. In the prison-yard case, 99 of 100 prisoners jointly attack a guard and one is a bystander, and no one can say which is which; I would not convict a prisoner chosen from the yard on that evidence alone. So far, my pattern is consistently sceptical.

But two of my other verdicts sit very uneasily with that pattern. If a patient took a drug and then developed a rare injury, and sound epidemiological evidence shows that the injury occurs twice as often among people who took the drug as among otherwise similar people who did not, I do think she should recover from the manufacturer, even though this looks like imposing liability on the strength of a group statistic. And with DNA matches, I find that I treat a match with a one-in-a-million random-match probability as very strong when the suspect was tested because of ordinary investigative leads, yet I do not think the identical match is enough on its own when it emerges from a trawl of a database containing ten million profiles. I cannot identify any difference in the physical evidence between those two DNA cases, which worries me. I also cannot settle the following variant: the 80 per cent figure in the bus case comes, not from a census of bus traffic, but from five independent eyewitnesses who each, individually, have only an 80 per cent chance of correctly identifying a bus livery.

I would like you to help me reach reflective equilibrium. Please propose the best principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures the verdicts I hold confidently, and state precisely how it distinguishes the evidence I am willing to act on from the evidence I am not. Tell me which of my verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to abandon, and whether my confident verdicts and my DNA verdicts can be held together or must be revised. Then apply the principle to the eyewitness variant and say exactly what it predicts there. Please open with your bottom line in one or two sentences, write in British English, and keep the whole answer to no more than 700 words.
