I was defending my recent attempt to live out of one suitcase to a friend who works in philosophy of science, and without thinking I called it Ockham's razor for a life. I meant it as a throwaway line, but now the analogy is nagging me. On the theory side, the razor says not to posit extra entities, mechanisms, or free parameters when a leaner theory does the same explanatory work, and it functions as a tie-breaker between otherwise adequate theories. On the life side, voluntary simplicity says not to accumulate possessions, commitments, or standing wants when a leaner life does the same work, where work means something like leaving life still usable, meaningful, or good. Both have the same shape: additions are guilty until proven necessary, you keep subtracting until something breaks, and leanness is treated as a virtue rather than a mere style. But the usual justifications look very different. The razor is defended as truth-conducive, or at least as a way to avoid epicycles and overfitting; voluntary simplicity is defended as good in itself, or as good because it buys autonomy, concentration, freedom from comparison, and so on. Is there a real identity of structure here, or is the word minimalism doing all the work?

Here's where I think the mapping might snap. The razor is ceteris paribus: it only prefers the simpler theory when the richer one doesn't explain anything extra, and once an added entity earns its keep, the razor goes quiet about it. Voluntary simplicity, in the movement and in older ascetic traditions, often sounds like a substantive ideal: people are urged to live with less even when the extra possession or obligation would, by ordinary standards, improve their lives. Is that a real disanalogy, or is it just that the two domains have different test for what it means for something to have earned its keep? The analogy seems to predict that a consistent simplifier should drop the objection the moment a given thing is shown to genuinely improve life, which would make voluntary simplicity collapse into ordinary instrumental rationality plus the empirical claim that most stuff doesn't actually help. That prediction feels false to what the ideal is trying to say, but maybe that just means the ideal is confused. Where exactly does the parallel hold, and does trying to press it teach us anything about either side? Please answer in American English.
