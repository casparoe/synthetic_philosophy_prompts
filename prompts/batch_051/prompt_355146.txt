One passage I keep coming back to in the Grand Inquisitor chapter of The Brothers Karamazov is when Ivan describes the old monastery poem about the Virgin visiting hell, and at the end the damned get a yearly respite and sing:

Thou art right, O Lord, very right,
Thou hast condemned us justly.

I read that not as a one-off bit of piety but as a miniature of what the Inquisitor later tells Christ: give people freedom and they will be miserable, because the burden is too much, and they will end up happier if you take over their consciences and feed them, and they will bless you for it. As someone who does welfare economics and behavioural public policy, I hear that as a claim about endogenous preferences or adaptive preferences: if the choice set shrinks, preferences will adjust, and measured welfare may not fall, maybe it rises. I'm not trained in philosophy, so I want the translation done honestly.

Restate the Inquisitor's argument about freedom and happiness in the vocabulary of welfare economics and behavioural paternalism—things like preferences, utility, welfare, menu choice, commitment devices, endogenous preferences, paternalism, and autonomy as a non-welfarist constraint—as faithfully as you can. Then report what does not survive: what the Inquisitor is saying that has no counterpart in that vocabulary, and what your translation had to stretch to fit. Finally, say whether the residue points to a blind spot in the economic framework or to the Inquisitor's argument doing something that the economics vocabulary rightly refuses to count as coherent. Please answer in British English.
