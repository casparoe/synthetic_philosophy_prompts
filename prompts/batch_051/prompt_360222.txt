Lately I have been catching myself applying a rule in arguments and wondering whether it is earned by the mathematics or just by impatience. The Bayesian convergence results are often glossed as showing that priors wash out: if two careful people with different starting assumptions are exposed to a long run of the same evidence, their posterior distributions should eventually come to agree. I have internalized this as a social-epistemic policy. When someone has looked at the same studies, read the same public record, and still lands far from me on a factual question, I treat that as strong evidence that at least one of us—usually, I assume, the other person—has failed to update, or is leaning on a prior that is not merely different but defective. I do not apply the same suspicion to my own starting point.

I am no longer sure I can defend that asymmetry. Laplace’s A Philosophical Essay on Probabilities says, near the beginning: “Strictly speaking it may even be said that nearly all our knowledge is problematical; and in the small number of things which we are able to know with certainty, even in the mathematical sciences themselves, the principal means for ascertaining truth—induction and analogy—are based on probabilities; so that the entire system of human knowledge is connected with the theory set forth in this essay.” I can read that as encouragement for the washing-out picture: since even our residual certainties are probabilistic, persistent disagreement should eventually become suspicious. But the theorem itself only says priors wash out under conditions—common support, correctly specified likelihoods, the true hypothesis not excluded, enough identically distributed data—and those conditions may rarely hold in real disputes. My question is whether my policy of treating persistent disagreement after shared evidence as a sign of the other person’s irrationality, while giving my own prior a free pass, is a defensible epistemic shortcut or a bias dressed up as a theorem. What has to be true about the evidence, the models, and the relation between my prior and the other person’s for the policy to be reliable? And which failure condition, if it turned out to be typical rather than rare, would most change your verdict?
