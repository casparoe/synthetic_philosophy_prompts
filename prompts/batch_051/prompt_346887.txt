Question: People are often told to be realistic about moral and political life, which is usually taken to mean that they should not let their hopes outrun what the facts support. Others reply that hope of exactly this kind—hope that goes beyond the evidence—is what keeps moral and political commitment alive. Is it morally wrong to hope for too much? Defend your answer by distinguishing hope from related attitudes such as prediction, expectation, and optimism.

Student's answer: Hope is not the same as prediction, because you can hope for an outcome while believing that it is unlikely. So hoping for more than the facts support is not automatically self-deception: self-deception is a matter of what you believe, while hope is a matter of what you desire. In fact, hope does its main work precisely when a good outcome is not probable. If the result were likely enough to expect, hope would be largely unnecessary. The real danger of hoping for too much is that it makes people passive, since they assume the outcome will arrive on its own. But that danger can be avoided if hope is kept strictly as a wish and is not allowed to alter one's beliefs. I therefore think hope that goes beyond the evidence is acceptable as long as two conditions are met: the person continues to hold accurate beliefs about how likely the outcome is, and the person continues to act toward the outcome they hope for. If those two conditions are met, hope itself cannot be morally wrong. What would be wrong is only expecting things without evidence or acting recklessly.
