I have been keeping a record of the verdicts my professor of philosophical theology gives when she is pressed on what final state a described person would justly receive. She always distinguishes desert from any prediction about what God will in fact do. Her recorded verdicts are these:

- She says of a person raised entirely outside the faith, who led a morally decent life and never repented of ordinary minor failings, that this person is not condemned but restored.
- Of a sincere believer who committed a serious wrong, repented before death, and made what restitution was possible, she says: restored.
- Of a person who encountered the faith intermittently but treated it as irrelevant, lived a comfortable and self-centered life, and died unrepentant, she says: annihilation, not restoration, and not unending conscious torment.
- Of a person who committed grave cruelties with a clear conscience, having been raised in an environment that presented such cruelty as normal, and who died unrepentant, she says: annihilation.
- Of a person baptized in childhood, who later drifted into indifference, lived a morally mixed life, and died without repentance, she says: annihilation.

Next week she will be asked about a new case: a person of ordinary intelligence with full and repeated exposure to the faith, who understood its claims, rejected them deliberately and persistently, and also lived a cruel and unrepentant life, harming others until death. Given this track record, should we predict that she will judge this person's just fate to be eternal conscious torment, annihilation, or restoration? Identify the principle or principles that fit all her earlier verdicts, say what each principle implies for the new case, and, if the precedents underdetermine the answer, give your best prediction with your reasons for preferring one principle over the others, and describe a further case that would settle which principle she is actually applying.
