I am not trained in philosophy. I came to Stoicism through the popular books and the therapy-adjacent material, and over the past few years I have also been reading the ancient sources in translation. The gap between the two keeps bothering me. Next month I am leading the monthly discussion at my local Stoicism meetup, and I have proposed a session arguing that the modern "dichotomy of control" is not a neutral restatement of ancient Stoicism but a distortion of it — and that the popular version quietly depends on commitments most people in the modern revival would not actually accept.

My position, briefly: the ancient Stoics treated the line between what is up to us and what is not as downstream of a larger account of what a person is and what makes a life go well. What is up to us is the use of judgment, desire, and choice; everything else — health, wealth, reputation, even whether an action we take succeeds — lies outside that, and carries only secondary value. That is why the old archer example makes sense to them: the skill is in aiming well, not in whether the wind gives the bullseye. The modern revival instead wants the dichotomy as a freestanding technique for reducing distress while continuing to care intensely about outcomes such as health, relationships, and career. So the modern version either stretches "in your control" to mean something like influence or effort — which is a different doctrine in need of its own defense — or it keeps the ancient meaning and commits its users to a detachment from their own bodies and from the success of their own actions that they do not actually live by. I want to argue that the revival should stop leaning on Epictetus's authority and either present its psychological claim on its own merits or give up the dichotomy as a principle.

The audience will be about forty people, mostly professionals in their thirties and forties. Several have told me the dichotomy of control is the most useful idea they have taken from Stoicism. One or two have read the Enchiridion in translation and will push back with details of the text; most will defend the practice on the grounds that it works and will be suspicious of anything that sounds like academic nitpicking; a few have read the modern books that add a third category of "partial control" and will offer that as a repair.

Write me a Q&A prep sheet for this discussion: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, in roughly the order they are likely to arise, each with a note on how damaging it is and phrased the way these people would phrase it. For each objection, tell me what I should concede, what I should hold, and a two-minute reply, and flag the point where that reply is soft. Do not give me generic textbook objections or a defense of Stoicism in general; give me the objections this audience will raise against my specific claim. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and say what makes it dangerous.
