At a seminar on contingency in history, a colleague dismissed a structural explanation of a major event by reciting the proverb "For want of a nail" — the one in which a lost nail costs a shoe, a horse, a rider, and ultimately a kingdom — and then concluded that any large historical outcome could have been prevented by an arbitrarily small change, so no structural explanation can be stable. I don't trust the move, but I can't say exactly why. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims this proverb might be used to assert — about counterfactual dependence, sensitivity to initial conditions, chains of necessary conditions, the brittleness of complex systems, chance, or narrative hindsight — and say for each whether it is trivial, plausible but limited, or false? In particular, which of these readings would actually license the conclusion that structural explanations of historical events are unstable, and which readings would leave the proverb true but perfectly compatible with such explanations?
