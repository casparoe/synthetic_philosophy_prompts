I'm a philosophy PhD student and I keep arguing with my advisor about whether philosophy makes progress. His view is that it clearly does, but only in a question-refinement sense: it doesn't accumulate answers the way the sciences do, it just makes some questions unaskable in their old form and makes certain positions unavailable except as historical reconstructions. So a present-day philosopher can't straightforwardly be a verificationist or a crude Cartesian in the way someone in 1920 could; the position hasn't exactly been refuted, but the distinctions and countermoves have made it a live option only if you ignore everything since. I find this story suspicious, because it might just be change plus a flattering self-description, an emperical claim dressed up as a conceptual one.

What I want from you: take the claim that philosophy progresses in that question-refinement sense, and tell me how you would test it. Design the strongest empirical test you can think of, say what kind of result would count for the claim and what would count against it, and then be candid about what the test could not settle even in principle. For instance, could you measure whether the set of live positions at a given time shrinks, or whether the questions asked in a field become more differentiated and precise over a few decades of journal articles? And if some residue is untestable, is that because progress is a normative notion, or because the claim itself is underdescribed? Don't give me a bibliography or citations, I just want reasoning. And write it all as continuous prose like an email: no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
