I have been reading the Vatican II document *Dei Verbum*, the Dogmatic Constitution on Divine Revelation, because a friend has been arguing that the historical-critical method is the only intellectually serious way to read Scripture. The passage that keeps coming back to me is from chapter III, paragraph 12, where the Council says that since the sacred authors are true authors, the interpreter must attend to literary forms, modes of feeling, speaking, and narrating, and to the circumstances of the writer's time and culture. It then says, in the translation I have, that the interpreter must search out what the sacred writers really intended, and what God has been pleased to manifest through their words, and that no less serious attention must be devoted to the content and unity of the whole of Scripture if the meaning of these sacred texts is to be accurately worked out. What unsettles me is the structure of the thing: it seems to hold together, in a single breath, the claim that the human author's historically conditioned intention is the object of inquiry and the claim that God's manifestation through those same words is also the object of inquiry, as though these are two descriptions of one act. I understand the plain exegetical point, but I cannot tell what kind of reading stance the document is actually describing. Does it mean that the historical sense is one track and the theological sense another, and that the interpreter runs both in parallel? Or does it mean that the historical inquiry itself, pursued far enough, discloses the divine intention as an additional depth in the human intention rather than a separate meaning layered on top? And if the second, what happens when the historical critic concludes that a sacred author did not intend, and could not have understood, something the Church has traditionally taken his words to mean? Is that a case where the two objects of inquiry come apart, and how does the document hold them together then? I am asking this as someone who used to treat historical criticism as the default setting, but who has recently become religious and now finds herself worrying that the Council is asking the same words to do two different kinds of work at once, without saying clearly how to relate them when they pull against each other. Is there a reading of this passage that makes it more than a hesitant compromise, and if so, what does it actually commit the reader to doing in practice?
