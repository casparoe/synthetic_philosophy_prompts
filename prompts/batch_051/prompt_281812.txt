Exam question: In this question, take the procreation asymmetry to be the view that (i) the presence of pain is bad; (ii) the presence of pleasure is good; (iii) the absence of pain is good even when no one exists to experience that absence; and (iv) the absence of pleasure is not bad unless someone is deprived of it. Explain how Benatar uses this asymmetry to argue that coming into existence is always a harm. Then assess whether the asymmetry is sufficient to establish that conclusion: identify at least one step an opponent could reasonably challenge, and explain how that challenge would affect the overall argument.

Student's answer:
Benatar's argument works by comparing a possible life with nonexistence. In a life, pain is present, which is bad, and pleasure is present, which is good. In nonexistence, pain is absent, which is good, and pleasure is absent, which is not bad because there is no one to be deprived. So nonexistence contains a real advantage—the absence of pain—while existence contains no corresponding advantage from its pleasures, because absent pleasures are not bad. Any possible life is therefore worse than never existing, no matter how much pleasure it contains, and coming into existence is always a harm.

The weak point is the fourth claim. An opponent might say that failing to create a happy child leaves the world with less pleasure than it could have had, and that this is bad. But this objection cannot succeed, because we ordinarily do not think that not having a child is bad for the child; there is no one to be deprived, which is exactly what the asymmetry says. The objection therefore assumes what it is trying to prove.

Once the asymmetry is granted, the practical conclusion follows directly. If coming into existence is always a harm relative to nonexistence, then every act of procreation harms the child it creates, and harming someone is wrong. Procreation is therefore always impermissible, and the state should discourage or prohibit it.
