I've been translating rhymed, metered poetry for a few years and have settled into a position I call the load-bearing form theory, which I'd like checked. The central claim is that a translation preserves a poem's form not by reproducing the source's meter or rhyme scheme as a surface pattern, but by reproducing what I call the poem's resistance profile: the points at which the source language's formal machinery pushes back against the poem's syntax or argument, so that a reader can feel the line trying to say one thing and the form forcing a compromise. A strict caesura that makes a line break tear an adjective from its noun, a rhyme that forces an unnatural inversion, a stanza turn that arrives before the thought does—those moments, on my view, are where form actually does its work. A translation is faithful to form exactly insofar as the target text creates comparably loaded moments at the corresponding places in the argument or image-structure, whether or not the devices match at the surface.

So I have defended translations that scan as free verse but are, I want to insist, formally exact: the target poem may abandon meter and rhyme, but it substitutes something else—syllabic count, internal echo, line-length pressure, a deliberately broken syntax—that applies the same kind of pressure at the same juncture. My collaborator says I am just confusing form with difficulty, or with my own feeling of effort while reading, and that I have quietly redefined fidelity so that anything I like counts. I want an honest assessment: is there a coherent and distinctive idea here once it is stated as strongly as possible, has it already been worked out under some established name, and what is the sharpest case the strongest version of it mishandles? Tell me whether the theory is salvageable and where it stops being a theory and becomes a license.
