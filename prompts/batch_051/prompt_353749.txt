I keep going back and forth on a clash about Woodward-style interventionism, and I'd like you to talk me through which side should yield. The theory pulls me hard: roughly, X causes Y when Y would change (or change its probability distribution) under an intervention on X, where an intervention is an idealised change in X that is isolated from other influences on Y and doesn't reach Y through some independent back-channel. I like that this gives causation a clean empirical handle, makes the correlation/causation contrast explicit, and welds causal talk to manipulation and control. But I've got a stubborn intuition that it gets the explanatory order backwards in two places. First, when I shove a chair across the room, the shoving feels like the paradigm case of causing, not like a case that earns it's causal status by passing an invariance test; the theory seems to be explaining the clear thing with the unclear thing. Second, and worse, the conditions for something to count as an intervention look causally loaded. To know whether my change to X is 'surgical' enough, or isn't acting through a common cause or through Y itself, I seem to need causal knowledge already. So the account can look circular: it characterises causation in terms of interventions, but interventions have to be picked out using causal judgements. My question is which should give. Should I treat the circularity worry as something the theory can dissolve, for example by spelling out interventions in purely statistical or epistemic terms, or by reading the theory as a schema we fill in from background causal knowledge rather than as a reductive analysis? Or should I treat it as a genuine crack, and either push toward a richer account that takes deliberate action as the unanalysed starting point, or demote interventionism to a handy formalism rather than a theory of what causation is? Give me the strongest version of each move, and say what would justify each one. As you answer, please separate what's more or less settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
