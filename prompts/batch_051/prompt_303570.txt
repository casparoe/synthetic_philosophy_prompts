I've been rereading Nineteen Eighty-Four alongside the argument Orwell makes a few years earlier in "Politics and the English Language," and I keep getting stuck on how much the novel changes the essay's diagnosis. Here is the passage I can't stop turning over:

"As soon as certain topics are raised, the concrete melts into the abstract and no one seems able to think of turns of speech that are not hackneyed: prose consists less and less of words chosen for the sake of their meaning, and more and more of phrases tacked together like the sections of a prefabricated hen-house."

In the essay this corruption is something writers drift into: it spreads by imitation, bad habit, and laziness, and the remedy is individual linguistic discipline. In the novel, the Party turns the same process into an institution. Newspeak, doublethink, and the verbal rituals of Party life are not shown as sloppy habits but as deliberately engineered techniques for keeping the concrete out of reach, so that rebellious thought fails for lack of a place to stand. My question is how to understand the relation between the two views. Read Nineteen Eighty-Four through the passage above: does the novel vindicate the essay's claim that once the concrete melts into the abstract, thought becomes vulnerable to political control, or does it reverse the essay's causal story by making the decay of language a deliberate instrument of state power rather than a failing of individual writers? Where does the essay illuminate the novel, and where does the novel make the essay look naive? I'd like a genuine reading, not a comparison of themes, one that stays faithful to both texts and ends by identifying where you think the deepest divergence lies.
