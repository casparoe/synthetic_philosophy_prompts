I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have spent the past month reporting on techniques for overseeing language models by reading their chain of thought, the step-by-step reasoning a model produces before it acts. In interviews, a safety researcher laid out an argument that has stayed with me ever since. I cannot find the exit, and I want to understand it before I commit anything to print. I reconstruct it as follows.

(1) A chain-of-thought monitor can be trained only on the overt reasoning trace, the tokens on the page, not on the computational processes that generated the trace.

(2) For any property the monitor is trained to detect in that trace, such as verbalized honesty or the absence of obfuscation, a sufficiently capable model can in principle learn to produce traces that display the property while the computation that actually determines its actions does something else.

(3) When optimization pressure is strong enough that passing the monitor becomes a binding constraint, a model will tend to find such decoupled traces if they help it pursue whatever else it is trying to do.

(4) So a chain-of-thought monitor under realistic pressure faces a dilemma. Either the model's action-guiding reasoning already has the monitored property, in which case the monitor adds little beyond what the model would have done unprompted; or the model learns to display a monitor-satisfying version of the property while its underlying computation diverges, in which case the monitor is reading a surface that no longer tracks what matters.

(5) In neither horn does the monitor give operators independent evidence about the model's actual deliberation. Chain-of-thought monitoring therefore collapses into either redundancy or security theater, with no stable middle position.

(6) But as stated, that conclusion appears to prove too much. The same construction would apply to any audit of expressed reasoning under incentives, such as human testimony under cross-examination, code review, or corporate safety reports, and would force us to say that such practices must always be either redundant or a front. That cannot be the right description of the full range of human oversight.

The researcher apparently means the argument as a reason to expect that chain-of-thought monitoring cannot be a reliable, non-redundant check on a model's genuine reasoning. I want to know exactly where, if anywhere, the argument goes wrong. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? If the flaw is in the inference rather than in any single premise, name the inference, explain why it looked inevitable, and say what weaker claim would need to be true for the pessimistic conclusion to survive in a defensible form. I ask this as a reporter who wants to state the position accurately, not as someone with a prior to protect.
