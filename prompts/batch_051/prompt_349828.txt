I do compliance work for an aquaculture certification scheme and I keep tripping over a double standard that nobody on the commitee can defend. Our finfish standard says farmed salmon have to be stunned before slaughter — percussive or electrical — and sets handling rules meant to reduce stress, with actual audit points attached. The crustacean chapter, for shrimp and prawns and the crabs in some supply chains, has essentially nothing at the moment of killing: they can be chilled slowly on ice, or go into the boiling water or the peeling machine alive, and the only audit points concern water quality, biosecurity, and desease. When I ask why, the answers are "that's how the industry works" and "they're just bugs, they don't feel it the same way."

I'm not asking for a campaign, I'm asking what the standard actually presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit theory of sentience, or moral standing, or welfare, that makes it coherent to say a salmons pain is worth regulating but a shrimps pain is not. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit with, and flag where it is underdetermined — for example, is the line really about nociception vs felt pain, vertebrates vs invertebrates, strength of evidence, public perception, or tractability? Then tell me what the standard would have to look like if we replaced that implicit view with (a) a sentientist view on which any being with any capacity for felt pain counts, and (b) a precautionary view on which we regulate whenever the evidence is merely ambiguous or hard to interpret. And if the standard's implicit theory is internally inconsistent, name the inconsistency.
