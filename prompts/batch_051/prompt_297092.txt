At a writers' residency, two historical novelists I respect argued into the night over the familiar claim that history is written by the victors, and I could not referee them. I am drafting a novel narrated by the survivors of a defeated insurrection, so the question is not idle for me. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: assess each move, say who is right about which point, identify where each overreaches, state what a defensible version of the claim would have to assert, and tell me whether the dispute is ultimately about power over the record or about a stronger relativism about historical truth that neither has defended.

(1) The first called the claim close to a truism: the side that wins a war or revolution comes to control the archives, the school curriculum, the monuments, and the publishers, so the large-scale story later generations inherit is systematically the conqueror's story.

(2) The second said this was too strong. Later memory is often dominated by the defeated: the sack of Troy is most memorably preserved in plays written from the losers' side; the defeated side in the American Civil War supplied the account taught in many schools for generations; exile and liberation narratives have outlasted the empires that tried to suppress them.

(3) The first replied that such cases only push the power one step back. A defeated people's account survives when some later power finds it useful — a regional elite in need of a founding story, a diaspora that builds institutions, a successor empire that romanticizes its fallen rival. The sieve is still power; it is just not the original battlefield's power.

(4) The second answered that the thesis had become unfalsifiable. If a winner's story survives, that is credited to power; if a loser's story survives, that too is credited to power. A claim that absorbs either outcome while remaining trivially true has stopped doing historical work.

(5) The first conceded that the word victor was doing too much work and restated: what acquires institutional authority — the grand narrative archived, taught, and funded — is fixed by whichever institution dominates the society in question, whatever its relation to the original victory. That, she said, is testable: trace whose past gets a state archive and whose survives only as a footnote.

(6) The second said that even this version mistakes custody of the archive for authorship of the past. Institutional control does not ensure that later generations believe the official story, and historians — and novelists — regularly overturn it from marginal sources. If a loser's account can later win the culture, then the power that writes history is more diffuse than institutional dominance, and the slogan trades a true point about record-keeping for a false one about whose story lasts.
