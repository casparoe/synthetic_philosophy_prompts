I am a science journalist who writes about the mind and the brain for a general readership, and I am more fluent in the vocabulary of scientific modeling than in academic metaphysics: observable variables, hidden constructs, model fit, identifiability, underdetermination, and the difference between a model that predicts new observations and one that merely redescribes them. I would like to use that vocabulary to get a genuine grip on the hard problem of consciousness, not just a loose analogy.

Restate the hard problem, in the form associated with David Chalmers, in that modeling vocabulary. For present purposes, treat the position as making four connected claims: first, that the easy problems of consciousness are those that ask how a physical system performs a function—discriminating stimuli, integrating information, reporting its own states, controlling behavior—while the hard problem asks why any performance of those functions is accompanied by subjective experience at all; second, that there is something it is like to be a conscious system, and this what-it-is-like character is the thing to be explained; third, that a complete description of functions and physical mechanisms leaves an explanatory gap, because it is always a further question why those mechanisms feel like something from the inside; and fourth, that consciousness is therefore a further fact over and above the functional and physical facts.

Go through those four claims one by one. For each, say what translates cleanly into the vocabulary of observables, hidden constructs, model fit, identifiability, and underdetermination; what translates only with distortion; and what has no expression in that vocabulary at all. Do not reach for the usual philosophical terms except as items to be translated; the exercise is to force the position through the modeling vocabulary and see what survives.

Then deliver the residue report. Does the vocabulary fail because it is too coarse, or does the failed translation show that the hard problem is less well posed than it looks? In particular, address whether an unobservable further fact can be represented as a hidden construct or as an identifiability failure, and whether the hard problem reduces to the claim that even ideal observations would underdetermine the choice between models that make identical predictions. Be explicit about what the translation cannot carry, and say whether that residue indicts the vocabulary or the original position.
