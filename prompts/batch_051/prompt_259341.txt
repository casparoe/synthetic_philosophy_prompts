Retired spectroscopist here — got into philosophy of physics about six months ago, so excuse the gaps. There's a variant of the many-worlds Born-rule puzzle I havent seen anyone lay out cleanly, and I'd like you to actually work through it rather than point me at the literature.

Start with the usual unequal-amplitude branching. Alice is about to make an ideal measurement on a two-outcome observable. The Up branch has Born weight 0.9 and the Down branch has 0.1. In many-worlds, both happen. Before the measurement, she has to choose between two bets, each costing $1. Bet U pays $10 if the outcome is Up and $0 if Down; Bet D pays $0 if Up and $10 if Down. Born-rule reasoning says take Bet U: its expected payout is $9, versus $1 for Bet D. A naive branch-counter says there is one Up branch and one Down branch, so each bet has an expected payout of $5, and the 0.9/0.1 weights are left unexplained.

Now the variation. After the measurement, but before Alice learns the result and before any payout, the Down branch is run through a harmless decoherence widget. The widget splits the Down branch into 1000 fine-grained subbranches. From Alice's point of view, every one of those 1000 Down subbranches is identical: same memories, same lab, same bank account, same payout. They differ only in, say, a few thousand environmental photons scattering one way rather than another. The Up branch is left as a single branch.

A naive branch-counter now has to say Down is about 1000 times as likely as Up, so Bet D has expected payout around $9.99 and Alice should switch to D. The Born-rule theorist says the widget changed nothing that matters: the weights are still 0.9 and 0.1, so Bet U remains the rational choice.

Work through what each standard response to the Born-rule problem should say about this exact setup: branch counting; amplitude-weighted self-locating uncertainty; the decision-theoretic approach that tries to derive the Born rule from rational preferences; and any other account of probability in a branching universe you think handles it. Does the widget really create new branches in the sense relevant to probability, or does it expose a flaw in the very idea of counting branches? If Alice should ignore the extra Down subbranches, exactly what principle lets her treat them as not counting, and does the decision-theoretic approach survive the fact that there are now 1001 descendant versions of her choosing payoffs, not two? I want the argument worked through with the numbers, not a reading list.
