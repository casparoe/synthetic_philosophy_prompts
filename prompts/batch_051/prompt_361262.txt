My betting club's rulebook says: 'No member may enter into a series of bets such that the member is guaranteed to lose money overall, even if each individual bet is acceptable to the member at the time the bet is made.' The clause was written to stop the classic money pump: a person with intransitive preferences simultaneously prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, and can be charged for a cycle of trades. But here is a case the drafters didn't have in mind. A member has hyperbolic discounting: she prefers $10 tomorrow to $12 in a week when she is deciding now, but when tomorrow arrives she prefers $12 a week later over $10 now. A bookie knows this and offers a sequence of small trades across several days, each of which she accepts because it looks good by her then-current discount function; the trades cumulatively leave her worse off than doing nothing. She never has the cycle as a single state of preference at one time, and at no point does she knowingly choose a series of bets at once. Does the rule as written catch her? If the rule's purpose is to protect members from exploitable preference structures, does that cover a member whose own future self is the counterparty to her past self? What would the drafters have said if someone had explained hyperbolic discounting at the drafting meeting? I care about the interpretive question, not about wether she should be expelled.
