Epistemic status: half-baked, haven't read the actual literature, just been chewing on this since the last few posts about digital minds.

I'm trying to write an essay with the working title "the boundary is not in the physics" and I'd like comments on the notes before I draft it. The claim is that when rationalists/EA-types ask which computations matter morally, they treat it as one question, but it's actually two, and the second one gets almost no airtime. The attribution question is: given a lump of atoms, which of the things it does count as implementing the morally relevant computation? The boundary question is: where does one such computation end and another begin, i.e. how many patients are in the machine next door?

My argument is that both are underdetermined by the hardware, in the strong sense that there's no fact of the matter until you pick a descriptive vocabulary and a carving-up into parts, inputs, and outputs. For attribution: with a loose-enough computational description, a thermostat or a reed flexing in wind is doing a little bit of self-modeling, and with a strict-enough one even a sleeping human brain might not clear the bar. I don't see a principled place to draw the line that isn't just a free parameter. The usual candidates (global workspace, integrated information, predictive processing) don't close the gap, they just move the free parameter further inside the theory: now you have to choose what counts as "the system" for phi, or what counts as a "module" for the workspace.

The boundary part is weirder and I think more original. Take a mind running in two redundant emulations at once. Is that one patient, two patients, or 1.5? The standard answer is that it depends on whether the two emulations share "the same experiences," but that just pushes the question into what makes experiences numerically the same, which is the same boundary problem again. Now slow one copy by 10^5. Now feed them slightly different inputs. At no point along this continuum does a new mind pop into existence; counting minds is an artifact of how you've drawn the boxes around the computation. I want to defend the line that moral patienthood is not a property of a physical system simpliciter, but of a (computation, decomposition) pair, and that the honest move is to sum moral weight over decompositions weighted by something like simplicity or predictive power, the way Solomonoff-ish priors sum over programs. Consequence: "how much moral weight is in this room" might just not have a sharp answer, and copies, backups, and dreamless sleep are graded cases rather than hard on/off switches.

I probably have the jargn slightly off and some of this might be reinventing existing views badly, which is fine, I'd rather know now.

So: exactly five comments on these notes, please. Each a sentence or two, no introduction, no conclusion. Prioritize whatever five comments would most improve the essay, e.g. the weakest step, the strongest objection, an assumption I'm not noticing, an implication I should have drawn, and where I'd need to get more formal.
