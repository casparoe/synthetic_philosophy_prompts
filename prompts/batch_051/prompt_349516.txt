We often blame a person whose strength expresses itself in domination or cruelty, as though he could have held that strength in check, while we tend to excuse or even praise a person whose weakness expresses itself in timidity, meekness, or failure to assert himself. In the First Essay of The Genealogy of Morals, Nietzsche writes: "To require of strength that it should not express itself as strength, that it should not be a wish to overpower, a wish to overthrow, a wish to become master, a thirst for enemies and antagonisms and triumphs, is just as absurd as to require of weakness that it should express itself as strength. A quantum of force is just such a quantum of movement, will, action—rather it is nothing else than just those very phenomena of moving, willing, acting, and can only appear otherwise in the misleading errors of language (and the fundamental fallacies of reason which have become petrified therein), which understands, and understands wrongly, all working as conditioned by a worker, by a 'subject.'" Does the asymmetry in our ordinary judgments rest on the very error Nietzsche describes, or can it be defended without treating the strong person's act as more optional than the weak person's failure to act? If you defend it, defend it against the case where the strong person's harm and the weak person's inaction are exactly parallel as expressions of their respective natures.
