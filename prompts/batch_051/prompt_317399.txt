I've got myself into a proper tangle about higher-order evidence—evidence about how good your own reasoning is—and about the usual thought that you ought to correct your first-order belief in light of it, on pain of a kind of epistemic akrasia (believing p while also believing your evidence doesn't support p). Could you act as a professional epistemologist who works on this stuff and walk me through my argument? I'd like you to think slowly, and start by brainstorming the strongest reading of each premise before you try to knock any of them down.

Here it is. (1) Higher-order evidence about one of your cognitive faculties is evidence about whether that faculty tracks the truth. (2) To decide how far to trust such evidence, you have to exercise some cognitive faculty: you weigh it, check whether it really applies to you, maybe run a little self-test. (3) If the evidence concerns a faculty that is also involved in that weighing, checking, and testing, then your assessment of the evidence is produced by the very faculty the evidence calls into question. (4) A conclusion about whether to trust a suspect faculty, when that conclusion is itself produced by that faculty, carries no rational weight—like asking a witness to testify that his own testimony is unreliable and then believing him. (5) Therefore, in such a case, higher-order evidence gives you no rational route to lowering your confidence: every route goes through the faculty the evidence indicts.

I'd have thought the rational thing is to lower my confidence in the conclusions I'm now drawing. But the argument says I can't, because the work of understanding the doctor's warning, judging that it applies to me, and so on, is all done with the same degraded reasoning.
