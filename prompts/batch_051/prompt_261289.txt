Act as a professional philosopher working on value theory and the ethics of economic growth. I read rationalist blogs and forums regularly, and I want a careful, structured argument, not a survey of positions. Start by making the argument's premises explicit, then identify which premise the development puts pressure on, and then give a clear verdict. Before committing, brainstorm the candidate repairs and what each one costs. Write in American English.

I want you to stress-test the instrumental defense of prosperity: the view that material abundance and per-capita economic growth are morally important because, and only because, they buy goods that matter for their own sake — longer and healthier lives, education, freedom from material want, higher average life satisfaction. That argument looked strongest when marginal income in a growing economy bought mostly non-positional goods: more food, better medicine, safer housing, goods whose value does not depend on outranking one's neighbors.

Now consider a development that makes this uncomfortable. In the already-rich countries, most additional consumption is increasingly positional: the marginal dollar buys a better rank in a zero-sum contest — admission to a selective school, a house in a preferred neighborhood, a status signal — and one person's rank gain is another's loss. Suppose, alongside that, decades of survey data show average self-reported life satisfaction in those countries has stayed roughly flat even while average income has doubled. If the instrumental view is right on its own terms, further abundance in such countries would now seem to be morally inert.

Does the instrumental defense of prosperity survive this change, or does it need repair? Which premise is under real pressure — the income-to-well-being link, the assumption that consumption is mostly non-positional, or the choice of average life satisfaction as the yardstick? Is there a defensible repair that preserves the moral importance of abundance without turning GDP into a fetish, for example abundance as insurance, optionality, problem-solving capacity, or funding for non-rival goods? And would such a repair be a defense of the original argument or a replacement of it?
