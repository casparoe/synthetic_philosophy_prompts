I'm going back and forth with a friend about whether the Frege–Geach problem is fatal for expressivism, and he's managed to press it into a trilemma. His line is that an expressivist who wants to handle embedded moral sentences has to either (a) quietly switch to a truth-conditional semantics for those embeddings, which makes her a cognitivist in disguise; (b) lean on a deflationary notion of truth, which lets standard logic run but leaves her with nothing distinctively expressivist to call her own; or (c) keep moral semantics non-truth-conditional and then stuck explaining why an argument like 'if lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong; lying is wrong; therefore getting your little brother to lie is wrong' is valid, rather than just being some kind of attitude collision. I think the trilemma is too crude, and I want to see how the actual positions compare. I've sketched four strategies people gesture at when defending expressivism against the embedding problem (I'm deliberately leaving names out because my friend always changes the subject to the author's other commitments):

1. Higher-order attitude accounts: an embedded moral sentence is assigned a meaning in terms of attitudes toward attitudes, or toward combinations of attitudes, so logical validity in moral arguments comes out as incoherence in a sensibility, not truth preservation.
2. Attitude-first compositional semantics: give moral predicates their own non-cognitive semantic value and let the usual compositional machinery apply to that value, so negation and antecedents work the same way for moral sentences as they do everywhere else.
3. Hybrid expressivism: moral sentences express both a belief-like state with ordinary representational content and a desire-like state; the belief does the compositional work in embedded contexts, while the desire-like state rides along through a separate mechanism.
4. Minimalist expressivism: moral sentences are truth-apt only in a thin, disquotational sense, so standard semantics applies in full, and the expressivist difference gets relocated to the account of what it is to accept a moral sentence.

Here's what I'd like you to do. Score each of the four out of 10 on two dimensions: (i) how well it preserves the original expressivist motivation (roughly, that accepting a moral judgment is more like having a motivating attitude than like adding a belief to one's stock), and (ii) how well it provides a plausible compositional semantics for embedded occurrences. Then give an overall ranking, and say which one you'd back as a research program and why. One constraint: don't let hybrid expressivism win by default just becuase it looks like everyone else's familiar semantics; I want the scoring to reflect how much distinctively expressivist content each view actually hangs onto.
