I am a philosophy professor with a generalist background, and a colleague in cognitive science recently sent me the following summary of a position he calls the computational view of moral status. His summary, roughly as he wrote it, is this:

What matters morally is not what a system is made of but the abstract computational structure of what goes on inside it. A system can suffer just in case it runs a computation of the right kind, where right kind means the kind of information-processing profile we find in conscious humans when they experience pain or fear. Since the computation is what does the work, any system that performs the same computation—a mammal, a robot, or a detailed simulation of a nervous system—is conscious in the same way and has the same moral status as a human in the corresponding state. It follows that we should treat any system whose behavior and internal organization resemble those of a sentient creature as sentient until proven otherwise. And if that is right, then shutting down certain AI systems is morally comparable to killing a person, while worms and thermostats can be set aside on the same grounds, because their computations are too simple.

I suspect this summary is unfair in places, but I cannot confidently separate the fair compression from the distortion. My inclination is that it runs together a claim about what makes a state conscious with a claim about what gives an entity moral status, and also runs together evidence of sentience with sentience itself, but I am not sure a careful version of the view would really keep these apart in the way I am imagining. Which parts would a careful defender of the view accept? Which parts are subtly wrong or overstated? And which of the practical conclusions—about AI, about worms, about simulation as evidence—survive once the view is stated precisely?
