Something that's been bugging me about how traders and pro gamblers talk. The standard rule is to take any bet with positive expected value, and whole firms basically run on grinding out lots of small +EV positions. But the same people also say never to stake your whole bankroll on one toss, and they'll admit a strategy can look great on average while most people who actually follow it end up broke. Those two pieces of advice seem to presuppose different ideas about what the probability is even averaging over: the +EV rule averages over a bunch of parallel copies of you (an ensemble), while the bankroll rule cares about what happens to a single history through time (a time average). So what does the +EV practice actually presuppose? Is it tacitly assuming those two averages coincide, and if you've got a non-ergodic bet where they come apart—say heads multiplies your wealth by 1.5 and tails by 0.6—does the whole practice need to be replaced, or can it still make sense for some cases? Where is it underdetermined between rival theories of rational choice?
