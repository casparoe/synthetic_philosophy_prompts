The dispute over whether brain death is really death has an odd shape: the bedside facts are settled, yet the debate has barely moved in decades. Everyone accepts that a patient who satisfies the whole-brain tests — no brainstem reflexes, no respiratory drive, no electrical activity, no intracranial blood flow — can be ventilated and maintain a heartbeat, circulation, digestion, wound healing, and, when pregnant, a developing fetus; everyone also accepts that the brain will never recover. The verdicts still divide. One side says the patient is dead because the organism's integrative unity is irreversibly destroyed, leaving a machine-perfused collection of organs. Another says the patient is not dead because circulation, metabolism, and healing continue, and death has always meant the cessation of the body's own breathing and circulation. A third says the decisive loss is consciousness and the capacity for it, which are clearly gone. My hypothesis is that this is neither an empirical uncertainty nor a merely verbal dispute, but a difference about which capacities — spontaneous breathing, whole-body integration, or consciousness — carry the weight of a human life, and about whose question that is. Diagnose the disagreement: is that hypothesis right, what kind of disagreement is it really, and what would count as progress — or why should none be expected?
