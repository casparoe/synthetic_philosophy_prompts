I am a novelist, and I want to understand the implicit theory behind a practice I work inside every day. Fiction is pervasively sorted by the writer's nationality or origin: bookshop windows announce new Scandinavian fiction; publishers' catalogues group titles under national rubrics such as the Nigerian novel or Korean literature in translation; translation grants, festival invitations, and prize committees are organized at least partly by country and region. A writer from a small or marginal literary culture finds that their book is presented abroad as a window into that country's life, and is then judged inauthentic if the window does not look sufficiently local. Yet the same publishing world tends to disclaim the general idea that a people has a stable collective temperament, and would treat a critic who said that people of country X are warm but fatalistic as having lapsed into stereotype.

I am not asking whether any of this is good or bad. I am asking what the practice presupposes. If nationality labels are doing real organizational and interpretive work, deciding what a novel is about, how it is read, and whether it is funded, then the practice seems to behave as though something answers to the label. But it may be that all of this can run on weaker commitments: shared language and institutions, the habits of a reading public, a marketing convenience that nobody takes literally. So I want you to reconstruct the practice's implicit view of national character, and to be precise about how much it actually commits to.

Specifically: Which view or views of national character does the practice strictly presuppose? That there is a coherent national character, that an author's origin causally shapes their fiction, that a work can express or represent a people, or something weaker? Which views does it merely fit with but not require? Which rival views about generalizing over peoples does the practice quietly rule out or sit badly with, and what would the practice have to look like if one of those rivals were true? I would also like to know where the practice is internally inconsistent: for example, where it treats the same generalization as a selling point when the country is marginal and as a stereotype when it is central, or where one part of the practice markets by nationality while another part disavows national categories.

Please give your main answer in prose, and then append a brief structured summary as a JSON object with the following fields:

- "practice": a one-sentence description of the practice under examination.
- "strict_presuppositions": an array of objects, each with "view" (a proposition the practice cannot do without) and "why_required" (what feature of the practice needs it).
- "compatible_not_required": an array of objects, each with "view" (a proposition that fits the practice but is not entailed) and "alternative_explanation" (how the practice could proceed without it).
- "rival_views_ruled_out": an array of objects, each with "view" (a proposition the practice sits badly with) and "tension" (where the friction appears).
- "internal_inconsistencies": an array of objects, each with "part_a", "part_b", and "tension".
- "underdetermined_choice_points": an array of objects, each with "choice_point" (a feature of the practice) and "rival_options" (the several views that could justify it).
- "revised_practice_under_main_rival": a short description of what the practice would look like if its main presupposition were replaced by its most plausible rival.
