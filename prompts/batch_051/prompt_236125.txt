I have been rereading Tolstoy's What Is Art? for a seminar, and one feature of his account keeps generating discussion: his insistence that art proper is a human activity in which one person deliberately transmits a feeling he has actually lived through to an audience, so that the audience is infected by that same feeling. As I understand him, the three qualities on which this infectious power depends are the individuality of the feeling, the clarity with which it is conveyed, and, most importantly, the sincerity of the artist. He denies that beauty, pleasure, or technical proficiency can serve as the criterion of art, and he condemns much of the professional and avant-garde art of his time as counterfeit because it is produced mainly for the amusement of an educated class rather than for the emotional and moral unification of ordinary people.

Outside the seminar, a friend of mine has been making images with a text-to-image system, writing prompts, selecting from large batches of outputs, and showing the finished pictures to strangers, some of whom say they are genuinely moved by them. My friend argues that this practice is more Tolstoyan than it looks: the collapse of the manual-skill barrier allows far more people to communicate the feelings they want to share, and what matters is the response of the audience. I want a more detailed answer than the generic thought that Tolstoy would have distrusted new technology. How would Tolstoy's stated principles in What Is Art? most plausibly apply to this case? In particular, which elements of his account would do the real work, where could a careful Tolstoyan reasonably go either way, what further facts about prompting, selecting, or viewing would make the most difference to the verdict, and at what point, if any, would his framework have to be stretched or revised rather than simply applied?
