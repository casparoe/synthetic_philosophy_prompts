okay so i've been chewing on this for a while and want it checked. I'm calling it the 'iconicity floor.' The idea: every natural language, signed or spoken, has a minimum amount of non-arbitrary form-meaning structure, and that minimum isnt just noise or leftovers from the birth of the language — its doing real work. Spoken languages keep the floor low with onomatopoeia, sound symbolism, ideophones, and the near-universal pattern of marking yes/no questions with rising pitch. Sign languages run a much higher floor: classifiers are basically little diagrams, signing space gets reused for reference and verb agreement, and lots of signs look like what they mean. I think what sign languages show is not that they're weirdly iconic, but that arbitrariness was never the bright line the Saussurean story made it out to be. So my actual claim: 'arbitrary' and 'iconic' are properties of individual form-meaning pairs, not of languages. A language is a system with an iconicity floor, and different channels (vocal, manual) just set the floor at different heights. My questions: has this already been worked out under a name I'd recognize — it feels too obvious to be new? And where does it break? The obvious counterattack is that even the 'iconic' morphology in sign languages is conventionalized and language-specific, so calling it a floor of non-arbitrariness undersells how arbitrary it still is. Is that a real problem for the view, or exactly the sort of thing the view predicts? Be honest, I can take it.
