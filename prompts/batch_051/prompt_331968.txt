You are a social epistemologist who works on misinformation, trust, and online communities. I lead the intervention-design team at a media-civics nonprofit that advises school systems, local newsrooms, and platform trust-and-safety staff on how to respond to unhealthy information environments in specific online communities. We keep reaching for the distinction between epistemic bubbles and echo chambers: an epistemic bubble being, roughly, a structure in which relevant outside information is simply missing from members' view, and an echo chamber being a structure in which members are brought to distrust outside voices so that contrary information is discredited before it can be weighed. I would like you to turn that distinction into a reusable decision procedure my staff, who have no philosophical training, can run in about half a day per community, to determine what kind of problem a community has and what kind of intervention, if any, the diagnosis supports. I am not asking for an essay on the distinction; I am asking for a working procedure.

The procedure should set out the questions to ask in the order they must be asked, with enough guidance that a staff member can recognize what counts as a sufficient answer at each step and why the next step depends on it. It should say where the procedure stops and classifies a community as an epistemic bubble, an echo chamber, or neither, and where it stops and hands the case to a person because the question has become one of values rather than diagnosis. It should mark the points at which judgment cannot be eliminated by the checklist, and it should state plainly what the procedure cannot do.

Then apply the procedure to three cases. First, a hobbyist forum whose members rarely see contrarian sources because the platform's recommendation system keeps feeding them more of what they already engage with. Second, a tightly moderated community that teaches new members to regard outside fact-checkers, academics, and journalists as hostile operatives. Third, a community that initially looks like the second but whose members' distrust of outsiders is a reasonable response to a documented history of being misrepresented by the same outlets now demanding their trust. Include among these applications at least one case where the procedure yields a recommendation you expect me and my staff will find unpalatable, and explain why the procedure nonetheless gives that answer.

Before you draft, think through the places where the bubble/echo-chamber distinction itself blurs or breaks down, and let those failure modes determine which questions and stopping rules the procedure includes. Write the whole thing in continuous prose, like a long memo or letter to me; do not use headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold text, italics, or tables.
