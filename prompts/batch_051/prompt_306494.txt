I have been teaching a small seminar on the history of mechanics, and a recurring dispute has forced me to ask what the charge of anachronism actually amounts to when we read old mathematics and science. One person in the room treats anachronism as a purely factual mistake: attributing to an author a concept or distinction that could not have been in play at the time. Another treats it as an evaluative mistake: judging past work by how far it falls short of what we now believe, as though the history were a slow crawl toward our present results. I need a working definition of anachronism, restricted to the reading of old mathematics and science, that separates legitimate modern paraphrase or reconstruction from genuine distortion, and that yields a defensible verdict on each of the following cases.

The cases the definition has to handle are these. Clear anachronism: saying that Ptolemaic astronomy is simply wrong because it is geocentric and does not use elliptical heliocentric orbits; and criticizing Euclid's Elements for failing to state the things about continuity and convergence that a modern real-analysis course treats as foundational. Clearly not anachronism: rewriting a Newtonian fluxional derivation in modern derivative notation while explicitly flagging the notation as ours; and restating an old theorem in modern set-theoretic language, again flagged as a reconstruction made for comparison. Borderline cases where I genuinely do not know what the definition should say: describing Mesopotamian numerical recipes as solving quadratic equations even though they give step-by-step procedures for particular numbers and never state a general symbolic formula; calling Democritus an early atomist, or saying he anticipated modern atomic theory; describing Archimedes' method of exhaustion as an informal or anticipatory version of the integral calculus; saying that Aristotle's physics is a failed empirical theory, as measured by the predictions it makes about motion; and saying that Newton's account of absolute space and time was refuted by relativity rather than replaced by a different project.

Give me a working definition of anachronism for this context, run it across all nine cases, and say explicitly where it delivers the wrong verdict or no verdict and what, if anything, you changed in response. I suspect the word is doing two jobs at once, one factual about what a historical author had the resources to mean, and one evaluative about treating our present state of knowledge as the standard the past was groping toward. If that is right, give me both definitions and tell me which of the borderline cases move between them; if it is wrong, defend a single definition that does both jobs.
