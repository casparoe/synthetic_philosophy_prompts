I'm writing a paper on the ethics of immigration, and I am trying to assemble a complete inventory of the principled arguments for the view that a legitimate state has a general right to exclude would-be immigrants. I am setting aside the familiar practical qualifications — screening criminals or excluding entrants during a public-health emergency — because even most defenders of open borders concede those are a different matter. What I want are arguments that the state may exclude in general, simply because it is a legitimate political community.

So far I have four.

(1) The cultural preservation argument: a political community has a legitimate interest in maintaining its distinctive form of life — its language, institutions, customs, and collective self-understanding — and since immigration changes all of these, the community may regulate admission so that its character is not transformed against the will of its members.

(2) The freedom of association argument: a legitimate state is entitled to self-determination, and a core part of self-determination is the freedom to choose with whom one will associate; just as a spouse, a club, or a religious congregation may decline to take on a new member, a political community may decline to enter into membership with outsiders.

(3) The democratic self-rule argument: a people cannot govern itself unless it controls the boundaries of the demos. If the electorate can be continuously altered from outside, the demos cannot set the terms of its own institutions, laws, and successors; control over entry is thus a precondition of meaningful democracy, not one policy choice among others. (I keep this separate from (2) because it appeals to the conditions of democratic control rather than to an analogy with private association.)

(4) The welfare-state solidarity argument: redistributive institutions depend on citizens' willingness to treat one another as participants in a common enterprise, and that willingness may be weakened by large, rapid, culturally heterogeneous immigration; states may therefore restrict entry to protect the trust and solidarity that sustain their welfare states.

I suspect this list is close to exhaustive in the literature, but I want to know whether there is a fifth argument with a genuinely different normative basis — not a restatement of one of the four, not a special case, and not a combination of them. By genuinely different I mean it should bottom out in a different value or principle, not in culture, association, democratic control, or solidarity. The argument should also be one someone could defend in good faith, not a slogan.

Then tell me honestly whether it earns a place on the list.

Structure your answer in four sections:

- 'Candidate' — state the additional argument in three to five sentences.
- 'Independence' — in two to four sentences, explain how its normative basis differs from the closest one or two of my four arguments.
- 'Pressure test' — in three to five sentences, say where the argument is strongest and where it is most vulnerable.
- 'Verdict' — in one or two sentences, judge whether it belongs on the list.
