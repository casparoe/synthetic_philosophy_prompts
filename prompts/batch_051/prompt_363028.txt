You are a moral philosopher who specializes in the ethics of collective action and individual contributions to large-scale harms, and you are thoroughly familiar with the philosophical debate over the inefficacy objection. I am a philosophy professor — my own work is mostly in philosophy of language — and a colleague in our environmental policy program has sent me a short paragraph she wrote for an interdisciplinary teaching module on climate and food ethics. She wants my judgment about whether it is fair before she circulates it, and I want to give her a considered reply rather than a hunch.

Her summary reads as follows. The inefficacy objection says that in large-scale collective harms such as climate change and industrial animal agriculture, a single person's contribution is causally irrelevant: these systems are so large and so buffered by the choices of millions of others that whether I fly less, insulate my home, or stop eating meat changes neither total emissions nor the number of animals killed. Since an action can be wrong only if it makes things worse for someone, and such personal choices make nobody worse off, no one can have a moral obligation to alter their consumption. She adds that talk of "doing one's part" merely assumes that harm is already under way and that there is some fixed quota of responsibility assigned to each person; without that, the phrase supplies no independent reason to abstain. Her practical conclusion is that ethical effort should be directed toward policy and legislation, where coordinated action can make a difference, while personal consumption should be treated as a morally neutral matter of lifestyle.

Please work through this paragraph sentence by sentence. Start by laying out the most charitable and accurate statement of the inefficacy objection, distinguishing what the objection actually establishes from what is sometimes claimed on its behalf. Then identify which parts of my colleague's summary are accurate, which are subtle overstatements or conflations, and which conclusions would survive once the summary is corrected. Pay special attention to the move from "no individual difference" to "no obligation," and to the further move from "no obligation" to "personal consumption is morally neutral." Before you commit to a verdict, brainstorm several different things someone might mean by saying that an individual action makes no difference — actual difference, expected difference, difference as a participant in a cooperative scheme, difference in a case with thresholds — and consider how each reading affects the soundness of the argument. Then explain how a defender of individual consumption obligations would respond to each point, and end with a clear verdict: is this summary fair enough to circulate as a fair representation of the debate, and if not, which single correction matters most?
