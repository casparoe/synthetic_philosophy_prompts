I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and I've spent the past few weeks interviewing philosophers of science about the pessimistic meta-induction—the argument that since a long line of once-successful theories (phlogiston, caloric, the ether) got rejected all the way down, we should expect today's best-confirmed theories to meet the same fate. One realist kept deflecting with a picture I've now heard from several people in the field: the history of science, she said, is like a succesion of maps of the same coastline. Early maps put the headlands in the wrong places, but they weren't pure fiction—they got a few coves right, and each new survey corrected them without tearing the whole thing up. Her conclusion was that a theory can be replaced and still have been partly true, so the pessimistic induction overstates how rude the historical record is to realism. Current theories won't be junked, just revised.

I'm not convinced the analogy is earning its keep, and I'd like an audit. First give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the coastline, the surveyor, the successive maps, the mistakes, the corrections. Then sort the disanalogies into the ones that matter and the ones that don't. I'm especially suspicous about two of them. Maps traffic in the observable shape of a place, while scientific theories posit unobservable stuff—fields, particles, dark matter—that we can't stand outside and photograph. And old maps didn't usually contain whole continents that turned out not to exist, the way the ether or caloric did. Does the realist's conclusion survive once those disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing the persuasive work? If you think a different analogy would do better, I'll take that too.
