Since my daughter was born last spring I have not been able to shake the thought that she might very easily never have existed; a different evening, a different month, and there would have been no such person at all. Over the same period I have been working through quantified modal logic, and I find myself genuinely impressed by the simplest formal semantics: classical quantifier clauses, one fixed domain of objects, and the usual clauses for the modal operators. On that semantics both Barcan schemas come out valid. The first, ∀x□Fx → □∀xFx, is equivalent to the claim that if it is possible that something is F, then something is possibly F; the second, □∀xFx → ∀x□Fx, is equivalent to the claim that if something is possibly F, then it is possible that something is F. On a varying-domain semantics, the validity of the first corresponds exactly to the condition that domains never grow as one moves to an accessible world, and the validity of the second to the condition that domains never shrink. Taken at face value, with the quantifier read as expressing existence, they jointly entail that the inventory of what exists is fixed across all possible worlds: no merely possible individuals, and no merely contingent ones.

Yet the intuition on the other side is as firm as any I have. It seems plain to me that my daughter might not have existed, and no less plain that there might have been a second child of ours who was never conceived — a possible person who is not actual. I know the standard moves: restrict the quantifier rules, allow domains to vary, or introduce an existence predicate. But those options appear to buy the intuition back only at the price of complications and distinctions that strike me as ad hoc, while the simple semantics has the virtues of uniformity and economy: one domain, one quantifier, one clause for each modal operator. I am not asking to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to go — whether there is a principled error theory that explains away our conviction that we and our children are contingent beings, and how one would test it — and what it would take for the theory to go, which means isolating the exact ingredient doing the work: the fixed domain, the classical quantifier clauses, the identification of quantification with existence, or something else. Or is there a reading on which both survive, one that keeps the formal results as truths about everything whatsoever while treating actual existence as a separately expressible matter? What would settle the choice between those options?
