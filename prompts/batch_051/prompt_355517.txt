In a meeting about allocating scarce intensive-care resources, I argued for a rule that diverts ventilators from patients with very low predicted survival to patients with much higher predicted survival, on the ground that this minimizes expected deaths overall. A colleague rejected this with "the ends don't justify the means," and then clarified that he thinks foreseen but unintended harm can be permissible, as in the standard switch case, but that intending the low-survival patients' deaths as one's route to saving others is not; he said this is what the doctrine of double effect amounts to. I suspect the slogan is running together several distinct principles. Please lay out a menu of the precise claims "the ends don't justify the means" could be asserting—for example, that good outcomes do not settle permissibility; that you may not use a person merely as a means; that intended harm is absolutely forbidden while merely foreseen harm is not; that there is some threshold of consequences beyond which trade-offs become forbidden; or that expected aggregate benefits cannot justify a rule that wrongs particular patients ex ante. For each candidate reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or overstated or false, and whether it actually supports rejecting the ventilator rule. Also say which reading, if any, adds something beyond the doctrine of double effect's intended/foreseen distinction, and which reading my colleague's conclusion actually requires.
