Construct a thought experiment for a graduate seminar on the moral boundaries of sentience. The deliverable is a single scenario, stated in at most two paragraphs, containing exactly three candidate patients: a biological human brain in a body, a neuron-level whole-brain emulation running on ordinary silicon hardware and driving a robot body, and a lookup-table device that produces the same behavior as the other two over the entire range of inputs the scenario makes relevant. Describe the lookup table so that its outputs are retrieved from a store of ready-made answers rather than generated by causal interactions among the system's own states, and so that an external designer, not the device, did the thinking. Constraints: no exotic physics, no perfect predictors, and no technology beyond brain emulations, lookup tables, and ordinary biological brains.

The scenario must make three views come apart in the following pattern:

1. Biological naturalism: only the biological brain is conscious.
2. Substrate-neutral computationalism about consciousness: the biological brain and the emulation are conscious; the lookup table is not.
3. A behavioral-disposition criterion: all three systems are credited with mental states, and hence moral status.

The verdicts should be generated by the views themselves, not stipulated in the description of the scenario. After stating the scenario, show the reasoning each view runs on each system. Then identify the structural feature that does the work in each split — in particular, say how much work the substrate is really doing once causal organization is held fixed, and whether the behavioral criterion peels off only because it ignores internal organization. Finally, explain what happens if that feature is removed or altered: does the case collapse into a standard Chinese room or China brain problem, or does a residual disagreement survive? End by anticipating the strongest objection — that the lookup table could be redescribed as implementing the right organization after all — and say whether that objection would collapse the second and third views into each other.
