For a graduate seminar on bounded rationality and decision theory, write a decision tree that takes a respondent from the shared starting point — that an agent with a goal and scarce time, information, and computational capacity can still be assessed as more or less rational in how it chooses to stop searching — to a named position on what makes a stopping procedure rational. Leave aside the purely descriptive question of how people actually choose, as well as skepticism about whether preferences are coherent enough to make maximization meaningful; the tree is about the standard to which a bounded choice procedure should be held.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of familiar labels: first identify the two or three yes/no commitments on which the positions genuinely come apart, run through the resulting combinations of answers, and only then place the actual positions as leaves. For any combination of answers that no one defends, say in a sentence whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because it has not been developed. Phrase every branching node as a question about a concrete case, answerable without already knowing which leaf it leads to.

Use the following cases as branch nodes, and replace them only with equally concrete alternatives you justify. (1) A hiring committee with a hard deadline screens candidates one at a time; the first candidate clears a bar the committee set in advance. Is hiring immediately the right move, or should the committee keep searching for the best candidate it might find? (2) A decision-maker raises the bar after easy successes and lowers it after a run of failures. Is this rational recalibration, or a bias the decision-maker should correct? (3) A simple recognition heuristic that ignores most cues beats a well-informed statistical model in a forecasting task because of how cues are correlated in that environment — the less-is-more effect. Is the heuristic the more rational procedure in that environment, or merely fortunate? (4) A cognitive system can run only a coarse approximation of a Bayesian ideal under its capacity costs. Is its rationality a matter of how close it comes to the ideal, or of something else?

The leaves must include at least the following, though you may add one if the grid opens up an occupied cell: maximizing expected utility with the costs of further search counted explicitly, so that satisficing is reinterpreted as optimal stopping; Simon-style satisficing, on which an aspiration level stops the search and the level adapts to experience; ecological rationality, on which heuristics are rational insofar as they exploit the structure of the task environment rather than approximate an internal optimum; resource-rational analysis, which treats bounded procedures as optimal given the costs of the cognitive resources they consume; and a hill-climbing view on which an agent keeps a course of action only while its returns beat a moving baseline rather than any global standard. Give each leaf a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment.

Do not suggest or cite any literature, and do not ask the respondent to sort themselves by label. Finally, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate. In particular, address the near-observational equivalence of Simon-style satisficing with adaptive aspiration levels and explicit optimal stopping under search costs: if both can be made to describe the same sequence of stopping decisions, say so, and give the one further question that would separate them. If distinguishing two leaves would require occupying a combination of answers you have already diagnosed as empty, say which.
