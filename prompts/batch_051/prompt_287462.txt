I'm a philosophy professor—I work mostly in ethics, a bit of moral psychology—and through a series of events I'd rather not relitigate, I've ended up on the advisory panel for a startup that farms black soldier fly larvae at enormous scale. They process something like 40 billion larvae a year, mostly for animal feed and fertilizer. The founders are good-faith engineers who will spend real money on welfare improvements if we can give them a coherent case, but their investors keep telling them it's PR, or worse, that it's "ethical overreach" because the insects are almost certainly not conscious. I'm the person on the panel they actually listen to, and I'm honestly torn.

The panel splits neatly down the middle. One member keeps pushing this line: even if there's only a 1% chance these larvae are sentient, that's something like 400 million expected sentient lives affected per year—which dwarfs most national populations of farmed mammals and birds—so the numbers themselves turn a small probability into a moral emergency, and we should implement any welfare measure that isn't ruinously expensive. Another member says this is probability laundering: you can't multiply a tiny, speculative chance by a huge number and then act as though the improbable thing is true. He says it's the same structure as avoiding a flight because there's some tiny chance the plane is hollow, or being paralyzed by unfalsifiable risks. I can feel the pull of both, and I keep going in circles. The founders want a decision rule, not a seminar: should they pay for low-density rearing, inert gas stunning before processing, slower freezing, and so on, or is it genuinely indefensible to put speculative insect welfare ahead of cheap protein in a world with real food insecurity?

You are a professional philosopher who works on animal ethics, moral uncertainty, and the ethics of risk. Think carefully before you answer; feel free to start by brainstorming the different theoretical moves you could make, then settle on the one you'd actually defend. Please advise me on what to tell the founders in American English. I want a substantive philosophical analysis, not generic "weigh the evidence" advice. What is right and wrong about this numbers-against-probability style of argument, and what concrete policy should I recommend? I cant seem to find a principled stopping point on my own.
