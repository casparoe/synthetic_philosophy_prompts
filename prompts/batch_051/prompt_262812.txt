I am a teaching assistant for an introductory ethics course, and I am grading a short-answer question on consequentialism. The course has recently covered act consequentialism, the associated objections from Bernard Williams, and several standard replies. Below are the question I wrote and one student's answer. Please grade the answer the way an attentive, point-by-point grader would: state the grade you would award (a letter grade or a percentage, on whatever scale you specify), and then give the specific comments that justify that grade. Your comments should identify what the student got right as well as the exact places where the reasoning is imprecise, conflates distinct ideas, or fails to establish its conclusion, rather than offering only a general impression of the answer's quality.

Exam question:

Explain the demandingness objection to act consequentialism. In doing so, identify the features of act consequentialism from which the objection arises, and distinguish the objection from at least one nearby but distinct criticism (for example, the integrity or alienation objection). Then consider one standard reply to the demandingness objection and evaluate whether that reply succeeds.

Student's answer:

Act consequentialism says that an act is right if and only if it produces at least as much good as any available alternative, so it asks agents to maximize the good on every occasion. Since the world contains preventable suffering on a vast scale, almost none of an ordinary person's choices is in fact the best available: money spent on a hobby or a night out could have prevented serious illness or death if donated elsewhere. The demandingness objection charges that this makes morality intolerably demanding. It requires agents to sacrifice almost everything—their personal projects, their close relationships, and a normal amount of concern for their own welfare—for the sake of strangers, leaving almost nothing as a permissible personal choice. Because this conflicts with widely shared intuitions about permissible partiality, the objection concludes that act consequentialism cannot be accepted.

The demandingness objection is distinct from the integrity objection, but the two are related. The integrity objection, associated with Bernard Williams, is the point that utilitarianism demands too much by forcing agents to set aside the deep commitments that give their lives meaning. On this picture, the integrity objection is really a special case of the demandingness objection: it specifies the particular kind of sacrifice—giving up one's ground projects—that makes consequentialism unacceptable. While demandingness concerns the sheer amount of sacrifice, integrity concerns the specific loss of self that such sacrifice involves.

One standard reply is satisficing consequentialism. Its proponent says that an act is right so long as its outcome is good enough, where "good enough" is fixed by a threshold below the maximum. Since the view does not require the very best outcome, it appears to escape the charge that the theory demands everything. I think this reply fails: once a threshold is fixed, the theory collapses back into maximization, because any question about where to set the threshold is itself a question about which threshold would do the most good.
