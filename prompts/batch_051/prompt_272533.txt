I run a monthly two-hour seminar on political concepts for working local and state political reporters. The room is ideologically mixed, and a fair number arrive already convinced that "democratic backsliding" is a phrase partisans use to delegitimate outcomes they dislike. Next month's topic is democratic backsliding. I don't want the session to end up as either a debunking of the term as pure smearing or a lecture that assumes the concept is neutral and well-defined. So don't explain democratic backsliding to me; I need a plan for how I should lead the session.

What has to be established first, before the term "backsliding" can do any real work, and what depends on that? I suspect the starting point is the distinction between losing policy fights and losing the ability to have policy fights at all — but tell me if something has to come before even that. What single example should carry the whole session, and what properties must it have and lack to do that work? The room will volunteer cases from Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States, but each seems to me to fail as a carrying example in a different way — too polarized, too legally contested, too historically saturated. Give me the criterion for the right example before you name one, and tell me which of the audience's volunteered cases I should let play which role in the sequence. Which misconceptions will predictably shape the discussion — for instance, that backsliding is just the other side winning, that a democracy dies by a single event rather than by degrees, or that some neutral scoreboard could settle whether it is happening — and at what point in the sequence should each be headed off rather than corrected afterward? What should I explicitly refuse to settle at this level, such as whether a particular country currently qualifies, and how should I say that so the group hears honesty rather than evasion? And what single question at the end will tell me whether they can use the concept as more than a slur or a slogan?

Be explicit with me about which parts of the plan rest on contested social-scientific choices and how heavily I should hedge them in the room. And if I've posed this wrong — if the concept is better taught as a site of political contest than as a tool with a nonpartisan core, and an honest session would foreground that contest instead of trying to resolve it — then say so plainly and give me the plan you would actually run, not the one that politely accommodates my framing.
