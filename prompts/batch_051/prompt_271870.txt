I've been on this neighborhood traffic-safety committee for a few months, and we're finally discussing whether to narrow the lanes and add curb extensions on a street where drivers constantly speed. Every time someone points out that the road itself basically dares drivers to go 45 in a 25, one member answers with "guns don't kill people, people kill people" — his point being that the asphault isn't the problem, the reckless drivers are, and that redesigning the street just lets bad drivers off the hook. He keeps saying we should spend the money on enforcement instead.

I can tell the slogan is doing real work in his argument, but I can't pin down exactly which claim it's making. It could be the narrow point that a road has no intentions and so can't be morally blamed, or the much stronger claim that the design of an object never meaningfully shapes what people do with it, so no artifact is ever political in any real sense. Those are very different claims, and only some of them would actually get him to his conclusion that we shouldn't bother redesigning the street. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses "guns don't kill people, people kill people" might be expressing when it gets used in a debate about whether a technology or piece of infrastructure has politics? For each reading, say whether it's just a platitude, something substantive and defensible, or something false, and then tell me which reading his argument actually needs in order to rule out the curb extensions. I'd rather have a menu of readings then one gloss, so I can bring it to the next meeting.
