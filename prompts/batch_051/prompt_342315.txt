So in my philosophy of mind class we've been reading Dennett on the three stances — the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance — and then some of the "real patterns" stuff where he says beliefs and desires are real patterns in behavior, not ghostly extra things but also not reducible to physics point-by-point. I'm also taking an operating systems course, and it hit me that debugging basically has the same three levels. When a program crashes, you can dig down to the hardware and signals (physical stance), or you can say "the function gets called twice because the loop condition is off by one" (design stance), or you can say "the scheduler thinks the mutex is free" or "the process wants to write to the buffer" and predict what it'll do next from that (intentional stance). In both cases you adopt the higher level when the lower one is too unwieldy, and in both cases you can make real, useful predictions at the higher level even though there isn't any neat one-to-one mapping down to the physical level.

But I can't tell whether this is deep or whether I'm just pattern-matching on "levels of explanation." Is the "thought" we attribute to a program in the intentional stance the same kind of real pattern as a belief in a person, or is there a difference in kind? If the parallel is real, does anything transfer one way or the other — for example, would Dennett's reasons for saying a chess computer literally has beliefs also apply to saying the OS literally believes a file is locked, or is the OS example missing something essential? Where exactly do the two cases come apart, if they do?
