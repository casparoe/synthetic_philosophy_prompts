A friend and I have been going back and forth about what a safety evaluation of an AI system even is, and I want to see the strongest version of one side of the argument written out instead of us just talking past each other. His line is that average-case numbers — benchmark scores, accuracy on a red-team suite, the fact that we tried really hard and didn't manage to break the thing — are basically worthless for high-stakes systems. A real adversary doesn't sample from your test distribution; she searches for the one input that breaks you. So the only thing worth having is a worst-case guarentee. I think that's too quick. For any actual learned system, a literal worst-case guarantee over all conceivable inputs is impossible, and even a guarantee over some formal class of inputs tends to be either vacuous or irrelevant, because the true worst case might be a token-salad string no real user would ever produce. So maybe average-case evaluation under a realistic deployment distribution isn't a disappointing second-best; maybe it's the only standard that is both achievable and actually action-guiding. Please make the strongest argument you can that AI alignment should be evaluated in average-case rather than worst-case terms, even for high-stakes systems. In doing so, take on what seems to me like the hardest objection: an average-case claim is only defined relative to a probability distribution over inputs, and in a genuinely novel or adversarial deployment there's no trusted distribution to fall back on, so the average-case position may be silently assuming the very thing a safety case is supposed to earn. You could also consider whether the right fix is to demand good expected performance over a whole family of plausible deployment distributions rather than a single one, and whether that's a way of saving average-case thinking or just rebranded worst-case thinking.
