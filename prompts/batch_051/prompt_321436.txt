In my course on the history of political economy, we have been reading Hume's essays on money and the balance of trade alongside present-day arguments about monetary stimulus and trade policy. During seminar, a classmate defended aggressive monetary expansion with an analogy: the economy is a body, and money is its lifeblood. A sluggish economy, on his telling, is a patient with poor circulation, and the obvious remedy is a transfusion — expand the money supply, ease credit, and get the blood moving again. He used the same analogy to treat a trade deficit as a hemorrhage: an outflow of money is blood the body cannot afford to lose, so the deficit is intrinsically harmful. The analogy was doing real work in the argument. It made monetary expansion sound intrinsically restorative rather than merely stimulative, and it made any outflow of money sound like a pathology, quite apart from what the economy was actually producing or consuming. I suspect the analogy is quietly importing features of a living body — a fixed optimal blood volume, a circulatory system that delivers something the organs metabolize, a patient who can bleed out — that have no clean counterpart in an economy. I also suspect the analogy sits uneasily with the Hume we have been reading, who denies that money is itself national wealth, distinguishes the short-run effect of an increase in money from the long-run adjustment of prices, and argues that international money flows tend to correct themselves through price changes. Please write a careful audit of how far the money-as-lifeblood analogy holds when measured against that Humean account of money and trade. Begin with an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the blood, the heart, the vessels, the organs, oxygen, the patient, and the patient's health. Be explicit about which features of a living body the analogy quietly imports into the economy, and which of those are doing the argumentative work. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the two conclusions the analogy was meant to carry — first, that monetary expansion is always the right response to sluggishness, and second, that an outflow of money through a trade deficit is always harmful — from the disanalogies that are merely cosmetic. In particular, address whether Hume's account of the interval before prices adjust can save a diluted version of the transfusion conclusion, and whether his account of the balance of trade dissolves the hemorrhage conclusion. End by saying what the lifeblood analogy is still good for, if anything, and whether an alternative comparison — say, money as a lubricant in a machine, which is closer to the Hume we have been reading — would license different conclusions.
