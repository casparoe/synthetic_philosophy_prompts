So I just got back from a long, going-in-circles argument with a friend about why human extinction is bad over and above the deaths it involves, and the place we got stuck is exactly the thing I want you to compare. My friend kept insisting the whole answer is just that countless future people get deprived of lives they would otherwise have lived, so extinction is bad because of all that lost value. I kept pushing back with Scheffler's afterlife conjecture: the idea that much of what we value now depends on our taking for granted that humanity will continue after us, and that if we believed extinction was coming right after our own deaths, a lot of what we do — research, writing, building institutions meant to outlast us — would start to feel pointless even though we ourselves would still live out our normal lives. So extinction would be bad in a second way, by hollowing out the present, not just emptying the future.

Compare those two ideas, because I couldnt get us past this. Is the afterlife conjecture really a distinct kind of badness, or does it reduce to the future-people point once you think it through? Does making the badness of extinction depend on what we happen to care about make it too contingent — say, if someone genuinely didn't need a human future for their projects to feel meaningful, would extinction be less bad for them, and would that be a problem for the conjecture? And is there a difference between saying extinction is bad and saying we wouldnt be able to go on valuing things if we knew about it? I'm less interested in which one wins than in getting clear about what kind of badness each is tracking.
