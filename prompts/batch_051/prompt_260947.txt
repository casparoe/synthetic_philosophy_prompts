I teach a course on probability and rationality, and I've reached the embarrassing point of not knowing what I think Dutch book arguments actually show. So I sat down and listed my own verdicts on a bunch of concrete betting cases, and now I want to know what principle, if any, captures them. Here's what I keep finding myself committed to. (1) If someone accepts a pair of bets on P and not-P at the same time, each priced in a way they regard as fair, and the pair guarantees a loss no matter what happens, that's just irrational. (2) But if the two bets are offered by different bookies at different times, and the person would accept each one on its own but never puts the package together, I'm much less willing to say the same thing—it feels more like they're exploitable than incoherent. (3) Still, if someone has the very same incoherent credences but never bets at all, because they have a standing policy against gambling, I want to say their credences are irrational anyway, even though no actual book can be made against them. (4) Meanwhile, someone who simply has no credence whatsoever about some out-of-the-way proposition seems fine to me; a gap isn't an incoherence. (5) And a perfectly coherent person who declines every bet because they hate risk is also fine. My working principle is something like: you're irrational exactly when there's a Dutch book you'd walk into even with the whole plan laid out in front of you. That gets (1) and (2), maybe (5), but it lets (3) off the hook, which I can't quite accept. Is there a principle that gets all five, or do I need to drop a verdict—and which one should go first?
