A friend who reads a lot of medieval philosophy has taken to ending moral arguments with a line he attributes to Abelard: the sin is in the consent, not the act. He has used it to claim that someone who tries to do wrong and is stopped by luck is exactly as blameworthy as someone who succeeds, that someone who causes harm by accident has done nothing wrong at all, and that a negligent person who never consciously chose to hurt anyone is morally in the clear. I cannot tell whether he is making one claim or several, and whether the line can actually carry all that weight. Lay out the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be making — about what counts as consent, about what separates a passing desire or temptation from consent, about whether the outward act and its results matter at all, about negligence and self-deception, and about how a failed attempt compares with a completed one — and say for each whether it is obviously true, substantive but defensible, or false, and which of those readings would actually support each of the three things he used it to argue.
