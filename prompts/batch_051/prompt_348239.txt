At a comparative cognition lab meeting, we were discussing whether a scrub jay that re-caches food after being watched by a potential thief is planning for future theft avoidance or simply running a behavior shaped by past associative contingencies. A senior researcher closed the debate by invoking Morgan's canon, roughly: do not attribute a higher psychical faculty to an animal when a lower one would explain the behavior. I have read that formulation in the context of C. Lloyd Morgan's Introduction to Comparative Psychology, but I am less sure what work it is doing. Please separate the distinct precise claims that this one-liner could be asserting: for example, a rule of epistemic modesty, a claim about the probability of lower explanations, an operational criterion for hypotheses, a metaphysical principle about animal minds, or something else. For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible methodological rule, or false or outdated in light of comparative cognition. Then tell me which reading, if any, actually supports using it to end the debate about the jays, and whether ending the debate that way is warranted.
