After our delusions lecture, two people on my course got into an argument and I couldn't referee it because I kept changing my mind. Neither of them would budge later either, so I'm outsourcing it.

Mina said the two-factor theory basically settles that delusions are beliefs. In Capgras, the first factor — a breakdown in the felt familiarity of a known face — supplies the content: 'that woman isn't actually my wife.' The second factor, a failure to reject wildly implausible candidate explanations, lets it through. So you get a belief formed from wierd input plus a broken filter. If someone sincerely asserts the thing, sometimes acts on it (divorcing the spouse, phoning the police), and is distressed by it, that's a belief.

Rob said the second factor is exactly the problem. If the system that's supposed to evaluate candidate beliefs is broken, then what comes out is not a belief in the full sense. It's closer to a powerful sense-of-strangeness plus a confabulated explanation — like muttering 'the place is haunted' when the lights flicker, without holding it the way you hold the belief that Paris is the capital of France.

Mina: that makes 'belief' far too demanding. Loads of ordinary beliefs are badly evidenced and resistant to evidence — superstitions, footballing loyalties, your uncle's politics — and no one says those aren't really beliefs. If responsiveness to evidence were the test, half our beliefs would fail. The Capgras patient can be sincere, distressed and act on their delusion; that's enough.

Rob: but they often don't act on it. Some patients will call their spouse an impostor to your face, then carry on living with her, chat with her normally, and never phone the police. That 'double-bookkeeping' is exactly what you'd expect if the delusion isn't a belief in the ordinary way: real beliefs are inferentially integrated and broadly guide action unless something else blocks them. A supposed belief sealed off from everything else isn't doing the job beliefs do.

Mina: double-bookkeeping can be handled inside the doxastic view. Beliefs never act on their own — you also need desires, courage, opportunity — and people compartmentalise all the time. Inconsistent behaviour only shows that one belief isn't sufficient for action, which we already knew.

Rob: then the view risks becoming trivial. If 'sincerely avowed' is enough, then 'delusions are beliefs' is true by definition but doesn't tell us anything; if you instead add evidence-responsiveness and action-guidance, then at least some delusions fail the test. So the interesting question isn't yes-or-no, it's which delusions, in what respects, and what the word 'belief' is doing there in the first place.
