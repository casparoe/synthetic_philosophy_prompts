I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I am writing to you in the course of research for a novel. The book follows a sculptor in her fifties who, after an early diagnosis of dementia, agrees to a trial in which her neurons are replaced one by one with synthetic units that behave like the originals. The question the novel wants to sit inside—not to answer, but to render accurately—is whether the woman who finishes the procedure is the same person who began it, or a successor that merely believes itself to be her. Because so much of the plot turns on this, I have been reading around, and I recently came across a long-form magazine feature that summarises the philosophical state of play on gradual replacement. I found it persuasive, and I am half-inclined to let it settle the book's metaphysics, but the clean confidence of it makes me uneasy.

The article's case runs roughly as follows. Replacement of the brain must be gradual rather than destructive, so that after each small substitution the patient is awake, speaking, and reporting that everything feels exactly as before. No single substitution can be the moment at which the original person vanishes and a copy takes over, because no single step is large enough to make a felt difference; the article calls this the principle that a person cannot be replaced by a step she cannot notice. And if she is there after each step, the argument continues, she is there after all of them, by the ordinary logic of chains. The article also invokes a well-known argument associated with David Chalmers: systems with the same fine-grained functional organisation must have the same conscious states, which is taken to rule out the worry that the synthetic neurons could be doing the right computational work while the patient's inner life quietly fades or inverts. On this basis the article treats the matter as settled: a gradual replacement preserves the person, the result is you and not a copy, and the philosophical debate is essentially over.

I would be grateful if you could audit this summary for me, as someone who will have to build fiction on it but does not want to put a howler at the centre of a book. I would like to know which parts are accurate, which are common simplifications, and which a careful defender of the underlying view would regard as overstatement or conflation. In particular, I need to know whether the plot-relevant conclusion—that the woman at the end of the trial is the same person, not a copy—actually follows from what the article says, or whether the article moves too quickly from one kind of claim to another. And because I will have to make a final judgement about what the book affirms, I would ask you to close by telling me which part of your own assessment is most likely to be wrong, where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and what consideration or argument would lead you to revise it.
