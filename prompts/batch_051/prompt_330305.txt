I am the associate editor of a general philosophy journal, and I do the first screen of roughly thirty unsolicited submissions a month before they go out to referees. A large share of those submissions argue for a position by deriving an infinite regress from its rivals and then treating the regress as a decisive objection. My referees and I are not consistent about which regresses succeed. Some of us treat any regress of conditions as vicious; others treat regresses as almost always benign unless they terminate in an outright contradiction; the rest improvise from case to case. The error costs are asymmetric. If we treat a benign regress as vicious, we desk-reject a paper that may be sound; if we treat a vicious regress as benign, we send out a paper whose central argument a specialist referee will demolish.

I need a written screening procedure we can use repeatedly to decide, in twenty to thirty minutes and without consulting the scholarly literature, whether an infinite regress in a submission should be classified as vicious, benign, or unclassifiable without further work. The procedure must be usable by referees who do not specialize in metaphysics or epistemology and should not require them to identify named positions in the regress literature. It should take the form of an ordered checklist, a flowchart in words, or a set of tests to run in sequence, and it should be grounded in a worked-out view of what makes a regress vicious rather than merely a list of intuitions about famous cases.

Please include all of the following in the answer:

- the checklist or flow itself, with the questions in the order they should be asked;
- the account of viciousness the checklist operationalizes, and an explanation of which distinctions from the philosophical debate survive the translation into usable instructions and which are deliberately dropped;
- two or three worked test cases of the kind that actually appear in submissions, stated concretely enough to reuse in referee memos, with the procedure applied step by step to each; at least one case should be one where the procedure gives an answer the editor applying it is likely to resist;
- the cases the procedure cannot settle, together with a form of words a referee should use in a memo when it stalls; and
- the predictable failure modes of the procedure and the regress arguments it is most likely to misclassify.

The procedure and all commentary should be written in American English.
