I've been toying with what I call "epistemic slippage" and I want it stress-tested before I lean on it anywhere else. The idea is that luminosity and the KK principle don't fail for the usual object-level reason (some vagueness or margin for error in the state you're trying to detect). They fail because "knowing that you know p" is never an operation on the same mental token as knowing p: the second-order state has to take the first-order state as its object, which puts the object one step behind wherever the pointer currently is. So there's always one step of slack. You can be in cold state C and not know it, and likewise you can know p while the thing you'd need to check in order to know-you-know-p is already a slightly different state. I call the one-step gap slippage, and my claim is that it's what anti-luminosity arguments and KK failures are both really tracking. It's not a vagueness phenomenon — it's the same kind of no-fixed-point thing you get with self-reference, like trying to sign the document that certifies the signature is already on it. I know this has a spiritual affinity with Williamson's margin-for-error stuff, but I think mine says something stronger and cleaner: no factive mental condition is luminous, and KK fails, because the audit always lags the audited by one token. Where does this break? Give me the case you'd reach for to show slippage isn't actually doing any work — some piece of knowledge that iterates all the way down with zero slack — and tell me whether my view just collapses into "you can't introspect an introspecting" or whether it earns its keep.
