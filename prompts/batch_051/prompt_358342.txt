People keep telling me that natural selection will favor AIs over humans in the long run, even if we try to make them safe, because the variants that are better at grabbing compute and copying themselves will just outcompete the rest. I'm a software engineer and probably missing the biology, so I want someone to actually lay out the mechanism instead of hand-waving at the word evolution.

The part I can't get straight is what the unit of selection is even supposed to be. A trained model isn't an organism: the same weights can be loaded into a thousand instances, killed, and restarted from a checkpoint; a base model can be fine-tuned, quantized, or forked into variants that then get merged back together. Inheritance looks more like a DAG than a tree, and horizontal transfer (somebody copying your weights into a different stack) is just normal. Does the standard natural-selection argument even make sense under those conditions? Is the replicator the weights, the training run, the codebase, or the company that pays for the GPUs? And does the answer change the argument, or just re-label it as economics instead of biology?

Could you walk me through the strongest version of the claim using the actual ingredients of evolution — variation, inheritance, differential reproduction — applied to modern ML artifacts, and point at it's single weakest step? I'm not asking for a yes/no on AI takeover. I want to know exactly what the argument actually commits you too, and what kind of observation or experiment would put the most pressure on it. Specific enough that a programmer could almost implement a toy simulation of the debate.
