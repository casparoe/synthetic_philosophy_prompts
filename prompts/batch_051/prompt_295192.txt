You are an expert on decision theory and institutional design. I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been trying to understand a new proposal from a longtermist donor coalition. Their rule: before any project can be funded, the grantee must submit an explicit expected-value calculation, with outcomes converted into "lives saved equivalent"; any scenario with a probability below one in a million must be entered as zero, unless the scenario involves existential risk or extinction risk, in which case the true probability can be used. The coalition says this avoids Pascal's-wager-style fanaticism where a tiny probability of an infinite or astronomically large payoff swamps everything. I want you to treat this as a mechanism, not a moral position. Think carefully and start by brainstorming failure modes. What does the rule incentivize applicants to do with their probability estimates and their definitions of extinction risk? Who gets funded and who gets crowded out? How do the exemptions get gamed? Are there second-order effects on what research gets produced and what "lives saved equivalent" even comes to mean? And can the rule be fixed without either gutting it or turning it into a fanatic's charter?
