In epistemology, the debate over the norm of assertion has run for decades without convergence. One camp holds that the norm is knowledge: you should assert that p only if you know that p, which elegantly explains why a statement of the form p but I do not know that p is defective and why you should not flatly assert that your lottery ticket lost. A second camp holds that the norm is truth, since what our practice holds against an assertion is above all that it is false, even when the speaker was justified in believing it. A third family holds that the norm concerns the speaker's mind rather than the world: one should assert only what one believes, or only what one is justified in believing. Each side now has well-rehearsed replies to the others' examples, and yet no one seems to move. Diagnose this impasse. Is it a substantive disagreement about a single shared object, a merely verbal dispute about the word assertion, or several camps answering different questions under one heading—one about the constitutive rule of a speech act, one about conversational warrant, and one about epistemic responsibility? Given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress, or whether we should stop expecting one unified norm.
