I'm a doctoral student in political theory, and I'm currently writing a chapter on self-sufficiency and autarky as ideals for persons and polities. As part of that work, I've been trying to get clear on Aristotle's treatment of self-sufficiency (autarkeia) in the Nicomachean Ethics, and I keep finding myself stuck on a passage in Book I, chapter 7 (roughly 1097b). The passage, in a public-domain translation, reads:

"From the point of view of self-sufficiency the same result seems to follow; for the complete good is thought to be self-sufficient. Now by self-sufficient we do not mean that which is sufficient for a man by himself, for one who lives a solitary life, but also for parents, children, wife, and in general for his friends and fellow citizens, since man is sociable by nature. But some limit must be set to this; for if we extend our requirement to ancestors and descendants and friends’ friends we are in for an infinite series. Let us examine this question, however, on another occasion; the self-sufficient we now define as that which when isolated makes life desirable and lacking in nothing; and such we think happiness to be; and further we think it most desirable of all things, without being counted as one good thing among others—if it were so counted it would clearly be made more desirable by the addition of even the least of goods; for that which is added becomes an excess of goods, and of goods the greater is always more desirable. Happiness, then, is something complete and self-sufficient, and is the end of action."

What I find puzzling is the internal structure of the passage. Aristotle begins with the intuitive idea that the final good must be self-sufficient, then immediately denies that "self-sufficient" means sufficient for a person by himself or for a solitary life, since it must also cover parents, children, wife, friends, and fellow citizens. But after conceding that this social extension needs some limit, he sets the limit question aside and defines the self-sufficient as "that which when isolated makes life desirable and lacking in nothing"—which sounds, on its face, like exactly the solitary reading he just rejected. I would be grateful for help parsing what "when isolated" is doing in that definition if it is not the solitary-life reading: is it isolating the final good from other goods, isolating happiness as a candidate, or something else? More generally, I want to understand whether the tension here is real or only apparent, because my chapter turns on whether Aristotelian autarkeia is compatible with dependence on others, and on whether self-sufficiency is best understood as a property of lives, of ends, or of communities. I'd appreciate a close, sentence-by-sentence reading that stays within what the passage itself supports, without appeal to secondary literature or to translation debates beyond the wording as given.
