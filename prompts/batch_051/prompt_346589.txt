I advise retail clients at a small brokerage, and every few weeks I have to review a product that shows a positive expected return per period but loses money for most clients who hold it over any realistic horizon. Last night a friend and I spent hours on exactly this: she says a positive expected value is just what it means for a proposition to be good, and I kept answering that she is averaging over parallel histories, while a client lives one history in which the time-average growth of the account is what actually matters. Please turn that distinction between time averages and ensemble averages into a reusable decision procedure for deciding, in under an hour, from only the documents a client sees, and in a form a compliance reviewer will accept, whether such a proposition should be recommended, rejected, or escalated to a human. The procedure should ask its questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, say where it stops with a rejection rather than asking for more analysis, identify the steps that are really judgment calls dressed up as arithmetic, and state what it cannot settle, including where the decision is one of values and belongs to a person. Run it on three files: a hypothetical daily-reset leveraged fund with a positive one-day arithmetic mean return but a negative compound growth rate for a fully invested holder on most paths; a weekly favorable coin-flip available to a client who would risk only one percent of capital each round; and a put option on a client's concentrated stock position that has a negative expected return but caps the drawdown that would otherwise end the account. Make sure at least one application returns an answer I will dislike, and be explicit about where the procedure hands the final call back to me.
