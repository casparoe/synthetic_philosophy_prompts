In Book 6, Part A, section 1 of the Mencius, James Legge translates Mencius's exchange with Gaozi as follows:

"The philosopher Gao said, 'Man's nature is like the qi-willow, and righteousness is like a cup or a bowl. The fashioning benevolence and righteousness out of man's nature is like the making cups and bowls from the qi-willow.' Mencius replied, 'Can you, leaving untouched the nature of the willow, make with it cups and bowls? You must do violence and injury to the willow, before you can make cups and bowls with it. If you must do violence and injury to the willow in order to make cups and bowls with it, on your principles you must in the same way do violence and injury to humanity in order to fashion from it benevolence and righteousness! Your words, alas! would certainly lead all men on to reckon benevolence and righteousness to be calamities.'"

Xunzi holds the opposing view that human nature is bad and that goodness must be produced in us by deliberate effort, much as a potter shapes clay or a carpenter steams and bends wood. He would likely turn the craftsman example against Mencius, insisting that shaping raw material is not an injury but the only route to anything worth calling virtue. Write a dialogue between Mencius and Xunzi in which Xunzi responds to this exact passage by appropriating the craftsman metaphor, and Mencius then tries to show why his objection does not collapse into Xunzi's position. Give them at least three substantive exchanges each. Each must answer the other's latest point directly, and neither may be a strawman. End the dialogue at the sharpest unresolved difference between them rather than at a reconciliation. Write in American English, and keep the dialogue to no more than 700 words, excluding the quoted passage.
