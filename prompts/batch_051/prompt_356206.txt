Last night I had a long argument with a friend about Camus's abolitionism. He was willing to concede that Camus was right about the guillotine, but he insisted that Camus's objections were aimed at that specific machine: its violence, its visibility, its occasional botches. Modern practice, he said, has answered precisely those objections. Lethal injection is quiet, medically supervised, and carried out out of sight; had Camus been faced with that instead of the blade, my friend argued, his case against capital punishment would have lost most of its force. I pushed back, but I came away unsure whether I could reconstruct the structure of Camus's actual position.

Set out what you take Camus's real case against the death penalty to be, as distinct from a case merely against the guillotine, and then extend it forward. What would he have made of lethal injection and the broader medicalization of execution? In particular, would a method that is widely described as humane answer his objections, or would it leave the core of the objection untouched, or make it stronger? Which of his arguments survive unchanged, which are weakened, and which, if any, are reinforced by the shift to lethal injection?
