For a novel I am planning I need to know where a novelist friend of mine, whom I shall call Clara, draws the line between what a writer may take from another person's life and what remains that person's own. Clara writes fiction built closely from real lives, and across two decades she has kept a private casuistry of cases in which she judged herself entitled, or not entitled, to use the material of a real person. She applies the distinction with startling consistency but will not state the rule. I can reconstruct six of her verdicts.

1. A neighbour's nightly custom of walking the perimeter of his garden while reciting the names of his dead siblings, observed from her window for years and transplanted, in her novel, into another country and another century. Verdict: permissible.

2. A colleague's confidence, offered over dinner, about the colleague's mother's institutionalization, used with names changed but the colleague plainly recognizable to friends they held in common. Verdict: impermissible.

3. A public figure's long-documented eating disorder, disclosed in the figure's own two memoirs and many interviews, used with the figure's identifying details intact. Verdict: permissible.

4. A former lover's letter recounting childhood abuse that he had never before shared, used while keeping his hometown and profession but changing his name and sex. Verdict: impermissible.

5. A schoolfriend's habit of shredding paper napkins before a meal, given to a background character who otherwise resembled no one. Verdict: permissible.

6. A stranger's public video diary about his divorce, borrowed for the skeleton of a plot with the stranger's first name kept. Verdict: permissible.

Now consider a new case. The walls of Clara's apartment are thin, and for some months she has been unable to avoid overhearing the man in the next flat deliver low, nightly monologues, evidently to his wife, in which he confesses that he once abandoned an earlier family and had lived under a second name. She knows him only through these overheard words, and she cannot seek permission without revealing that she has heard what he believed was private. She wishes to use the situation itself — an abandoned first family, a rebuilt life under another name, a confession long deferred — but to change its setting, period, professions, and circumstances so thoroughly that no reader, including the man, could connect the story to him.

Predict Clara's verdict, but do not stop there. Lay out the rule or rules consistent with all six earlier verdicts, state what each candidate rule predicts about the new case, and give your best prediction with reasons for preferring one rule over the others. Then describe one further case, specified concretely, whose verdict would settle which rule Clara is actually running. In doing so, face the underlying question directly: is information about a person in some property-like sense theirs, so that using it without leave is always a taking, or is the wrong in these cases better located elsewhere — in breach of confidence, exposure, or harm?
