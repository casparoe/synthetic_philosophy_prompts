I am a novelist, and the book I am writing turns on a question I initially assumed was only psychological but now suspect is partly conceptual. My protagonist receives a psychiatric diagnosis in her early twenties, and over the following decade that diagnosis becomes the dominant idiom of her self-understanding: she retells her childhood memories through it, explains her failures and her choices in its terms, and arranges her plans around evading whatever the diagnosis is supposed to predict. I want the novel to be honest about a causal question that the story keeps pressing against: did the label change her, or did it merely name something that was already there? In my reading I encountered Ian Hacking's looping effect, the idea that classifying people can alter the people classified, and that those alterations can in turn force the classification itself to be revised. I find the thesis evocative, but I have rarely seen it stated in a form sharp enough to be put to empirical test rather than merely illustrated.

I would like you to design the best test you can of the looping-effect claim, applied to a single psychiatric category of your choosing. The design must satisfy these constraints. It must be a study a real researcher could in principle run. It must be able to distinguish looping from the two rival readings I most worry about: mere relabeling of symptoms, and improved detection of a condition that was present all along. And it must be narrow enough that a positive result would support looping for the category studied without licensing a blanket claim about every psychiatric kind. Where the design allows, keep distinct the individual-level claim that this person was changed by being classified and the kind-level claim that the population of classified people changed, and that the diagnostic criteria then changed with them; say whether the individual case can borrow support from the kind-level test or needs its own. Then walk me through what result would favor the looping reading and why, what result would favor the label-reveals-rather-than-changes reading and why, and what part of the looping idea no empirical design of this sort could settle even in principle. I am less interested in a bibliography than in a design precise enough to serve as the factual spine of a scene in which a researcher and my protagonist argue about what her diagnosis did to her.
