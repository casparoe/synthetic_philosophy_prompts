In my formal epistemology course we've been doing the principle of indifference, and it's made me realise I basically run my whole life on a version of it. My rule is: if I have no evidence that discriminates among a finite list of possibilities I can actually state, I assign equal credence to each. Like, if I genuinely have no idea whether my flatmate's package will arrive today, tomorrow, or the day after, I go 1/3 each and decide whether to stay in based on that. I know its supposed to be in trouble because of Bertrand's paradox: with a cube factory where all you know is that the side length is between 0 and 1, you get different answers depending on whether you put the uniform distribution over side length, face area, or volume — the probability the side is under 1/2 comes out 1/2, 1/4, or 1/8. So the same ignorance plus a different way of describing the possibilities gives different numbers, which seems to blow up the idea that ignorance alone picks a unique distribution. But I want to know whether my everyday version survives that. My outcome lists are always fixed and kind of 'natural', not a continuous parameter I could redescribe however I want. Though honestly the same thing can happen with finite lists: if I'd framed the package as 'within two days' vs 'after two days,' I'd have given the late outcome 1/2 instead of 1/3, so maybe 'natural list' is doing more work than I want to admit. Is my rule defensible as is, or is it the same mistake in better clothes? If it needs fixing, what's the closest defensible version — does it have to say which partitions count as legitimate, and can it say that without being circular? My friend says the right response in these cases is to go imprecise and not assign point probabilities at all, just have a range of credences. Does that actually dodge Bertrand, or does it just move the arbitrariness somewhere else? I'd honestly rather keep my 1/3s if there's a version of the rule that lets me, but I want to know whether that's rational or just comfortable.
