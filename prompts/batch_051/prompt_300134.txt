I've been going through Grice in a philosophy of language class, and the simplified account of speaker meaning we were given is roughly this: a person means something by what they do only if they intend (1) to produce some response in the hearer, (2) for the hearer to recognize that intention, and (3) for that recognition to be at least part of the hearer's reason for responding that way. I know the real Grice has more bells and whistles, but let's work with this version.

Here's the case I keep going back and forth on. Rosa can't speak because of a neurological condition; she writes using a speech-generating device she controls with eye gaze, and the device has predictive text trained on years of her old journals and emails, so it sometimes finishes her sentences in a voice that sounds very "her" even when she didn't select those exact words. One night she and her sister are arguing about who has to look after their mother. Rosa selects the sentence "I can't keep doing this," meaning she can't keep carrying the care routine alone. The device renders it as "I can't keep doing this to you." Her sister reads it as an accusation — that she, the sister, has been making things worse. Rosa watches the sister's face fall, understands the misrendering, and lets the sentence stand: partly because its also a little true, partly because correcting it would mean reopening a fight she can't face. Apply the three criteria to Rosa's situation and ask whether she meant "I can't keep doing this to you" by that output. Work through the criteria honestly, and say where the case pushes back on them — for instance, is the rendered sentence really her utterance at all, can an intention be formed after the words appear (retroactively ratified rather than planned), and does meaning really require that she intend the sister to recognize her intention? Finally, tell me how confident you are in your verdict, and single out the point you're least sure about.
