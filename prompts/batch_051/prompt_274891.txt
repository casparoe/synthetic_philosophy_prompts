I am a retired physical scientist who has spent the past few years working through decision theory, and I have become preoccupied with a question about updateless decision theory that I have not seen posed squarely. I will first state the argument as I understand it, then describe the development that worries me.

The updateless argument, as I understand it, runs as follows. In Newcomb-like problems, conditionalizing one's choice on the observation one in fact has is a mistake when that observation is correlated with the outcome in a way one's act does not bring about. For then choosing the act that looks best conditional on the observation simply concedes the correlation; a better general policy was available all along. The prescription is to choose a policy — a function from each observation one might have had to an action — as if from a standpoint before learning which observation one occupies, and then to execute that policy's output at the observation one actually occupies. Evidential and causal decision theory appear to fail in complementary cases, and the updateless diagnosis is that both fail for the same underlying reason: each first conditionalizes on present information and only then chooses, and that two-step structure is what allows the correlation to be set against the agent.

The setting this argument silently presumes, however, is a Cartesian one. The agent sits at a fixed boundary, with an input channel, a definite space of possible observations, and a world that runs outside it; it surveys its possible observations from a vantage outside them and selects a global policy. That is exactly the picture that the recent embedded-agency literature has called into question. On the embedded view, the agent is itself part of the world: its computations are physical events with causal consequences; its representations, its estimates of its own options, and its acts of choosing are themselves events that can be correlated with outcomes; and there may be no external standpoint from which a well-defined prior over the observations the agent might have made can be fixed, because the agent's own construction, self-modification, and interaction with its environment are among the possibilities that would have to be enumerated in advance.

My question is whether the updateless argument survives this change of setting, and if so in what form. Which premise is actually bearing the load, and does the embedded picture break it? If it breaks, can it be repaired into something weaker but still distinct from evidential and causal decision theory — perhaps a notion of acting on a policy whose expected value is computed by a process inside the world, under a distribution that is itself one of the things the agent may come to revise? Or does updatelessness turn out to be intelligible only from the outside view, and collapse once the agent is placed inside the world? I am asking what becomes of the argument, not for a survey of the literature.
