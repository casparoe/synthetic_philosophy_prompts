I'm putting together a seminar on Heidegger's tool-analysis and I keep going back and forth with a friend in human-computer interaction about whether the whole "breakdown" move still does the philosophical work Heidegger wanted it to do. The picture as I understand it runs like this. When you're absorbed in hammering, the hammer is ready-to-hand (Zuhandenheit): it withdraws, and you don't attend to it as an object, you attend to the work. The surrounding web — the bench, the nails, the project, other people — is also there, but not as a set of items laid out for inspection. Then the hammer breaks, or is missing, or the work gets interrupted some other way, and suddenly the hammer jumps out along with the whole network of purposes it belonged to; it becomes present-at-hand (Vorhandenheit), a thing with properties that can be scrutinized. So on this reading, breakdown is bearing a lot of weight: it's the main place where the normally invisible background of equipment becomes visible at all.

Now here's what I can't settle. Suppose we're in a modern workshop where every tool is instrumented: strain gauges, battery telemetry, a dashboard logging each use, predictive maintenance flags, the works. The hammer never has to fail for it to be "noticed" — it's always already thematized as an object with a status, a wear curve, and a slot in a workflow diagram. Its use is cheaply logged and visible to everyone in the shop. So does Heidegger's argument survive that development, or not? Is the always-on dashboard just a different route to the same revelation — a kind of breakdown-in-advance that lights up the referential whole without anything actually breaking — or does it undercut the premise that equipment is normally inconspicuous and only steps forward in failure? If the argument survives, say exactly which claim survives unchanged and which claim has to be revised; if it dies, say what the remaining insight about tools and interruption should be called instead.
