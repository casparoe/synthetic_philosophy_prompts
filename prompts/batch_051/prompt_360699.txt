Consider a familiar objection to the doctrine of original sin. The doctrine holds that every human being is born guilty, or at least liable to divine condemnation, because of a sin committed by our first ancestor before any of us existed. The familiar objection runs: strict, backward-looking guilt can attach only to what a person has done or willfully omitted. A newborn has done nothing and chosen nothing, so charging that newborn with another's sin is unjust and arguably unintelligible. Call the load-bearing assumption the voluntarist premise: backward-looking moral responsibility requires some voluntary act or omission by the very person who is held responsible.

Now notice a social development that appears to put that premise under pressure. In much contemporary discussion of reparations, colonial wrongs, environmental debt, and structural injustice, people are said to be complicit, culpable, or duty-bound to repair harms they never personally chose, simply because they were born into a nation, beneficiary class, institution, or line of descent standing in a historical wrong. Sometimes the language approaches inherited guilt; sometimes it is softened to inherited obligation or responsibility for repair. But either way, a large part of contemporary moral thought now treats unchosen, inherited relations as capable of grounding backward-looking responsibility of some kind.

Does the voluntarist objection to original sin survive this development, or does it need repair? Is inherited responsibility for historical wrongs relevantly similar to inherited guilt for the first sin, or is the difference principled (for example, because historical injustices still configure present institutions and benefits, while the fall does not, or because duties of repair concern what we must now do whereas guilt concerns what we now deserve)? If the objection can be repaired, state the strongest repaired version and what it still rules out; if it cannot be repaired, say what the honest position is for someone who accepts inherited responsibility in the social case but rejects original sin. Focus on the structure of the argument rather than on citing positions.
