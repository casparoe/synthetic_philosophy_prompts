Two members of my philosophy reading group argued about a passage in chapter five of Hobbes's *Leviathan* (1651). The passage, as Daniel summarized it, says that geometry is demonstrable because the lines and figures from which we reason are drawn and described by ourselves, so we know the causes of what we demonstrate; in natural philosophy, by contrast, we must trace backwards from effects to their possible causes and can never achieve the same certainty. Daniel read this as a thesis about what a creature can genuinely know: you can have scientific knowledge only of what you yourself make or do, because only then do you know the causes. Mara thought that over-inflates the text. She said Hobbes's point is about the conventional, stipulative character of geometrical first principles: the geometer does not produce the space itself, but chooses the definitions and constructions, and the demonstrations are certain because they follow from those chosen terms. Daniel replied that this reading cannot explain why Hobbes singles out geometry and withholds the same status from physics, since physics also begins with definitions. Mara answered that the difference is causal: the geometer's object, as represented in the proof, is wholly in our power, whereas the causes of natural bodies are not; definitional choice alone is not the issue. Daniel then pushed: if the criterion is that we must make the object in order to know it, how can Hobbes also claim a demonstrative science of politics, where human passions and their causes are not of our making? Mara replied that this is precisely the pressure point: either Hobbes was inconsistent, or Daniel's maker-knowledge criterion is too strong. They asked me to referee. Please go through this exchange move by move: for each stage, who is right about what, and what interpretation of the passage best resolves or dissolves their disagreement?
