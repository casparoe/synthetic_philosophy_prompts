Act as a senior scholar of comparative memory politics who has chaired enough seminars to know how hostile Q&As in area-studies departments actually go. I'm giving a paper at a German and European studies center next month, and the room will be mostly historians of postwar Germany, plus a historian of modern Japan, a historian of the American South, and a political scientist who works on Turkey. They are not, as a group, friendly to big cross-national comparisons; the Germanists think Vergangenheitsbewältigung is a term of art you earn the right to use only after years in the archives, and the Japan and Turkey people think Holocaust-centered memory categories distort their cases from the start.

The thesis I'm defending is something like: Germany's "working through the past" is usually treated as an exportable model, but compared with Japan, Turkey, and the American South it looks less like a transferable technique of memory politics and more like the contingent product of total defeat, foreign occupation, and elite turnover. My move is that "coming to terms with the past" is not one thing; each case is working through a different failure with different mechanisms, and holding them all to the German benchmark flattens them into a single teleology of healthy versus denialist memory. My sketch of the argument, so you have something to aim at: the German path depended on (1) unconditional defeat and occupation, (2) real elite turnover, and (3) sustained external and generational pressure from the 1960s onward. Japan had defeat and occupation but only partial elite turnover; Turkey had national refoundation without foreign occupation and then decades of official forgetting; the American South had defeat and Reconstruction but then a successful Lost Cause counter-memory, written back into institutions. So what people want to import from Germany may be the one part of it that cannot be imported: losing a total war and being occupied.
