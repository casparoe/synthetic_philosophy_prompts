I am a high school student, and I recently watched a school disciplinary decision that left me confused about a principle people seem to treat as obvious. A classmate was given a serious sanction for violating a rule she had no way to know about: it appeared only in a buried section of an online handbook students are never directed to, and it had not been announced or enforced before. When she said she had not known the rule existed, the administrator ended the discussion with the familiar maxim, "Ignorance of the law is no excuse."

I want to understand precisely what that maxim is asserting, because it seems to slide between several very different claims. It could mean only that a rule's binding force does not vanish just because someone is unaware of it, which sounds almost tautological. It could mean that a legal system is entitled to act as though everyone knows the law, because the law is published and ignorance is usually avoidable. Or it could mean that a person who violates an unknown rule is just as blameworthy as one who violates it knowingly.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that "Ignorance of the law is no excuse" could be making? For each reading, please say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible but only under certain conditions, or actually false, and identify what it presupposes about how knowable the rule was, how the person's ignorance arose, and whose knowledge matters. Finally, tell me which reading or readings the maxim needs in order to justify punishing someone who could not reasonably have learned of the rule, and whether those readings survive the move from a legal system—where rules are at least supposed to be public, stable, and accessible—to an institution like a school.
