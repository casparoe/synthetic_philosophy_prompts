Below are notes for a short essay I'm drafting, titled "Unrigorous, or Differently Rigorous?" I'd like your comments on the argument, not line edits.

The essay's core claim is that many familiar dismissals of premodern mathematics as unrigorous, confused, or merely heuristic are quietly anachronistic. They treat "rigor" as one thing—roughly, the modern norm of deductive proof from explicit axioms—and then judge earlier work by its distance from that norm. I want to argue that this is like judging a sonnet by the standards of a haiku: the mistake isn't harshness, but applying evaluative categories that don't transfer. I distinguish three failure modes. First, precursor reading: treating an earlier concept as the not-yet-arrived version of a modern one, as when the Greek method of exhaustion is read as a primitive limit argument. Second, proof-standard anachronism: dismissing an argument as invalid because it relies on diagrammatic reasoning or unstated default assumptions that its own community accepted. Third, concept anachronism: imposing modern distinctions—algebraic versus geometric, number versus magnitude—on older texts that did not draw those lines.

My own worry is that the argument threatens to prove too much. If every practice is judged only by its own standards, then no practice can be compared with any other, and the apparently historical claim that "rigor changed" becomes unstatable from inside the view. I also suspect the argument cuts against my own evaluative vocabulary: I still want to say that some old arguments are simply mistaken.

Please comment on: (a) whether the three-way distinction is clean, or whether the last two modes collapse into each other; (b) whether the relativism worry is genuine or manufactured; and (c) whether the final sentence concedes too much. Also flag where the argument leans on a contested reading of older mathematical practice. For each major point you make, say how confident you are, and single out the point you are least sure of.
