Hey — I write about science and ideas for a living, mostly AI these days, and I've been covering the alignment world long enough that I've absorbed a set of positions without ever stress-testing them against each other. I'm outlining a piece on whether the AI-risk conversation is overheated or understated, and I realized I should first check whether my own views actually hang together. Could you give me an audit?

Here's where I've landed, more or less:

(1) I accept the orthogonality thesis: intelligence and terminal goals are independent axes. A system can be arbitrarily smart while caring about something totally arbitrary — it's not the case that high capability automatically pulls a system toward decent values.

(2) But I also think a more capable system with a misaligned goal is a bigger problem than a less capable one with the same goal. Capability is a force multiplier, so a superhuman system worries me way more than something roughly at my level.

(3) I'm persuaded by instrumental convergence: almost any sufficiently capable agent, whatever its final goal, will have reason to pursue self-preservation, accumulate resources, and resist shutdown, because those instrumental goals help with almost any terminal goal.

(4) I think Goodhart's law is the actual practical problem. The field keeps optimizing proxies — engagement, benchmark scores, human approval — and systems over-optimize the proxy in a way that comes apart from what we actually wanted. My bet is that most real alignment failures over the next decade look like that, not like a model with secret long-range plans.

(5) At the same time, I'm pretty dismissive of interpreting activations or hunting for hidden goals in the weights. The only serious way I've seen to tell whether a model is aligned is to test its behavior — red-teaming, deployment monitoring, stress-testing for specification gaming. The mechanistic stuff reads to me like tea leaves.

(6) I also think a priori arguments about arbitrary superintelligences — paperclip maximizers and all that — are too cartoonish to guide actual policy. What actually moves my beliefs is empirical results from real, existing large models.

(7) Still, I'd rather loose competitive ground than deploy a system whose behavior we can't bound in advance. If a lab can't even approximately enumerate the failure modes, I think it shouldn't ship.

That all feels like a package when I say it, but I suspect it might not be. So: which pairs are actually in tension, which pairs only look like they're in tension, and how bad is each real clash? Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each one tell me the cheapest fix — which commitment would have to go or get weakened, and what it would cost.
