I have been developing a principle I call constitutional sampling, and I want an honest assessment of it, including whether it already exists under a better name.

The principle: a political assembly is democratically legitimate to the extent that it is a statistically representative sample of the population it governs. On this view, elections and sortition — choosing legislators by lot — are not two procedures for achieving the same end; they express different accounts of what legitimacy consists in. Elections treat legitimacy as authorization by choice: the ruled confer power through the act of selecting. Sortition treats legitimacy as authorization by resemblance: the assembly stands in for the people because it reproduces their composition rather than their preferences.

This suggests a stronger claim. The usual case for lottocracy, understood as government by randomly selected bodies, is framed as a repair: breaking elite capture, reducing polarization, making government more responsive. I think its real force is that it changes the currency of legitimacy from choice to resemblance. My difficulty is that resemblance is a property of samples, not obviously a property of a political relation. An assembly that mirrored the population could still be unaccountable or foolish, and being selected by lot does not by itself make it the case that this stranger may make binding decisions for me. Yet every remedy I try reintroduces consent, accountability, or electoral authorization — precisely the criteria the principle was meant to displace.

So: is constitutional sampling a genuine alternative foundation for democratic legitimacy, or is it a metaphor doing most of the work? If the core idea already exists in discussions of sortition or representation, what is it called, and where does my version depart from it? And what is the sharpest objection — the point at which the principle, stated in its strongest form, fails rather than merely becomes uncomfortable?
