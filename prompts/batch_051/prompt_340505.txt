I'm a novelist — biographical fiction, mostly, so this one cuts close — and I sit on the board of a small literary estate that controls the rights to the novels and letters of a mid-century novelist called Adela Marsh. (Not a real person; I'm compressing a handful of real cases I've seen.) A production company wants to adapt her best-known novel, but only on condition they can add a subplot in which Marsh, during the Red Scare, denounced a fellow writer to protect her own visa. There's no archival evidence she did anything like it; the screenwriter argues the scene gives the film moral complexity and the other writer was never actually blacklisted anyway. Half the board says approving it makes us accomplices to defaming a woman who can't answer back. The other half says the dead have no reputations left to injure and everyone knows biopics invent things. Draft the board's short written decision — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with reasons both wings can live with, and be explicit about wether we owe Marsh herself anything, or the real dead generally once we make characters of them.
