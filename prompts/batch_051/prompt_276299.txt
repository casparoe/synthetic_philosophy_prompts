I find myself drawn to a case-first methodology in philosophy: intuitions about particular cases are the basic data, and general principles earn their keep by systematizing those data; when a principle and a firm case intuition conflict, the principle is ordinarily the thing I expect to revise. Yet I also hold some principles with more confidence than I hold any particular case judgment. The clearest is the transitivity of comparative notions like betterness: if A is better than B and B is better than C, then A is better than C. Presented with a purported cycle — A better than B, B better than C, but C better than A — I do not feel the pull to doubt transitivity; I feel the pull to doubt the case, or one of the pairwise judgments, or the assumption that the same betterness relation is in play at each step. A similar certainty attaches to a kind of local simplicity: a single recalcitrant case should not force an epicyclic patchwork of principles, and if it seems to, I suspect the case judgment before the simplicity requirement. That looks like a genuine collision between my methodology and my actual epistemic priorities.
