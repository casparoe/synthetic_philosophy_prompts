An essay on decision theory that I read gives the following account of Good's theorem, and then uses it to settle a debate about whether rational people can decline information:

'Good's theorem proves mathematically that free information can never make you worse off in expectation. The reasoning is essentially trivial: whatever evidence arrives, you are always free to ignore it and do what you would have done anyway; so your expected payoff after receiving the evidence is never lower than before, and it is strictly higher whenever the evidence could change your action. A rational agent therefore never prefers ignorance. People who say there are some things it is better not to know are confusing a vague dread with the expectation calculus: free information is always a pure asset, whether the decision is a private one, a business decision, or a move in a game against other people.'

Later, the essay invokes the theorem to argue that a patient who declines a free genetic test for a late-onset condition, on the grounds that she would rather not know, is being irrational. That conclusion feels too strong to me, but I don't know whether the problem is in Good's result itself, in this summary of it, or in the application. Work through the quoted account claim by claim and give me an accuracy audit. I want to know: what Good's theorem actually establishes and under what assumptions; which parts of the quoted account a careful decision theorist would endorse and which they would resist; where the essay slides from the theorem to a stronger conclusion; and whether the genetic-test conclusion survives once the accurate core is stated carefully.
