I teach philosophy of AI, and there's a debate I keep coming back to that feels wierdly stuck, and I want a diagnosis rather than a survey. The question is whether "low impact"—or "avoiding side effects"—can be defined for an AI without first having a full specification of what is valuable. One camp defines impact as divergence from a baseline where the AI did nothing (or never existed); that route needs a metric for divergence, which already smuggles in a standard of which changes matter, and if the baseline is "no AI," then the AI's own existence counts as impact. A second camp tries to make the agent care only about its narrow task and be indifferent to everything else; that sounds safe until you notice that indifferent is not harmless—an indifferent agent can still take a shortcut that wrecks everything as long as the task gets done, and it has no reason to avoid a gamble whose expected consequences for the world are terrible. A third camp says impact is lost flexibility: an action is low-impact if it leaves a wide range of possible future goals still attainable. I like that one, but the choice of which goals count as "the range" looks like value theory with the serial numbers filed off. Each camp has clean counterexamples to the others, and every new proposal is a patch that gets a counterexample in the next paper. My working hypothesis is that "impact" is doing triple duty—physical difference, moral difference, lost flexibility—and the debate has stalled because people think they share a question when they don't. I want you to commit to a diagnosis: is it verbal, substantive but ill-posed, or is there a hidden formal question underneath? And given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress, or should we stop expecting the term to do this job?
