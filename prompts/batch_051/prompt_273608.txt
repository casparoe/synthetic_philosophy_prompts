I'm teaching an advanced undergrad seminar this term on computationalism, and I want to give them a paper prompt that pits two ways of thinking about machine intelligence against each other. I've been rereading Turing's Computing Machinery and Intelligence, and there's a passage in his discussion of Lady Lovelace's objection that I keep coming back to. Turing presents Lovelace as claiming that the Analytical Engine has no pretensions to originate anything: it can only do whatever we know how to order it to perform. He then considers whether a machine could ever surprise its programmer, and if so whether that would show originality or just a mistake in the programming. The way he handles that has always struck me as subtle, because he seems to concede something to Lovelace while also wanting to insist that machines can surprise us in a serious sense.

Here's what I want the students to work through. Compare Turing's treatment of the Lovelace objection with the claim made by some computationalists that the brain literally is a computer, where cognition is computation over mental representations. On the face of it, Turing and the computationalists should be allies: they both think the mind's capacities can be duplicated by a machine. But Turing's discussion of originality seems to put pressure on the idea that a computational system's behaviour is fixed once its program and input are fixed. Is that pressure real, or does it rest on a confusion between computationalism as an empirical thesis about the brain and Lovelace's worry about what an engineered system can do? In particular, is there a reading of computationalism that can make room for the kind of originality or surprise Turing was trying to rescue, without giving up the claim that the brain is a computer? I'd like the students to say where they think Turing actually stand on this, and whether his discussion is best read as a version of computationalism, a qualification of it, or something else.
