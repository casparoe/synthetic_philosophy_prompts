I teach an upper-level philosophy of language seminar, and next week's topic is whether grammatical categories—first the major parts of speech, then case and adpositions (prepositions and postpositions)—are natural kinds. I want a handout with a decision tree that sorts a student into a position on this question by a short series of concrete questions, without their needing to know the positions' labels in advance. The exercise is meant to reveal that the debate is really several debates: how category membership is grounded, why noun-like and verb-like classes recur across languages, whether categories have sharp boundaries, and what natural-kind status is being required to mean.

Please build the tree under the following constraints. Make the root node the question whether a word's part-of-speech category is fixed semantically (by what it denotes) or formal-distributionally (by what it combines with and how it inflects), because I want the discussion to start from the inadequacy of the schoolbook gloss that nouns simply name persons, places, or things. Each internal node should be a question a student can answer without already knowing the names of the positions, and each leaf should carry the position's usual label, or "unnamed," together with one sentence on the view's most awkward commitment.

The branches should include at least these four questions, though you may add others if the logic demands them:

(1) Whether the recurrence of noun-like and verb-like classes across unrelated languages is best explained by the structure of the world itself, by a species-specific language faculty, or substantially by inherited habits of grammatical description and cross-linguistic comparison.
(2) Whether gradient or borderline membership in a category—event-denoting nominals such as "destruction," or a nominalized verb such as "the run"—is compatible with that category's being a natural kind, or whether natural kinds require near-sharp boundaries.
(3) Whether natural-kind status demands mind-independence or only explanatory robustness within a science of language.
(4) Whether the considerations that would make the major parts of speech natural kinds transfer to case and adpositions, or whether those relational categories require a separate branch with different tests.

The leaves must include at least: a strong realist position on which the major parts of speech carve at joints in extra-linguistic reality; an innatist or cognitive-universalist position on which they are natural kinds of the grammar itself; a prototype or statistical-cluster position on which the categories are robust but graded; a conventionalist position on which they are imposed by descriptive practice rather than discovered; and the position that is the natural home of someone who treats the noun-verb contrast as a natural kind while remaining deflationary about case and adpositions. Where a combination of answers corresponds to no defended position, say so, with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of positions the tree cannot cleanly separate and say what extra question would separate them.

Keep the tree to at most eight branching questions and between six and nine leaves. It should be navigable in about ten minutes by a student working with the test cases "destruction," "redness," "the run," a putative adjective-less language, and two or three ordinary English prepositions. The deeper point the handout should force is that "natural kind" is asked to do several different jobs in this debate; the tree should make visible, rather than paper over, the possibility of being realistic about the lexical categories in one sense of naturalness and deflationary about the relational categories in another.
