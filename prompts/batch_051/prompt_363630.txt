I am a philosopher working through what I actually believe about the value of information, drawing on my verdicts about particular cases rather than on general theory. Please act as a professional philosopher with expertise in formal epistemology and decision theory, think through the cases one at a time before trying to unify them, and begin by brainstorming the distinct reasons a piece of information might be unwelcome.

The background principle I am inclined to accept is Good's theorem, stated roughly as follows: for an agent who maximizes expected utility and updates by conditionalization, a piece of information that costs nothing cannot lower the agent's expected utility, provided the agent remains free to ignore it, the set of available acts is otherwise unchanged, and utility depends only on the acts' outcomes. The expected value of information is therefore always nonnegative, and it is zero only when no possible observation would change the agent's choice.

Yet my verdicts on cases keep pulling me away from that principle:

1. Umbrella. Offered a free, perfectly reliable forecast of whether it will rain before I decide whether to carry an umbrella, I take it and regard it as genuinely valuable.

2. Untreatable diagnosis. Offered a free test that would tell me with certainty whether I will develop a progressive, untreatable illness that no action of mine can affect, I decline; I would pay a substantial amount not to receive the result. There are no decisions to make with it, and I judge that being informed would make me worse off.

3. Spoiled film. Before watching a suspense film, someone offers to tell me the twist ending. I refuse. It would change no choice I make, but knowing the ending would drain the experience, and I count that as a real loss, not an aesthetic pretense.

4. Duty to disclose. While negotiating the sale of my company, I can choose whether to run a review of the accounts. Running it will tell me whether a certain legal defect exists; once I know, the law requires me to disclose it to the buyer or abandon the deal on worse terms. I avoid the review and would pay to avoid the information, not for emotional reasons but because receiving it would remove options I currently have.

5. Inadmissible evidence. As a juror, I know that a piece of evidence has been ruled inadmissible and that I am instructed to disregard it. I also believe that hearing it would shift my probability judgments in a way I cannot fully undo, making my final verdict less likely to track the admissible evidence. I would rather not hear it; the information would make me less reliable, not more.

I want to know whether one principle gets all five verdicts, or close. Please state a principle—or a small set of candidate principles—designed to capture as much of this pattern as possible, and say exactly what each clause is for. For each candidate, say which of my verdicts it accommodates and which it cannot, and at what cost in complexity; be honest about where the pressure is. Then apply your preferred principle to a further case I have not settled. I am offered a free, perfectly reliable psychological test that predicts, with high accuracy, that under extreme stress I would betray a promise to a person I love. I could in principle use the result to change how I structure my commitments, or to prepare others; I also know it would be painful to learn and might change how I see myself. Should I take the test? What does your principle say, and does it capture what you would actually advise?
