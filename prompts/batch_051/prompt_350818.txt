I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. In ordinary moral judgment we rank wrongs without much unease: a mugging is worse than a stolen sandwich, and a murder is worse than either. But when the wrongs reach the scale of historical atrocities—the transatlantic slave trade, the Holocaust, Stalin's purges, the Khmer Rouge killings—many people, myself included, feel that ranking them is obscene, as if the question "which was worse?" insults the victims. As an engineer I know the difference between a total order and a partial order, and I wonder whether moral badness is partial-order structured—in which case the failure to compare should already appear among small wrongs—or whether something changes at scale. Is there a principled difference between comparing ordinary wrongs and comparing atrocities, or is the refusal to compare merely a social taboo that comes with the size of the numbers? I want the strongest case that the two situations are the same in kind, the strongest case that they differ in kind, and a verdict on whether the ordinary asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned.
