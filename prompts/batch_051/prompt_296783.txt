I keep circling back to Émilie du Châtelet’s Réflexions sur le Bonheur (I’ve been reading the old Wikisource text), and this passage won’t let me go:

Les moralistes qui disent aux humains : Réprimez vos passions et maîtrisez vos desirs, si vous voulez être heureux, ne connoissent pas le chemin du bonheur. On n’est heureux que par des goûts ou des passions satisfaites, parce qu’on n’est pas toujours assez heureux pour avoir des passions, et qu’au défaut des passions, il faut bien se contenter des goûts.

My rough English: the moralists who tell people to repress their passions and master their desires if they want to be happy don’t know the route to happiness. One is happy only through satisfied tastes or passions, because one isn’t always lucky enough to have passions, and absent passions one has to settle for tastes.

A friend from my reading group, who did some philosophy in grad school, summarized her position for me as something like: du Châtelet is a hedonist who thinks happiness is just a matter of maximizing intense pleasurable feeling; she rejects the moralists’ advice to master the passions because that would frustrate the only route to happiness; and, in the same essay, she says reason should be kept away from our pleasant illusions because those illusions do more for us than truth does. I’m a retired biochemist, so my instinct is to check the summary against the actual passage before I accept his gloss. His summary sounds right in a broad way, but the passage says a couple of things — about the absence of passions, about goûts, about what the moralists “don’t know” — that make me suspect the hedonist label is doing some work her own text doesn’t quite do. Is his summary fair? Go through it claim by claim and tell me what the passage and the essay support, what is overstatement, and whether her view really amounts to maximizing intense pleasure.
