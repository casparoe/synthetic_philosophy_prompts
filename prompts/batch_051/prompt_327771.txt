Next month I'm giving a single 90-minute session on Adam Smith's impartial spectator to a mixed group of econ PhD students and philosophy MA students, and I want a plan for the session, not the session itself. The econ students know Smith only as the invisible-hand guy and think sympathy means feeling sorry for someone; the philosophy students will already be using the phrase impartial spectator but I doubt they can say what work its doing. Tell me the order the ideas have to go in: what has to be in place before the impartial spectator even becomes intelligible to them, and what depends on what. Give me the one example or case that should carry the whole session, what it has to be able to do, and what it must not distract them with. The main thing I want is the misconception map. I'm expecting three: "sympathy is feeling bad for someone," "the spectator is just the average of actual onlookers or what society will approve of," and "oh, so Smith thinks we're deep down altruistic." For each, tell me exactly where in the sequence to head it off, not just that I should head it off. I also need to know where the gap between being praised and being praiseworthy gets introduced relative to the spectator: can they get the spectator before they feel that gap, or does the gap have to come first? End with one check-for-understanding question that would show me they've got the spectator as a constructed, correcting standpoint rather than a real observer or a conscience. And tell me what to deliberately leave out at this level — I'm already inclined to skip the religious framing and the figurative inner-voice stuff unless skipping it badly distorts the view, in which case tell me exactly what it costs.
