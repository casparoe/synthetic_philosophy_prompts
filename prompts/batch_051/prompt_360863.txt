Hey, I write about science and ideas, and I've been down a rabbit hole on a decision-theory thought experiment called counterfactual mugging. Let me make sure the mechanics are right, because it matters for the question. A super-accurate predictor flips a fair coin. Heads, the predictor shows up and asks you for $100. Tails, the predictor gives you $10,000 — but only if you would have handed over the $100 on heads. So when you get the request, you know your on the heads branch: the ten grand is on a branch that now isnt going to happen, and nothing you do anymore can cause it to have happened. Causal decision theory says refusing is just common sense; the updateless / logical decision theory crowd says pay anyway, because the policy pay-on-heads is what would have earned the $10,000 across the coin, ex ante. There's a cousin puzzle, XOR blackmail, where the blackmailer only sends the demand to agents predicted to pay, so the arrival of the demand is already evidence about what kind of agent you are.

The claim people build on top of this is that paying is the uniquely rational move — not just allowed, not just defensible, but required, so that refusing is a defect like weakness of will, not a rival theory of rationality. As a journalist this makes me want to know whether the claim has any empirical teeth or whether the word should shields it from every possible observation.

You're an expert in decision theory, experimental economics, and the philosophy of rationality. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming two or three possible designs before settling on one. Then give me the strongest concrete experiment you can — real participants, real money, real predictions, something that could actually be run — that separates the hypothesis that people are applying an across-branch, updateless rule from the hypothesis that they refuse because the $100 bill is vivid and the counterfactual $10,000 is just a story, or because they hold a coherent non-updateless theory of rationality rather than falling short of their own. Include at least a rough way to distinguish endorsing the policy ex ante from actually complying once the branch is known, since I suspect a lot of the fight hides in that gap. Say exactly what result would count for the claim and what result against it. Then be honest about the residue: if the result came out cleanly in favor, what precisely would it show, and what would it still not settle — especially the part about what an ideal rational agent should do, as opposed to what humans do or endorse.
