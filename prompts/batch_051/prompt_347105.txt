Consider an intervention that reverses the usual observability of private information in sequential choice. In the standard picture behind rational herding, each agent chooses after observing what earlier agents did, while their private signals stay hidden; cascades arise because choices convey only coarse information about those signals. Suppose that, starting next year, the dominant recommendation platform in a large advanced economy changes its interface everywhere at once: every user must record a private assessment of an item (a 0–10 score plus a stated confidence) before seeing any social information. After recording, the user sees the full sequence of previous users' timestamped assessments in the order they were made, but is never shown previous users' final choices, purchases, or aggregate ratings. The platform can verify when an assessment was recorded but cannot verify its sincerity. The change applies uniformly to restaurants, films, consumer products, and news sources; nothing else about the platform or its users changes.

Give a reasoned prediction of how attention and consumption in those four categories look ten years later. Do not write a story; give a chain of reasoning about what agents can infer, what incentives they face, and how behavior adapts. Address, at minimum: whether popularity tracks quality more or less closely than today in each category; whether herding disappears or simply relocates, for example into herding on stated confidence or on strategically fake signals; how users and sellers try to manipulate the system and how quickly; and which categories change most. For each major step, indicate how confident you are and what assumption, if it failed, would reverse that prediction. Conclude with a verdict on whether the intervention eliminates information cascades or merely changes their shape.
