Two members of my reading group recently argued about a scenario in which a virus makes every living human infertile, so that no more children will be born and the species dies out of old age in roughly a century. No one is killed or made ill, and no one's lifespan is shortened; the only immediate effects are the distress people feel at the news and the disappointment of those who had wanted children.

Elinor said the scenario is nevertheless bad in a further way. Much of what gives our present activities their point—cancer research, building institutions, preserving a language, composing music—derives that point from an assumed human future. If a person knew for certain that humanity would end soon after their own death, their present work on a long-term project would lose its point now, not later; this shows that a continuing human world is a background condition for value we already have, not merely a source of future beneficiaries.

Marcus replied that this confuses being distressed or disappointed with having been made worse off. If no existing person is wronged and no future person exists to be deprived, it is hard to see how the extinction is bad beyond the felt distress, which is only a reaction to losing a deeply entrenched assumption.

Elinor answered that not all badness need attach to harm to an identifiable person: a world in which a symphony is left unfinished, or in which the last speaker of a language dies with no one left to speak it, seems worse than one in which these are completed and preserved, quite apart from whether anyone ever enjoys them.

Marcus said that would be an aesthetic loss rather than a moral one, and that it locates the badness in absent future goods rather than in anything bad now.

Elinor responded that he was assuming that experienced welfare is the only currency of value, which is exactly the point at issue; the real fault line, she said, lies between person-affecting and impersonal conceptions of value.

They asked me to adjudicate, and I could not. Please go through the exchange and identify who is right about which specific point, where the argument is substantive rather than merely verbal, and who is more right overall, if either. In doing so, separate what is fairly settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
