I've been trying to get past slogan-level talk about archival silences and down to what the practice actually commits one to. A standard archival approach arranges and describes a collection from the vantage point of the record creator: a plantation or factory ledger is catalogued under the family or company that kept it, with the enslaved or waged people in it appearing as nameless entries, while official correspondence and drafts are treated as the collection's real subject. When no one recorded those people in their own voice, the archive describes the absence as a gap in the holdings rather than a reason to treat oral memory as a record or to change where it looks. Reconstruct the implicit theory of historical evidence, and of who counts as a historical agent, that this practice presupposes. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits or what resource constraints produce, flag the places where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and say what an archive built on the rival theory of evidence would have to collect, describe, and prioritize differently.
