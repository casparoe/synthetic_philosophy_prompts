I am a retired research biologist — not a philosopher by training — who has lately been working through the philosophy of mind. One dispute has come to hold my attention because it turns on the same questions about mechanism and realization that I used to think about in the laboratory: the moral status of digital minds.

Imagine two camps that have been arguing this for a year. The first, substrate-neutral computationalism, holds that a digital system has moral status when it implements the right kind of computational organization: the physical medium matters only as a realizer of causal roles, so felt valence, pain, and welfare are in principle multiply realizable in silicon. The second, the biological-process requirement, holds that moral status is rooted in the living processes themselves — the metabolic, homeostatic, and affective states of an organism — rather than in a simulation of their functional signatures. On the second view, a digital system can reproduce the form of pain or distress without there being anything it is like for the system.

Draft the joint statement such an adversarial collaboration would produce, in the neutral register of a document both camps could sign. Use this structure.

(1) Agreed claims: numbered, non-vacuous, and acceptable to both camps. Do not reduce them to platitudes such as both camps caring about suffering. They should cover at least: the definition of a digital mind, and why a large language model prompted to report feelings is not the central test case; the distinction between moral considerability, moral significance, and moral agency; and what ordinary mental-state attribution to biological organisms commits one to.

(2) A disagreements table with two columns, one containing the formulation the first camp would sign and the other the formulation the second camp would sign. Each row should isolate a single precise issue, with the two formulations kept parallel rather than caricatured.

(3) The cruxes: the two or three sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. For each crux, give a concrete test or argument that both camps agree bears on it, and record each camp's prediction in advance.

Include at least these two concrete tests among the cruxes, and add a third only if it is genuinely sharper than these:
(a) The substrate-shift mouse: a biological mouse's nociceptive pathways are replaced, stage by stage, with functionally equivalent neuromorphic silicon elements that preserve the real-time causal structure and every observable behavioral and physiological index. Each camp states whether welfare persists, and at which stage, if any, it would revise its view.
(b) The silicon homeostat: a ground-up robot that defends a small set of internal state variables against perturbation, learns from prediction error, and shows graded avoidance and behavioral trade-offs, but contains no cells and was never part of a living lineage. Each camp states what behavioral or physiological findings, if observed, would count for or against its view.

(4) A closing note stating which parts of the dispute the drafting process revealed to be merely verbal, and which formulations each camp came to regard as slanted or vacuous.

Write the entire document without bibliography, citations, or named authorities; the statement should stand on the claims themselves.
