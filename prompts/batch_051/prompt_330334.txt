At a dinner party, two colleagues of mine went deeper than usual into a criminal case in the news: a man convicted of a premeditated, unprovoked assault. Both wanted him imprisoned; they disagreed about why, and about whether the idea of desert should appear anywhere in the justification.

Marta argued that no one is ultimately responsible for what they do: genes, upbringing, and brain processes none of us chose produce our actions, so retributive desert is an illusion. The only defensible basis for imprisonment, she claimed, is a quarantine logic — we remove the dangerous person to protect others, as we would isolate the carrier of an infectious disease, and nothing about his suffering is made intrinsically right by what he did.

Jon replied that this depends on a maximally demanding picture of free will. Responsibility does not require being an uncaused cause; it requires ordinary competence — the capacity to deliberate, exercise self-control, and adjust to reasons. That competence is real, comes in degrees, and is exactly what desert tracks. He added that the quarantine picture is both too thin and too callous: it treats a responsible wrongdoer as a mere vector of danger, and it has no obvious barrier to preventively detaining people who are dangerous but have done nothing culpable.

Marta answered that "competence" is doing all the moral work while being only a psychological property: it can ground forward-looking practices of holding people responsible, but not basic desert. Proportionality, she said, can instead be grounded in risk and in a least-restrictive-means principle, with the conviction serving as evidence of dangerousness and as a due-process constraint — not as a license to make someone suffer for its own sake.

Jon countered that her model quietly borrows what it claims to have rejected. The reason we treat a culpable attacker differently from an innocent carrier of equal dangerousness is a desert-like judgment, and without some such judgment she could not object in principle to pure preventive detention. The constraints she cites, he said, are fragments of the very responsibility practices she says she has abandoned.

I was asked to referee and could not. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the four substantive moves (Marta's opening, Jon's reply, Marta's answer, Jon's final objection) from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is right about which sub-question — what free will must be for desert to apply, whether compatibilist competence can support basic desert or only instrumental holding-responsible, and whether proportionality can be rebuilt without desert — point out anything important both are missing, and say who is more right overall.
