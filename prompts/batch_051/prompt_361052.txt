I run a small reading group for graduate students on the philosophy of doing good, and last week one member circulated a numbered argument he says shows that the standard effective-altruist practice of comparing interventions by expected value per marginal dollar is either self-defeating or forces us to admit that systemic change can never be justified on cost-effectiveness grounds. The group split, and rather than let us talk past each other, I would like the argument diagnosed by someone who can price the different escapes.

Here is the argument, cleaned up. (1) If an intervention is better than another, that is because it produces more expected value per marginal dollar. (2) Expected value per marginal dollar is measured by the counterfactual difference the marginal dollar makes, compared with the world in which it is not given. (3) For direct interventions, such as distributing bednets, unconditional cash transfers, or deworming, the marginal dollar makes a concrete, legible difference: it buys roughly one more unit of a well-evidenced good. (4) For systemic interventions, such as passing a law, changing an institution, or shifting a norm through organizing or litigation, the marginal dollar makes no identifiable difference: the campaign either has enough money to win or it does not, and no individual donor can point to the bit of the world her dollar changed. (5) Therefore, by (1) and (2), direct interventions nearly always beat systemic interventions on cost-effectiveness, and a rigor-minded donor should prefer the direct ones. (6) But if every donor followed (5), the movements, litigation, policy research, and organizing that generate large-scale change would be systematically underfunded, since on this view they could only be funded by donors willing to set cost-effectiveness aside. (7) A principle for doing good that would predictably leave the highest-leverage changes unfunded if everyone followed it is a bad principle. (8) Therefore something in (1)–(5) must be rejected.

I would most like to keep (1) and (7). I am less attached to (3), (4), and (5), but I cannot tell whether the right move is to reject one of them, to reject the inference from (5) to (6), or to give up on (1) itself, and I want each candidate rejection priced rather than bundled. Please act as a professional philosopher of ethics and formal decision theory. Take your time: first lay out the candidate diagnoses, then commit to which step should be rejected and explain why that step looked fine, and then explain what each of the main alternatives costs, including what a corrected decision procedure for doing good would look like on each diagnosis. Lead with your diagnosis in one or two sentences, and keep the whole response under 900 words.
