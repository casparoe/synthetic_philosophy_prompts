Two friends of mine, Sam and Priya, got into it in my epistemology reading group last month, and they've both been asking me who won. I genuinly can't say. The argument started over Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (the 1895 Mind dialogue), specifically the part after the Tortoise has accepted A, B, and C but still won't accept Z. Sam read this bit aloud:

"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"
"You might," the candid hero admitted; "though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical."

Then it went roughly like this.

(1) Sam said that's the whole regress problem in miniature: if every reason has to be backed by another reason, the chain never ends and you never actually get to the conclusion. So for any belief to be justified, some beliefs must be justified without getting their justification from further beliefs. That's just foundationalism.

(2) Priya said he's reading it as a story about beliefs justifying beliefs, but it's not. The Tortoise accepts all the premises fine; what he refuses is the rule of inference, the move from A and B to Z. So the point is that a rule isn't another premise — it's a different kind of thing. It doesn't prove we need basic beliefs; it just shows justification was never a matter of piling up propositions.

(3) Sam replied that "different kind of thing" only moves the problem up one level. The Tortoise can ask why we use that rule, or why that standard of justification is any good, and any answer you give is another premise needing another rule. So you still need something that isn't itself up for justification — maybe not a basic belief, but a basic commitment, a way of seeing. The foundationalist point survives.

(4) Priya said that's a confession, not a solution: if the foundation isn't up for justification, then it isn't justified, and if you try to justify it (it's self-evident, it's formed reliably), you've written down one more premise and the Tortoise is off again. She said the Tortoise is actually on the infinitist's side — there will always be one more hypothetical, and being justified is being able to keep taking the next step when a challenge comes, not reaching a last step.

(5) Sam said that can't be right, because the dialogue's whole joke is that the Tortoise takes every single step and still never grants Z. Endless stepping is the failure of justification, not what justification is. The only way Achilles ever gets anywhere is by eventually saying: accept the rule or stop reasoning. That's what a foundation does, and it isn't giving another premise — it's the condition for premises to matter.

(6) Priya said "the condition for premises to matter" sounds an awful lot like her view relocated: the rule is fine, the premises and the rule and the act of inferring all work together, and no single item is holding up the rest — which, she said, is coherentist if anything, not foundationalist. Sam said that's just cheating by spreading the foundation across the whole network when a single justifier was asked for.

At that point somebody said Priya was doing coherentism in (2) and (6) but infinitism in (4), and she said fine, maybe the anti-foundationalist line gets to use both. Anyway. Please adjudicate the whole exchange move by move: which specific claims are right or wrong, what Carroll actually does and doesn't show for the regress problem, where coherentism and infinitism each fit, and who is more right overall.
