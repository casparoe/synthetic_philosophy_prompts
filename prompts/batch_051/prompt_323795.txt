I help run a science fiction reading group, and we are about to spend several sessions on Isaac Asimov's original Foundation stories — Foundation, Foundation and Empire, and Second Foundation, not the later prequels and sequels. I recently read Karl Popper's The Poverty of Historicism on my own, and I can't stop thinking about the two together. Popper's central argument, as I understand it, is that there cannot be laws of history: the future course of history is shaped by what people will come to know, and we cannot now predict the future growth of our knowledge. Hari Seldon's psychohistory seems to claim exactly what Popper says is impossible — a mathematical science that predicts the broad sweep of galactic history centuries in advance — but it is built with safeguards that look suspiciously like Popper's own objections: the population must be large enough for individual unpredictability to wash out statistically, it must not know the predictions, and even then the Plan needs the Second Foundation to nudge it back on course when the Mule, an unpredictable mutant, breaks it. I'd like a committed reading of the trilogy through Popper's argument. Does Asimov actually give psychohistory the features Popper says a historical science cannot have, or does the plot quietly concede Popper's point through the secrecy of the Plan, the recorded appearances of Seldon at each crisis, and the Second Foundation's corrections? Where does the fiction genuinely put pressure on Popper rather than sidestepping him — for instance by making imperial decline predictable because it is already in progress, or by treating history as statistical mass-behavior rather than a law of inevitable stages? And where does the story reveal the predictions to be not laws at all but engineered, managed outcomes? Please take a definite position rather than laying out the options, and defend it by pointing to specific story beats and choices the characters make — the Encyclopedia as a cover story, the way the crises resolve, the treatment of the Mule. I want a reading that honors both sides: one that doesn't reduce the trilogy to an illustration of Popper, and one that doesn't soften Popper's argument to fit the stories. What I most want to know is what the fiction adds to the philosophical debate that a plain statement of the argument cannot show.
