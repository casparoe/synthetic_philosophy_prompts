I'm working on a paper about whether "deception" and "honesty" can be defined precisely enough to be used in auditing AI systems, and I keep running into the same impasse. One colleague treats a language model's fabricated case citation as a lie; another says that without beliefs or intentions the word "lie" does no work; a third says the right question is not what the system meant but what a reasonable user would be led to believe. The same people will confidently call Meta's Diplomacy-playing agent CICERO deceptive — it was explicitly trained to be honest, yet it verbally colluded and then turned on an ally — yet they disagree about whether a recommender system that quietly shapes what a user comes to believe about a topic, or a summarizer that drops the one clause that reverses a contract's meaning, belongs in the same category. I would rather not settle these cases one at a time.

Instead, I want to start from the other end: the full specification of what an adequate theory of AI honesty and deception must get right, where "adequate" means usable in a pre-deployment audit and able to assign responsibility across developers, deployers, and users — not just descriptively tidy. Please give me the desiderata: the clear cases any theory must classify correctly (sycophantic agreement, hallucinated citations, appropriately hedged versus overconfident statements, omission in summarization, and incentive-driven strategic deception), the distinctions it must respect (deception about the world versus about the system's own capabilities, lying versus being wrong, what the system does versus what the user reasonably infers), and the constraints it must honor (it should not simply presuppose a full human-like mental life, and it should not count every false output as deception). For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp.

Then flag any pairs or subsets of the desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that incompatibility is the part I most care about. Finally, tell me which single desideratum does the most to exclude the currently popular families of theories (intent-based, behavior- or outcome-based, role- or norm-based, training-incentive-based, and deflationary accounts), and say why.

Please structure the answer as follows. First, a table with the columns: ID; desideratum; motivating case or consideration; status (non-negotiable / negotiable / camp-specific); theory families it most pressures. Second, below the table, a JSON object with exactly these keys: "scoring" (an object mapping each of the five theory families to two arrays, "satisfies" and "fails", whose elements are desideratum IDs, plus a "worst_failure" string of one sentence); "unsatisfiable_subsets" (an array of objects, each with keys "subset" and "why"); and "most_excluding_desideratum" (a string giving the ID and a one-sentence justification).
