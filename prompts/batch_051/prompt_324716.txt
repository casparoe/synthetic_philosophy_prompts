I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about whether a psychopath knows that what he does is wrong. She kept insisting on a difference between knowing that other people call something wrong and knowing that it is wrong yourself, but she would not state the difference as a theory; she was only comfortable ruling on particular cases, so I wrote down the verdicts she did commit to.

She said a man with the standard psychopathic profile — he can name which acts are considered wrong, predicts exactly how others will judge him, sees no real difference between wrong and against the rules, and reports no guilt, no empathy, no unease — does not know that what he does is wrong; he only knows how his actions are labeled. She said a woman I will call Mary, who reads other people's emotions only with effort and describes her own morality as a set of explicit rules she reasoned her way to, but who treats those rules as more serious than mere convention, as not up for amendment by anyone's permission, and as reasons that regularly govern her behavior, does know that it is wrong when she violates them. She said a young child who can say that hitting is wrong and even identify when another child has done wrong, but whose own conduct is barely constrained by the rule, who explains the wrongness only by saying that the adults say so and would be angry, and whose distress afterward is fear of punishment rather than remorse, does not know that hitting is wrong — not yet. She said a man who, after a head injury, could still answer moral questions correctly yet stopped making choices that reflected those answers, and who when asked why it would be wrong to strike someone for fun could say only that it was against the rules, much as he would explain a parking restriction, does not know it either. And she said a man who has learned his community's moral code only as a set of threats — he can state the rules and complies when he expects to be caught, but gives no sign of seeing anything wrong himself — does not know that the things he refrains from are wrong.

Now take a man I will call Suleiman, raised in a small sect. He is genuinely moved by its code: it has become his self-conception, he feels real guilt at violating it, and he restrains himself even when no one is watching. But he treats every transgression as a single kind — for him, striking a child and arriving late to evening prayers are wrong in exactly the same way, namely because they break a rule laid down by the community's founder. He says, and apparently feels, that if the founder had never laid down the prohibition, striking the child would not have been wrong at all. Would my friend say Suleiman knows that striking a child is wrong? I am not mainly after the yes or no; I want the rule she seems to be running, the other rule or rules that fit her earlier verdicts just as well, what each of them predicts about Suleiman, which prediction you would bet on and why, and the further case that would best distinguish the competing rules from one another. Please answer in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no separate introduction or conclusion.
