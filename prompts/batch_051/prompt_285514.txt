Take the removal of four Confederate monuments by the city of New Orleans in 2017—the statues of Robert E. Lee, Jefferson Davis, and P. G. T. Beauregard, and the Battle of Liberty Place obelisk—and analyze it as a case for Vergangenheitsbewältigung, understood as the practice by which a society works through a burdensome past rather than mastering or forgetting it, so that present civic life rests on acknowledged responsibility. You may assume the following salient facts: the monuments were erected decades after the Civil War, largely during the Jim Crow era; the city council voted for their removal in 2015; after years of litigation they were taken down in 2017; and Mayor Mitch Landrieu defended the removal in a widely reported speech, arguing that the monuments presented a false, sanitized history. Analyze the case in both directions. First, state what the working-through model would have said the city faced: who owed what to whom, whether removal alone constitutes working through or merely erasure, and what acknowledgment beyond removal the model would require. Second, identify where the episode fits the model and where it strains it—for instance, these were public honors for the Confederacy rather than markers of contrition or mourning of the kind central to postwar German memory practice, and the decision came from a local government rather than the nation as a whole. Third, say what the episode shows about the model itself: does it confirm it, does it show that the model is too bound to the German case to transfer, or does it suggest that working through can require removal rather than preservation? Be disciplined about who is the player, what the options are, and what counts as evidence, and say plainly where the concept is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
