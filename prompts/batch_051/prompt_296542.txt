I am a high school student, and I have been reading about choice under bounded rationality for an independent study. I have ended up defending a specific view, and I want someone to show me exactly where it is vulnerable. The view is this: satisficing—choosing the first option that meets or exceeds an aspiration level rather than trying to identify the best available option—is not merely a concession to costly computation, limited time, and limited information. It can be fully rational even for an agent with unlimited time and unlimited information. My reasons are, first, that the phrase "all available options" has no non-arbitrary endpoint once an agent can generate options instead of merely surveying a pre-existing menu; second, that an agent's preferences are often constructed during search rather than fixed in advance, so optimization may be optimizing over values that did not exist before the search; and third, that relentless optimization can undermine the very ends it is meant to serve.

Now I want to know what would count against this view. I am not interested in the easy objection that real agents are computationally limited, which my view already concedes; I want the objections that survive even granting my idealizations. Which observations about how actual agents set, adapt, and sometimes abandon their aspiration levels would count against me, and which would merely show that satisficing is difficult to do well? Which formal results about choice, dominance, or transitivity would force me to give the view up, and which would a committed satisficer accept without embarrassment? Which intuitions or arguments about the difference between "good enough" and "best" should I treat as decisive, and which are question-begging against satisficing from the start? Please sort these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say what my position predicts about real choice behavior that a maximizing account does not. I would personally find most persuasive a demonstration that satisficing systematically selects options the agent would reject if the full option set and the agent's settled preferences were somehow made available; but I suspect a committed defender could always blame a badly chosen aspiration level rather than satisficing itself. Tell me honestly whether the view has been insulated by that kind of reply, and whether such insulation is a problem for a theory of rational choice or exactly what we should expect from one.
