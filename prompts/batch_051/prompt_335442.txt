For the better part of a year, the political philosophy discussion group I run has been stuck on the dispute between Rawlsians and Harsanyi-style expected-utility theorists about how to reason behind the veil of ignorance, and I have come to think that the impasse itself—rather than either side's first-order view—is what needs explaining.

The Rawlsian side holds that the parties in the original position face uncertainty without probabilities: they know the general facts about society but, because the veil blocks everything particular about themselves, they have no basis for assigning likelihoods to their place in it. Under that kind of uncertainty, the rational choice rule is maximin—select the arrangement whose worst representative position is best—especially because the choice is final, the stakes are a whole life, and the worst outcomes are unacceptable. That, on this side, yields the priority of liberty and the difference principle.

The Harsanyi camp reconstructs the choice differently. It treats ignorance of one's own position as a limiting case of ordinary uncertainty, so the standard apparatus still applies: a rational chooser must have coherent probabilities, and impartiality plus symmetry forces equal subjective probabilities over positions. Maximizing expected utility under those equiprobabilities yields average utilitarianism. The two camps therefore face each other off: maximin versus expected utility, the difference principle versus average utility, no probabilities versus equal probabilities.

They have been trading the same charges for decades. The Harsanyi side says Rawls covertly imports an extreme and unexplained aversion to risk into the veil, and that refusing to assign probabilities is either irrational or empty. The Rawls side says Harsanyi is assuming away precisely the form of ignorance the veil is meant to model, and that converting radical uncertainty into a lottery over positions changes the question from fairness under ignorance to a stochastic welfare calculation. Each side holds that the other's central move simply begs the question.

I want a diagnosis of why this has stalled. Is it a substantive disagreement about rational choice under unquantified uncertainty? A verbal disagreement about what "rational" or "impartial" means here? Are they answering different questions under one heading—Rawls asking how to design a basic structure each citizen could live with across a lifetime, Harsanyi asking what an impartial evaluator of end-states should prefer? Or does the disagreement bottom out in different fixed intuitions, with the Rawlsian taking worst-case protection as the test case and the Harsanyian taking average-case improvement as the test case?

One detail makes me suspect the debate is misframed. Decision theory under ignorance contains a third prominent rule—minimax regret—that also needs no probabilities but would push toward neither maximin nor expected utility. Neither camp engages it seriously, which suggests the debate may not really be about which no-probability decision rule is correct. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions under a single label, but I am not confident: both sides insist they are addressing exactly the same problem, and I want that hypothesis tested rather than assumed.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, precisely, and given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected?
