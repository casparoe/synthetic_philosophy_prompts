I am a biostatistician at a public-health agency, and I have been pulled into a dispute that will shape our internal guidance on how study results may be summarized for a non-specialist governing board. The trigger was a dashboard about a treatment's effect: everything it said was literally true, but it omitted the elevated dropout rate, and several board members walked away with a more favorable view of the treatment than the underlying data support. In the argument that followed, a senior colleague insisted that this is why the distinction between lying and misleading collapses under pressure, and she closed with the proverb that a half-truth is a whole lie. I need to know exactly what that sentence commits her to, because our new policy will be written around whichever of its possible claims turns out to be defensible.

Take "a half-truth is a whole lie" and separate the distinct precise claims it could be used to make about the relation between misleading and lying. For each candidate claim, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false; say which notion of lying it presupposes (for example, must the speaker assert a falsehood? must the speaker intend to deceive?); and say what further moral or empirical premise that reading needs in order to be true. Then return to my colleague's argument. She did not allege that the dashboard team intended to deceive anyone, only that their summary would predictably produce a false belief in its audience. Which candidate claims survive that concession, and which ones collapse once intent and falsity are set aside? I want a menu of readings with verdicts rather than a single interpretation, because the policy language will be anchored to whichever reading actually does the work her conclusion needs.
