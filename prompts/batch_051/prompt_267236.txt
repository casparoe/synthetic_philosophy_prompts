I've gotten stuck trying to write a syllabus for a one-off seminar on simplicity in ethical theory, and I'd like something to give the students as orientation. I want a list of desiderata that any adequate account of simplicity/parsimony as a theoretical virtue in normative ethics has to meet. I keep thinking about Sidgwick's preface to The Methods of Ethics, where he writes:

"I have thought that the predominance in the minds of moralists of a desire to edify has impeded the real progress of ethical science: and that this would be benefited by an application to it of the same disinterested curiosity to which we chiefly owe the great discoveries of physics."

That's him explicitly borrowing the scientific ethos. But in physics simplicity gets to play a strong role because the data are cleaner and we have independent reason to think the world is governed by few laws. In ethics, though, the data are often our intuitions about cases, and those are messy and heavily contested. So I don't want a handout that just sides with the utilitarians or the intuitionists. I want the requirements themselves: what does a view have to do to earn the right to appeal to simplicity when it's deciding between moral theories? Specifically, what must it say about the difference between reducing the number of principles, reducing the number of fundamental value-bearers, and reducing the number of unexplained exception-clauses? Which conflict cases between a simple principle and a scattered pattern of intuitions must it resolve, and which is it allowed to leave as a wash? Which requirements are non-negotiable, and which are just the aesthetic taste of one camp? And if some subset of the desiderata can't be jointly satisfied, flag that—that's the part I'd actually want to teach around. Don't bother with a scorecard of theories; I'll have them do that once they've internalized the criteria.
