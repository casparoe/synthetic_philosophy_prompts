There is an old line of thought that deliberately aiming at happiness makes a person less happy. The thought seems to have two parts. The first is that happiness is a by-product: it comes from being absorbed in something else—a skill, a person, a task—done for its own sake, and making happiness the goal empties the activity of the very thing that generates the happiness. The second is that the act of stopping to monitor how happy you are pulls you out of the experience and thins it out. I have been trying to figure out how much of this old puzzle survives a change that wasn't around when people first noticed it: the happiness can now be aimed at through real-time instrumentation. Imagine a watch that tracks its wearer's happiness on its own—body signals, movement, tone of voice, perhaps a once-a-day one-tap rating—and predicts which next action will most raise the wearer's happiness score over the day. When such a prediction crosses a threshold, the watch simply nudges: sit outside instead of scrolling, call a particular friend, start the task being avoided. The wearer's only policy is to follow the nudges; assume they never inspect the score. Grant, for the sake of the thought problem, that the score really does track happiness and that the nudges do raise happiness in the short run. Does the old argument still apply to the person who follows the watch? If happiness is being deliberately aimed at only in this delegated, behind-the-scenes way, is there still a reason to expect them to end up less happy than someone who forgets all scores and simply gets absorbed in things? If yes, tell me which part of the old argument still has force and whether it has to be stated differently. If no, tell me exactly which premise has broken and whether the old argument can be repaired into something weaker but still true. I would also like the answer to include a note on what would change your mind—what finding or consideration, if established, would flip your verdict—and where you think your reasoning is most likely to give way first.
