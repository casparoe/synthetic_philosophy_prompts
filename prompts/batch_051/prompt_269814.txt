I don't have formal philosophical training, but I've been working through Spinoza's Ethics in translation on my own. In Part III of the Ethics, he defines emotion as follows:

"By emotion I mean the modifications of the body, whereby the active power of the said body is increased or diminished, aided or constrained, and also the ideas of such modifications."

I want to use this definition to think through whether hope, and optimism more generally, are best understood as moral virtues or as epistemic vices. I've arranged my reasoning into numbered steps, but I suspect I may be sliding between different senses of "passive," and I can't tell whether the argument is sound or where exactly it goes wrong. Here is the argument:

(1) Hope is an emotion.

(2) Every emotion is a modification of the body whereby the body's active power is increased or diminished, aided or constrained, together with the idea of that modification.

(3) In hope, the body's power is increased only through the idea of a future good whose occurrence is uncertain and not in our control.

(4) An increase in power that depends on an uncertain outcome is inconstant, because it can be reversed by the idea of that outcome's failing to occur.

(5) Whatever renders the mind's power inconstant makes the mind passive rather than active.

(6) Virtue is a way of acting, not a way of being acted upon.

(7) Therefore hope is not a virtue.

I find the conclusion too sweeping, but I cannot pinpoint which step is the faulty one. Is the argument valid as stated? If it is not, which step fails, and what is the strongest reading of that step that would make the argument go through? If it is valid, does it show only that hope falls short of Spinoza's unusually demanding conception of virtue, or does it show that hope is never a moral virtue at all? Be specific about the step and the reading of it.
