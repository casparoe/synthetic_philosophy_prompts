Okay so I've been going down a rabbit hole on acausal threats and surrogate goals — mostly from AI safety blogs and forum threads, not academic stuff — and I've reconstructed an argument that now feels like it proves way to much. I'd like you to act as an expert decision theorist / philosopher and tell me where it breaks. Please think carefully before answering, and maybe brainstorm a few candidate diagnoses before settling on the one you'd actually defend.

Here's my understanding of the setup, in my own words. An acausal threat is one where the threatener can predict you well enough that the punishment is conditional on its *prediction* of your action, not on your action itself. Gareth says: I'll hurt you unless my prediction says you'll pay me. Once the prediction is fixed, paying doesn't causally change whether you get hurt — it's just evidence about which way the prediction already went. Fine.

So here's the argument:

1. If you're the type who pays acausal threats, any good predictor can milk you forever: it predicts you'll pay, so it never actually has to punish, and you keep paying. Paying is a terrible policy.

2. So the obvious fix is to be the type who doesn't pay. If you're reliably a refuser, then a rational predictor won't even threaten you, because it would just end up punishing you and getting nothing.

3. But refusing has to be more than a tactic. If you refuse only because you calculated that refusing usually makes threats go away, then a sophisticated predictor can model that calculation. It can see where your tactic would cave — for instance, if the demanded amount is small enough, or the threatened harm is large enough, or the threat is framed to fall outside what your tactic covers — and it can aim its threat exactly there. So tactical refusal doesn't really protect you.

4. What actually protects you, the blogs seem to say, is a *surrogate goal*: you have to genuinely want to refuse acausal threats for their own sake, not because refusing pays. You have to self-modify into caring about refusal itself so that you won't cave even when caving would be locally rational. But that means rewriting your own values to survive blackmail, which already sounds insane.

5. And even that doesn't escape. Suppose you do genuinely refuse. A predictor that knows this can simply not bother extracting payment and instead punish you anyway (it predicted you'd refuse, so from it's point of view the punishment is necessary to keep its threats credible). Or it can threaten something your surrogate goal doesn't cover — some other action entirely — and now your refusal-goal is silent. So you'd need another surrogate goal for that, and then another, with no natural stopping point.

6. So whatever policy you have — paying, tactical refusal, genuine refusal — a good predictor can find a threat that makes that policy lose. Since you can't choose to be unpredictable (a predictor with your source code can model you anyway), every readable agent is helpless against acausal blackmail. The only winning move is to have no readable decision procedure, which isn't a move you can make.

7. That conclusion is absurd. It would mean the best predictor dominates everyone regardless of actual power, and all rational agents should burn their resources trying to become unreadable, which is impossible. So the notion of an acausal threat must be incoherent somewhere.

I want to hold onto 1 and 2 pretty hard — paying really does look like a mug's game, and refusing-as-character really does look like it screens off threats. So I suspect the rot is in 3 or 5, but I can't tell whether that's a real flaw or just me wanting the absurd conclusion to be false. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? And if it doesn't, which premise should I give up, and what does that cost?
