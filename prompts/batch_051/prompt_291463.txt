I recently retired from a career in physical chemistry and, having spent forty years expecting every process to have a mechanism, I have been unsettled by the philosophical literature on self-deception, which seems to describe a process that cannot have one. Deceiving another person requires the deceiver to know the truth and the deceived person to be kept from it; self-deception appears to demand that I occupy both roles at once, simultaneously knowing that a proposition is false and engineering my own belief that it is true. If I avoid the contradiction by dividing the mind into a knowing part and a believing part, the phenomenon begins to look less like self-deception than like one internal subsystem running a disinformation campaign against another, and it becomes unclear why the resulting state should count as one person believing anything at all.

I would like you to write a pantoum, four quatrains, in the voice of someone in the middle of an episode of self-deception—perhaps a person who has strong evidence for an unwelcome conclusion and is trying, with only partial success, to keep that conclusion from becoming fully accepted. Use the usual pantoum recurrence, in which the second and fourth lines of each quatrain return as the first and third lines of the next, and the final quatrain draws the poem’s opening lines back into its close. The form should not be decorative. I want the returning lines to do the philosophical work: each time a line recurs in its new position, it should enact the way the disavowed truth keeps being smuggled back into the speaker’s attempt to hold the preferred belief, or the way the preferred belief is shown up, by its own insistence, as a cover for what it denies.

The poem should leave the paradox open rather than resolve it—no tidy conversion scene in which the speaker simply admits the truth, and no sermon about self-honesty. A reader familiar with the problem should recognize both of its standard strands: the synchronic puzzle of how one mind can at once know and not know, and the diachronic puzzle of how a plan to induce a belief could succeed while the planner remains aware of what the plan is up to. A reader unfamiliar with the literature should still find it a precise and intelligible portrait of a mind caught in a loop of its own making.
