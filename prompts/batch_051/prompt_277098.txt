I'm preparing for a political philosophy seminar on Rousseau, and in a previous discussion someone defended a coercive public health measure by appealing to Rousseau's idea that people who refuse to obey the general will can legitimately be forced to be free. The slogan was doing a lot of work, and most people treated it as obviously decisive. I suspect it contains several different claims that need to be separated before it can justify anything.

Please give me a section called Readings of about 500 words that lays out at least four distinct precisifications of what it could mean to say that someone can be forced to be free. Distinguish at least these possibilities: compelling outward compliance with a law, correcting a person's mistaken judgment about their own good, transforming their desires so that they come to identify with the general will, and compelling them to obey a law they themselves have willed as a citizen even though they now resist it as a private individual.

Then give a section called Assessment of about 300 words that says for each reading whether it is platitudinous, substantive but defensible, or false or authoritarian, and why.

Finally, give a section called Which reading the public health argument needs of about 200 words explaining which precise interpretation would actually support the use of the slogan in that debate, and whether the argument still succeeds once that reading is made explicit.
