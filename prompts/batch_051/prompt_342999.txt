Last month, at a literary festival where I had been speaking about my novels, I ended up at a late dinner seated between two people who spent the entire evening arguing about a proposal I had not encountered before. The proposal (they referred to it as "debate," and described it as one strategy for keeping advanced AI systems honest) works like this. For any question a human judge cannot independently verify — whether a complicated piece of code contains a subtle security flaw, whether a long proof is sound — one trains two copies of the system to argue opposite sides before the human and rewards whichever side persuades the judge. The hope is that even a judge too weak to solve the problem directly can reliably pick the truthful side, because a true claim can be defended honestly under questioning while a false claim tends to fall apart. My two neighbors disagreed about whether something they called the obfuscated argument destroys this hope. They used the phrase for a persuasive case in favor of a false conclusion, built to be so long, technical, or artfully arranged that the judge cannot find the specific error and may nevertheless be swayed by the display of confidence and precision.

Ravi said the scheme is broken in principle, because any debater cleverer than the judge can manufacture an obfuscated argument for the false side, and the honest side cannot refute it without locating and exposing the flaw, which is exactly what the judge's weakness prevents. Marta answered that this proves too much, since juries, scientific referees, and investors can all be fooled by sophisticated error, yet those institutions still do real epistemic work; debate, like them, only needs deception to be harder than honesty on average, not impossible. Ravi came back that courts and peer review have external checks — cross-examination, discovery, outside experts, the right to say one does not understand — whereas debate deliberately gives the judge no check beyond the arguments themselves, and a judge cannot tell the difference between finding a claim incomprehensible because it is beyond him and finding it incomprehensible because it is nonsense. Marta replied that debate need not be so defenseless: a debater can identify a single suspicious step and ask for it to be decomposed into smaller claims, so the protocol could reward exposing opacity rather than rewarding fluent confidence. Ravi said decomposition merely moves the problem down a level, because a superhuman obfuscator can make the chain of sub-claims arbitrarily deep, and the truthful side is sometimes the one that cannot offer a satisfyingly simple explanation precisely because the truth is genuinely complicated. Marta conceded that in such cases no scheme that relies on a weak judge can help, but said this is a general limit on using human judges, not a special flaw in debate; the real question, she thought, is whether obfuscation gives falsehood a systematic advantage rather than merely a few contrived cases. Ravi's final word was that the entire appeal of debate was to handle cases where the judge cannot solve the underlying problem, so if the proposal only works when the truth can be made simple enough for the judge, it fails exactly where it was supposed to be useful.

They asked me to referee and I honestly could not.
