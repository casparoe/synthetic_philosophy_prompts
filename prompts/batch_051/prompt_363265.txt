I've been arguing with a friend about whether a trained AI—something with no evolutionary history to speak of—could literally want anything, or whether "want" is just a predictive shorthand we use for things whose behavior we haven't derived from simpler parts. We both kept reaching for some distinction between really wanting and merely as-if wanting, and neither of us could state it. So can you build that distinction from scratch? Give me a working definition of "wants" (or "has a goal," if you think that's the better basic term) that classifies these:

- a person saving for retirement, mostly out of fear of being broke later
- a chess engine that sacrifices its queen because doing so raises the probability of winning
- a thermostat wired into a heating system
- a slime mold growing toward a food source
- a plant turning toward a window
- an LLM doing a single forward pass to predict the next token
- a reinforcement learning agent trained on an Atari game to maximize its score
- a person with a compulsion or addiction who says "I don't want this" while reaching for it
- a paperclip maximizer in the usual thought experiment
- a corporation that reliably acts to maximize shareholder value, even when the humans inside it don't individually care much

I have settled views on maybe four of these and I keep fliping on the rest. My hunch is that the word does two jobs: one about the machinery actually inside the thing, and one about the most useful way to predict it from outside. If you agree, give me the two definitions and say which cases the two definitions split on—the chess engine and the compulsion case feel like the ones where the split matters most. If instead you think there's one definition that does all the work, run it through all ten and tell me which of its verdicts is least secure.
