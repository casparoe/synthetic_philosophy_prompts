I work on a team that maintains an automated underwriting service. We have a written policy — a few hundred pages of rules for deciding whether to approve a loan — and for years we have implemented it as ordinary code, clause by clause; when the policy changes, we patch the code. A vendor now proposes to replace that code with a trained model, which we can query as a black box but cannot inspect. They make a claim that bothers me, though I have trouble saying exactly why: they claim the model implements the policy, rather than merely approximating its verdicts on past applications.

Their evidence is that the model agrees with our old code on every historical case we have and on every synthetic case we have generated from the policy. One of my colleagues says this is empty. For any finite set of cases, there are infinitely many functions that agree on exactly those cases and diverge somewhere else; for any test suite we propose, he can produce a shadow rule that matches the policy on every case in the suite and then approves everything with an application ID above the largest ID the suite happens to contain. No finite test suite can separate the policy from that kind of shadow rule. Another colleague says this is a philosopher's worry with no engineering content: "follows the rule" just means "keeps matching the policy on everything we can think to test," and the vendor's model has done that.

I do not find either answer satisfying. I want to know whether the claim "the system is following the rule" can be brought under test at all, and if so, what the strongest version of that test is. Design it concretely: the inputs, the comparisons, the result that would favor the model's genuinely implementing the policy, and the result that would favor its being only a high-quality approximation. Then be precise about what the test could not settle, even in principle — for instance, whether the model could still be following a shadow rule that diverges on an input we have not tried, or whether "following a rule" outruns any finite behavioral evidence. I am less interested in a verdict than in seeing exactly where the empirical evidence runs out and what, if anything, is left over.
