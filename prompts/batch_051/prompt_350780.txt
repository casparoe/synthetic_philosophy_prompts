After describing the standard examples, the author offers this summary: "The lottery paradox shows that in a sufficiently large fair lottery, you cannot know that your ticket will lose, however high the probability that it will, because there is always an uneliminated chance of winning. This is taken to show that justified true belief is not sufficient for knowledge: you may be justified in believing your ticket will lose, and your belief may be true, but you still do not know it. The preface paradox, by contrast, shows that a careful author can be justified in believing each sentence in her own book while also being justified in believing that at least one sentence in it is false. The usual lesson drawn from the two paradoxes together is that rational belief cannot be governed by probabilistic standards at all: since probabilistic standards would license believing every ticket loses while also believing one will win, the only stable alternative is to require certainty for rational belief."

Could you go through this paragraph sentence by sentence and tell me what is accurate, what is a common simplification or conflation, and what is a distortion?
