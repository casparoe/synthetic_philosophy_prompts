I retired from population genetics a few years back and have since fallen into epistemology and game theory, mostly by arguing with people online. I keep coming back to Aumann's old result that rational Bayesians with a common prior can't agree to disagree. I want a thought experiment built to order that pushes on how that theorem can look like its about to apply and then quietly not apply.

Set it up like this. Alice and Bob are ideal Bayesian reasoners—no computational limits, no motivated reasoning—and they start with the same prior over some finite set of possible states. It's common knowledge that there both rational and that the prior is shared. Each privately observes which cell of their own information partition the true state falls in. They then simultaneously announce, truthfully, a single number: their posterior probability for some fixed event E. One round only. After they hear each other's numbers and update, I want them to still assign different probabilities to E, and I want it to be clear that neither is being irrational.

The culprit has to be common knowledge of the information, not the prior and not the rationality. Specifically, announcing a scalar number shouldn't by itself make either person's posterior common knowledge; that's the gap the example should exploit. Don't use different priors, don't use lying, and don't use bounded rationality as the explanation.

Constraints: keep the state space to no more than six states and the partitions coarse enough to draw on a napkin. No private signals beyond the partition cells. E should be something a normal person could care about—rain on a picnic, a package arriving, that sort of thing. And the disagreement should survive each person's honest update on the number they actually hear, in the sense that each can give a clear reason for not moving all the way to the other's posterior.

Then walk me through it like a proof, not a story. Give the state space, the prior, both partitions, and E. Pick the actual state and show what each person believes before the announcement, what each infers from the number the other announces, and why each is entitled to keep, or only partly revise, their posterior. Pinpoint the exact failure of common knowledge: what does Alice fail to know about Bob's partition, or about what Bob would have announced in other states, and what's the first rung of the everyone-knows-that-everyone-knows ladder that breaks. Then change the protocol minimally so agreement is forced in one round—I suspect it's having them announce the set of states they consider possible rather than a bare probability, but show me whether that actually works and why. Last, play defense attorney for the Aumann result: what does its defender say about the example, does it refute the theorem or just withhold a condition the theorem needs, and is the scalar-announcement problem a real epistemic limitation or just a communication error.
