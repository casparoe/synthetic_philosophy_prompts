I teach a upper-level undergraduate game theory course (mostly econ and math majors), and I'm putting together a problem set on mixed strategies. I need one problem on iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies where the whole point is that you have to eliminate by a mixed strategy, not just a pure one. Students in this course leave thinking IEDS is just "delete whatever row is pairwise beaten by another row," and I want a question that forces them to actually compute expected payoffs against mixtures.

Use the game below. Row player's payoff is first. I've written it so that in the full 3x3, no pure strategy strictly dominates another for either player, but there *is* mixed-strategy dominance if students are careful; the first elimination has to be by a mixture, and after that a second elimination also has to be by a mixture before the remaining path is pure. Please can you draft:

(i) the problem statement itself — make it a two-part exercise. Part (a): apply iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies (allowing mixed strategies) and report what survives. Part (b): say what would happen if only pure-strategy dominance were allowed, and explain briefly why allowing mixtures changes the result.

(ii) a full model answer. It should go round by round: at each round state exactly which strategy is eliminated and by what pure or mixed strategy, and show the payoff inequalities against every remaining strategy of the other player (not just one or two). End by stating the surviving profile(s) and its payoff.

(iii) a marking scheme out of 20. For each move, give the marks and a one-line note on what specific mistake a student who misses those marks has probably made. It should seperate an A answer (gets all four eliminations, verifies each one fully, never quietly uses weak dominance, and handles the pure-strategy-only contrast) from a B answer (gets the sequence but skips verification of the mixed eliminations, or doesn't say what survives under pure-strategy-only).

Game:

```
          L            C            R
T   (3, 0.5)     (0, 2)      (1, 0)
M   (1, 5)       (1, 0)      (1, 0)
B   (0, 0.5)     (3, 0)      (2, 2)
```

The exercise is aimed at advanced undergrads and should be doable in about 25 minutes. It's fine if the model answer is concise, maybe 400–500 words, but the inequalities need to be all there.
