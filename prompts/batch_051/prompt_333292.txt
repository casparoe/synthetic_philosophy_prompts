I want you to analyze Elizabeth Anscombe's attempt to stop Oxford from giving Harry Truman an honorary degree as a case study in the just-war rule of noncombatant immunity, run the analysis in both directions, and then say where the framework starts to strain. Here's the background as I understand it: in 1956 Oxford planned to award Truman an honorary degree, largely on the strength of his having ended the Second World War. Anscombe, a philosopher and a Catholic who was at Somerville College at the time, argued that dropping the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki wasn't a legitimate act of war but the deliberate killing of innocent civilians as a means of forcing a surrender, and that this is murder no matter how good the end is. The two bombs killed something like 100,000 to 200,000 people, depending on when you stop counting deaths from radiation, and the overwhelming majority were civilians; Japan surrendered a few days after Nagasaki.

I don't need a recap of the history except where it matters. What I want is the careful mapping. First, make the relevant piece of just-war theory precise enough to do real work: the in bello requirement of discrimination, or noncombatant immunity, plus the doctrine of double effect. Apply them to Truman's decision and to Anscombe's objection. Walk through the concrete considerations on both sides: the blockade, the prior firebombing of civilian areas, the projected invasion casualties, the insistence on unconditional surrender, the fact that Hiroshima and Nagasaki did have genuine military targets, the proposal for a demonstration drop first, and the fact that Japan had committed its own atrocities. Say which of these the theory can take on board and which it has to set aside, and what verdict it delivers.

Then flip it. What does this episode show about just-war theory and about Anscombe's own absolutism? Does the theory come out vindicated, embarrassed, or just not built for a case like this? Where does it go silent or get stretched, for instance around the idea of a population being collectively guilty, or around the gap between intending civilian deaths and merely forseeing them at this scale? And is there a reading of double effect on which Truman still comes out clean, or does the distinction collapse when the civilian deaths are not really a side effect but the mechanism that was supposed to produce the surrender? I suspect Anscombe's fury was only partly about the bomb and partly about the degree ceremony as a public blessing on it, so tell me honestly how much the ceremony itself adds or doesn't add to the analysis.
