I am a database engineer, and I have just spent a long evening arguing with a friend, a historian of philosophy, about Augustine's account of time in Book XI of the Confessions. As my friend summarized it, Augustine's problem is this: the past no longer exists, the future does not yet exist, and the present, if it is a dimensionless point, has no duration; yet we measure times as long and short, and we compare them. Augustine's resolution is that past and future have their being only in the present operations of the mind — the past as memory, the future as expectation, the present as attention — and that time is best understood as a stretching of the mind rather than as a feature of the world existing independently of it. I answered that this sounded to me like a rough, pre-theoretical version of distinctions my own field already draws: valid time versus transaction time in a bitemporal table, an append-only event log from which current state is derived by replay, and the materialized views one builds on top of that log. My friend challenged me to make the translation precise rather than hand-wavy, and to be honest about what does not survive it.

Please restate Augustine's position as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use as a database engineer — valid time (when a fact holds in the world), transaction time (when it is recorded in the system), bitemporal tables, event sourcing, append-only logs, replay, materialized views, point-in-time queries, and the distinction between current state and history. Then give me the residue report: which of Augustine's claims translate cleanly, which ones you had to distort to fit, and which have no expression in this vocabulary at all. Finally, tell me whether the residue points to something my vocabulary cannot say but Augustine could, or to something Augustine's account was never precise enough to say. I want to know whether my friend or I won the argument, or whether the argument itself was misframed.
