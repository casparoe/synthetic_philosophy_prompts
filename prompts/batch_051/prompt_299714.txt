I am presenting a paper next month at a workshop on statehood and recognition. The audience is mostly public international lawyers who reason from practice—treaties, advisory opinions, and what states actually do—plus two constructivist scholars of international relations who are suspicious of any claim that legal categories track a pre-legal fact of the matter, and one political philosopher who works on Hegelian recognition and treats the constitutive view as a dim cousin of a deeper point about being a subject.

My thesis is that the declaratory theory of recognition is, in substance, correct, and that the constitutive theory rests on a category mistake. An entity that satisfies the standard customary criteria of statehood—a permanent population, a defined territory, an effective government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states—is a state under international law whether or not other states acknowledge it. What recognition does is not create statehood but make statehood's incidents exercisable: it gates standing, treaty relations, and participation in international organizations.

My argument, as I intend to press it, runs as follows. (1) The sources of the law on statehood do not list recognition among the criteria for being a state; they list the four familiar criteria. (2) Since recognition is not among the criteria, an entity satisfying them is a state regardless of whether it is recognized. (3) The genuine disabilities of unrecognized states—in standing before courts, in treaty-making, in membership of international organizations—show only that recognition gates rights and participation, not that it gates statehood; these are different legal positions. (4) Therefore the constitutive theory assigns recognition more work than it can bear, and the debate persists because the two sides run statehood together with the rights that recognition makes exercisable.
