Question: Explain Hempel's dilemma for defining "the physical," and then say whether the dilemma can be escaped by defining the physical as whatever an ideal, completed future physics would describe.

Student's answer: Hempel's dilemma is that any attempt to define "physical" faces a choice between two bad options. If "physical" means whatever current physics says exists, then physicalism is almost certainly false, because current physics is incomplete and will keep revising its ontology. If "physical" means whatever an ideal, completed physics says exists, then physicalism becomes trivial: an ideal physics is by definition a complete account of whatever exists, so if Cartesian souls existed, the completed physics would have to include them. So Hempel showed physicalism is either false or empty. But I think the dilemma can be escaped by saying that "physical" doesn't mean what current physics or ideal physics says, but what physics aims to describe—the kind of thing physics is in the business of describing. On that definition, physicalism is substantive and probably true.
