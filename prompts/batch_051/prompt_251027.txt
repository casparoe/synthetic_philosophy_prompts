I've been trying to figure out what "corrupt" actually means in politics, and I've realized I use the word with total confidence while being completely unable to define it — which is probably a sign I'm using it to do alot of work. Can you give me a working definition of political corruption that gets the following cases right? Some of these I'm sure are corrupt, some I'm sure aren't, and a few I honestly can't decide, so please run the definition through each one and say which verdicts are shaky.

For context, a friend of mine keeps insisting the real issue is dependence: a system can be corrupt even if nobody ever trades a vote for money, just because officials need their donors to keep their jobs. I can't tell whether that's a second concept or part of the same one, and I'd like you to settle that too.

The cases:

- A candidate accepts the largest check the law allows from a donor, and later votes for a bill the donor has publicly pushed for years — a bill the candidate also supported before the donation.
- A lobbyist emails a legislative aide the exact text of a provision that becomes law; two years later the aide's boss leaves office and takes a job at the lobbyist's firm.
- A member of Congress buys stock based on confidential committee briefings.
- A senator refuses to confirm an agency head until the agency approves a project in the senator's home state.
- A former regulator takes a job with the company she used to regulate, advising it on how to deal with her old agency.
- A billionaire spends a few million through an outside group on ads praising a candidate, without coordinating with the campaign.
- A legislator votes for a bill partly because it will raise property values in her district.
- A city council member awards a contract to a friend's firm, though the firm did submit the best bid under the stated criteria.
- A party leader tells a donor he'll be "taken care of" if he agrees not to run against the party's preferred candidate in a primary.
- A nonprofit that doesn't disclose its donors runs ads two weeks before an election saying "call Senator X and thank her for fighting for clean water."

I don't need the legal answer for each one — I want the concept. Give me the definition you'd actually defend, run it through all ten cases, and be honest about where it forces a verdict you wouldn't have reached otherwise. And please commit — don't just hand me three candidate definitions and leave me to pick.
