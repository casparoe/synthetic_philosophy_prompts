I am a novelist finishing a legal thriller set in a small town, and one of the book's hinge scenes has become a philosophical problem I cannot solve to my satisfaction. The town has on its books a sign ordinance, passed four decades ago, requiring a permit and a small annual fee for any projecting wooden shop sign. It has never once been enforced: three successive mayors, the current police chief, and nearly every shopkeeper on Main Street have been in open violation since the day it passed, with no fines, no notices, and no one in town who remembers the law exists. Early in the novel, a newly elected mayor with a personal grievance against a particular business owner unearths the ordinance and enforces it exactly as written against that owner alone, while leaving every other violator alone. My protagonist, the targeted owner's lawyer, argues before the town council that this is not a legitimate act of government, but I keep writing him into the same two weak positions: that it is unfair and that it is selectively applied. What I want is the strongest principled argument that a law which is formally valid and still on the books can nonetheless be illegitimate to enforce after decades of open, universal non-enforcement—and I also want to know whether my mayor has a genuinely respectable reply. Under what conditions, if any, should long disuse defeat an otherwise valid law, and what would the opposing side's best case be? I would rather the scene turn on the real philosophical structure of the problem than on my protagonist's rhetoric.
