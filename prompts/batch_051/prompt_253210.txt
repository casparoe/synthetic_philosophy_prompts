A colleague is convinced the World Happiness Report doesn't measure happiness. His line is that the ranking rests on one Gallup question—place your current life on a ladder from 0 to 10, with the top anchored to your best possible life—and that it mostly measures how people interpret a metaphor, how willing they are to give extreme answers, and how much they trust pollsters, not how they actually feel day to day. I push back that the ladder correlates with suicide rates and depression, which we'd independently call unhappiness. He says that's circular: those are just other operationalizations of the same unobservable thing, so we never get outside the circle. Design the strongest empirical test you can of whether the ladder question measures happiness rather than noise, response style, or self-presentation. Say concretely what data and comparison you'd use, what result would push you to each side, and what even the best result cant settle—including whether a country that tops the ranking should therefore try to. Then tell me which finding would most change your mind, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
