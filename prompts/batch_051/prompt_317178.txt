I run a philosophy of law reading group, and two of us have been going round in circles on the Hart–Devlin debate for months. One holds a Devlin-style legal moralist view: the criminal law may properly enforce a society's shared morality because the society's survival depends on that shared morality staying intact, so consensual private vice that loosens shared moral sentiment is a legitimate object of the criminal law. The other holds a Hart/Mill-style liberal view: all that can justify criminalisation is the prevention of harm to others, and neither moral offensiveness nor the erosion of shared sentiment counts as harm in the needed sense. Write the document an adversarial collaboration between those two positions would produce, not a dialogue and not a verdict on who is right. It should have: (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can actually sign, phrased so they aren't vacuous; (2) a table of the precise residual disagreements, with each side's view worded as that side would accept it; (3) a section of cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, and for each crux a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Do the drafting work: find formulations that are neutral without being empty, and seperate the disagreements that are genuine from the ones that were mostly about words. Don't just re-run the textbook summary of Hart and Devlin; we're especially interested in whether the dispute bottoms out in an empirical prediction about what decriminalising consensual private vice does to a society's shared morality, and what would test it. Work into at least one crux the case of a proposal to repeal a Sunday-trading ban that is defended only on the ground that it protects Sunday as a shared day of rest, and record what each side predicts would happen to the relevant norms. At the end, add a short note saying how confident you are in the document and which element you're least sure you've got right.
