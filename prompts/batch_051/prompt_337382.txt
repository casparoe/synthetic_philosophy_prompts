Please give me a precise credence in the following proposition, and show the decomposition that produces the number rather than giving a bare estimate.

Proposition: Consider a Pascal's-mugging-type case. A stranger asks an agent for a small but non-trivial payment, claiming that if the agent does not pay, an astronomically large but finite harm will befall an astronomically large number of people. The only evidence for the claim is the stranger's testimony. After competent reflection, the agent assigns probability at most 10^-12 to the claim; multiplying the stated harm by that probability yields an expected loss vastly exceeding the payment. In this situation, a fully rational agent is rationally permitted to refuse to pay and is not rationally required to comply with naive expected-value reasoning.

For each of the following component claims, assign a rough probability and give a sentence or two of justification; if you think a component is not well-posed, say so and explain what repairs are needed:

1. The case is well-posed: the relevant utilities and probabilities are determinate enough for expected-value arithmetic to deliver a verdict at all.
2. Standard expected utility theory, correctly applied, actually entails paying in this case; the fanatical verdict is not merely an artifact of unbounded utilities, misleading probability talk, or a careless calculation.
3. A rational agent may assign lower decision weight to a claim whose sole evidential basis is unverifiable testimony from an interested party, because the claim has passed through an adversarial selection process, so the agent's first-order probability should not simply be mapped onto decision weight.
4. Independently of testimony-specific doubts, rational decision-making may apply a threshold or non-linear weighting in the extreme tails, treating sufficiently small probabilities as negligible for decision even when the stakes are enormous.
5. The correct theory of practical rationality must retain expected value maximization even in this case; the rational agent is required to pay, and the intuitive resistance is a psychological fact rather than a normative one.

Then combine these components: give your overall credence in the proposition; explain, at least informally, how the component probabilities combine to produce it, being explicit about dependence assumptions; identify the single component to which your overall credence is most sensitive; and state one argument or body of evidence, conceptual or formal, that would most move your number.

Finally, I put my credence in the proposition at about 0.3, and a colleague puts hers at about 0.8. On your decomposition, which component most likely separates us, and what would each of us need to believe about that component for our respective credences to be stable?
