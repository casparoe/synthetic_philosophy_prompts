I'm taking an early modern philosophy course and we just read the Introduction to Berkeley's *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge*. Our TA summed it up like this: Berkeley is basically a nominalist about universals, so he denies that there are abstract general ideas. The problem is that we use one word—like "triangle" or "motion"—for lots of different particular perceptions, and then we trick ourselves into thinking there must be some single abstract thing behind it that the word names. His main argument is that you cannot form a mental image of a triangle that is neither equilateral, isosceles, nor scalene, so there can't be an abstract idea of a triangle, and therefore a universal like "triangle" is just a word we use to refer loosely to many particular triangles. Once you see that, the whole problem of universals dissolves: all there are is particular ideas and the words we paste onto them.

But then I read the third section of the Introduction, where Berkeley says: "But, perhaps, we may be too partial to ourselves in placing the fault originally in our faculties, and not rather in the wrong use we make of them." That makes it sound like the diagnosis isn't just that language misleads us, but that we're using our own faculties badly—which seems like a different claim. I'm also not sure "just a word" is fair, because Berkeley seems to think general words do real work through their relation to particular ideas. I suspect the TA's summary is partly unfair, but I can't tell exactly where the line is. Which pieces should I accept, which are subtly misrepresenting him, and does the conclusion that universals are nothing but words actually follow from what Berkeley says?
