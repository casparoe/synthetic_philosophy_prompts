Over the years I have kept an informal list of judgments about when a way of valuing something strikes me as idolatrous or misdirected, rather than merely unusual or not to my taste, and the list refuses to cohere. Here it is.

A widower treats his late wife's wedding ring as a kind of presence: he talks to it, will not have it cleaned, and says he consults it before serious decisions. I do not find this wrong.

A collector keeps a fine violin locked in a vault, never plays it, and will not let a musician near it, on the grounds that owning it is the whole point. I find this a wrong way to value a violin, even though it is his property and no one is harmed.

A father values his daughter's childhood drawings so highly that he would save them from a fire before a Rembrandt in the same house. I cannot bring myself to call his valuing misplaced or excessive.

A man treats his nationality as sacred: he cannot hear it criticized, believes it entitles him to favor a less deserving compatriot over a more deserving foreigner, and says its flourishing matters to him more than anything else. I find this idolatrous in a way the father's attitude toward the drawings is not.

A scholar says truth is the only thing she values unconditionally; she would sacrifice any friendship or project for a small increment of truth, though she harms no one. I find this the wrong way to value truth, not merely an unusual way.

A hedonist, after reflection, treats pleasure as the only thing of final value and lives by that consistently. I do not find this idolatrous in the way the nationalist's attitude is idolatrous, though I cannot yet say why.

What principle — or at most a small set of candidate principles — captures these judgments? State it precisely, and say which case or clause motivates each element of it. I especially want to know whether my approval of the father and my condemnations of the collector, the nationalist, and the scholar can be held together, or whether some verdict has to give. Then give the principle's verdicts on three cases I have not listed: a monk who lets family ties lapse for exclusive devotion to God as he understands God; a fan who grieves her team's defeats like personal bereavements but harms no one; and someone who values another human being chiefly as an occasion for experiencing beauty. Be explicit about which of my judgments the resulting account cannot accommodate, and whether that should overturn the judgment or the principle. I am asking for a committed answer: defend the account you judge best, with reasons, rather than laying out the options.
