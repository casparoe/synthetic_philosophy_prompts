As part of the final exam in a course on Renaissance political thought, students were given the following passage from Niccolò Machiavelli's *The Prince*, chapter 25, in the W. K. Marriott translation:

> "Changes in estate also issue from this, for if, to one who governs himself with caution and patience, times and affairs converge in such a way that his administration is successful, his fortune is made; but if times and affairs change, he is ruined if he does not change his course of action. But a man is not often found sufficiently circumspect to know how to accommodate himself to the change, both because he cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to do, and also because, having always prospered by acting in one way, he cannot be persuaded that it is well to leave it; and, therefore, the cautious man, when it is time to turn adventurous, does not know how to do it, hence he is ruined; but had he changed his conduct with the times fortune would not have changed."

The exam question read: "Using this passage as your point of departure, and drawing where needed on the rest of chapter 25, reconstruct Machiavelli's account of why a prince who has prospered can nonetheless be ruined, and of the respective roles of fortune and virtù in that account. Then evaluate whether this account leaves any room for a prince of fixed character to succeed through changing circumstances, and state precisely what the passage identifies as the obstacle to changing one's course of action."

One student submitted the following answer:

"Machiavelli is responding to people who say that fortune and God govern all human affairs and that prudence and effort are useless. He rejects that fatalism only partly: fortune, he admits, is the arbiter of about half of our actions, while the rest are left to us. The river image makes the point. Fortune is like a river in flood, sweeping away whatever is unprepared; but once the weather is fair, men can build embankments and channels so that the next flood is contained. Virtù is exactly this: the preparatory energy and foresight that build defenses before fortune turns, rather than after the damage is done. That is why Italy, being an open country without such defenses, suffers the invasions it does.

Machiavelli then moves from general preparation to the fit between a prince's manner of acting and his times. He says the prince who directs his actions according to the spirit of the times succeeds, while the one whose actions do not accord with the times fails. Since times change, the supreme political virtue is adaptability: a prince should treat caution and boldness as interchangeable instruments and choose whichever fits the moment. Julius II prospered because his impetuosity happened to suit his circumstances; had he lived longer and turned cautious when circumstances required it, he would have remained secure.

This leaves almost no room for a prince of fixed character. A naturally cautious prince will flourish while circumstances reward caution, but once they change he is ruined, because his past success blinds him to the need for a different course. The only ruler safe from fortune is the one who deliberately cultivates a flexible character, treating every style as a tool rather than a habit. That is what Machiavelli is recommending when he says that if the cautious man had changed his conduct with the times, fortune would not have changed."
