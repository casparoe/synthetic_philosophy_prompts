I work in an energy planning body, not in academia, and I keep meeting a particular analogy used to frame long-term strategy. People say a civilisation is like an organism: its total energy throughput is its metabolism, and the Kardashev scale—planetary, stellar, galactic—is best read as a growth chart. A civilisation that keeps raising the amount of energy it draws from its environment is maturing; one that flattens off or reduces its use is stagnating. I have also come across John Barrow's inward-looking scale, which ranks civilisations by the smallest scale at which they can deliberately rearrange matter, and I cannot work out whether that simply adds another axis or whether it exposes something wrong with the organism picture. I would like you to audit the organism-and-metabolism analogy as it is actually used in these debates. Give me the correspondence table: what is meant to play the role of the organism's body, its metabolic rate, its growth, its homeostasis, its lifespan? Then show where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are merely incidental. Which features of organisms does the analogy quietly import—a single body, shared interests, a predictable ageing process—that a civilisation may lack? In particular, does the conclusion that ever-rising energy consumption is the right measure of advancement survive once the disanalogies and Barrow's inward scale are on the table, or was the analogy carrying that conclusion all along? If the analogy cannot be repaired, say what a better analogy would be.
