I direct the quantitative methods sequence for a doctoral program in the social and health sciences, and for years I have watched the same unresolved argument about how to teach and do causal inference. I am not asking for a verdict about which side is right; I am asking for a diagnosis of why the dispute has stalled and what kind of disagreement it really is.

One camp, associated with Judea Pearl, holds that causal questions should be posed and answered within structural causal models: a system of autonomous equations and a directed graph, with interventions represented by the do-operator and the do-calculus as the rules for transporting information between the observational and interventional levels. On this view causal reasoning forms a hierarchy of three levels — associational, interventional, and counterfactual — and the higher levels cannot in general be reduced to the lower ones; in particular, individual counterfactuals require structural equations, not merely distributions. The other camp, associated with Donald Rubin and the potential-outcomes tradition, treats causal inference as a missing-data problem: each unit has potential outcomes under each treatment, only one is ever observed, and the analyst's job is to model the assignment mechanism and identify an estimand such as an average treatment effect under assumptions like unconfoundedness or exchangeability. The two camps agree on a great deal — randomized experiments, many adjustment formulas, and large parts of applied practice — yet they have argued for decades about what a causal model must contain, whether individual-level counterfactuals are meaningful, and which questions are genuinely causal. The arguments are well rehearsed, and I almost never see anyone move.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is only partly about mathematics or identification. I suspect the potential-outcomes side wants a principled framework for drawing causal conclusions from finite data, while the structural side wants a representational system in which mechanisms, hypothetical interventions, and counterfactual queries can be expressed and answered, including queries with no obvious experimental counterpart — and that under this hypothesis the two are largely answering different questions under a single heading. But I can see a more substantive reading as well: if the structural-causal side is right that the counterfactual level cannot be recovered from the lower levels, then the potential-outcomes framework is not merely less expressive but incomplete in an important way.

Please diagnose the impasse. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two communities answering different questions? What kind of disagreement is it really — one about facts, about standards of explanation or evidence, about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually commits itself to, not a summary of the two frameworks. Then say what would count as progress under that diagnosis: a formal result, a worked example one framework handles and the other does not, a distinction that dissolves the apparent conflict, a change of question, or an argument that no unification should be expected and the field should simply teach both. Please write the answer in American English.
