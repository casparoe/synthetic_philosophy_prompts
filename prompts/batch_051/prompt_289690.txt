I have been reading about moral philosophy and machine learning separately, and I want to know whether a parallel I keep noticing holds up. Act-consequentialism identifies the right action with the action that produces the most impartially considered value, and the standard demandingness objection is that this threatens to consume almost any life: if my friendships, hobbies, and family time produce marginally less impartial value than the same resources sent elsewhere, I seem required to sacrifice them. This reminds me strongly of Goodhart-style problems in machine learning: once you optimize a system against a single quantitative objective, the system tends to exploit that objective, and the objective stops tracking the broader set of things you actually wanted. In both cases, a single maximizing criterion ends up sacrificing considerations that were never meant to be left behind. Is this a genuine identity of structure, or am I just pattern-matching? If it is genuine, does anything from the machine-learning discussion of reward hacking, specification gaming, or multi-objective tradeoffs transfer back to the debate over whether consequentialism is too demanding? If it is only a loose analogy, say exactly where the two cases come apart. I would rather receive a committed verdict than a survey of possible views.
