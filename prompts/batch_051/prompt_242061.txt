You're an expert on decision theory, embedded agency, and the philosophy of agents — the kind of person who actually enjoys arguing about where the boundary between an agent and its environment should be drawn. I'd like you to think carefully, brainstorm the candidate readings before settling on any, and then lay the whole thing out systematically.

At my AI-safety reading group the other night we were arguing about whether a sufficiently capable model can be treated, for practical purposes, as a utility maximizer over world states, and someone cut the whole discussion off with "the agent is part of the environment — you can't draw a clean box around it." Everyone nodded and we moved on, but I'm convinced that sentence is doing more work then it's earned. It gets used, as far as I can tell, to do at least three different jobs: (1) to dismiss the assumption of a fixed input/output channel between agent and world, (2) to gesture at the fact that the agent's model of the world has to include the model itself, so you get self-reference problems, and (3) to claim that counterfactuals like "what the agent would have done if it had chosen otherwise" are ill-defined until you specify how the world would have differed, since the world includes other agents (and maybe copies or simulations of the agent) that respond to its policy. Those feel like very different claims, and I doubt they all stand or fall together.

Separate out the precise theses that "the agent is part of the environment" could actually be asserting, from the boring physical claim (the agent is made of atoms in the same world as everything else) through the methodological ones (where you draw the agent/environment boundary is a modeling choice rather than a natural fact) up to the radical ones (the environment contains predictors and simulations of the agent, so any decision theory that assumes the environment responds only to the agent's actual actions and not to its policy is empirically false). For each reading, say whether it's a near-tautology, a substantive and defensible methodological point, or something strong enough that it's probably false, and say what it would actually license. Then tell me which reading or readings would genuinely support the dismissal of the utility-function-over-world-states formalism, which readings people usually mean when they say the line, and flag the pairs of readings that get conflated in practice.
