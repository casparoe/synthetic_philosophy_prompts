I have been collecting cases of actions directed at objects, places, and figures that others treat as sacred, and my verdicts on them are less consistent than I would like. I want to know whether anything principled unifies them, or whether I should revise some of my reactions.

Here are six cases, with my honest verdicts.

1. A man burns an American flag in a steel drum on his own driveway, alone, on Memorial Day, as a private political protest. I think this is permissible and should not be punished; I would not do it, but nothing about it strikes me as wrong.

2. A tourist with no religious upbringing attends a Catholic Mass out of curiosity, keeps a consecrated Communion wafer rather than consuming it, and later films herself eating it with jam, captioning the video "just a cracker." I think this is ignorant and rude, but not seriously wrong; I would tell her not to do it again.

3. A far-right group burns copies of the Quran on the public pavement directly outside a mosque during Friday prayers, timing it so that worshippers must walk past. I think this is seriously wrong, and it should be stopped—not merely because people are offended.

4. A municipally funded gallery displays a photograph of a plastic crucifix standing in a jar of urine. I would not choose to see it, but I do not think the state should remove it; a free society should be able to carry this sort of provocation.

5. A tourist takes a grinning, thumbs-up selfie with friends at a Holocaust memorial. I think this is wrong, not just bad taste, even though it harms no one and breaks no law.

6. A lapsed Catholic obtains a consecrated host by deception, stomps on it in an empty church, and uploads the video. I think this is seriously wrong. But I worry it is uncomfortably close to the tourist case, and I cannot yet say why they come apart.

My best attempt at a principle is this: desecrating what others hold sacred is wrong exactly when it is a public communicative act, aimed at an identifiable group, intended to express contempt toward that group, and carried out where the group cannot reasonably avoid encountering it. But I can already see this lets the memorial selfie off the hook, since the tourist intends no contempt, and it may let the lapsed Catholic's online video off too, since a video is avoidable. It also feels less like a principle than a list of escape hatches.

Please state a repaired or rival principle—at most three—and test each against all six cases, indicating which clause is doing the work in each case. If no defensible principle keeps all six of my verdicts without becoming an unprincipled patchwork, tell me which one or two I should abandon. Then apply your best candidate to a case I keep going back and forth on: a comedy troupe stages a play in which the Virgin Mary is depicted as promiscuous, foul-mouthed, and cynical; the show is advertised publicly, and a nearby parish holds a prayer vigil outside the theatre on opening night but does not block anyone from entering. Is the production wrong, permissible, or something greyer on your view?

Keep the whole answer under 500 words; use a numbered list if you give more than one principle.
