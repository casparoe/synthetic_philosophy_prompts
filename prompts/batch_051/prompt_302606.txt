Okay so I keep going back and forth on whether Dawkins-style memetics is a serious theory of culture or just a metaphor that got out of hand. The original argument, roughly, was that evolution by natural selection only requires variation, selection, and heredity, and culture has all three because memes get copied from person to person with enough fidelity to act as the units of selection. But a lot of the pushback since then has landed on the copying-fidelity part: cultural transmission is not mostly high-fidelity replication, its reconstruction. When someone tells you an idea, you don't store the exact representation and copy it; you remember the gist, regenerate something close enough, smooth over details that don't fit your existing categories, and blend what you heard with what you already believe. What moves between people looks more like a cloud of near-variants than a particulate meme, and between any two people the "unit" can shift a little.

So my question is whether the Darwinian theory of culture survives that change of assumption. Does the reconstruction point hit a load-bearing premise, or just one optional way of cashing out the heredity requirement? If it does real damage, is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still worth saying, and what does that version still commit you to? And if you think the whole thing is basically dead, tell me what a non-Darwinian account of cultural change would need to explain that the Darwinian story handled well, and whether any alternative actually does. Also, since I want to know where to aim my skepticism: tell me which argument or empirical result would most likely flip your verdict the other way, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. I'd rather have a calibrated answer than a strong one.
