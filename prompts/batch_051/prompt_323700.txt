I'm a journalist who covers the science and ideas beat, and for an essay I'm writing I want to think through the idea that humility is being recommended too readily as an intellectual and ethical corrective. I plan to read Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day as a test case, and I need a reading that is philosophically sharp rather than merely thematic.

The novel's narrator, the English butler Stevens, has spent his life perfecting an ideal of self-effacing professional service. He defers to his employer, Lord Darlington, on questions large and small; he suppresses his own political views and his personal wishes; and he treats the restraint that keeps him from speaking honestly to the housekeeper Miss Kenton as the mark of professional dignity. Late in the book, as he drives across England and looks back, he confronts evidence that Darlington was less the principled statesman than the gullible instrument of appeasement-friendly circles, and that his own inability to step out of his role cost him an intimate relationship and, arguably, a self of his own. The novel is often read as a tragedy of repression. I want to read it instead as a case study in the ethics of humility — intellectual, moral, and social — and I want the reading to be unsparing rather than merely illustrative.

The question is not whether Stevens is sympathetic, but whether anything about his humility is a genuine virtue. Distinguish the three registers as rigorously as you can: intellectual humility (about what one is entitled to believe), moral humility (about one's standing to judge or demand), and social modesty (about occupying space or claiming recognition). Then use those distinctions to read the novel's central scenes and choices. Where does Stevens's deference look like a real virtue, and where does it shade into servility, credulity, or the abdication of moral responsibility? Does the novel imply that humility is virtuous only under conditions — for example, only when one has chosen a worthy object of deference and has kept the ability to step out of the role — or does it go further and suggest that the missing virtue is self-respect rather than humility? I would also like to know where the novel resists these clean philosophical categories, and what The Remains of the Day contributes to the debate over whether humility is a virtue that an abstract argument would miss.
