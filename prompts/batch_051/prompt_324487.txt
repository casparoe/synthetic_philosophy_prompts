My school's Global Issues Club is currently divided over how to spend the money we raised this semester. One side wants to give unrestricted cash transfers to households facing acute food insecurity; the other side wants to fund a program that provides small-plot farmers or fishers with training and equipment so they can support themselves. During the debate, a club member ended the discussion by quoting the proverb, "Give a man a fish and you feed him for a day; teach a man to fish and you feed him for a lifetime." He used it to settle the question in favor of the training program, as though it were self-evident that skills are the durable, systemic fix while direct cash is only a temporary patch. I am not convinced the proverb settles anything, and I would like to arrive at the next meeting with a more careful reading of it. Could you lay out the distinct claims that proverb could be making — about effectiveness, dignity, durability, or justice — as a menu of readings from the obvious to the revisionary, and say which of those readings actually support his conclusion? I would especially like to know where the proverb silently assumes exactly what a systems approach would question: whether the natural resource is still there, who owns or controls it, whether the trained person can reach a market, and whether cash transfers might better let households address the constraints they themselves identify as urgent. Is "teach a man to fish" genuinely a systems-level intervention, or is it only a less temporary marginal intervention dressed up as one? What does the proverb get wrong, or hide, from the point of view of someone who wants to change structures rather than individual capacities?
