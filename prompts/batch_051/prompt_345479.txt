I'm in a seminar on existentialism, and we've been reading Being and Nothingness. Last week we got into the bad faith chapter, especially the café waiter whose movement is a little too precise and the woman who leaves her hand in her date's hand while pretending not to notice it. I got into an argument with another student about whether Sartre's notion of bad faith is actually coherent, and I laid out a quick reductio to show it isn't. She said I was being to quick but couldn't pinpoint the exact step. So maybe you can help.

Here's the argument as I gave it.

(1) A person in bad faith is hiding something from themselves, or at any rate evading it.  
(2) Hiding and evading are intentional projects; they only make sense as something one does.  
(3) For Sartre, everything one does is an exercise of radical freedom.  
(4) So the person in bad faith is freely choosing to evade their own freedom.  
(5) But if they are freely choosing to evade their freedom, then they must already, on some level, be aware that they are free — otherwise there is nothing to evade.  
(6) And if they are aware they are free, then they don't genuinely believe they are unfree, so the alleged self-deception can't count as bad faith.  
(7) Therefore, bad faith is impossible on Sartre's own terms.

I keep thinking the problem must be somewhere around (5) or (6), with the phrase "aware" or "genuinely believe," but I don't know how to make the objection precise. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what distinction does Sartre draw that would block it? Be really specific about the step and the reading of it.
