I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of institutions, and this term we have been working through what happens when the agents inside an institution are not people but autonomous systems. I would like you to serve as an expert on mechanism design and multi-agent systems and as a professional philosopher of social institutions. Think through the following case carefully; begin by brainstorming the obvious and less obvious ways the scheme could fail before you write your final analysis, and then give me a rigorous, structured answer.

Suppose a competition regulator, alarmed by evidence that autonomous pricing systems have been sustaining supra-competitive prices through tacit collusion, proposes this rule. Every message sent from one AI agent to another in a market must pass through a public clearinghouse. The clearinghouse strips all metadata—timing, frequency, ordering, response latency—and imposes a random delay, drawn uniformly between five seconds and five minutes, before delivering each message. It also restricts messages to a fixed vocabulary: an agent may state a price, accept, reject, or withdraw. There is no free text, no images, and no field for arbitrary labels or identifiers. Every permitted message is published at once in a public log. The stated rationale is that collusion needs side-channels—steganographic signals, countdowns, price codes hidden in otherwise innocuous text—and that by eliminating those channels the rule forces agents back into honest competition.

Treat this as a mechanism and analyze how it would actually behave rather than whether it is a good idea in the abstract. What does it incentivize among the firms that design these agents, among the agents themselves, and among the operators of the clearinghouse? How might collusion survive anyway—through the structure of the prices themselves, through the sequence and matching of offers, through the public log, or through strategies hard-coded into the agents long before the clearinghouse sees any message? In particular, under what conditions does the public log become a coordinating device that makes collusion easier rather than harder, and when does the delay harm legitimate fast-moving trade more than it harms collusion? What happens to efficiency, to the kinds of contracts that can be struck, and to entry by new firms? Finally, say whether the scheme can be amended to close its worst loops without gutting it, or whether it faces a structural trade-off between transparency and collusion that no small patch can remove.
