I am trying to sort out what I actually think about the difference between knowing how to do something and knowing facts about it, and I would like you to help me find the principle behind my reactions to a set of cases. I will give you my reactions, then a hard case, and then I want to know what account of knowing how best captures them.

A concert pianist was forced by arthritis to stop performing twenty years ago. She can still talk a student through a difficult passage with precision: where to place the weight, how to shape a phrase, what to adjust when the voicing turns muddy. She says she knows how to play the passage. I think she does.

A sports scientist has studied the mechanics of swimming for years and can give a complete and accurate account of how to swim, but he has never been in the water and cannot stay afloat. I do not think he knows how to swim.

A bricklayer learned entirely on site and produces straight, level, well-bonded walls with complete reliability. When asked to explain his method, he cannot put it into words; he says he works by feel. I think he knows how to build a wall.

A trainee pilot has passed every written and procedural examination and can recite the emergency checklists flawlessly. But her training has used only a non-functional cockpit mock-up; she has never manipulated live controls. The first time she is given control of a simulator that responds like the real aircraft, she freezes and cannot execute even the most basic of the procedures. I do not think she knows how to fly.

Those four verdicts set up two cross-cutting pairs. The pianist and the pilot both possess a great deal of correct descriptive information and both presently fail in performance, yet I count only the pianist as knowing how. The oceanographer and the bricklayer present the reverse combination: the first has descriptive knowledge without performance, the second performance without descriptive knowledge, yet only the bricklayer counts as knowing how. The two obvious one-factor views each fail against these verdicts. If knowing how were simply possessing the correct descriptive account of the activity, the oceanographer and the pilot would know how. If knowing how were simply the present capacity to perform, the pianist would not know how. Neither consequence seems right to me.

One further case sits at the edge, and I keep moving. An organist who played the same prelude every week for decades suffers a small stroke. She can still play the piece straight through when conditions are exactly as familiar, but she cannot begin in another key, cannot adjust the tempo when the congregation lags, cannot correct a wrong note, and cannot tell a student what she is doing. Sometimes I think she still knows how to play the prelude; other times I think something essential has been lost.

What principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures my clear verdicts? Please state each candidate precisely, say what it predicts for the organist, and say which of my stated verdicts, if any, it forces me to give up. I would also like to know what the account says about someone who has never played golf but sinks a long putt on the first attempt: does he know how to sink that putt?
