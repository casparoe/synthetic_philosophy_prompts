I'm a software engineer with no formal background in politics or philosophy, and maybe that's why I cant stop treating this as a system-design problem. A Swiss friend of mine is always going on about subsidiarity—that decisions should live at the lowest level that can actually handle them—and after one of those arguments we invented a rule for the US. Assume the rule actually gets ratified as a constitutional amendment in 2026, and it works like this. Any state legislature can, by a simple majority of both chambers (or the one chamber in Nebraska), declare that a particular federal statute or regulation no longer applies inside that state, effective ninety days later. Congress can reimpose the law by passing a resolution through both chambers by a three-fifths majority, and to keep this from turning into a separation-of-powers side quest, assume the president's signature isn't required and the courts won't entertain challenges to the procedure. So nothing gets struck down for the whole country; a law just gets switched off for one state until Congress affirmatively switches it back on.

What I want is a thirty-year prediction of how that plays out, run as a chain of mechanisms and incentives rather than a story. Which kinds of federal law do you expect to be switched off first—environmental, labor, civil rights, financial, immigration enforcement—and which would almost never be touched? Does nullification cluster in a handful of states and settle into a permanent patchwork, or does it spread, or do states quietly stop using it alot because businesses, insurers, and hospitals hate the regulatory uncertainty? Walk me through how Congress, federal agencies, and companies adapt: conditions on federal grants, waiting periods, states copying each other's nullification votes, private certification and insurance standards, enforcement against activity that crosses state lines. And be explicit about confidence: which of your predictions are robust, which are guesses, and what would have to be true about the party system or the economy for the whole outcome to look different.
