I've been trying to get my head around why people think the minimality condition in causal Bayes nets is enough to get you causation, and I keep running into the same wall. My understanding is roughly this: the causal Markov condition says the independences implied by d-separation in the graph actually hold in the population; minimality says the graph is economically chosen, in the sense that you can't delete an edge without creating a conditional independence the data doesn't have; and faithfulness says the run goes the other way too—every independence in the data is one that d-separation in the graph would predict, so there are no "accidental" independences. I know the textbook-y result that faithfulness implies minimality but not conversely, but I've never seen a clean, small, checkable case where the two come apart in an interesting way, where minimality is satisfied but faithfulness actively points at the wrong causal story.

So construct me a thought experiment to those specs. Three measured variables, call them A, B, C; no latent common causes; no selection bias; binary or Gaussian, whichever is easiest to check by hand. Stipulate the true mechanisms as A→B, B→C, and A→C all being real, but set the parameters so the direct A→C effect exactly cancels the indirect A→B→C effect, leaving A statistically independent of C in the population. Make that the only unfaithful independence: A and B should remain dependent, B and C should remain dependent, and conditioning on B should create (or expose) a dependence between A and C. Then do the full audit. First, verify that the true graph satisfies causal Markov and minimality. Second, show that the same distribution also satisfies causal Markov and minimality for the collider A→B←C, so minimality can't decide between them. Third, show that a constraint-based search that assumes faithfulness (the usual "remove an edge when two variables are independent, then orient an unshielded triple as a collider when the two outer variables become dependent after conditioning on the middle one" routine) would infer the collider from the independence A⊥C plus the dependence A⊥C∣B, and explain why the true graph gets reject as unfaithful even though it's the actual mechanism. Fourth, say which structural feature of the case is doing the real work, what happens if you perturb the cancellation just enough that it's no longer exact, and how a defender of faithfulness would push back—is this just a measure-zero trick no serious causal learner should worry about, or does it reveal something about what the faithfulness assumption is actually buying?
