I am a novelist, and in researching how institutions draw the line between science and non-science I have become preoccupied with a particular practice. American trial judges are instructed to act as gatekeepers for expert testimony: before allowing a scientific or technical expert to speak, they are told to consider whether the expert's theory or technique is testable and has actually been tested, whether it has passed peer review and publication, what its known or potential error rate is, and whether it is generally accepted by the relevant scientific community. I want to understand what this practice presupposes about the demarcation problem. Does it presuppose a falsificationist criterion of demarcation, a broader reliability or pragmatic criterion, or some unstable hybrid of the two? Which rival views of demarcation does it quietly rule out, and does it rule them out strictly or only by fitting them poorly? I am especially puzzled by an apparent tension: the same courts have continued to admit forensic comparison disciplines such as fingerprint and toolmark analysis, whose claims are not readily stated as risky, falsifiable predictions, even while they invoke testability to exclude other expert claims. If the view of demarcation the practice presupposes were replaced by a rival, what would have to change about the practice, and what would stay the same?
