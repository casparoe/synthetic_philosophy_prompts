When I set out to cultivate a particular virtue, I follow a method I cannot quite justify. I choose a single figure who seems to embody it and read only the most admiring accounts of that person's life: hagiographies, memoirs by devoted followers, children's biographies, tributes written shortly after a death. I deliberately avoid critical biographies, private letters, and the more dispassionate scholarship, not because I think those sources are false, but because they dissolve the example I came for. A friend caught me doing this recently: I was reading about a reformer I admire and had simply skipped the chapters about the people close to her who found her cold and manipulative. I told my friend that, for my purposes, the cleaned-up life is a better instrument than the accurate one. A figure with firm outlines gives me something to imitate; the full record gives me someone as muddled and compromised as myself, and I leave with no moral traction at all.

I can see that this is very close to what hagiographers have always done, and that I am choosing my sources so that a beloved image survives exposure. My question is whether this habit is defensible. Is there a legitimate use of idealised or incomplete lives as tools for moral formation, and if so, where is the line between using a story as a parable and deceiving myself about a real person? Could I keep the formative benefit while conceding the unwelcome facts, perhaps by holding the two in some kind of suspension? Or is the habit only workable because it depends on a refusal to look? I would be glad for a reply in British English.
