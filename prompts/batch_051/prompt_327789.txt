You are a formal epistemologist and decision theorist whose research sits at the boundary between Bayesian epistemology and the foundations of rational choice, with a specialty in imprecise probabilities. I run a reading group for philosophers and statistically literate scientists, and we have spent several months on the debate between precise and imprecise Bayesians without moving anyone. We agree on the mathematics, we agree on how to describe the standard cases under each formalism (Ellsberg's urn and its variants, and dilation cases in which conditioning on any cell of a partition appears to widen one's interval estimates), and the discussion still returns to the same deadlock.

The precise Bayesians in the group hold that a rationally ideal doxastic state is a single probability function updated by conditionalization, and that rational choice is expected-utility maximization relative to that function. They grant that people dislike ambiguity and that evidence often underdetermines a point-valued probability, but they treat the move to sets of probabilities as either a representation of incomplete preferences or a failure of the action-guidingness they take a belief state to require: a set does not always select an act, and when it does, it does so only through a supplementary decision rule that the theory has not justified from its own commitments.

The imprecise probabilists hold that evidence can warrant a convex set of probability functions rather than a single one, and that rationality does not require manufacturing a precision the evidence does not supply. They read the Ellsberg preferences as permissible rather than as a bias to be corrected, and they take the fact that many precise priors pass the same evidence-based constraints to be itself the correct representation of the agent's epistemic position. Where the set does not pick out a unique act, that is not a bug but a mark of where belief runs out and decision must proceed on grounds belief cannot supply.

The two sides seem to accept each other's formalism completely; nobody disputes the theorems about coherence, convexity, or interval-valued expectation. My working hypothesis is that the debate is really about the function of a doxastic state: precise Bayesians implicitly demand that a rational state be, by itself, a complete decision rule that yields a verdict in every choice problem, while imprecise probabilists take the state's function to be to represent the evidence faithfully, with decision guidance as a downstream and sometimes partial consequence. If that hypothesis is right, the impasse is a normative-conceptual dispute about what credences are for, and no experiment or theorem will break it. But I am unsure, and I want that hypothesis tested rather than confirmed on the cheap.
