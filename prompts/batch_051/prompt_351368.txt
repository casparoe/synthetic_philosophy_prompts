I am currently reading for a course called 'Games, Proof, and Strategy', which sits between combinatorial game theory and the philosophy of mathematics. In the most recent seminar we worked through the strategy-stealing argument for Hex: assume, for the sake of contradiction, that the second player has a winning strategy; the first player makes an arbitrary opening move and thereafter follows that strategy, and whenever the strategy instructs her to play the cell she has already occupied she makes another arbitrary move instead, treating the extra stone as harmless. Since Hex cannot end in a draw, it follows that the first player must have a winning strategy, although the proof never exhibits one. Our tutor then asked what the argument actually assumes, and the room divided. One camp said the load-bearing assumption is monotonicity, the claim that an extra stone of your own colour can never turn a winning position into a losing one. A second camp said monotonicity is not doing the work on its own, and that the real work is done by the symmetry between the two players together with the absence of draws. A third camp objected that the argument is non-constructive: it establishes that a strategy exists without telling anyone what to play, and this is a defect rather than a mere curiosity. A fourth camp deflated the whole thing, saying the proof is just determinacy (the fact that in a finite perfect-information game with no draws one of the two players must have a winning strategy) plus excluded middle, with the stealing itself contributing nothing independent.

I would like you to write a decision tree for our study group that sorts people by what they actually believe about the strategy-stealing assumption, rather than by which label they already prefer. Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. First set out the axes on which the positions genuinely come apart — for example, whether an extra move or stone can ever be worse than passing, whether draws are possible, whether the game has the right symmetry for a stolen strategy to be transposed, whether the proof is required to yield a playable strategy, and whether the game is determined. Then lay out the combinations of answers that are logically open and locate the actual positions within that space. The leaves must include at least the four positions my seminar produced — the monotonicity view, the symmetry-plus-non-draw view, the constructive-criticism view, and the determinacy-deflation view — along with any further positions needed to make the tree honest. For each unoccupied combination, add one sentence saying whether no one holds it because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and explicitly flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate.

The instrument is for second-year students who have met Hex and the argument once and do not yet know the technical vocabulary, so each node should be a concrete question or case a student can answer without already knowing the positions — a question about a specific game or a specific commitment, never a question about which theory they like. The cases that must appear in the questions are at least Hex, Chomp or Bridg-It, a chess-style zugzwang position, and noughts and crosses as a case where draws are possible. Keep the tree to at most six branching questions and eight leaves so it fits in a fifty-minute seminar, and please write your answer in British English.
