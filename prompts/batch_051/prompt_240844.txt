I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of religion, and every time I teach the relation between Paul and James I have the sense of watching a debate that has not moved in centuries but merely re-performs itself in fresh vocabulary. The apparent conflict is familiar. In Romans and Galatians, Paul insists that justification comes through faith and not through observing the prescriptions of the law. The Epistle of James, in its second chapter, insists with equal directness that faith is dead when it produces no actions and that a person is justified by deeds, not by belief alone. The standard harmonizations have been on the table since antiquity and are entirely predictable: the two authors use faith in different senses, works in different senses, justified in different senses; they address different audiences and different pastoral crises; one speaks of the root of the Christian life and the other of its fruit. The striking fact is not that these harmonizations are available but that they have not produced convergence. Each side survives the other's best argument and the dispute simply restarts at the next level of abstraction.

I am not asking for another harmonization, and not for a verdict on who read the texts correctly. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is and why it stalls. Is it substantive, verbal, or several different investigations running under one heading — an exegetical investigation into what each author meant, a doctrinal investigation into what the received corpus as a whole commits one to, and a historical investigation into what conflict the earliest movement was negotiating? Is a hidden empirical or historical question doing the work, for instance about whether Paul means by works of the law moral effort in general or, as a prominent modern line of Pauline scholarship maintains, the specific identity-marking observances that divided Jewish from gentile members of the movement — or about the social situation of the community James addresses? Or is the impasse generated by antecedent commitments, such as the conviction that a received corpus must speak with a single voice or that visible tension is itself a mark of authenticity and development, commitments that no amount of lexical detail could overturn?

State your diagnosis and defend it by engaging the positions I have described rather than surveying them. Then tell me what would change your mind about that diagnosis: which argument, historical finding, or conceptual distinction would lead you to a different account of why the debate stalls, and where in your own reasoning the failure is most likely to occur. Keep the response under 500 words.
