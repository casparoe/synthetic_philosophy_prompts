I'm taking a course on free will, and my instructor keeps using a digger wasp as her running illustration of what unfree action looks like. As she tells it, a Sphex wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the entrance of her burrow, leaves it there, goes inside to inspect the burrow, comes back out, and then pulls the cricket in. But if the experimenter moves the cricket a few inches while the wasp is inside, the wasp does not simply drag the cricket straight into the burrow; she drags it back to the entrance, goes inside to inspect again, comes out, and repeats the whole sequence each time the cricket is moved, seemingly unable to skip an inspection that has become pointless. The instructor calls such behavior sphexish: a fixed action pattern, set off by a simple cue and run to completion without interruption by a change in circumstances the creature is otherwise well equipped to detect. She then generalizes the idea: a reflex, a compulsion, a bureaucracy following its own procedure, a computer program executing a subroutine — all are sphexish in the same way. And she uses this to support a compatibilist conclusion: what we want from free will is not uncaused choosing but the opposite of sphexishness, namely a capacity for behavior to remain sensitive to a broad range of considerations, including newly arriving ones, so that whether the sequence continues depends on what those considerations are rather than on the bare fact that the sequence was triggered.

I would like an audit of how far this analogy — the wasp as a subroutine, and free action as its negation — actually holds. Please give me the correspondence table in full: what corresponds to the subroutine call, the input variable, the missing condition check, the loop, and the bug. Then say where the mapping breaks. In particular, I already suspect one disanalogy: the wasp's rigidity is largely an artifact of a contrived situation, since in its normal environment the sequence normally only needs to run once and single-minded routine is adaptive rather than defective. Is that right, and does it matter for the instructor's argument? I also want to know whether non-sphexishness is too weak or too strong an account of free will — a chess engine is flexible within its domain without being free in any interesting sense, while a person can act freely while following a fixed plan — and whether the instructor's compatibilist conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the wasp was doing most of the argumentative work.
