I host a small philosophy of science podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher known for defending an interventionist account of causation. His central idea, roughly, is that causal claims are claims about what would happen under interventions: X causes Y just in case, if you could wiggle X while holding fixed the other causes of Y, Y (or its probability distribution) would change, and this X–Y relationship has to be invariant across a range of those wiggles. My audience has heard that one-sentence version a dozen times, so I dont want an episode that's just him restating it. Could you act as a professional philosopher of causation and draft eight to ten interview questions that push past the elevator pitch?

I'd like the questions to hit at least these pressure points, each with a sharp follow-up keyed to the reply you'd expect him to give. First, the circularity worry: the official definition of an intervention says the intervention must itself be a cause of X, and must not influence Y except by a route that goes through X — which sounds like causal language is being used to define causation. I want one question pressing whether that's fatal, and a follow-up for his usual line that the circularity is harmless because he's not after a reductive definition, just a more perspicuous one. Second, the gap between possible and actual manipulation: what does the view say about causal claims involving things we couldn't in practice wiggle, like the end-Cretaceous impact, early-universe physics, or a one-off economic shock in a single country's history? If a merely possible intervention is enough, is that anything more than saying the causal graph already says so? Third, how much invariance is enough: an X–Y relation can hold under a narrow range of interventions or under very many of them. Where's the line between a causal relation and a fragile correlation, and isn't that line doing the real philosophical work? Fourth, whether the account handles messy token-causal cases like preemption, omissions, and double prevention, as opposed to clean type-level statistical relationships. And fifth, whether interventionism is ultimately an epistemology of causal discovery dressed up as a metaphysics of causation — and what he'd lose by just admitting that.

Before you write the full list, start by brainstorming the two or three replies he'd most likely reach for on each point, so every question is built to outrun the standard answer rather than just invite it. Make the questions sound like a real conversation, not a review article, and write everything in American English. Thanks!
