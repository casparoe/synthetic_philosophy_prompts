Act as an intellectual historian of American political thought. In my AP U.S. History class, we studied the debate over whether the United States should annex the Philippines after the Spanish-American War. The basic facts: in 1898 the United States took the Philippines from Spain; after a long national argument, the Senate approved annexation narrowly; and the Philippine-American War that followed lasted until 1902 and was far more brutal than annexation supporters acknowledged. Many supporters said America's motives were exceptional—not imperial, but a unique mission to prepare the islands for liberty and self-government—while opponents, including the Anti-Imperialist League, replied that ruling Filipinos without their consent betrayed the founding commitment to self-government. Analyze this episode through the lens of American exceptionalism, but keep three senses distinct throughout: (1) a historical thesis about America's uniquely different development; (2) a normative claim that America has a special moral role in the world; and (3) an ideology that shapes what people treat as obvious, legitimate, or necessary. Start by explaining how you understand each sense and what each would lead one to expect from the two sides. Then map the episode onto the concept carefully: which side was actually appealing to which sense, what the concept explains well, and where it breaks down or stays silent—for instance, about economic and strategic motives, race, or the conduct of the war. End with a considered verdict on what this episode shows about American exceptionalism: does the case confirm it, refute it, or reveal that the concept was doing different work than either a historical or a moral thesis? Take your time, reason explicitly, and say plainly where the lens has nothing useful to contribute rather than forcing it to explain everything.
