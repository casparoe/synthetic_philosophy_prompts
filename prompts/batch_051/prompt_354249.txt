I'm running a 90-minute session of an upper-level undergrad seminar on concepts and metaphor (14 students, bright but lazy about the readings). We've just read the first couple chapters of Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences, and the claim I want them to actually feel is that analogy is not one cognitive tool among others but the engine of all thought, including perfectly ordinary categorization — putting a new thing under an old label is always an analogical stretch, and the "same" category gets rebuilt differently depending on which old cases launched it. On the page they find this either vacuous or wildly overstated, and I don't want to just lecture at them.

I have a half-baked idea: a sorting game where each small group gets an identical deck of deliberately ambiguous cards (shapes, fills, markings, etc.) and has to extend a category like "glom" from a different starting exemplar in each group, so every group builds a different boundary and then, when they merge, they argue about which objects are "really" glom and discover the boundaries are incommensurable because the category was a different analogy in each case. But I'm not sure that disagreement will reliably happen, or that it won't just look like vague pattern-spotting.

Design the activity properly: exact cards or a printable sheet, group roles, a runnable timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the idea (seeing-as, analogical extension, concept slippage, essences as after-the-fact). Also warn me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the claim the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply by assertion.
