I teach a graduate seminar on the ethics of reproduction, and this year I got tired of poking holes in other peoples views without ever writing down my own, so I made myself produce a view profile on selection and liberal eugenics to show the students. Problem is, now I'm not sure it all coheres. Here it is, roughly:

(1) Procreative beneficence: if a couple is already doing IVF and has several viable embryos, they have a genuine moral reason to implant the one with the best expected life, and in easy cases—one embryo with cystic fibrosis, everything else equal—it's a duty. The reason doesn't only extend to disease; if there's decent evidence about predicted wellbeing or temperament, that counts too.

(2) A hard line against state eugenics: coercion, incentives, or official rankings of good genes are wrong full stop. The line between state eugenics and private parental choice isn't one of degree but of kind, because the historical evil came from state power, not from parents wanting a healthy child.

(3) The expressivist argument against selecting against disability is mostly overstated: a parent who screens out a deaf embryo isn't thereby saying Deaf people's lives are worthless, and the choice can sit alongside full respect for Deaf people and Deaf culture.

(4) Nevertheless I want to say some selections are genuinely wrong, not merely unaesthetic: deliberately selecting for deafness, as some Deaf parents have wanted, when a hearing embryo is available at no extra cost is something I'd object to.

(5) Non-identity: in most embryo-selection cases, whoever is born wouldn't have existed otherwise, so the resulting child isn't made worse off by the choice, assuming a life worth living. Talk of harm to the child is therefore the wrong currency; wrongness has to be impersonal or about the parents' reasons.

(6) A kind of liberal neutrality: the state can't permissibly rank ways of life, but private citizens may legitimately use their own conception of a good life in choosing an embryo, so selecting for a child likely to flourish in the family's way of life is fine.

Act as an expert philosopher of bioethics and audit these commitments. Start by brainstorming, pair by pair, which ones pull against each other, and then seperate the apparent tensions from the real ones. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I'd also like a worry at the end about whether the whole set can be held by one reasonable person without bad faith.
