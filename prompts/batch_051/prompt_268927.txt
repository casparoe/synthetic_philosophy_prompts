I keep returning to a parallel between the benign-violation account of humor and the way the immune system calibrates its responses to possible threats, and I want help deciding whether it is deep or superficial. The thought is roughly this. On one influential account, a joke, a near-miss, or a bit of slapstick is funny only when something is appraised both as a violation — a breach of a norm, a small danger signal of some kind — and as benign, because the context says the danger is not really dangerous. Too much threat and the event is merely unpleasant or traumatic; too little and it is merely pleasant or boring. A friend who works in immunology described allergy immunotherapy to me, and I could not shake the feeling that the structure is similar. The immune system also responds selectively to stimuli marked as both potentially harmful and worth responding to, and it can be trained to suppress a response to a genuine allergen by presenting that allergen in carefully dosed, controlled, increasingly benign conditions until the system stops treating it as a threat. In both cases, a system seems to use a safety signal not simply to switch a response off, but to recalibrate what counts as a threat in the first place.

You are a professional philosopher of cognitive science with enough philosophy of biology to check the other side of the analogy. Please think through this carefully and begin by laying out both mechanisms in enough detail that we are not simply comparing the words "threat" and "safe." Then state the mapping precisely: what would have to be true of humor, and true of immune tolerance, for a result on one side to transfer to the other? Finally, give me the strongest falsification test you can think of: name something the parallel appears to predict — for example about repeated exposure to a joke, desensitization to a whole category of violation, or individual differences in threat sensitivity — that is probably false, and tell me whether its falsity undermines the analogy or only marks its limits.
