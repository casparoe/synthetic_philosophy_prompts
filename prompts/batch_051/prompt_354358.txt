I am a philosophy professor, and I have been turning over a puzzle that started at a long dinner with a colleague in theoretical neuroscience and resurfaced last week in my graduate seminar on immortality. She pressed a disarmingly simple observation on me. If a mind is realized in a substrate with only finitely many total states — or with a finite bound on discrimination, so that finer differences are not differences for the mind itself — then, by something like the pigeonhole principle, an endless life must eventually repeat an exact total mental state, and in any case the number of distinct experiences open to such a mind is at most finite. I find the reasoning compelling, because it has exactly the virtues I want in a theory: it uses few assumptions, makes no appeal to substance dualism, yields a definite conclusion, and generalizes naturally from finite automata to any mind with bounded resolution. It seems to license the claim that for every finite mind there is, in principle, a completed catalogue of possible experiences, and that a sufficiently long life would eventually reach a point after which no moment is of a type the mind had not already occupied.

And yet, as a claim about my own experiential life, I find the conclusion almost impossible to accept. It seems to me that I can always encounter something genuinely new — a landscape seen from an unfamiliar angle, a mathematical idea I had never assembled in that order, a fresh way of hearing a passage I thought I had exhausted. I am not merely reporting an inability to survey the catalogue; I am tempted to say that the space of possible experience is open-ended, so that there is no completed list of all the experiences I could ever have. I can feel the force of both sides, and I do not want a referee who simply announces a winner. I want you to work through what would justify each possible resolution: giving up the intuition as an artifact of our inability to survey our own finite bounds; abandoning or amending the theory because it individuates experience by internal machinery and misses content that is constitutively tied to its objects; or finding a reading on which the finitude of the machinery and the openness of experience are both true at once.

Please act as an expert philosopher specializing in the philosophy of mind and metaphysics. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the strongest formulations of the finite-mind argument and of the intuition of inexhaustibility, including the key distinctions each side can exploit. Then offer your own negotiated verdict, and say explicitly what each side would have to concede under each resolution. Finally, flag any place where my formulation above may already beg the question — for example, by sliding between states, discriminations, and experiences.
