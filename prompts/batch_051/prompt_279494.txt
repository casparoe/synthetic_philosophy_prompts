You are a philosopher of law who specialises in causation and responsibility. Work through the following carefully, beginning by brainstorming the distinctions you will need before you settle on a final assessment.

In most common-law jurisdictions, a bystander who watches a toddler drown in a shallow pond, and who could save the child at trivial cost, commits no crime by standing by. Yet a parent who lets her own child drown in that same pond may be guilty of manslaughter or murder; a lifeguard on duty who does nothing may likewise be liable; and so may someone who carelessly created the danger and then failed to avert it. The identical omission is therefore treated as giving rise to no criminal liability at all in one kind of case, and as a cause of death in another, depending on the defendant's relationship to the victim or to the situation.

What theory of causation by omission does this pattern presuppose, if any? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguishing what the doctrine strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. In particular, consider whether the law is committed to treating omissions as genuine causes, or whether it can get by with a purely counterfactual 'but-for' test plus an independent doctrine of legal duty. Then explain what, if anything, would have to change if the law adopted, instead, (a) the metaphysical view that omissions are never causes because they are absences rather than events, and (b) the moral view that every capable bystander owes a duty of easy rescue. End by saying whether the existing pattern reflects a single consistent view of causation by omission or is philosophically underdetermined.
