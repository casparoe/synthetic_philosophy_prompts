I'm working on a piece about how people who wrote about math before, like, the 1700s understood "one" — not the numeral, but the very idea of a single thing. I keep coming back to Euclid's number theory books, because he opens Book VII with a pair of definitions that strike me as both strangely metaphysical and maybe secretly circular. Here's the passage I mean:

"A unit is that by virtue of which each of the things that exist is called one.
A number is a multitude composed of units."

The context that's got me stuck: Euclid treats the unit and the number as different kinds of thing, so a unit isn't a number, and numbers only start at 2 (or at least at a collection of units). That already feels alien to a modern reader — we treat 1 as a number, and "unit" as just a name for a chosen size or quantity. But what I can't decide is whether the first definition is doing real work or just punting. It defines a unit by appeal to the fact that each existing thing can be "called one," which seems to smuggle in exactly the idea it's supposed to define: being one or being a unit. And then the second definition immediately makes a number depend on units, so I can't tell if Euclid is defining counting from a base concept of oneness, or whether he's assuming we already know how to count and just dressing it up as definitions.

Could you help me parse what the relationship between "unit," "one," and "number" is actually supposed to be here — is the unit/one distinction just an artifact of how Greek mathematics was done (for instance because one was considered indivisible or not a "multitude"), or does it reveal something deeper about what a number has to be? And in the first definition, what's the "by virtue of which" doing — is that meant as a definition, a metaphysical claim about existing things, or just a remark about how we talk? I'd love an explanation that doesn't assume I already know the modern answer, cause right now the passage feels like it's defining the unit in terms of itself and then building all of arithmetic on top of it.
