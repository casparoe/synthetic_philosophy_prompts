In a graduate epistemology seminar, we have been wrestling with G. E. Moore’s celebrated attempt to prove the existence of an external world by holding up his hands and inferring that at least two material objects exist. Moore’s own conditions for a good proof, as usually reconstructed, are that the premises are known, the conclusion follows from them, and the premises are distinct from the conclusion. The standard skeptical reply is that the premise about his hands cannot be known unless he has already ruled out dreaming, evil-demon, or brain-in-vat scenarios, so the proof begs the question. Moore’s defenders often argue that this merely applies a modus tollens where Moore applies modus ponens and that our ordinary grounds for believing we have hands are far more certain than any skeptical scenario. But a deeper worry is that the argument may be circular in the specific sense of exhibiting a transmission failure: the warrant for the premise already presupposes the very external-world conclusion the proof is meant to establish. A further Wittgenstein-inspired worry is that the assertion that one has hands, on certain occasions, functions not as an ordinary empirical knowledge claim but as a hinge proposition that belongs to the framework within which doubting and justifying make sense.

Write an essay evaluating whether Moore’s argument succeeds as a proof of an external world. Address at least the skeptical-premise objection, the transmission-failure objection, and the hinge-proposition objection. In particular, explain which objection you consider the most damaging and why. Defend a clear verdict: for example, you may conclude that Moore succeeds only under a low-stakes fallibilism, that he manages an anti-skeptical display but not a proof in his own terms, or that one form of the argument survives the objections. Do not present a merely balanced survey; argue for your assessment.
