I have spent years reading scattered arguments about the classic choice between one person subjected to a lifetime of severe torment and an astronomically large finite number of people each receiving a single dust speck in the eye, and I now want the specification before the arguments. Write the desiderata for a theory of how tiny harms aggregate — or fail to aggregate — into something that can outweigh a severe harm. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and separate the non-negotiable constraints from the preferences of particular camps (impartial welfare consequentialism, negative utilitarianism, lexical thresholds, person-affecting views, and so on). Mark which distinctions an adequate theory cannot collapse, such as severity of a harm versus number of instances, or one person receiving many small harms versus many people each receiving one. The deliverable is the list of requirements and the pressure among them; do not spend most of the response surveying named positions.

Assume adequacy is relative to a practical purpose: a decision-maker who can either prevent one very bad outcome or prevent a vast number of small discomforts, now or across the long-run future, needs to know which choice the theory licenses and why. Say, for each desideratum, what breaks if it is dropped.

The part I most want to see is the joint-satisfiability analysis. Are there subsets of the desiderata that cannot all be honored at once? For instance, if one holds both (a) any non-zero harm, repeated sufficiently many times across sufficiently many people, outweighs any finite harm, and (b) there is at least one harm so severe that no number of dust specks may be purchased with it, can both survive in a single theory? If not, map the forced choices rather than silently resolving them.

Finally, give a brief statement of which single requirement does the most to divide defensible theories from the rest, and why.
