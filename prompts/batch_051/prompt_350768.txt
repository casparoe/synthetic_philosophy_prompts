Douglas Hofstadter's short satirical piece "A Person Paper on Purity in Language" (written under the pseudonym William Satire) gets its force from a transposition: it imagines a society otherwise like ours in which the unmarked, generic term for a person is a racial term — the analogue of "man" in compounds like "chairman" — while another racial category is the marked one, so that a neutral reform like "person" strikes the narrator as artificial, clunky, and politically intrusive. By moving the grammar of gender onto race, the essay tries to make readers feel the hidden parochialism of what they had simply experienced as natural usage.
