I am a graduate student in political economy, and I have been asked to write a seminar paper defending a controversial reading of Hume. The reading centers on the thought experiment in Hume’s essay Of Money, from the 1752 Political Discourses, where he has us suppose that four-fifths of all the money in Great Britain vanishes in a single night. His stated conclusion is that all prices and wages would fall in proportion and the country would afterward be no richer or poorer in real goods. I want to defend the stronger thesis that this thought experiment, together with Hume’s discussion in Of the Balance of Trade of money’s natural tendency to distribute itself among nations, commits Hume to the view that a favourable balance of trade is not a legitimate long-run goal of policy: policies aimed at keeping money inside a country are either ineffective in real terms or harmful if they restrict commerce.

Please argue for that stronger reading as convincingly as you can, and do not settle for the safer interpretation that Hume simply wanted to warn against merchants’ superstitious attachment to money. Take a definite position and give reasons for it rather than surveying the alternatives. In particular, address the most serious objection: that Hume himself says, in the same essays, that during the interval before prices adjust, an influx of money can stimulate industry and that a nation can suffer while money drains away. Make the case that these concessions are compatible with the stronger reading rather than a retreat from it, and end by identifying one place where the stronger reading overreads the text.
