I keep returning to the debate over philosophical zombies and the conceivability argument, and I've come to suspect that the standard account of what the two sides disagree about is wrong. Both sides accept the same physics, and no neuroscientific or behavioral result appears able to move anyone. The anti-physicalist side holds that a world physically identical to ours but with no subjective experience is conceivable, and that this conceivability is evidence that such a world is possible, which would refute physicalism. The physicalist side answers with the familiar moves: zombies are not genuinely conceivable once you scrutinize the idea, conceivability does not entail possibility, or the zombie intuition is an artifact of how phenomenal concepts work and carries no modal weight. After decades of this, each side seems to regard the other's reply as missing the point rather than false.

My working theory is that the real disagreement is not about conceivability or modality at all but about what an explanation of consciousness has to accomplish. The anti-physicalist side will only count an account as explaining experience if it lets you see why this particular physical configuration gives rise to this particular feeling rather than another one or none; the physicalist side tends to think that demand is exactly the kind of demand no empirical explanation ever meets, and that nothing is left unexplained when it is not met. On this diagnosis, the zombie argument is the idiom in which a deeper dispute about explanatory standards gets expressed.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a survey or a verdict on the argument itself. Is my hypothesis right? If so, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a worked-out account of what explanation requires, or a change of question? If it is wrong, what is the underlying disagreement, and why has it stayed so stable across decades and across very capable people?
