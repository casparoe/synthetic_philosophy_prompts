Heres one. Nozick's experience machine, standard setup, except one assumption is changed. Suppose the machine's designers can in principle produce only finitely many distinct total experiences, becuase a finite mind can only discriminate finitely many distinct experiential states. Make the number enormous—way more than anyone could get through in a lifetime—and have the machine wipe the relevant memories as it cycles, so every repeat feels completely novel from the inside. You know all of this before you choose whether to plug in.

How does this change the standard argument against plugging in? Go through the usual reasons (wanting to actually do things, wanting to be a certain kind of person, wanting contact with reality rather than a simulation) and say which get stronger or weaker once the menu is known to be finite but subjectively unbounded. Does the hedonist's reply get easier or harder here: is a known-but-undetectable loop worse than an infinite menu, or does the finiteness not matter because real life would be experientially finite too once you fix the mind doing the experiencing?
