I'm a retired seismologist, and forty years of looking at earthquake catalogs may have left me with an overdeveloped fondness for symmetry—if two cases are factually the same, I expect our moral reaction to match. Lately I've been reading moral philosophy and I keep tripping over an asymmetry that wont go away. We treat someone who gives a kidney to a stranger and then gives away most of her income as a hero. Magazines profile her, parishes hold her up, people mention her as proof that humans aren't hopeless. But the moment a moral theory says that isn't above and beyond the call of duty but exactly what duty demands—that you and I are doing something wrong when we keep our second kidney and our savings—people say the theory is absurdly demanding and stop listening. Same act, same facts. Call it supererogatory and it makes a saint; call it obligatory and the theory that says so is a nonstarter. Is there a principle that actually justifies that asymmetry, or is it just a way to feel warm about the hero while keeping the money? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the tight version: the act and all its consequences are identical, and the only thing that changes is the label 'beyond duty' versus 'required.' Then give me a verdict—keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give up one of the two reactions. And because I'm new to this, please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on supererogation and demandingness, and dont let any one camp's view come out sounding like the consensus.
