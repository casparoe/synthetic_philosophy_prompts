Here is a decision-theoretic argument I built while reading about acausal threats — threats whose influence on a target runs through correlation or prediction rather than through any causal channel. I now find its conclusion absurd, though I cannot locate the bad step.

(1) A threat is effective when it ties the probability of a bad outcome to the target's action: resisting sharply raises the probability of the harm compared with complying.
(2) From inside the decision, all the target has to go on are those conditional probabilities — P(harm | comply) and P(harm | resist). Whether the action would cause the harm to come about, or action and harm merely share some common source, makes no difference to the two numbers the decision must use.
(3) So whenever resisting is associated with a higher probability of harm — for whatever reason, and however the association arises — the target has the same kind of reason to comply as under an ordinary threat.
(4) But such action–harm associations can arise with no threatener and no causal path from action to harm. Suppose a genetic variant independently causes both a strong liking for cigarettes and a higher risk of lung disease; the smoking itself does not worsen the disease risk. For such a person, P(lung disease | refrain) is lower than P(lung disease | smoke).
(5) By (3), that genetic variant is a threat: it gives the smoker the same kind of reason to refrain as a blackmailer's ultimatum does.
(6) Yet a bundle of genes is not a threat, and someone who refrains on that basis is not being coerced; he is being pushed around by a correlation. Since (6) is obvious, at least one earlier step must go.

Where does the argument go wrong, and what do I pay for repairing it? I would most like to keep (1) and (2), since they look like plain expected-utility bookkeeping; but I cannot see how to keep them without ending at (5). If the fault lies in (2), (3), or in the slide from reason to threat, say exactly which, why that step looked innocent, and what the main rival positions — choosing by what your action would cause versus choosing by what it would be evidence of — each have to pay to escape the conclusion.
