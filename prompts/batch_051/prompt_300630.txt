ok so in my Kant/early modern class we've been on transcendental arguments and the prof gave us a rough checklist for spotting one. my notes have it as: (a) start from something the skeptic already grants, usually some fact about experience or self-consciousness; (b) show that this starting point is possible only if some further condition C holds; (c) the 'only if' needs to be conceptual or a priori, not just a physical or psychological regularity; (d) conclude that C actually obtains, not just that we must believe it or that it's useful to presuppose it.

a classmate tried to run this as a transcendental argument in a paper:

1. I am conscious of a temporally extended, unified stream of experience — memory, anticipation, and current awareness all belong to a single point of view.
2. A unified stream of that kind is possible only if there is one continuing subject that holds its parts together.
3. So there really is a persisting self.

so does this actually count as a transcendental argument by the checklist above? go criterion by criterion and say where it clearly passes, where it fails, and where the checklist just doesnt settle it. and then say whether you think the example shows the checklist is missing something — like maybe (d) is too strong, or the step from 2 to 3 is doing more work than the form lets on.

whichever way you come out, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning after, no preamble and no restating the question.
