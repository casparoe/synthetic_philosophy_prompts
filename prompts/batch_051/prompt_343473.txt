I work in software, and I keep running into the same argument in discussions of free will, with different packaging each time. The argument is built on the stock example of the digger wasp: a wasp that paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the threshold of her burrow, and then goes in alone to inspect the burrow before pulling the cricket in. If an experimenter moves the cricket a few centimeters away while the wasp is inside, she comes back out, drags the cricket to the threshold again, and goes in to inspect again; she will repeat this loop for as long as the experimenter keeps moving the cricket, never skipping the inspection even though the burrow has already been checked. The behavior looks purposeful, but it turns out to be a rigid trigger-action routine. A colleague of mine calls this kind of rigidity sphexishness and has been using the example to make a broader claim: human beings are the same machinery with more branches. On his view, what we call deliberating, revisiting a decision, or acting freely is just a larger lookup table of trigger-action routines. When an unexpected input changes a variable that the current routine does not read, we too re-run the routine and its side effects instead of recomputing the goal. He concludes that the difference between us and the wasp is one of code size, not kind, and that talk of free will is only a label for the unexamined gaps in that lookup table. I find the example striking, but I suspect the analogy is carrying more of the conclusion than he admits, and I want it audited. Please do the following: build the correspondence table between the wasp situation and human behavior — what plays the role of the stimulus, the routine, the inspection, the side effect, the goal, and the changed variable; identify where the mapping breaks down or quietly imports features the target may not have; separate the breakdowns that actually matter for his conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic; and tell me whether the conclusion survives once the relevant breakdowns are on the table. If a better analogy would do the work more honestly, say what it is.
