I have been reading about Simpson's paradox, and I have come to hold what feels like a strict and principled rule: once an overall comparison reverses inside every subgroup, the aggregate comparison should be abandoned, because it is an artifact of how the groups happen to be mixed together. I accept the arithmetic, and I can reproduce a case myself. Suppose two tutoring centers are being compared. Center A passes a higher percentage of its students overall. But among students who arrive with weak preparation, Center B passes a higher percentage, and among students who arrive with strong preparation, Center B also passes a higher percentage. Center A's overall edge comes entirely from enrolling many more strong-preparation students, who pass at high rates at both centers.

So far this fits my rule: the subgroup comparisons are what matter, and the overall comparison should be discarded. But now suppose I am a student who must choose a center without knowing whether I will arrive with strong or weak preparation. I find that I cannot make myself believe that Center A is not genuinely the better choice for me in that position. The fact that a higher share of its students pass overall feels like a real fact about what happens at Center A, not an illusion that dissolves the moment I inspect subgroups.

I am caught between my principle and my intuition. Is the overall comparison an artifact that should never guide a decision, or does it answer a different but legitimate question from the one the subgroup comparisons answer? Give me the strongest version of each side, and then tell me which I should give up, or how I should restate the principle so that both the rule and the intuition can be true.
