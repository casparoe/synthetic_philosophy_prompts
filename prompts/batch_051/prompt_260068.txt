In a criminal justice class, we discussed two ways the law responds to defendants with serious mental conditions, and I am trying to understand what theory of moral knowledge the difference presupposes. A defendant with schizophrenia who commits a crime while in the grip of a delusion — for example, believing that a stranger is an enemy agent sent to harm him — may be found not guilty by reason of insanity. The idea behind such a verdict need not be that the delusion removed all physical control over his body; it can be that, at the moment of the act, he did not understand the nature of what he was doing or did not understand that it was wrong in the way the law requires. By contrast, a defendant diagnosed as a psychopath — someone who can accurately describe the rules, the harm to the victim, and the likely penalties, but who seems to lack empathy, guilt, and remorse and does not appear to care that the victim is a person — is in most jurisdictions held fully responsible. The diagnosis rarely grounds an insanity defense, and in some sentencing systems a demonstrated lack of remorse is treated as an aggravating factor, pointing to future dangerousness or to a particularly cold character.

These two responses seem to depend on different views of what it is to know that an act is wrong. The delusional defendant is excused, at least in part, for lacking something we might call cognitive: contact with reality, or the ability to apply to his own conduct the concepts that make an act wrong. The psychopathic defendant is punished as if his emotional deficit did not count as ignorance at all, or as if whatever he lacks were irrelevant to blame. What picture of moral knowledge and responsibility does this pattern of legal practice presuppose? In particular, does it commit the law to the view that grasping a moral rule is a matter of being able to state and apply it correctly, independent of empathy or guilt? Or can the same pattern be defended on the view that empathy and guilt are part of genuine moral knowledge, by holding either that the psychopath's ignorance is itself culpable or that criminal responsibility tracks knowledge of the law rather than full moral understanding? And what should we make of the aggravating use of lack of remorse — is the law there treating an emotional impairment, perhaps part of the disorder itself, as if it were a freely chosen attitude, and would that be consistent with the theory implicit in the insanity defense? In answering, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — the points at which several competing accounts of moral knowledge and responsibility would each license this same set of outcomes.
