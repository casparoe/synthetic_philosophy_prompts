I'm taking a class on philosophy of mind and something from it spilled into a weird argument with my brother. My uncle had a small stroke a few months ago, and one of the strangest things is how he talks about pain now. If he burns his hand on the stove, he'll say it stings, he can point to exactly where it is, he even says it's a sharp burning feeling. But he doesn't pull away or wince, and he says it doesn't bother him at all—like the sensation is just information, not anything he wants to stop. He once held his hand under hot water until my brother yanked it away because his skin was turning red.

My brother thinks this proves pain isn't necessarily bad. His line is: if the feeling isn't unpleasant, it isn't pain in the full sense; the badness is what makes pain pain. My intuition is that there's still a real pain there, and its badness is somehow detatched from the sensation, but I can't articulate why that would matter, or which one of us is even making the better point. I realize this is partly a medical thing, but I'm more interested in the conceptual side: is the badness of pain essential to it, or can you have pain that is genuinely pain but not bad to have? And if my uncle really does have pain that isn't bad for him, does that change how we should think about pain in people who do suffer from it?
