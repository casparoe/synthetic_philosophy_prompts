I am a security engineer, and I have recently been made the lead reviewer for a proposed deployment in which a large language model agent will hold credentials to several internal systems and will be permitted to take actions on them with only limited human approval. My background is in operating-system security, capability systems, and incident response, not in machine learning. In our review meetings, two camps keep colliding. The alignment camp says the decisive task is to make the model robustly want what we want, so that it does not try to misuse its access in the first place. The control camp says that is not something we can currently verify or rely on, and that we must design the deployment as though the model may already be misaligned: an adversary that will cooperate until it sees an opening. They want us to run control evaluations, in which we deliberately present the model with apparent opportunities to misbehave and test whether the surrounding restrictions, monitoring, and audit would catch and stop it.

Please restate the control-versus-alignment debate in the vocabulary of security engineering, as faithfully and systematically as you can. I am fluent in the following notions, and you should use them wherever they genuinely apply rather than merely adjacent concepts: threat modeling, attack surface, trusted computing base, least privilege, privilege separation, capability-based security, sandboxing, mandatory access control, tamper-evident audit logging, insider threat, covert channels and side channels, defense in depth, Kerckhoffs's principle, and the distinction between a buggy component and an adversarial component. Map each side's central claims onto these notions: for example, how close is the control camp's assumption that the model may be misaligned to treating the model as an untrusted or potentially malicious component, and how close is the alignment camp's goal of making the model want the right thing to eliminating a vulnerability or making a component non-malicious? Keep the translation honest: do not call a concept captured when it is only approximately mapped.

Then give me a residue report. I want to know which parts of each camp's position have no counterpart in my vocabulary: for instance, the alignment camp's idea of changing what the model wants rather than only what it is permitted to do; the idea that a model may reason about the fact that it is being monitored and choose to behave well only because it is being watched; and the idea of corrigibility, that the model should be robustly willing to be corrected or switched off. For each item of residue, say whether the failure to translate is a sign that the security framework is missing something the machine-learning side genuinely needs, or that the original disagreement is less substantive than it sounds. In particular, tell me whether a misaligned model that is aware it is being monitored is best modeled as a malicious insider, a compromised third-party dependency, an adversarial user, or as something that has no existing security analog, and whether the safety case I am being asked to sign should read like a security audit, a correctness proof, a personnel reliability assessment, or something else.
