I am a novelist writing a thought experiment in which a historian of antiquity is forced to confront the modern argument that, for the very distant past, the strongest evidence comes from material and environmental sources—archaeology, settlement patterns, soil science, genetics—rather than from literary tradition. I want Thucydides to be the test case, and I want his own words to constrain the prediction. This is the passage I am using, from Thucydides, *History of the Peloponnesian War*, in Richard Crawley's translation, from the section often called the Archaeology:

"The richest soils were always most subject to this change of masters; such as the district now called Thessaly, Boeotia, most of the Peloponnese, Arcadia excepted, and the most fertile parts of the rest of Hellas. The goodness of the land favoured the aggrandizement of particular individuals, and thus created faction which proved a fertile source of ruin. It also invited invasion. Accordingly Attica, from the poverty of its soil enjoying from a very remote period freedom from faction, never changed its inhabitants."

Taking on the role of a professional historian of ancient historiography, predict how Thucydides, reasoning from the commitments displayed in this passage, would respond to the modern debate about whether material evidence should outrank textual testimony when they conflict about the deep past. Would he greet the modern programme as an ally, a correction, or a rival to his own inferences? Where would he accept its conclusions, and where would he resist them?
