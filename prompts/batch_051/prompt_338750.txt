I run a philosophy club at my high school; we have about fifteen members and meet for one hour after school, and we try to do a hands-on exercise rather than listen to me explain a topic. Lately we have been discussing self-amending rule systems: sets of rules that include procedures for changing the rules, where the rules about amendment can themselves be amended, and the strange situations that arise when the rule for changing rules is changed by following that very rule. I want to run a session in which the members play a short game with a simple written constitution, so that at some point in the game they find themselves facing one of these paradoxes as a live decision rather than as an abstract problem.

The specific phenomenon I want them to feel is the paradox of self-amendment. Suppose the game's constitution says that rules may be changed by a two-thirds vote, and that this amendment rule may itself be changed like any other rule. If the group then amends the amendment rule to require a unanimous vote, the change looks legal under the old rule, but the new rule seems to make its own adoption illegal by its own standard. I would like the activity to be designed so that the players, through their own play, are led to attempt something like this—perhaps because they want to lock in a rule that benefits them—and so that the group then has to decide, with no higher rule to appeal to, whether the change counts. Please provide the initial rules and materials, a timeline for the hour, instructions for how the facilitator should run the game without announcing the point in advance, a debrief that maps what happened onto the relevant ideas (legal validity versus what the players actually accept, the moment of self-amendment, and the absence of an external umpire), a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and an honest note on which aspect of the paradox a classroom game cannot reproduce and must instead be supplied more abstractly during the debrief.
