I organize a small movie discussion group, and we're doing Terminator 2: Judgment Day next month. I don't have a background in philosophy, but I've been working through an idea on my own from one part of Alan Turing's 1950 paper on machine intelligence—not the imitation-game test, but the argument near the end of the paper. Turing suggests there that trying to design an adult-level thinking machine all at once is the wrong approach, and that the better path is to build something closer to a child's mind and then educate it, more or less the way one would teach a child, so the machine acquires its abilities by learning rather than having them installed finished. I'd like to read the movie through that idea. The film seems to stage something like it: the T-800 arrives as a powerful machine whose responses are set in advance, and partway through Sarah and John switch its processor from a fixed condition to one that lets it learn and change, after which John teaches it a lot of small human things—casually spoken phrases, why it shouldn't kill someone just because it would be convenient, what a human life is worth, and eventually something like how to say goodbye. That looks like the movie dramatizing Turing's point that the machine becomes something more responsible, maybe something closer to a person, only because it is taught inside a relationship rather than given finished rules ahead of time. But I can't tell how far the movie is actually agreeing with Turing, and where it is quietly depending on things Turing's account doesn't supply: the Terminator's initial programming and goals, its built-in capacity to learn in the first place, or its apparent concern for John. I'd like a critical reading that takes both the film and the child-machine idea seriously and is faithful in both directions. Point me to the specific scenes that genuinely illustrate Turing's claim; say where the film complicates the idea or leans on something else; and end with what the film adds to the philosophical picture that Turing's paper by itself doesn't show, if anything. I'll be leading a discussion with people who know the movie well but haven't read Turing, so write for that audience. Also, tell me how confident you are in the overall reading, and single out the one interpretive point or scene-reading you're least sure about.
