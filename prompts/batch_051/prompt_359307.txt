(2) But I don't think the sadness is anyone's actual occurrent feeling. Not the composer's, not the performer's, not the audience's. You can have a sad piece with no one in the room feeling sad.
(3) I also think emotions are at least partly intentional: sadness is typically about or directed at something — you're sad about a loss, or that someone left. A state with no object at all is a free-floating mood, not the full emotion.
(4) Yet instrumental music doesn't seem to be about anything in that sense. A sad adagio isn't sad about anything; there's no object you could point to as what the music is sad about.
(5) For why it sounds sad, I lean on the resemblance story: slow tempo, low register, descending contours, soft dynamics — these features resemble the way sad people speak and move, and that resemblance does the perceptual work.
(6) Finally, I don't think you have to feel sad to correctly hear the music as sad. A completely unmoved listener can still hear the sadness, and a performer can produce a sad-sounding phrase while feeling fine, the way an actor can act grief without grieving.
