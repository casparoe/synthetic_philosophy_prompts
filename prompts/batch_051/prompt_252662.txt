I've been working through the start of Book VII of Euclid's Elements, and I keep coming back to the first two definitions: "A unit is that by virtue of which each of the things that exist is called one. A number is a multitude composed of units." I've basically come around to the view that this is right about what a unit is: units aren't numbers, they're prior to number—the one in virtue of which something gets counted as one, not themselves something counted. I want to know what would count against this view, and I mean really against it, not just the standard modern story. Which intuitions, cases, and formal results does it stand or fall with: zero, negative numbers, fractions, the imaginry unit being called a unit, singleton sets, counting a lone object, measurement? Tell me which of these a defender of the Euclid-style view would concede as counting against them, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Is the view well insulated, and if so is that a problem?
