After years of reading rationalist blogs and forums, and then watching a metrics redesign go sideways at work, I've been collecting my own verdicts about Goodhart's law — roughly, the idea that a measure stops being a good measure once it is used as a target. I'd like to know what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to.

A factory pays workers per unit assembled; workers start skipping quality checks and assembling sloppily. I say the per-unit count stopped tracking what management actually wanted, quality-adjusted output, once it became the target.

A video platform ranks recommendations by predicted watch time; the feed fills with outrage and cliffhanger clickbait, and watch time rises while user satisfaction falls. I say the metric corrupted.

A hospital evaluates surgeons by raw thirty-day mortality rate; surgeons quietly decline high-risk patients. I say the metric corrupted, though by changing the patient pool rather than by faking numbers.

A call center targets average call-handling time; agents rush customers and sometimes disconnect to reset the clock. I say the metric corrupted.

A school pays students per book read, verified by a short quiz; students shift to short, easy books. I lean toward saying the metric corrupted, but with less confidence, since reading volume did go up.

A business makes profit its central target; it cuts quality and customer service to hit quarterly numbers and then loses customers over time. I do not think this is a Goodhart case at all — profit was the actual objective, not a proxy — so the failure was a bad strategy rather than metric corruption.

A marathoner targets her finish time; she improves her training, nutrition, and pacing, and the time drops. I say the metric did not corrupt, because the measure is nearly identical to what she cares about.

My rough rule is that a measure corrupts when it is only a proxy for the real target and the agent has a cheaper way to move the proxy than the target. But that rule does not explain why the marathoner's finish time survives (it is, after all, a proxy for running ability), and it tells me nothing about a measure that directly measures one value while proxying another. Is there one principle that captures all seven verdicts? If a single principle forces me to overturn one of them, which should go and why? And what does your principle predict for the case I cannot settle: a university department whose ranking includes the employment rate of its graduates, and which responds by preferentially admitting applicants who already have job offers?
