So last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether a community's collective memory of an event should be trusted over the written historical record when the two flatly contradict each other. I wanted to go case by case—sometimes the archive is what's been sanitized, sometimes a community's memory has itself been reshaped by later politics. But my friend kept saying "history is written by the victors" like that settled it, and concluded that because the official record is produced by whoever holds power, we should always side with the community's memory.

I feel like that one-liner is doing way more work than it can bear, but I couldn't articulate exactly how to push back. Can you seperate out the different precise claims "history is written by the victors" could be making—about who gets to produce records, whose interests those records reflect, how complete or neutral they are, what the absence of a record proves, whether any non-power-serving account is even possible—and say for each whether it's trivially true, substantive and defensible, or outright false? Then tell me which precise claim my friend actually needs to get from that slogan to "trust the community's memory over the record," and whether any reading genuinely supports that inference. And just to twist it a little: what should the slogan say about a case where the community's memory has itself become the official story, say after a revolution—does it stop cutting against the record there?
