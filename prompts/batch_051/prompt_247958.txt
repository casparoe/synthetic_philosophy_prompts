I'm a philosophy PhD student working on moral heuristics, and I've hit the point where every paper on the golden rule makes me want to tear my hair out. Half of them defend the positive version (treat others how you'd want to be treated), half defend the negative version (don't do to someone what you'd hate having done to you), and people slide between "it's a decision procedure" and "it's just a heuristic" whenever it's convenient. Before I read one more attempt to fix or bury the thing, I want the spec: what would an adequate account of the golden rule actually have to get right?

Write the desiderata. For each one, give the case or consideration that forces it. Cover at least these: the masochist, the judge who has to sentence someone, the person who wants to be left alone when you'd want company, the abusive partner who wants to know where his fleeing partner is, and the fact that the rule shows up across traditions (Confucian shu, Hillel's negative version, the gospels, Kantian universalizability) but cashes out differently in each. Dont just list cases — say which distinctions the theory can't collapse: positive vs negative formulation; treating someone as you'd want to be treated vs as they'd want vs as they should want; a criterion of rightness vs a developmental rule of thumb. Say too which background facts it can't bulldoze.

Then score the main contenders against the list, briefly: the literal preference-projection reading, the platinum-rule fix, the Kantian universalizability reading, and the view that the golden rule is just a handy summary of impartiality rather than an axiom. Flag any pair of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied. And put the one-sentence answer to this question first, before anything else: which single requirement knocks out the most popular readings? That's the part I actually need.

Hard cap: 800 words. A table for the desiderata is fine, but the killer-requirement verdict has to be the first sentence.
