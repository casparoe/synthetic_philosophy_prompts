I have been arguing with a friend about the lies adults tell children, and I have written down my own reactions to a series of cases in the hope that a single principle is hiding behind them. I would like you to act as a professional moral philosopher working on deception and trust, and to treat my verdicts as data: begin by brainstorming several candidate principles, then test them against each case before settling on the strongest one.

Here are the cases. (1) Each Christmas I tell my five-year-old that Santa comes down the chimney and leaves the presents, and I go to some trouble to keep the story going. Verdict: fine. (2) I put money under her pillow and let her believe the tooth fairy took the tooth. Fine. (3) When she asks directly, at age nine, whether Santa is real, I tell her the truth. Fine, and I think it would be wrong to keep insisting. (4) After my mother died, I told her that Grandma is watching over her from heaven, though I do not believe it. Not fine, and I regret having said it. (5) Before a vaccination, I told her the needle would not hurt. Not fine. (6) I tell her that her drawing is the most beautiful thing I have ever seen, when it plainly is not. Fine. (7) Her father is mostly absent and rarely calls, but I tell her he is just very busy and loves her very much. Not fine. (8) The case I cannot settle: after our old dog was put down, I told her the dog had gone to live on a farm with a big yard. I keep going back and forth on it.

My rough rule is that the fine cases are shared make-believe that the child will eventually be let in on, while the bad cases are private falsehoods that distort how the child understands a real relationship or her own body. But I already see problems: the heaven remark is repeated by the rest of the family, so it is 'shared' in some sense, and Santa is not merely make-believe to a five-year-old but a belief about how the world works. Please give me the best principle you can, stated precisely; tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which it should lead me to revise; and say what it implies both for the dog case and for one further case I have been avoiding: telling a child that a parent died in an accident when it was suicide. I do not need a survey of the literature, just a careful reconstruction and test of the principle. Please end your reply with a single-sentence summary on its own line, beginning with 'Summary:'.
