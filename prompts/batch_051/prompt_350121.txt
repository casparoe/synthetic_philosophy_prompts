I'm putting together a little thinking file for myself on something that's been bugging me since I retired and started reading philosophy full-time: whether an omission—like a gardener's failure to water the plant—can genuinely be a cause. I came to this from physics, where I always thought of causes as pushes and fields and conserved quantities sloshing around, so the idea that a nothing could cause a something took some getting used to.

Here are the standard positions or arguments I've collected so far, and I don't need these repeated:

1. The counterfactual dependence view: the omission caused the outcome just in case the outcome depended counterfactually on it; had the person done the omitted act, the outcome would not have happened. On this view omissions can be causes, full stop.

2. The physical production view (I believe Dowe is the main name here): causation requires a physical process transferring conserved quantities, and since an omission is an absence there is no process there to transfer anything, so omissions cannot literally cause; at best we cite them in explanations.

3. The norm or salience view (Hart and Honoré are the names that kept coming up): omissions only get selected as causes against a background of what was normal or required. Causation by omission is therefore relative to a norm, not a raw feature of the world.

4. Ned Hall's two-concepts resolution: omissions are causes in the dependence sense but not in the production sense, so the whole dispute dissolves once we sort out which concept of causation we are using.

5. The structural equations or causal modeling approach (Halpern and Pearl): you model the omission as just one value of a variable, say gardener-waters = 0, and then the usual actual-causation machinery gives you a verdict. The absence is not a spooky negative event; it is a value assignment.

I want one more position or argument that is not on this list and not just a restatement, special case, or combination of anything above. Ideally I want something that handles the too-many-omissions explosion—my failing to water the plant is also my failing to water it at 7:05, my failing to water it on Tuesday, my failing to fly to the moon, and so on—without simply appealing to norms or salience as the selector, if any such thing exists. Then tell me honestly whether it's any good, and how it sits alongside the five views above.

Please think like a professional philosopher who works on causation and absences, and start with some braindstorming before you settle on your final answer.
