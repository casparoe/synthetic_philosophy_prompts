"Lay out the Downsian 'paradox of voting': why does the expected-utility calculus of voting seem to imply that almost no one should vote? Then explain how Riker and Ordeshook's 'duty' term was meant to resolve it, and say whether you think it actually resolves the paradox or merely relabels it."

Heres the student's answer (typed up as written):

"The paradox of voting arises because the expected instrumental benefit of voting is p times B, where p is the probability that one's ballot is decisive and B is the utility difference between one's preferred candidate winning and the other winning. Since p is vanishingly small in any large electorate, pB will be tiny. Once we subtract the cost C of getting to the polls, the expected utility pB − C is negative for nearly everyone, which is why the Downsian model seems to imply that rational citizens should abstain. Yet millions turn out. Riker and Ordeshook fixed this by adding a D term for civic duty and expressive satisfaction, so the calculus becomes pB + D − C. This dissolves the paradox, because it shows that voting can be perfectly rational once the duty payoff is included. One caveat is that this only works if pB stays positive, so that the duty term has something to work on; but p is never literally zero in a finite electorate, so the model is saved without giving up rational choice."
