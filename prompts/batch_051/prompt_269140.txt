I cover physics and occasionally economics for a general-interest magazine, so a steady part of my job is deciding when a clever, counterintuitive theory deserves to be presented as the likely truth and when it shoud stay filed under "provocative but probably wrong." Over the years I've settled into a personal rule. A theory is allowed to overrule my own firm intuitions, and the firm intuitions of the researchers I interview, only if it has passed what I privately call the receipt test: it needs at least two surprising, specific predictions that later came out right, where its main rival would not have predicted the same thing. Merely explaining the known facts after the fact, no matter how elegantly, doesn't earn the right to boss my gut around. But once a theory has passed the receipt test, I become—my editor's word—annoying. I keep treating its implications as the responsible working view even when they contradict a strong intuition I can't shake, and even when a majority of people I trust are telling me I've gotten overattached to a pretty story.

Lately I've started to worry that this is less a principled epistemology than a personality trait: I like being the person who didn't fall for common sense, and I suspect the receipt test is flexible enough that I can bless whatever theory I want. So I'm curious how defensible this actually is. Is there a real, principled threshold at which verified surprising predictions should outweigh strong and widely shared intuition? And how would I catch myself, from the inside, when I'm applying the rule in bad faith—for instance by deciding only after the fact that a prediction was surprising enough, or by giving the theory credit for predictions that were vaguer than I let on? Are there domains where even passing the receipt test shouldn't let a theory override a strong intuition?
