Suppose that a genuine agreement—in the normative sense relevant to whether someone is actually bound to honor terms, not merely legally exposed to them—requires all four of the following:

(1) Assent: the person performs an act whose conventional meaning is acceptance of the terms, and does so without the usual vitiating conditions such as force or fraud.
(2) Comprehension: the person has, or could reasonably have acquired, a working grasp of the central content of the terms: what she gives, what she receives, and the main obligations and risks she takes on.
(3) Agency: the acceptance is attributable to her as an exercise of her own judgment, rather than being extracted through manipulation, exploitation of predictable inattention, or the absence of any acceptable alternative.
(4) Coverage: the terms to which she is later held do not substantially outrun the terms she could plausibly be said to have accepted.

Now consider a standard clickwrap case. A person signs up for a messaging service she needs to stay in contact with family, coworkers, and friends, nearly all of whom use only that service. To proceed, she must click a box labeled "I agree" next to a link to terms running to many thousands of words. She reads the opening screen and understands that the service is free in exchange for targeted advertising and that disputes go to arbitration. She does not read further. Buried in the terms are two clauses she would find surprising: one waives her right to join a class action, and another lets the company change material terms on thirty days' notice, with continued use counting as acceptance of the changes. She clicks "I agree" and begins using the service.

Does this person genuinely agree to the terms, under the criteria above? Go through the four criteria one by one, and for each say whether it is clearly satisfied, clearly not satisfied, or genuinely borderline. Where a criterion gives a borderline result, say whether the difficulty lies in the case or in the criterion itself. Then say whether the criteria should be revised in light of this case, and if so, how. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material—for example, be explicit about which claims about how courts treat clickwrap are settled and which are contested—and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
