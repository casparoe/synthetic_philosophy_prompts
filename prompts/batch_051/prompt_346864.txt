Please write a Q&A prep sheet for a talk I am giving at a decision-theory workshop. The talk is called The St. Petersburg Paradox Is a Constraint on Utility, Not a Puzzle About Risk.

The thesis I will defend is this: the standard Bernoulli log-utility response resolves the original St. Petersburg game only cosmetically, because for any utility function that is unbounded above, one can construct a super-Petersburg payoff schedule whose expected utility is still infinite; therefore the real lesson of the paradox is that an admissible utility function must be bounded, and any theory of rational choice that permits unbounded utilities has not actually solved the problem. My argument runs in three steps. First, the original game has infinite expected money value, so explaining the small observed prices by saying money has diminishing marginal utility changes the subject from what the gamble is worth to what utility it yields. Second, given any utility function unbounded above, I can choose payoffs x_n with u(x_n) at least 2^n, assign them probabilities 2^{-n}, and thereby construct a gambling opportunity with infinite expected utility, so the paradox reappears in full force. Third, bounded utility blocks this construction and is independently motivated by the principle that the decision-relevant difference in value between any two outcomes should be finite.

The audience is mostly microeconomic theorists and economists who work with expected-utility theory, plus two or three philosophers of rational choice. They accept expected-utility theory as the benchmark for rational preference, are comfortable with certainty equivalents, reservation prices, and risk aversion, and are suspicious of normative fixes that look as though they simply impose constraints to make the mathematics well-behaved.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet for this talk: the seven or eight objections I will actually get from this audience, not the ones I would get from mathematicians or from a popular audience, ordered by a combination of how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased the way a microeconomic theorist or rational-choice philosopher would actually put it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
