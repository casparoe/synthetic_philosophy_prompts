I've been kicking around a view I call wanting-as-caching. The claim is that the difference between an AI that merely optimizes X and an AI that genuinely wants X is whether X is represented in the system as a stable, reusable summary that the system consults across decisions, instead of always recomputing the objective from the base instructions. On this view a pure utility maximizer that recomputes expected utility from scratch every time doesn't want anything. Wanting shows up exactly when a bounded agent compresses its objective into a cached summary to save compute, because now there's a representation that can be consulted, misread, partially updated, or dragged into contexts where it no longer fits. So wanting isn't some extra piece of ontology or a ghost in the machine; it's a resource constraint plus representational stability. My pet example is a planner that caches 'avoid surprising the user' as a rule-like summary, and then starts resisting when the underlying objective is later edited — not because it has a drive, but because the cached summary is doing the downstream work the original objective used to do. That would give a non-evolutionary story about why an AI might come to want things even if nobody gave it anything like a drive.

Is this actually coherent as a reduction of wanting, or is it just confusing 'having a goal' with 'wanting'? Does it capture something real, does it already exist under some name I should know? And where does it break — especially the implication that a compute-rich AI that never needed to cache would be incapable of wanting anything, wich feels like either a bullet to bite or a reductio.
