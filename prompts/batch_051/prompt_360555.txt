I recently read Susan Wolf's essay on moral saints for a philosophy discussion group, and I keep thinking about whether her argument still works. Wolf argues that a person whose every action is as morally good as possible would be so single-mindedly devoted to morality that they would lose the non-moral interests, humor, and personality that make a human life desirable, so moral sainthood is not an ideal we should aim for. The argument seems to assume that maximal moral goodness has to be realized by a particular sort of saintly character who pursues moral concerns directly, hour by hour. But a modern effective altruist might object that we can now pursue something close to maximal moral impact indirectly—through career choice, automatic donations, and institutional participation—while keeping much of the texture of ordinary life intact. My question is whether Wolf's argument survives that development. Which premise of the argument does the indirect, institutionalized pursuit of moral impact actually put pressure on, and is there a repaired version of the argument that still says something important about the moral-saint ideal—or does the argument only succeed against a dated picture of what doing good looks like?
