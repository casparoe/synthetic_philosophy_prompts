I spent most of my career as a physical chemist, and lately I've been reading philosophy of action and decision theory. Something keeps nagging me and I can't tell if its a real insight or just pattern-matching. In the lab I did a fair amount of feedback control—locking a laser to a reference, stabilizing a temperature bath, that sort of thing. The standard distinction there is between open-loop control, where you compute the whole control trajectory ahead of time and then just execute it, and closed-loop control, where you only ever decide the next small move from the current error signal. Open-loop works beautifully when your model of the system is accurate and disturbances are small; it falls apart when the model drifts or something unexpected kicks the system. Closed-loop is robust to that, but it can hunt, oscillate, or chase noise, and it's only as good as the error signal you can measure in the moment.

This maps onto the planning-versus-improvisation debate almost too neatly. A plan looks like an open-loop trajectory: you work out the steps in advance and then execute. Improvisation looks like closed-loop control: you read the situation as it unfolds and make the next move from where you actually are, not where the plan says you should be. And the engineering intuition even seems to say that planning is overrated in exactly the conditions where feedback shines—unpredictable environment, decent real-time information, small cost of mid-course correction.

But I've been around enough to suspect the analogy hides as much as it reveals. A person with a plan isn't just running a stored trajectory; the plan changes the agent, not only the action sequence. It can make commitments credible, shape what the person attends to, or keep a long-horizon goal in view when local feedback would steer toward whatever feels urgent. A thermostat has nothing like that. Also, human improvisation seems to rest on a huge amount of prior preparation—a jazz musician isn't responding from nothing; there's a repertoire, a harmonic grammar, years of training. That's not at all like a bare feedback loop.

So here's what I'd like: an essay-style answer on whether this parallel is deep or superficial. State the mapping precisely, say what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer from control theory to human action, and name at least one thing the parallel seems to predict that is false, or one non-obvious thing it gets right. And since I keep going back and forth, please also tell me what would change your mind: which consideration would flip your verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
