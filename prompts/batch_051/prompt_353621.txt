I've been working through a proof—often called a triviality result for indicative conditionals—that was shown to me in a seminar. The proof claims that on one natural reading of the idea that the probability of a conditional is the corresponding conditional probability, no substantive credence function could satisfy that idea. I want to know exactly where, if anywhere, the proof goes wrong.

Let P be any rational credence function obeying the usual probability axioms, and suppose the following thesis holds for every rational credence function, not only for P as it currently is: for any propositions A and C with P(A) > 0, the probability of the indicative conditional "if A then C" equals P(C|A). Now fix A and C such that P(A∧C) > 0 and P(A∧¬C) > 0. The argument runs as follows.

(1) P(if A then C) = P(C|A).
(2) By the law of total probability, P(if A then C) = P(if A then C | C)·P(C) + P(if A then C | ¬C)·P(¬C).
(3) The term P(if A then C | C) is the probability the thesis assigns to the conditional after conditionalizing on C; since the thesis was assumed to hold for every rational credence function, it applies to P(·|C) and gives P(C | A∧C), which equals 1.
(4) Likewise, P(if A then C | ¬C) = P(C | A∧¬C), which equals 0.
(5) Therefore P(if A then C) = 1·P(C) + 0·P(¬C) = P(C).
(6) Combining (1) and (5), P(C|A) = P(C), so A and C are probabilistically independent.
(7) Since A and C were arbitrary except for the two overlap conditions, this independence is forced for all such pairs; it follows, or so the proof claims, that P can assign positive probability to at most two mutually incompatible propositions—a triviality result that is absurd for any credence function over a realistically rich set of possibilities.

One friend says the real problem is (2), because if conditionals do not express propositions you cannot write P(if A then C | C) at all; another says (3) is the culprit, because the thesis was only ever meant to constrain a single credence function rather than every credence function obtained from it by conditionalizing. I do not see why either objection should matter. Is the argument valid as it stands? If not, give the exact step that fails and the most natural reading of the thesis on which it is blocked. If it is valid, say which premise I should abandon, and whether restricting the thesis to my current credence function would actually avoid the triviality.
