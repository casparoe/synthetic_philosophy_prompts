Consider this passage from Avicenna's On the Soul (De Anima): "One of us must suppose that he was just created at a stroke, fully developed and perfectly formed but with his vision shrouded from perceiving all external objects – created floating in the air or in the space, not buffeted by any perceptible current of the air that supports him, his limbs separated and kept out of contact with one another, so that they do not feel each other. Then let the subject consider whether he would affirm the existence of his self. There is no doubt that he would affirm his own existence, although not affirming the reality of any of his limbs or inner organs, his bowels, or heart or brain or any external thing. Indeed he would affirm the existence of this self of his while not affirming that it had any length, breadth or depth. And if it were possible for him in such a state to imagine a hand or any other organ, he would not imagine it to be a part of himself or a condition of his existence."

A modern sensory-deprivation study asks subjects to float in a sealed, lightless, soundproof tank of skin-temperature salt water, with their limbs supported apart so they cannot touch one another. Several subjects report that, far from experiencing a bare "I" aware of itself without bodily parts, they gradually lose any bounded sense of self: their limbs feel as though they are no longer theirs, the boundary between body and water blurs, and they describe the experience as one of losing the self rather than finding it. Does this empirical result refute Avicenna's floating man argument, or does it miss the force of the thought experiment? Explain what Avicenna should say about these subjects, and whether his conclusion about the self requires the imagined state to be psychologically realizable or only intellectually conceivable.
