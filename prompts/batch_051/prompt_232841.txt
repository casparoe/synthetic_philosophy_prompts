I'm a retired physical chemist, and I've spent the last few months reading around in research ethics. The debate that's currently eating my free time is clinical equipoise in randomized trials. As I understand it, one camp says a randomized trial is ethical only when there's genuine, honest disagreement in the expert medical community about which arm is better — so that no enrolled patient is being denied a treatment their own doctor would confidently prefer. The other camp says that's the wrong standard: a physician can have a firm personal preference and still ethically randomize patients, because what does the moral work is informed consent, adequate risk/benefit review, safety monitoring, and the social value of answering the question, not some community-level uncertainty. This second camp also likes to point out that clinical equipoise has awkward implications once the accumulating data start to favor one arm, since the expert community usually doesn't form a consensus the moment the data tip.

I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher who works in research ethics and has watched these two camps argue past eachother for years. Please think carefully, and maybe brainstorm the possible cruxes before drafting.

Write the document these two camps should have produced after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want it to include:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can sign. These should be substantive — about what enrolling someone in a trial involves, what informed consent can and cannot do, what obligations investigators have, how individual equipoise (a single clinician's uncertainty) differs from clinical equipoise (disagreement across the community of experts), and what both sides think about continuing a trial after the data start to lean. Don't make them so watered down they become empty.

2. The precise remaining disagreements, each formulated in wording that its own side would accept — no strawmanning. If the two sides use "equipoise" or "therapeutic obligation" in different ways, flag that.

3. A disagreements table, with columns for: the crux (a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side); a concrete case or type of evidence both sides agree bears on it; and each side's prediction written down in advance.

4. An honest note on which parts of the dispute are merely verbal and which are substantive.

The two positions are roughly (a) clinical equipoise is a necessary condition for the ethics of randomized trials, and (b) clinical equipoise is neither necessary nor the right lens. You can tighten each formulation, but don't decide which is right.

Please make sure the statement confronts at least these three pressure points: a trial where the data begin to tip toward one arm before the monitoring board stops it; a clinician who personally prefers drug A but is randomizing patients between A and B; and a trial run by researchers who are not the patient's treating physician. Also, where the two camps predict different outcomes from the same case or evidence, spell out what each side would stake on it.
