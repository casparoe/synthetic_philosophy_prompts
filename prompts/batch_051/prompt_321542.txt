I've been trying to force myself into taking a side in the debate over whether love answers to value already present in the beloved or, instead, creates the value it seems to find, and I keep ending up with an argument that refutes both options at once. That result feels too tidy, so I suspect I've built a trick into it. Please go through the argument and assess it properly. First, say whether the argument is valid. Second, if it is invalid, identify the first premise or inference at which it fails and explain exactly what goes wrong; if it is valid, identify the most rejectable premise and say why. Third, distinguish two readings of the beloved's value—value in particular qualities versus value in the person as such—and say which of the three main positions is best placed to block which step: the view that loving concern confers value (often associated with Frankfurt), the view that love appraises a value already there in the beloved (Velleman), or the view that love is justified by the relationship rather than by the beloved's qualities (Kolodny).

(1) If love is an appraisal, then loving someone is a response to value the lover detects in the beloved, and the love is correct only if that value is really there.

(2) Whatever value a person has independently of being loved resides in their qualities—traits, virtues, and features—and value of this kind is in principle gradable and comparable across persons.

(3) If (1) and (2) hold, then whenever someone else instantiates the relevant qualities to a higher degree, the lover has a pro tanto reason, and absent defeating considerations a rational requirement, to transfer their love to the better instantiation.

(4) But love does not work like that: a lover who trades up as soon as a more impressive instance of the same qualities appears is not the paradigm of a good lover. The attachment we call love treats the beloved as irreplaceable.

(5) Therefore, love is not an appraisal of the beloved's value.

(6) Since love is not appraisal, it follows that love must be bestowal: the beloved's value to the lover is conferred by the lover's caring, not discovered by it.

(7) If love is bestowal, then no love can be answerable to a standard external to the love itself; in particular, it cannot be mistaken about its object.

(8) But love can be mistaken about its object: a person can love a false image of someone, and we criticize that love as unfitting rather than merely unfortunate.

(9) Therefore bestowal is also false, and the appraisal/bestowal distinction collapses into a dilemma with no acceptable horn.

Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails—and would replacing "qualities" in premise (2) with "the beloved's personhood" save the argument, or merely relocate the problem?
