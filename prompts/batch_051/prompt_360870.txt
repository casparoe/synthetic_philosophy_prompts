I cover the science of mental health for a general-interest magazine, and I keep returning to Ian Hacking's looping effects because they explain something my reporting brushes against repeatedly: a diagnostic category is introduced, people come to understand themselves through it, and the category changes as a result. I want a workshop that makes this mechanism visceral rather than merely explained.

The setting: 16 to 20 early-career journalists who cover health, science, and social policy, all of whom have read a short plain-language summary of the idea beforehand. We will have 90 minutes. I want a complete runnable activity and a debrief script, with exact instructions, materials, and timings; someone should be able to run it from your description alone. The controlling constraint is that the idea must emerge from what the participants do rather than from what I say. I should not name or explain the looping effect until the end.

The activity should create a real sorting of participants into a small number of invented kinds from observed behavior or a fast performance task, not from preassigned roles—and it must not invoke any real psychiatric diagnosis or have participants role-play mental illness. The invented classification must carry real consequences within the exercise, through scoring, tasks, access, or status, so that being sorted matters. Once everyone knows how they have been classified, the same observations or tasks should be repeated. Participants should find that their awareness of the label has altered their behavior, and that the criteria now sort them differently than before: the kind has moved under them. A mid-session rule change or re-scoring, announced in response to what the facilitator sees, would be one way to make the instability of the classification itself visible rather than assumed.

In the debrief I need a script that walks the group, in their own words, from what happened to these distinctions: indifferent kinds versus interactive kinds; the loop from classification, to self-understanding, to changed behavior, to revised classification; how this differs from a self-fulfilling prophecy or plain stereotype threat; and why a human kind is a moving target that institutions must hold in place rather than a fixed fact discovered from outside. Tell me what will most likely go wrong when this is run—for example, participants gaming the classification or refusing to take the scoring seriously—and how to debrief that failure so it becomes evidence for the point instead of noise. Finally, be frank about what the exercise cannot reproduce: the years over which real looping unfolds, the administrative and legal machinery that stabilizes actual diagnoses, and the gulf between 90 minutes of artificial sorting and being classified by an institution that has power over you.
