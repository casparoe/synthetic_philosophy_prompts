It starts from the St Petersburg paradox, but he says the usual fix — diminishing marginal utility of money — doesn't do what people think, and that this forces a choice between two ugly options. Let me reconstruct it, numbered the way he wrote it on the board.

(1) A rational agent maximizes expected utility: weight the utility of each outcome by its probability, sum over all outcomes, and prefer the option with the bigger sum.
(2) Utility of money is unbounded above: for any utility level U, there is some amount of money whose utility is at least U. He takes this as obvious, since more money is always at least a little bit better no matter how much you already have.
(3) For any finite entry fee p, there is a St Petersburg-style game whose expected utility after paying p is infinite. Toss a fair coin until it lands heads; if the first heads comes on toss n, you get r_n dollars. The r_n are all finite amounts. Since utility is unbounded, choose them so that u(r_n − p) ≥ 2^n. Then the expected utility of paying p to play is (1/2)u(r_1 − p) + (1/4)u(r_2 − p) + ..., which is at least 1 + 1 + 1 + ..., so it diverges. He adds that this is exactly why Bernoulli's fix is a band-aid: log-dollars are unbounded too, so you can rerun the same construction against log-utility.
(4) When one option has infinite expected utility and the alternative — declining to play and keeping your money — has finite utility, expected utility theory says you should take the infinite one.
(5) Since p was arbitrary, for every finite fee there is some coin-toss game of this form that expected utility theory says a rational agent should pay to play.
(6) So expected utility theory can never let a rational agent draw a line: no matter how much you would have to give up, there's some long-shot coin game it says is worth it. But rational people can refuse to bankrupt themselves for a long shot, and no theory of rationality should be forced to call that a mistake. Something has gone badly wrong, either in expected utility theory or in the unboundedness assumption.

He thinks the only exits are: make rational utility functions bounded above, or restrict expected utility theory to options with finite expected utility, in which case the theory goes silent exactly where the paradox bites.
