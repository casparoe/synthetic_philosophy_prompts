Perjury law typically punishes only statements that are false. A witness who answers each question with a literally true statement, selected and phrased so that the court is left with a false impression, has usually not committed perjury, even when the intent to mislead is obvious. Yet in other parts of the same legal systems, a commercial actor can be liable for fraud for saying only true things if the selection, omission, or framing of those truths leaves a materially false impression; securities disclosure rules, for example, treat an omitted fact as actionable when its absence makes what was said misleading. So the same underlying conduct — using truths to engineer a false belief — is treated as legally blameless in one setting and as a serious civil wrong in another.

What view of the moral difference between lying and misleading, if any, does this pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of deception that the perjury rule appears to rely on, and say whether the fraud rule presupposes a rival theory or only a different application of the same one; distinguish what each rule strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Then assess whether there is a coherent philosophical position that licenses treating courtroom testimony and commercial disclosure differently in this way, or whether the divergence is best explained as institutional accident. Finally, state what would have to change in each practice if the law adopted a single unified principle — either that only literal falsehoods are the special wrong, or that inducing a false belief is the wrong regardless of whether it is done by falsehood or by selection and omission.
