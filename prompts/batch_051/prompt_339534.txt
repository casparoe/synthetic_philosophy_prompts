In most democracies, it is a crime for an ordinary citizen to sell or trade a vote, and the prohibition is rarely controversial: the vote is treated as a civic trust that may not be alienated for private advantage. Yet in the very same political systems, legislators routinely trade votes with one another, backing each other's bills, amendments, or earmarks in exchange for reciprocal support. This practice, often called logrolling, is generally legal and is widely described as a normal and even indispensable part of assembling legislative majorities. Both practices involve using one's vote as a bargaining chip to obtain something one wants; both can move the outcome away from what the person would have judged best on the merits taken in isolation; both treat a vote as in practice alienable. But the private citizen who does it is corrupt, while the legislator who does it is doing their job.

The asymmetry is stark: one case is paradigmatically condemned, the other paradigmatically accepted. Is there a principle that genuinely separates them, or is this an inconsistency in how we think about what a vote is? I want you to construct the strongest available justification for the asymmetry. Consider candidate distinctions such as the difference between a principal's vote and an agent's vote, private gain versus constituent interest, the scale and transparency of the exchange, accountability to an electorate, the expressive character of an ordinary citizen's vote, and whether legislative votes are better modeled on votes in a private association than on votes in a mass electorate. Then test that justification against hard variants before reaching a verdict. For example: a legislator who trades votes to secure a measure that benefits a donor or campaign contributor; a citizen who arranges reciprocal vote-trades with neighbors on issues each cares about unequally; a legislature that adopts a rule banning logrolling; and a thought experiment in which ordinary citizens' votes are made freely alienable. End by saying explicitly whether you would keep the asymmetry, reject it in favor of banning legislative logrolling, reject it in favor of permitting individual vote-selling, or defend some more nuanced position, and explain why.
