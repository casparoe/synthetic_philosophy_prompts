I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about whether it's actually right to say that a word has a meaning — or whether what a word really has is just a fuzzy cloud of uses, which we tidy up into dictionary senses after the fact. I've talked to lexicographers who say the numbered senses in an entry are partly a filing convention, and to corpus linguists who say sense boundaries are an artifact of the way dictionaries get made. I'd love a sharper account than I've been able to put together myself.

So here's the task. You're an expert in semantics and the philosophy of language. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming three or four candinate definitions and stress-testing them against the hardest cases below before you commit to one.

I want a working definition of "the meaning of a word" — a definition of what it is for a word to have a meaning, and of what that meaning consists in. It should correctly classify these cases (where "correctly" just means by your own best lights, since I care more about how the definition handles pressure than about the final labels):

1. "cat" in an ordinary sentence.
2. "asdkjfh," typed when someone's hand slipped.
3. "bank" in "the bank called about my loan" versus "we ate lunch on the bank." One word with two meanings, two words that sound alike, or a distinction your definition doesn't need to draw?
4. "run," the verb, across: run a marathon, run a business, run water, run a fever, run a program, a run in a stocking, a run of bad luck. Dictionaries split this into dozens of senses. Is that a fact about the meaning of "run," or a filing convention?
5. A fresh metaphor: "the negotiation was a slow-motion car crash." Does "car crash" now have a new meaning, or the same meaning stretched?
6. "I." Its referent changes with every speaker and occasion. Does it have a stable meaning, and what would that meaning be?
7. "Ouch." Does it mean something, or just express something?
8. A name like "Iris," used for a particular person. Does it have a meaning?
9. A typo, like "definately" written by someone who clearly intends "definitely." Does "definately" have a meaning?
10. A stipulated term: an author writes, "For this paper, 'spike' will mean any firing within 2 milliseconds of the previous one." Does "spike" have a meaning here, two meanings, or something else?
11. A word in a language you don't know, seen on a sign. You wouldn't call the word meaningless; it has a meaning you just can't recover. What would a definition have to look like to respect that intuition?

Once you have a definition, run it against all eleven cases. Say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, then revise it or defend the suprising verdicts. Tell me which of your verdicts is least secure and why. And if you end up thinking the question "do words have meanings?" is itself the trap, say what question you'd ask instead.
