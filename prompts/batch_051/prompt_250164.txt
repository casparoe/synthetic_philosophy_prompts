I am a philosopher who works on the ethics of records and have been asked for an opinion on the interpretation of a clause in a university special collections library's standard deed of gift. The clause, unchanged since it was drafted in 1968, reads:

"All materials accepted into the collection shall be preserved in perpetuity and shall be made available without restriction to any qualified researcher, without regard to the content or viewpoint of the materials."

The case before me is this. A regional human rights archive has transferred to the library a collection that includes 412 empty file folders from a former state security headquarters. Each folder bears a typed label with a person's name, an arrest date, and a case number, but the contents were removed and destroyed during a documented purge in the 1980s; the folders are the only surviving trace that these individuals were arrested. The transferring archive argues that the folders are themselves records — records of destruction — and that preserving and opening them is exactly what the clause demands. A member of the library's board argues that the drafters had documents in mind, that an empty folder is not a "material," and that in any case there is no "content" to be made available.

I am less interested in a practical recommendation than in the interpretive question: does the clause reach these folders? I would like to know what the standard approaches — the words, the purpose, what the drafters would have said if asked, and what a reasonable reader would take the clause to mean — each yield here, and where they come apart. I would also like to know whether refusing to preserve and open the folders would amount to a further silencing, and whether a reading that includes them can do so without doing violence to the language.
