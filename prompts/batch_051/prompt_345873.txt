I am a PhD student in philosophy of law, and I keep seeing one line put to work in debates about evidence. In a recent seminar on legal epistemology, a colleague defended the claim that a defendant should not be convicted on a DNA match alone, even with a minute random-match probability, by insisting on what he called a bedrock principle, which I would summarize as: "you cannot find someone liable on bare statistical evidence." A second colleague replied that this is just number-phobia: if the statistics make guilt overwhelmingly likely, refusing to convict fails to track the facts. The same slogan appears in the blue bus case (the plaintiff can prove only that most of the buses on the route belonged to the defendant's company) and the gatecrasher case (the event organizer can prove only that a bare majority of attendees failed to pay). I suspect this one sentence is being used to mean several different things, and I want them separated before I take a side.

Please disambiguate the slogan. State the distinct precise principles it could be asserting — for example, about what courts actually do, about what kind of evidence is required before a legal verdict may be reached, about what bare statistics do or do not justify epistemically, about the content of a verdict as an assertion about this particular defendant, and about how a defendant may permissibly be treated. For each reading, say whether it is an accurate description of practice, a defensible normative or epistemic principle, or a mistake, noting any serious qualifications. Be explicit about which readings depend on the criminal standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt and which would also apply to the civil preponderance standard. Finally, identify which reading would actually underwrite my colleague's DNA claim, and which reading the skeptic is attacking.
