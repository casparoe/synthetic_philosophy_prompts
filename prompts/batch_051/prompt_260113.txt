Last night a friend and I argued at length about what Hofstadter's MU-puzzle actually shows. The puzzle is a small formal system. One starts with the string MI and may apply four rules: (i) if a string ends in I, append a U; (ii) from Mx one may form Mxx; (iii) any occurrence of III may be replaced by a U; and (iv) any occurrence of UU may be deleted. The puzzle asks whether MU is reachable. The standard resolution steps outside the string-manipulation rules and counts I's: the first rule preserves the number of I's, the second doubles it, the third removes exactly three I's, and the fourth preserves it. Since MI begins with one I, and doubling modulo 3 toggles between 1 and 2, the number of I's is never a multiple of 3; MU, however, has no I's at all, and zero is a multiple of 3. This is the move Hofstadter describes as jumping out of the system, often abbreviated JOOTSing.

My friend spent the evening defending an analogy she takes to be pervasive: a formal system is like a game. On her picture, the strings are positions, the inference rules are legal moves, a derivation is a play of the game, and the I-counting argument is like stopping play and proving a theorem about chess rather than making another move. From this she concluded that every formal system has an outside, that understanding a system is the kind of act that cannot itself be captured inside the system, and that any attempt to model all of mathematical thought as a single formal game is doomed. I resisted, but I am no longer sure which of my objections were genuine.

I want you to audit the analogy rather than referee the dispute. What corresponds to what, and where does the mapping come apart? Which features of games does the analogy quietly import into formal systems—players with goals, rules as conventions, a board or positions existing independently of play, the possibility of leaving the game—and which of those features matter for the conclusion my friend drew? In particular, does the I-counting argument genuinely jump outside the MIU system, or does it merely translate MIU into arithmetic, a larger system with its own rules and its own unexamined outside? How much of her conclusion survives once the disanalogies are made explicit, and if the game analogy is defective, what better analogy or description captures what is actually going on when one proves that MU is underivable?
