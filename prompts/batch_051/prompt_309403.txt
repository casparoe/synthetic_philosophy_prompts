I'm trying to get straight why Sidgwick, of all people, spent so much time on psychical research. As best I can reconstruct, his motives were something like these four:

(1) The dualism of practical reason. In his big book on ethics he argues that rational egoism and utilitarianism are both defensible but can pull apart, and he can't prove they coincide. If personal survival after death could be made empirically plausible, there'd be room for something like posthumous sanctions or a moral order that eventually reconciles self-interest with duty, so the survival question had real ethical stakes for him.

(2) The mind/body question. Telepathy or veridical apparitions, if established, would be evidence that mental life isn't wholly a function of the brain, which would open a crack in materialism without yet committing him to full-blown survival.

(3) A debunking or protective motive. He thought the spirtualist scene was full of fraud that preyed on grieving people, and that careful investigators were doing a public service by sorting fake mediums from whatever residue couldn't be explained away.

(4) A methodological one. He thought philosophy couldn't settle whether we survive death, so it should be treated as a plain empirical question, and the Society for Psychical Research was basically the only institution trying to do that seriously.

I keep having the feeling there's a fifth motive, something a bit less discussed, that isn't just a restatement or combination of these. Give me one, and then say honestly whether it's a motive Sidgwick himself would have owned, or more of a reason his later defenders would give on his behalf. Also say whether it's any good as a reason, not just historically plausible.
