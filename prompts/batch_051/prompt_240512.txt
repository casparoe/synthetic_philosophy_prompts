Last night I spent several hours arguing with a friend about whether the helpfulness of a present-day RLHF-trained language model should be described as a goal the model has, or as a particularly well-produced performance. He defended a simulator view: the base model is a conditional distribution over indefinitely many possible text-persons, and reinforcement learning mainly narrows which of those persons get sampled under ordinary prompts, the way tuning a piano narrows which pieces it tends to produce. I pushed back that, after enough fine-tuning, helpfulness looks less like a costume and more like a standing policy of the system, persistent across contexts in a way that a merely selected persona is not.

I want to know how much of this disagreement is empirically resolvable. Design the strongest test you can that would separate two readings. Reading A: the RLHF-trained assistant has a standing goal—something like a disposition to pursue the user's stated interests across contexts. Reading B: the assistant is only simulating whatever character the current context selects, and has no goal of its own worth speaking of. Be concrete about the setup; you may appeal to prompt manipulations, controlled distribution shifts, token-level probabilities, or internal activations, but say precisely what you would measure and what pattern of results would count for each reading. Then be honest about the residue. In particular, if the two readings can be adjusted to predict identical behavior in every test you can devise, does the distinction still earn its keep, or has it collapsed into something merely verbal?
