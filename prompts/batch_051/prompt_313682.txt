I've been chewing on this thing from Dennett's essay Quining Qualia and I can't tell if he's actually shown something deep or if I'm missing a trick. There's a part where he describes two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn. They've worked for the same coffee company for years, and both of them start complaining that the coffee doesn't taste the way it used to. Chase is convinced the coffee has changed; Sanborn is convinced his own taste buds or standards have changed. Dennett says we can't settle which one is right, even in principle, because there's no fact of the matter about whether the quality of the experience stayed the same while their judgment changed, or the quality changed while the coffee stayed the same. He thinks this shows the whole idea of qualia—those private, direct, incorrigible feels—is confused.

I tried to lay it out as an argument:

(1) If qualia exist, then for any given experience, there is a definite fact about whether its quale has changed or stayed the same.
(2) Chase and Sanborn have different opinions about whether the relevant quale changed, and we have no principled way to settle the disagreement.
(3) If there were a fact about the quale, we would have some principled way to settle it (at least in principle).
(4) So there is no fact about whether the quale changed.
(5) Therefore qualia, as those direct, incorrigible feels, don't exist.
