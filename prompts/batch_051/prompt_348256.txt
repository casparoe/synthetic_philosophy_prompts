I keep running into the same line in procrastination/akrasia corners of the internet — "the spirit is willing but the flesh is weak" — and it gets used to end arguments I don't think it actually settles. Someone will lay out a case about a New Year's resolution collapsing by February, or why they didn't go to the gym, and another person will just say that line as if it were an explanation rather than a restatement of the problem. The thread that bugged me most was explicitly about hyperbolic discounting: a poster described planning to get up at 6, then hitting snooze thinking that tomorrow-morning-them will deserve the extra sleep, and someone replied "the spirit is willing but the flesh is weak" and a bunch of people nodded along.

I'd like you to pick apart what precise claims that proverb could be making when people use it in this area. Separate the readings that are just banal (people sometimes fail to do what they wanted) from readings that are substantive and defensible, from readings that are probably false or that smuggle in a very particular model of the self. In particular, I want to know which reading, if any, would actually do the work it was being made to do in that thread — explaining a preference reversal over time as ordinary akrasia, as a conflict between two time-slices with different discount rates, or as something else — and which readings make the hyperbolic discounting story redundant or incompatible with the proverb. Also please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, because I've seen each of these readings presented as obviously correct in the comments.
