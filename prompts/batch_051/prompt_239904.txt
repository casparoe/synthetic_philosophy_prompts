Write a decision tree for an undergraduate aesthetics discussion section that leads from a student's judgments about four concrete cases to a named position on whether, when, and why cultural appropriation in art is wrong. Treat cultural appropriation provisionally as an individual's or institution's use of an expressive resource — an art form, style, image, story, or motif — drawn from a cultural group that has been marginalized, or that has systematically less access to the institutions that reward such use. Do not build your own exact definition into the tree; let the cases do that work. The instrument must sort students by what they say about cases, not by the label they would choose, so no node may mention a position's name, and no node may simply ask whether a culture owns its art.

Use these four cases as the fixed material:

- Case A, sacred regalia. A fashion house sends models down the runway wearing garments that reproduce a Native American tribe's sacred ceremonial regalia. The tribe was not consulted, is not compensated, and is not acknowledged by name.
- Case B, profitable outsider. A white American musician becomes wealthy and critically celebrated performing a style that was created by Black musicians under conditions of legal segregation and economic exclusion, while many of the style's originators died poor and obscure.
- Case C, borrowed narrative. A non-Chinese novelist wins a major literary prize for a novel set during China's Cultural Revolution and told through Chinese characters; some Chinese reviewers say the book clarifies that history for outsiders, others that it distorts it.
- Case D, insider hybrid. An Indigenous painter uses her community's sacred designs in canvases that deliberately fuse them with Western modernist abstraction for the global art market. Elders in her community are divided over whether this preserves or profanes the designs.

Build the tree from the following four dimensions rather than from a list of labels:

1. Proprietariness: whether and when a cultural group holds a collective right of consent or ownership over a resource.
2. Sacredness and identity: whether some resources are wrong to use because they are sacred or identity-constituting, in a way that even consent cannot cure.
3. Extraction and distribution: whether the central wrong is that outsiders capture economic and reputational value while the source group remains shut out of the same reward structure.
4. Epistemic and representational authority: whether the central wrong is that outsider use distorts, erases, or arrogates the public meaning of the resource.

Do the following with the logical space: place each leaf by its pattern of answers along these dimensions; where a combination is unoccupied, flag it and say briefly whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, deeply unattractive, or merely unexplored; and flag any two positions the tree cannot separate on the strength of these four cases.

The leaves should include at least the following, each given a descriptive label rather than a philosopher's name: a collective-ownership view; a consent-and-remediation view; an extraction view; a sacred-meaning view; a representational-harm view; a background-injustice view; an anti-essentialist view that treats cultures as open and their boundaries as imposed; and a free-expression view on which the uses in question are rarely or never wrong. Add further leaves only if the four dimensions genuinely force them.

Write the response as a structured handout with these sections, in this order:

1. The tree (roughly 650 words). Begin with a root question about one of the cases, then present each later node as a concrete question or a general claim a student could answer without knowing the literature. Number the nodes and leaves for easy reference in discussion.

2. Position directory (roughly 300 words). For each leaf, give its name, its one-sentence core claim, and one sentence stating the most awkward commitment someone occupying that leaf must accept.

3. Unoccupied and inseparable combinations (roughly 250 words). List the empty cells the tree predicts, diagnose each as incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored, and identify which leaf pairs the tree cannot separate and why.

4. Instructor's diagnostic note (roughly 150 words). Describe the two most likely ways students will game or misread the tree in a classroom setting, and say how to correct for them during discussion.
