I have been trying to pin down where epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment actually come apart, because in the familiar bank and airport cases they simply deliver the same verdict through different machinery: one raises and lowers the standard for the word "knows" in an attributor's mouth, while the other makes knowledge itself sensitive to the practical stakes of the subject. I turned to W. K. Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief" as a stress test, and I keep going back and forth about which view, if either, Clifford's argument most naturally supports. My suspicion is that Clifford sits closer to pragmatic encroachment, because the lives at stake in his story seem to do the real normative work; but I want that suspicion tested rather than confirmed. The passage I am working from is his verdict on the shipowner:

"What shall we say of him? Surely this, that he was verily guilty of the death of those men. It is admitted that he did sincerely believe in the soundness of his ship; but the sincerity of his conviction can in no wise help him, because he had no right to believe on such evidence as was before him. He had acquired his belief not by honestly earning it in patient investigation, but by stifling his doubts. And although in the end he may have felt so sure about it that he could not think otherwise, yet inasmuch as he had knowingly and willingly worked himself into that frame of mind, he must be held responsible for it."

What I want is a disciplined comparison run through this passage, not a survey. Interpret Clifford's charge first: is it a claim about what the shipowner knew, what he was justified in believing, or only about how he acquired his belief? Then take each theory and say what it predicts about whether the shipowner knew, before the sinking, that the vessel was seaworthy, and about whether an observer in a low-stakes context could truthfully say he knew. Pay special attention to whose stakes matter on each view, and to whether the attribution is being made before or after the ship goes down. Then tell me whether this case genuinely discriminates between the two views. If it does not, construct the smallest change—varying who faces the high stakes, who makes the attribution, and when—that forces them apart, and give each theory's verdict in that case. Finally, say whether Clifford's focus on the ethics of belief-formation is closer to one of the two views, or aimed at a target neither is designed to hit.
