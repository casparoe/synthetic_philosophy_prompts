I am a philosopher of social science visiting a history department this spring to give a paper defending a broadly Popperian version of the poverty-of-historicism claim: there cannot be laws of history, even probabilistic or ceteris-paribus ones, because the stable regularities historical inquiry uncovers depend on the actors' not knowing about them. Once a purported law is published and becomes part of the environment in which people decide, it changes the very behavior that would have sustained it — a reflexivity that runs deeper in social life than in the natural sciences, where the phenomena do not read your results and adjust their conduct. The audience will be around thirty academic historians: two quantitative historians who work on sixteenth- and seventeenth-century price and demographic cycles and consider repeated structural pressures the closest thing to laws, a diplomatic historian hostile to abstraction in principle, and a cluster of junior faculty with no fixed methodological commitments. I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a further defense of the paper: the objections I will actually get from this specific room, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way a historian would put it, with a two-minute reply, a note on where that reply is weakest, and a clear flag for which objection I should concede something to up front rather than argue away. Please include the question I should most hope nobody asks.
