A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about what kind of claim the Church–Turing thesis actually is — he wanted to treat it as something close to a definition or a convention, while I kept insisting that it says something substantive about the limits of mechanical computability — and the argument left me with a set of commitments I had never written down. I now need to put them into a short paper, and before I do, I want an audit of whether they hang together. Here is the position I find myself holding:

(1) The Church–Turing thesis is a substantive identity claim: the informal notion of an effectively computable function is coextensive with the formal class of Turing-computable functions. It is not a definition, a convention, or a preference for one formalism over others.

(2) The thesis cannot be proved mathematically, because one side of the identity is an informal, open-textured notion; its support therefore has to be non-demonstrative.

(3) The strongest support for the thesis is the convergence of many independently motivated formalisms — Turing machines, the lambda-calculus, general recursive functions, and the rest — on exactly the same class of functions. I regard this convergence as decisive enough that, absent a concrete counterexample, doubt would be unreasonable.

(4) Nevertheless, the thesis has real content and is refutable in principle: if someone produced a step-by-step procedure that the mathematical community, after careful reflection, genuinely judged to be effective but that computed a function outside the Turing-computable class, I would count the thesis as overturned.

(5) I do not think the standard supposed counterexamples have this force. An oracle that decides the halting problem would not refute the thesis, because an oracle is not given by an effective procedure in the relevant sense. A non-algorithmic faculty of human mathematical intuition, if one exists, would not refute it either, for the same reason. And a physical process that computed a non-Turing-computable function would bear on the physical Church–Turing thesis, not on the thesis itself.

(6) I am agnostic about that physical thesis, precisely because it is an empirical claim about what the physics of our world permits, and I do not think the formal convergence mentioned in (3) settles it one way or the other.

I am not asking for a lecture on the Church–Turing thesis, and I am not asking which of these claims is most defensible in isolation. What I want is a consistency audit: which commitments genuinely pull against one another, which pairs only appear to be in tension, how severe each real tension is, and what specifically would have to give in order to resolve it. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating all of them as equally serious, and be specific about what the cheapest revision would cost.
