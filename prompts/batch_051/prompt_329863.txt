For my aesthetics class we're reading Tolstoy's What Is Art? and I keep getting stuck on his central claim that what makes something a work of art is not beauty or technique or accuracy of representation but infection: the artist has a feeling, embodies it in some public thing (a painting, a song, a story), and the audience catches that same feeling the way you catch a cold, and how good the art is depends mostly on how strong and how wide that infection is. He also says sincerity is a big part of it, that a work can only infect people if the artist actually had the feeling — or something along those lines. My professor called this an empirical core hiding inside what looks like a definition, and I can't decide whether that's right or whether its just a metaphor.

So here's what I want to work out. Suppose you wanted to test Tolstoy's claim against, say, a formalist view where what matters is the structure and arrangement of the material, or an expression view where what matters is that the work genuinely externalizes the artist's state regardless of whether anyone catches it. What would your experiment actually do? I want the concrete design: what you'd operationalize as infection, what comparison you'd run, what pattern of results would favor Tolstoy's picture and what pattern would favor a rival. And then be really honest about the residue — what a positive result would still not show about sincerity, about whether infection is doing any work distinctively artistic rather than just being ordinary emotional contagion, and whether the evaluative side of his theory (good art transmits the good feelings) is testable at all. Is this a case where a test gets at only a small, maybe uninteresting part of the theory, or can it actually get at the thing Tolstoy cared about?
