Since I retired from chemistry I've been trying to teach myself philosophy of langauge, and last week two friends of mine got into it over a question I'd brought up, so I mostly listened and now I want someone to referee, because I genuinely couldn't tell who had the better of it.

The question was whether a language could do without indexicals entirely: no "I" or "you," no "now" or "here" or "this," nothing whose contribution shifts with who says it, where, or when. Paul said the answer is obviously yes, since you can replace "I" with the speaker's name, "now" with a clock time, "here" with a place description, and you're left with a language whose words all have fixed reference but that can still express everything ours can. Tessa said he'd hidden the indexicals rather than removed them, because which name, which time, and which place get slotted in depends on who's talking, when, and where; that rule is itself context-sensitive, so the machinery is still there, just moved out of the dictionary and into the instructions for use. Paul answered that the words themselves are context-free and that was all his claim required; a rule for what to say on an occasion is not a word, and any language already assumes speakers know who and where they are. Tessa then switched to action. Suppose you know the meeting starts at 20:00 and you glance at a clock reading 19:59; the reason you run is not just that you hold those two eternal facts, but that you take this clock to be yours and its reading to be current. If your mind contained only context-free sentences, you'd still need something like "that clock is here now" to connect the facts to your legs, and that connecting thought is just what Paul's language refuses to supply. Paul replied that the connection doesn't have to be represented in words at all; animals flee from danger without saying anything like "there's a threat near me," so the action-relevant part may be non-linguistic and a language with no indexicals wouldn't stop anyone from acting. Tessa said that changed the question, from what a language can express to what a creature can do without language; a language that can't mark the difference between "the danger is near Tessa" and "the danger is near me" is expressively incomplete, even if a speaker's unspoken sense of self makes up for it in practice. Paul's last move was to say the difference she was pointing at belongs to thought, not to shared language; language describes the world, and the self-locating uptake is a private matter for each hearer. Tessa said that can't be right, because we use the same shared sentence types to do exactly that work: "you're bleeding" locates the addressee relative to the utterance, and no paraphrase with names and dates preserves that function.

Then they looked at me and I had to admit I couldn't sort it out, so here I am. Walk through the exchange and say who's right at which step, which points actually matter, and whether the question they ended on, whether such a language would be expressively incomplete, is the same as the question they started on, namely whether such a language could exist at all. If both of them are talking past each other or if there's a better way to frame the issue, say that too.
