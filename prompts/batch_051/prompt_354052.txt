I recently watched a close friend fall into a kind of doubt I could not classify. It was not about one corrigible claim; he began to treat things I had always assumed were beyond question for any sane person — that his memories are broadly his own, that the people around him are not engaged in systematic deception, that the world did not come into existence five minutes ago — as open questions he could not close. When I tried to answer him with reasons, I noticed that every reason I reached for leaned on something at least as exposed. A colleague pointed me toward the literature on hinge epistemology, rooted in Wittgenstein's On Certainty, and I have been reading around it for a few months. The readings describe positions; what I want is a specification.

Treat this as a requirements exercise. The theory to be specified is a theory of the rational structure of doubt and inquiry: an account of what distinguishes ordinary empirical claims that can sensibly be doubted, tested, and revised from the commitments that function as hinges — the certainties that particular doubts press on but that are not themselves questioned while that inquiry proceeds. Fix the purpose as follows: the theory must be adequate to the way actual finite inquirers manage to trust, doubt, learn, and revise in the course of real inquiry, not merely adequate as a reply to radical skepticism. Adequacy means adequacy for that purpose.

Write the desiderata list. For each requirement: state it as a condition the theory must meet; give the case, phenomenon, or consideration that makes it a requirement; and mark it as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp. The list should cover at least three kinds of material:

- phenomena a theory must handle (for example, Moore-type cases in which a proposition seems both utterly certain and strangely ungroundable; the fact that universal doubt presents itself as a kind of incapacity rather than a live intellectual option; the fact that hinges can shift over time without everything collapsing at once);
- distinctions it must respect and not collapse (for example, between a hinge and an ordinary high-confidence empirical claim; between being immune to doubt in practice and being exempt from it in some stronger sense; between hinge status and mere subjective certainty);
- adjacent results and constraints it must sit alongside (for example, closure under known entailment, the role of testimony and memory in sustaining certainty, the possibility of rational disagreement), including the places where those constraints pull against the hinge picture.

Treat those as prompts for thought, not as fixed headings: build the actual list yourself, with the reason each item belongs.

Then answer two further questions. First, which single requirement, taken seriously, does the most work in excluding the otherwise natural ways of theorizing about such certainties — foundationalist, contextualist, externalist, or hybrids of those? Second, is any subset of the requirements jointly unsatisfiable as stated? If so, identify the subset and explain the nature of the conflict. That second question is the part I most want to see.

No bibliography or page references; I want the reasoning, not a reading list. Hard limit: 1,000 words.
