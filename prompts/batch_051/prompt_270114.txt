A friend of mine lost his dad a few years ago, and he told me something that has stuck with me. He says he doesn't just remember his dad; when he's in a tricky situation he finds himself running a little model of him — he can hear the old man's take on a new problem, including jokes the guy never actually made, and sometimes he catches himself arguing with it as if it had a mind of its own. Late one night this turned into a full Hofstadter rabbit hole: the idea that a self is just a pattern, and that a lower-resolution copy of that pattern keeps running in the brains of the people who knew you. So, he said, a sliver of his father is litterally still around inside him — not as a soul, just as an abstract pattern instantiated in his neurons. I can maybe imagine testing predictive accuracy on novel cases, but that at best shows someone has a detailed generative model of a dead person; it doesn't by itself show any kind of self is running.

I'm in a philosophy of mind course, and I keep going back and forth on weather that claim is profound-and-literal or just a poetic way of saying people internalize the dead and get good at predicting them. So here's what I'd like: design the best test you can for the claim that a degraded version of one person's self is genuinely instantiated in another person's brain, rather than the person merely remembering facts and mannerisms. Say what result would favor each reading, say why the two readings are so hard to pull apart experimentally, and then be honest about what no test, however good, could settle — for instance whether the lower-resolution pattern is conscious on its own, whether it's continuous with the deceased's self in any way that matters, or whether the whole framing is a category mistake. The part I most want is what the test leaves untouched, because that's where I suspect the real action is.
