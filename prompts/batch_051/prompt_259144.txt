I'm giving a seminar next month to a political science department — mostly democratic theorists and comparativists who work on party systems, plus a couple of intellectual historians — defending a qualified version of Simone Weil's critique of political parties. I want to be ready for Q&A.

My thesis is that Weil's core diagnosis survives even if her abolitionist conclusion doesn't. I argue that the party form itself, not this or that party, has the three features she identified: a party exists to grow and to prevail, it exerts collective pressure on its members' judgment, and it offers its own slogans as a substitute for thinking. That makes parties structurally at odds with the honest formation of political judgment, in a way that better internal party culture, proportional representation, or a larger number of parties cannot fix; the pressure is built into the form. My institutional conclusion is not outright abolition but radical demotion: strip parties of their near-monopoly over nominations, ballot access, agenda control, and the framing of elections as team competition, and transfer some of those functions to issue-specific, time-limited citizen bodies and sortition. So I am exposed on both ends: I need the structural claim to be stronger than merely that parties are often bad, and I need the institutional program to survive the charge that it is utopian or that it would recreate parties under other names.

The audience is not hostile to Weil specifically so much as to anti-party argument in general. They hold that competitive parties are how voters hold governments accountable, how legislative coordination gets solved, and how ordinary citizens get dependable shortcuts for political judgment; the comparativists will immediately say that nonpartisan elections and weak-party systems produce de facto parties anyway. I know I will get the accountability objection and the parties-will-regrow objection, and I need those handled early and well. But I also need the objections I am not expecting, especially from the comparativists, and the one that does the most damage — which I suspect is about what fills the vacuum if parties are demoted: money, media, organized interest groups?

Act as a political theorist who knows Weil's critique closely and also knows the mainstream democratic-theory and comparative literatures on parties and party systems. Start by brainstorming a larger list of candidate objections, with one sentence on each saying whether it is likely, damaging, or both, before selecting and ordering the final set. Then produce my prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way the relevant audience member would actually put it, with a two-minute reply, a note on where that reply is softest, what I should concede early and what I should refuse to give up, and the single weakest point in my own position I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. End with the question I am hoping nobody raises.
