You are a professional philosopher specializing in Bayesian epistemology and diachronic rationality. Work through the problem carefully, and begin by brainstorming a range of possible defeaters before sorting them.

I hold a position I am tempted to treat as a norm of rationality: van Fraassen's Reflection Principle. Stated informally, for any proposition A and any later time t', my current degree of belief in A should equal my current expectation of the degree of belief I will have in A at t'. More formally, P_t(A) = E_t[P_{t'}(A)], or equivalently, where the conditional is defined, P_t(A | P_{t'}(A) = x) = x. The spirit of the principle is that my future self is not a stranger but the continuation of the same mind, usually with at least as much evidence; a rational present self should therefore defer to its rational future self, and anticipating a future change of mind that one cannot now endorse should itself be irrational.

What would count against this position? I want a map of its exposure, not a survey of objections. In your answer:

1. Separate the merely awkward from the potentially decisive: which cases, arguments, intuitions, or formal results should merely push a defender toward a qualified version, and which should make them give up the principle altogether?
2. Say what the principle's defenders would themselves concede counts against it — not just what critics claim.
3. Address these test cases explicitly: (a) I am about to take a pill that will, reliably and without providing any new evidence, raise my credence in some proposition from moderate to near-certain, and I know this is how the pill works; (b) an agent knows her future self tends to confabulate vivid memories, so she expects to later become much more confident in a proposition without gaining any new information; (c) an agent enters into a binding precommitment against her own anticipated future judgments. What should each case do to the principle?
4. Assess whether the usual qualifications — defer to the future self only when you do not anticipate irrationality, self-deception, coercion, drug-induced credence, or loss of information — are honest refinements that preserve a substantive norm, or ad hoc shields that make the principle unfalsifiable. If they insulate it, state whether and why that is or is not a defect in a principle of this kind.
5. Say what Reflection prohibits that rival accounts of diachronic rationality would permit, and what its rivals predict that it cannot accommodate. Where does it genuinely take a risk, and where has it been qualified into saying little?

Conclude with your considered view: what is the strongest surviving version of the principle, what does it still forbid, and what would count against even that version?
