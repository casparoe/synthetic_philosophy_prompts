I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training. Lately I keep thinking about the doctrine of original sin in version-control terms, and I want to test whether the model sheds light on the inherited-guilt side of the doctrine. Take the standard Augustinian position: all natural descendants of the first human being inherit a genuine guilt, so that even a newborn is born liable to condemnation before any personal action. Now vary it: suppose what is inherited is not a shared criminal status but only a corrupted default state, something like a bug in a base branch that every later fork inherits, while guilt itself is stored in a per-agent ledger and attaches only to that agent’s own actions. In the variation, the inherited defect is real and universal, but it is not itself blameworthy for the descendant. Work through how the standard analysis changes. Does the variation collapse back into the weaker view that infants inherit only damage and no guilt, or does it leave room for a strong doctrine of original sin framed entirely as an inherited corruption that still needs grace? I would like the answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. The five items should cover: (1) what the standard position says about what is inherited; (2) what the variation changes in the analysis; (3) which structural feature of the variation does the work; (4) what happens if that feature is removed; and (5) what a defender of the standard inherited-guilt view would say is lost in the variation.
