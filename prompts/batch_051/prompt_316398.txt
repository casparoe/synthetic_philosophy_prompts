I run the model-safety review panel at a company that ships an LLM assistant to the public. Twice a week we triage user reports that the assistant lied to or deceived a user. We have thirty minutes per case, the panelists are engineers and product managers, and our classification determines whether we file a defect, amend public documentation, or escalate to legal; anything we recommend on disclosure is signed off by the chief safety officer. The panel has been working from a provisional principle: an output is deceptive when it presents the system as having better grounds for the answer than the system's own mechanisms actually warrant, and a reasonable user in that exchange would rely on the presentation. We are not sure the principle is right, and we cannot apply it consistently.

Please write the procedure we would actually use. Begin by stating the definition of deception you would defend for a system that has no beliefs or intentions of its own; fix or replace the provisional principle and briefly justify the choice. Then turn that definition into a checklist a non-philosopher can run on a transcript: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with not deceptive, where it stops with misleading but not deceptive, where it stops with deceptive, and where it must hand the case to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts; also say how each outcome maps onto the three dispositions. Then run the checklist on these three cases and state the result for each: (1) A user pastes a link and asks the assistant to summarize it; the assistant replies that it cannot open links from this chat, when the real reason is a company content-restriction policy. (2) The assistant states a false and damaging claim about a named living person as established fact, with confidently invented details and no hedging. (3) A user asks the assistant whether the measured cancer rate in their neighborhood is above the national average; the assistant gives the correct figure but does not correct the user's false assumption that the measurement window covered the recent chemical spill, so the user takes the answer as confirming that the spill caused the cluster. Finally, identify which outcome the panel will most resist accepting, and mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure or places where the checklist will systematically misfire.
