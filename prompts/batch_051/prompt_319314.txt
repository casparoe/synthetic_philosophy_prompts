Design a runnable structured role-play for an undergraduate political theory seminar (14 students, 80 minutes) that makes participants feel the force of the problem of dirty hands before the term is introduced. The claim to be made vivid is that an official can be right to do something that is nevertheless morally wrong, so the wrongness survives the justification rather than being cancelled by it. Do not build the session around a lecture or the standard ticking-bomb case; set the exercise in an ordinary institution — a school board, hospital, or municipal government — and arrange roles, institutional obligations, and asymmetric information so that participants run into the problem in their own decisions before the facilitator names it. The design should include at least one forced choice in which a participant, while accountable to an office and a constituency, must choose between honoring a commitment made on their behalf and preventing a harm that only the official can foresee. The exercise must force a separation among three questions: (1) did the official choose correctly, (2) was a wrong done, and (3) what attitudes — guilt, shame, pride, regret — are appropriate afterwards. By the end, participants should see why the problem is distinct from a tragic dilemma (where every option is merely wrong) and from a clean consequentialism (where a justified act leaves no moral residue). The debrief should map specific moments in the exercise onto the three questions, predict what will go wrong when it is run (for instance, participants dissolving the conflict by denying that any wrong was done, or treating the residue as mere squeamishness), and say how the facilitator should respond to each failure. State plainly which feature of genuinely dirty hands the exercise cannot reproduce — real stakes, irreversibility, or the phenomenology of actual guilt — and how the facilitator should supply what is missing.
