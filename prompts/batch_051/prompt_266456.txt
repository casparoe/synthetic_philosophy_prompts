I run a philosophy-and-technology club at a big state school — about eighteen undergrads, mostly not philosophy majors, one 90-minute evening meeting. I want an activity that makes them feel the erosion of understanding that comes with epistemic dependance on AI: the way a group can be reliably correct while its own grip on why it's correct quietly thins out, and how the feeling of understanding can keep pace with the answer even when the understanding isn't there. No lecture up front, and I don't want the concept named until the debrief.

The key thing I want built in is a two-phase structure. In phase one they have to solve some tasks (classification, explanation, prediction — whatever you pick) with an 'oracle' available: something that gives correct answers or intermediate steps but, like a model, doesn't build up the skill for them. In phase two the oracle goes away and they get a transfer task of the same form but slightly changed, and ideally the teams that leaned hardest on the oracle should be the ones that now flounder most. I'd like the scoring set up so answers and explanations are tracked seperately, so the gap between getting-it-right and understanding-it becomes visible in their own numbers, not in a facilitator's speech. Rules, materials, a timeline, an incentive scheme that doesn't require real money, and a debrief that maps what happened in each phase to the idea. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm especially worried about teams gaming the oracle or treating it as a search engine rather than as a crutch — and be honest about what a one-evening simulation can't reproduce about a process that actually takes months or years.
