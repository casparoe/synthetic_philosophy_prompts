Two friends of mine from the local rationalist meetup got into it about van Fraassen's reflection principle, and I still don't know how to score the exchange. The principle, as they were using it, is that your current credence in a proposition should equal your expected future credence in it: roughly, if you know that tomorrow you'll be 70% on rain, you should be 70% on rain today. It's supposed to follow from Bayesian coherence plus the fact that tomorrow's opinion is, in effect, tomorrow's evidence. I've been reading LessWrong-style stuff for years, so I can follow the Dutch book talk, but I honestly couldn't settle this one.

(1) A said violations of the principle are incoherent in the same way intransitive preferences are: if you're 40% now but expect to be 70% tomorrow, a bookie can bet against you at today's price and against you again at tomorrow's price, and you'll take a combination of bets that loses no matter what else you learn. The principle, he said, just says your prices have to line up over time or you're exploitable.

(2) B said that can't be the whole story, because take this case: tonight I'm going to take a pill that will chemically make me 90% confident that my visa application will be approved, regardless of what evidence shows up later, and I know all of this now. The pill gives me no information about the visa. Reflection says my current credence should already be 90%. That's absurd; it would mean I should update on an anticipated bias the way I update on anticipated evidence.

(3) A said the priniciple was never meant for that. It only applies when your future self is an epistemic successor: at least as informed and no less rational than you are now. A chemically rigged future self obviously isn't. Universal laws have presuppositions — like Newton's laws failing in non-inertial frames — and the pill case just falls outside the domain.

(4) B said this is exactly where the retreat gets suspicious. Either the principle is a formal coherence constraint with no competence condition, in which case the pill refutes it, or it says "defer to your future self exactly when your future self deserves deference," which is circular and does no work — it no longer tells you anything from inside. And "no less rational" does too much: where's the cut between pill-drunk and just exhausted, or between exhausted and merely having read a persuasive blog post? If the line is "trustworthy process," B said, you've stopped doing diachronic coherence and started doing expert aggregation.

(5) A said B was making a two-way distinction where there's a continuum, and the continuum is fine. You don't need to know whether your future self is "rational" or not; you have a prior over how reliable the process that forms your future self will be — just like you have over any forecaster. So you defer in proportion to expected trustworthiness: almost fully to your well-rested, better-informed tomorrow self, almost not at all to your future pill self, and somewhere in between for a future self running on two hours of sleep. That's not circular; it's just Bayesianism that treats your future self as one more signal.

(6) B said A just gave away the game. The original reflection principle said your future credence merits deference because it's yours, automatically, as a matter of synchronic coherence. Once A says deference is proportional to the trustworthiness of the process that produced it, the principle stops being a distinctive norm at all — it's just ordinary evidence pooling wearing a name. The Dutch book argument in (1) only works if you already treat tomorrow's opinion as authoritative; the pill case shows you can rationally refuse to, and then the alleged incoherence is just the cost of declining to defer to a forecaster you've correctly judged to be broken.

Please adjudicate the whole exchange move by move. For each numbered move, rate it as clearly mistaken, mostly wrong, mixed, mostly right, or clearly right, with a sentence or two saying what it gets or misses. Then say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing — I can tell this is partly a fight about what the principle was even for, but I can't make the final call.

At the very end, after your full answer, add a final line that begins exactly "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase naming who is more right overall (A, B, or mixed) and the single biggest reason.
