I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I keep running into an argument, inspired by Hume's discussion of miracles, that now seems to me to prove too much. Here is the argument as I reconstruct it.

(1) Testimony that an event E occurred can rationally justify believing E only if the probability that the testimony would occur as it did even if E had not occurred is lower than the prior improbability of E itself.

(2) A miracle is a violation of a law of nature, and a law of nature is established by uniform past experience. So for any real miracle, the prior improbability of the event is greater than the probability that the witnesses are lying or deceived.

(3) Therefore, testimony can never rationally justify belief in a miracle.

(4) The reasoning in steps (1) through (3) applies to any report that conflicts with a maximally well-established empirical generalization, not only to reports of religious miracles.

(5) The history of science contains reports that conflicted with maximally well-established empirical generalizations of their day and were later accepted—early accounts of meteorites are one example, since they were long dismissed because stones were not supposed to fall from the sky.

(6) If steps (1) through (3) are sound, then those reports could not have been rationally accepted at the time they were made.

(7) But some such acceptances are hard to dismiss as irrational; we now treat them as episodes of scientific progress.

(8) So either the argument against miracles contains a mistake, or it commits us to saying that rational scientific theory-change was never rational.

I would like to keep steps (1) and (2), because they seem to capture the thought that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. But something must go. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?
