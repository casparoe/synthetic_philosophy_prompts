Lately I have been trying to get clearer on what makes a historical analogy for transformative AI substantively informative rather than merely evocative. As a software engineer rather than a historian or philosopher, I have settled on a rough working checklist for a technology to count as a good analogy: (1) it compressed the time between technical feasibility and large-scale social consequences; (2) it changed the distribution of capabilities across actors — in the strongest cases by shifting the offense–defense balance, as nuclear weapons arguably did, or by decentralizing a capability that had previously been held by a few powerful institutions, as the printing press arguably did; (3) its consequences were poorly anticipated by well-placed contemporaries; and (4) its effects depended heavily on complementary institutions and standards, not just on the artifact itself. The Industrial Revolution strikes me as fitting only loosely on this picture, since it was a decades-long economic transition rather than a single technology.

I recently found myself thinking about the standardized shipping container as a candidate I had never seen placed alongside nuclear weapons and the printing press. It spread from the late 1950s and very quickly reorganized where production could be located, which ports and manufacturing regions won or lost, and how cheaply goods could move across borders; many incumbent shipping firms, unions, and port cities demonstrably misjudged its consequences, and its full impact required standardized equipment, port infrastructure, and legal changes rather than just the box itself. But it was not a weapon and does not seem to have shifted the offense–defense balance in any straightforward way, and the capability it created was not the sort of thing an individual or small group could wield against a much larger adversary. Treat it as a test case for my checklist. Does the shipping container qualify as a genuinely analogous transformative technology, or does its failure on criterion (2) show that my criterion is too narrow? If you think the criterion should be revised, I would like you to propose the more general feature of which the offense–defense balance is only one instance, go through the other criteria honestly, and say whether the case shows that any of them needs to be dropped or replaced as well.
