Public institutions increasingly justify play and leisure through their measurable downstream effects: unstructured play is defended as improving executive function and academic outcomes, workplace recreation as boosting creativity and reducing burnout, and time away from screens as supporting attention and mental health. At the same time, a tradition associated with Huizinga and Pieper treats play and leisure as ends in themselves—activities whose point lies within the doing rather than in results they happen to produce. On that view, part of what makes play play is that it is not undertaken for the sake of outcomes, and part of what makes leisure leisure is that it is not merely recuperation that serves further work.

Imagine a school board that preserves an hour of unstructured play solely because a study shows it raises test scores. A purist objects that this rationale is self-defeating: the board is not really valuing play, only a set of testable effects, and it would eliminate play without intellectual cost if the evidence changed. Worse, the instrumental rationale may seep into the practice itself, teaching children to regard play as a technique for obtaining something else and thereby eroding the absorbed, self-forgetting quality on which play's distinctive value depends. The board replies that public institutions must justify expenditures in reasons citizens can audit, and that an activity can be both intrinsically worthwhile and instrumentally useful; the child's play, it argues, can remain fully its own end even when the adults' reasons for protecting it are instrumental.

Write an essay on this controversy. Weigh the strongest versions of both positions and reach a definite, justified conclusion about whether—and under what conditions—an institution can coherently value play or leisure as an end in itself while defending it in purely instrumental terms. A nuanced verdict is welcome, but do not end non-committally.
