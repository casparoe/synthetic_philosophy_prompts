I keep finding myself pushed toward the view that simplicity is a first-class virtue in ethical theory, not just a tiebreaker. If a theory with one master principle—say, that the only thing that ultimately matters is how good the resulting state of the world is—can systematize 95% of my considered judgments, parsimony seems to license me to treat the remaining 5% as noise: the kind of thing you debunk as framing effects, self-serving bias, or heuristics that misfire at the edges. And I find that genuinely compelling, partly because it gives me a clean decision procedure: no real dilemmas, no unrankable values, no mysterious 'weight' between different kinds of consideration. It feels like physics versus epicycles.

But I also have a firm intuition I can't shake. When I imagine breaking a promise I made to a friend who is now dead, in a case where nobody would ever find out and doing so would produce a small amount of extra happiness for a stranger, it seems flat out obvious to me that I shouldn't. And the wrongness doesn't feel like a small dose of disutility that got outweighed; it feels like a different kind of thing—a requirement, not a value I'm comparing. Putting 'betrayal' on a scale with 'stranger's mild pleasure' feels like a category mistake, not a weighing error.

So that's the clash. Methodological parsimony says the one-currency theory wins and I should debunk the intuition as a heuristic; the intuition says the theory is leaving something out that no amount of parsimony earns back. Give me the strongest version of each side. What exactly would justify debunking the intuition rather than amending the theory, and what exactly would justify adding a second kind of consideration and just eatting the parsimony cost? Is there a reading on which they're compatable—e.g. parsimony governs explanatory structure rather than the number of fundamental kinds—or is that just a relabeling? I want a verdict, but one that says what each move costs, not just which side you'd pick.
