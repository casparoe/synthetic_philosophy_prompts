I'm a philosophy of mind PhD student, and I've been trying to get the causal exclusion argument into a form I can actually defend, and I've talked myself into epiphenomenalism, which can't be right. Am I messing something up? Here's my reconstruction:

(1) Any mental state—a pain, an intention—is realized by some neural state, and that neural state's causal powers are completely fixed by its physical properties.
(2) If a lower-level realizer already has its causal powers fixed, then a higher-level state realized by it adds no causal powers over and above the realizer.
(3) So a pain adds no causal powers over and above it's neural realizer.
(4) But if the pain adds no causal powers over and above its realizer, then the neural realizer alone is sufficient for all the behavior the pain was supposed to cause, and the pain itself does no causal work.
(5) Therefore the pain, considered as a mental state, doesn't cause anything—only the neural state does.

I have a feeling step (4) is where the sleight of hand is, but I can't say exactly which reading of 'does no causal work' makes it true and which makes it false. Is the argument valid as it stands? If not, point to the step; if it is valid, tell me which premise I should reject and on what grounds. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion.
