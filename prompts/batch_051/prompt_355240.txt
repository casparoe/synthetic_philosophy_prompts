I've come around to a suffering-focused view in ethics, and what I want is a map of how exposed the view actually is — not another attack on a strawman version of it. The core I'm committed to is this: preventing extreme suffering has lexical priority over everything else. No amout of mild wellbeing, and no amount of relieved mild unhappiness, can make up for allowing a single being to endure something like torture. I also lean strongly toward an asymmetry about creation: bringing a new happy person into existence has little or no positive moral value in itself, while bringing someone into existence whose life would contain extreme suffering is a serious wrong; and a universe with a trillion blissful lives plus one person being tortured is, in my book, worse than a empty universe. I'm genuinely unsure how to trade mild suffering against mild happiness, but that feels downstream of the commitments I actually care about.

I already know the standard objections people reach for — that the view seems to imply an empty world is best, maybe even that we should painlessly end all life, and the thought experiments where one tiny discomfort is supposed to beat any amount of happiness. I want to go past those. Tell me what would have to be true for the position to be false or badly damaged. Which specific commitment is doing the most work and is most fragile — the lexical priority, the creation asymmetry, or something else? What possible cases, empirical findings about how happiness and suffering actually relate, or results in adjacent areas (decision theory, population ethics, theories of well-being) would count against each of them? Which of these would a sophisticated defender of the view concede as genuine costs or live escape routes, and which would they dismiss as attacking a different view? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I'd like an honest answer about whether the lexical priority claim is well insulated because it's functioning more like an axiom than an empirical claim. The thing that would probably move me most is a small, concrete case where the view tells me to do something monstrous to a person in front of me, not a population-ethics thought experiment with 10^50 people. But don't cater to that — give me the actual map of what the view risks.
