I want you to act like the one person in the room who has to actually make a call about this. Say a small nonprofit — three staff, patchy funding, maybe five years before everyone burns out — decides to work on the ownership-and-ethics mess around stuff whose creator is gone, unreachable, or just unknown: orphan works like old photographs and out-of-print books, but also abandoned software, from a dead maintainer's GitHub repo to a 1980s lab utility that some university still runs because nobody can get it replaced. They ask you: what should we actually try to settle first?

Don't give me a survey of the area. Give me a defended ranking. Pick the two or three open questions — legal, moral, conceptual, practical, however you cut it — that are most worth resolving over the next five years, where 'worth resolving' means the answer would change what archives, courts, maintainers, or users actually do. Then tell me what you're deprioritizing and why, and what would make you reorder the list.

For candidate questions, think in terms of things like: does 'the owner is gone' do any real philosophical work, or is 'orphan status' just a practical problem of finding someone to ask? Should long, good-faith use of abandoned software by a community create any kind of standing or quasi-ownership? Is the bottleneck mostly liability risk from copying, or is it more about what counts as a diligent search for an owner? You don't have to stick to these — they're just to show the level of specificity im after.

Be explicit about the criteria your using (importance, tractability, neglectedness, how much downstream stuff depends on the answer), and keep it grounded in that nonprofit's actual options, not philosophy-in-general.
