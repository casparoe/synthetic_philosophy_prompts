In the philosophy of language reading group I attend, one analogy keeps surfacing and doing most of the work in a running debate. The comparison, as its proponents frame it, is this: the categories of traditional grammar — parts of speech, case distinctions, the class of prepositions — are like species in biology or elements in chemistry. Just as 'gold' names a natural kind rather than an arbitrary collection of shiny yellow substances, words like 'noun,' 'verb,' and 'dative' are said to name real kinds that grammars discover, not conventions that grammarians impose. The analogy is used to license a specific conclusion: when unrelated languages exhibit broadly similar word classes and case systems, we should treat that convergence as evidence of something real in the structure of human cognition, and those who dismiss these categories as parochial or merely useful descriptive shortcuts should bear the burden of proof.

I would like an audit of how far that analogy actually carries. Start with the correspondence table: what corresponds to the species or element, the taxonomist or chemist, shared ancestry or atomic number, speciation or transmutation, the field guide or the periodic table? Then set out the disanalogies and sort them into the ones that matter for the intended conclusion and the ones that do not. For instance: species are individuated partly by genealogy and reproductive isolation, whereas grammatical categories are individuated by distribution and function; elements are discrete and few, whereas grammatical categories grade into one another and languages draw their boundaries differently; and even biological taxonomy is disputed in ways the analogy's users may be quietly shelving. I also want to know what the analogy imports from the source that the target lacks — discreteness, hidden essences, the idea that a classification is discovered rather than made, the assumption that universality is automatically evidence of naturalness — and whether the conclusion survives once those imports are made explicit.

Finally, if the analogy breaks under the load, propose a better one — a toolbox, a coordinate system, a set of musical genres, or something else — and say what it captures that the natural-kind analogy distorts.
