For several months I have been helping a trial judge in a state civil court draft findings and orders, and I have noticed a stable but hard-to-name pattern in how she treats a certain kind of evidence. I now have to anticipate how she will rule in a case pending before her, and I am no longer sure the pattern in her record adds up to a single clear principle.

Her four recent decisions run like this. First, in a suit by a pedestrian struck by a bus on a route served by only two companies, where the only evidence was that about 80 percent of the buses on that route at that hour belonged to the defendant and the pedestrian could not identify the bus, she entered judgment for the defendant. Second, in a suit by a promoter against a spectator who allegedly entered an event without paying, where the only evidence was that 501 of 1,000 attendees had not paid and the spectator was one of those attendees, she entered judgment for the spectator. Third, in a civil suit by a prison guard against one inmate for battery during a yard fight, where 99 of the 100 inmates in the yard had joined the attack, one had not, and no one could say whether the defendant was the one who held back, she entered judgment for the defendant even though the probability that any given inmate joined the attack was roughly 99 percent. Fourth, in a suit where the only evidence was the plaintiff's testimony that the defendant struck him, and the defendant's expert testified that eyewitnesses under those conditions are accurate only about 60 percent of the time, she entered judgment for the plaintiff; she reasoned that the civil standard is more likely than not rather than certainty, and that the testimony, if credited, went directly to this defendant and this act.

The case now before her involves a casino that sued a patron to recover a large payout and to enforce a ban, claiming he counted cards. The casino's only evidence is a statistical analysis of the patron's own betting records from the sessions in dispute: his wagers rose and fell in near-perfect step with the shifting composition of the deck, and the casino's expert says an honest player following any plausible strategy would produce such a pattern with a probability under 0.1 percent. No one observed the patron counting cards, using a device, or receiving help, and he says he was simply lucky. A colleague who has followed her decisions says the case is easy: she will rule for the patron, because her rule is just that no one should lose on a probability alone. My own suspicion is that her rule is narrower than my colleague thinks, and that what bothers her is evidence drawn from the composition of a group, while an inference built from the party's own conduct might be enough for her. My question is this: if the judge wants to remain consistent with her four earlier decisions, how should she rule, and which principle best explains her record? Do her earlier cases already settle the casino case, or do they underdetermine it? If they underdetermine it, what does each candidate rule predict, which earlier case is the closest analog, and what further fact or case would cleanly tell the two rules apart?
