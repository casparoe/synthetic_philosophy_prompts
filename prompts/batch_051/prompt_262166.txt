I am not a student of philosophy, but I recently got into a long argument with my cousin, who took some courses in it, about whether any of our beliefs can really be defended. He summarized Hume's skepticism like this: because our reasoning faculty is fallible, every belief is at best a probability; any judgment we make about that probability is itself a product of the same fallible faculty, so it carries a new uncertainty, which must in turn be checked, and so on. Since each check lowers our confidence a little, the chain never terminates, and in the end no belief survives it. From this, my cousin concluded that the only intellectually honest stance would be total suspension of judgment, and he said Hume's later appeal to nature and habit is just an admission that he could not live with his own conclusion — a psychological escape hatch rather than an answer.

Afterwards I tried to check him against the text, and I found this passage in Book 1, Part 4, Section 1 of A Treatise of Human Nature:

"Having thus found in every probability, beside the original uncertainty inherent in the subject, a new uncertainty deriv'd from the weakness of that faculty, which judges, and having adjusted these two together, we are oblig'd by our reason to add a new doubt deriv'd from the possibility of error in the estimation we make of the truth and fidelity of our faculties. This is a doubt, which immediately occurs to us, and of which, if we wou'd closely pursue our reason, we cannot avoid giving a decision. But this decision, tho' it shou'd be favourable to our preceeding judgment, being founded only on probability, must weaken still further our first evidence, and must itself be weaken'd by a fourth doubt of the same kind, and so on in infinitum; till at last there remain nothing of the original probability, however great we may suppose it to have been, and however small the diminution by every new uncertainty. No finite object can subsist under a decrease repeated in infinitum; and even the vastest quantity, which can enter into human imagination, must in this manner be reduc'd to nothing."

I can see how the first half of my cousin's summary tracks this passage, but I am not sure the rest follows. Is it fair to read the passage as showing that total suspension of judgment is the intellectually honest stance, and that Hume's turn to nature and habit is a way of abandoning an argument he cannot answer? Or is my cousin converting a description of what reason does when it reflects on itself into a recommendation about what we ought to believe? I would be grateful for a careful audit of his summary: which parts the passage genuinely supports, which parts overstate or shift the point, and whether the "failure of nerve" conclusion is actually in the text.
