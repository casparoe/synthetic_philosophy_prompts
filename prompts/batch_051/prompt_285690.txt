Been going down a rabbithole on the Humeans-vs-anti-Humeans debate about laws of nature, and the analogy I keep seeing people use to make anti-Humeanism feel intuitive is that the laws of nature are the universe's source code. On that picture the code isn't just a description: it's what actually makes the machine do what it does, so laws have genuine oomph, not just bookkeeping. The Humean retort I've seen treats a law more like a function you reverse-engeneered from a giant log file—handy compression, but it doesn't run anything. I want a careful audit of how far this source-code analogy can actually be pushed. Give me a mapping (what's the interpretor? the program state? what would a crash or an unhandled exception correspond to?), then spell out where the mapping breaks, and especially what the phrase 'source code' quietly imports—like an author, a compiler, or the idea that the rules execute in step with the events. The bottom line I'm after: does the anti-Humean conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is the analogy doing most of the work for it? If the Humean side has a better programming analogy than log-file compression, I'd want that too.
