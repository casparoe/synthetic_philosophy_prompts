I'm working on a novel about a bioengineer who designs organisms from scratch, and I went down a research hole on what makes a biological trait a function. The view that's stuck with me is the etiological (selected-effects) one: a part's function is what it was selected for. Hearts pump blood and that's their function because pumping is what made hearts spread; the thumping sound is just a side effect. This is supposed to fix the problem with the causal-role view, which says a function is whatever a part does for the system, but then a tumor's function is killing its host and the norms come out wrong. Now here's the development I keep chewing on: my bioengineer builds organisms with no ancestors, so theres no selection history at all. Her pump circulates solvent, her light organ signals, and she treats them as functioning or malfunctioning. On the etiological story, strictly, these aren't functions at all until selection has had time to act, which sounds like the view has just decided in advance that synthetic biology is doing function-talk wrong. Does the argument for the etiological account survive this, or does the development show it was leaning on a premise it shouldn't have? Which premise does the development bear on, and if I repair it by treating the designer's choice as a kind of selection, am I saving the view or smuggling in intelligent design by another name?
