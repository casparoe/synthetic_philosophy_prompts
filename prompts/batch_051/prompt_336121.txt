I'm a software engineer and I don't have any formal philosophy background, but lately I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether suicide can ever be rational/morally ok. He kept saying it's always wrong, so I started reading David Hume's essay *Of Suicide* to see what the other side actually says. The essay has this move near the beginning where Hume says that if suicide is a crime, it must be one against God, against our neighbours, or against ourselves, and then he checks each category. I tried to turn that into a numbered argument so I could test it, because my instinct is that a conclusion that makes people this uncomfortable must have a hidden bug somewhere. Here's my reconstruction:

(1) If suicide is always wrong, then every act of suicide must be wrong either because it's a transgression against God, or because it's a transgression against other people, or because it's a transgression against oneself.

(2) It can't be a transgression against God, because God governs the world through general laws, and human choices about their own lives happen inside those laws. If it's okay to divert a river, remove a limb, or refuse treatment, then ending one's life isn't a special intrusion into God's business.

(3) It can't be a transgression against other people, because dying removes you from society; it doesn't actively injure society unless you have specific duties to particular people, like young kids or creditors. And most people, Hume says, are at best a burden to society or at least nobody has the right to keep them alive against their will.

(4) It can't be a transgression against oneself, because if a person's future is likely to contain more misery than happiness, then prudence itself could recommend ending that life, the same way prudence recommends avoiding any other foreseeable harm.

So it follows that suicide isn't always wrong; sometimes it can be a reasonable choice someone makes. The steps sound individually plausible to me, but I can't tell if I've built the argument correctly or if I'm just bad at spotting the trick. Can you tell me: is this argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails and what would a corrected version of that step look like?
