A friend and I spent last evening arguing about Gilbert Ryle's account of category mistakes in The Concept of Mind, and by the end he had constructed an argument that seems to me to prove too much. I cannot accept the conclusion, but I also cannot see which step fails. I would most like to keep Ryle's diagnosis of the Cartesian picture and the plain truth of ordinary mental-state claims.

Here is the argument as he laid it out.

(1) In The Concept of Mind, Ryle argues that the Cartesian picture goes wrong by treating the mind as an additional thing over and above a person's conduct and dispositions—the way someone goes wrong when he has seen the colleges, libraries, playing fields, and administrative offices of a university and then asks to be shown the university as if it were one more institution alongside them.

(2) A category mistake occurs when an expression belonging to one logical category is treated as if it belonged to another, and when that happens the expression fails to pick out any item of the kind the speaker supposes.

(3) If the theoretical Cartesian use of the word mind involves a category mistake, then there is no such thing as the mind to which beliefs, pains, desires, and the rest could belong.

(4) But ordinary mental-state attributions—for instance, that she believes it will rain, or that he has a sharp pain—are true only if there is a mind, or mental states, that the relevant predicates answer to.

(5) Therefore, from (3) and (4), ordinary mental-state attributions cannot be true.

(6) Yet some such attributions plainly are true: my friend and I held beliefs about Ryle during our argument, and one of us had a headache for half of it.

(7) So Ryle's category-mistake diagnosis, if accepted, forces us to deny the existence of beliefs and pains, which is absurd. Therefore the argument must go wrong somewhere.

Which premise or step do you reject? Explain why that premise looked fine, and what rejecting it costs. In particular, if the flaw is in (4), say how Ryle can allow ordinary mental-state sentences to be true without thereby reintroducing the very ghost he meant to exorcise.
