I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind: sixteen students, seventy-five-minute sessions, no special equipment. Next session is on split-brain patients and the unity of consciousness, and I want to open with a structured in-class game rather than a lecture. The activity should make students feel the force of the central problem before any terms are handed to them: a person can act on information that the part of them that speaks does not have, and yet the speaking part immediately produces a fluent, confident explanation of the action — an explanation that can be straightforwardly wrong. The idea I want left hanging in the room is the question whether this is one conscious subject with divided access, two subjects, or one subject whose verbal self-report is best understood as post hoc storytelling.

Design the game for me. Give me: roles and their constraints, with one role acting on information the speaking role lacks and the speaking role scored for fluency and coherence rather than accuracy; printable or improvable materials; a trial-by-trial procedure; a point scheme that needs no real money and is set up so that confabulation, rather than honest uncertainty, is the rewarded behavior; and a 75-minute timeline with setup, three or four role-rotating rounds, and a debrief. The pressure should come from the rules and incentives, not from anything I say; I should not use the words "confabulation" or "unity of consciousness" until the debrief.

The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto (i) the distinction between unified access and unified subjectivity, (ii) confabulation as something the students just did under incentives, and (iii) the one-subject and two-subjects readings of the split-brain findings. It should be candid about what the game cannot reproduce — in particular, the patient's own sense of being one person and the actual neural facts — and tell me what I need to supply explicitly afterward. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, such as the speaking role refusing to fabricate or the roles signaling covertly, and how to adjust the rules so a failure becomes part of the lesson rather than a ruined run.

Finally, include a short note on where your design leans on a contested reading of the split-brain literature, and say which empirical result or interpretive argument would most change how you would frame the game or its debrief. I ask because I want the students to meet the strongest version of the one-subject view, not a caricature of it.
