Write a five-stanza pantoum, twenty lines in all, about an information cascade. In a pantoum, the second and fourth lines of each stanza become the first and third lines of the next stanza, and the final stanza returns the poem's first and third lines as its second and fourth, so the whole poem is woven from a small, inherited stock of lines. Use that recycling structure to enact the cascade rather than merely describe it.

Set the poem at a queue of people arriving one by one at a fork and choosing between two doors, trails, wells, or some other pair of options that look close to indistinguishable. Each arrival carries a private hunch about which option is right, but can see where everyone ahead has chosen to go. Let the recurring lines play the role of publicly observed history — the earlier choices the next arrival cannot ignore — and let the newly introduced lines play the private signal, the hint the individual alone receives. In each stanza, show the decision being made: the private line is fitted into, and in the end disciplined by, a structure the inherited lines have already fixed. The form should make visible why a rational person can set aside their own signal because the accumulated weight of observed choices outweighs it.

Be accurate to the model. The herding should be individually rational — every arrival is reasoning from the evidence available, not mindlessly copying — but path-dependent: the first few choices set the direction, and the crowd can lock onto the wrong option while no single member is credulous. Let the wrong option win, and let the closing stanza's return to the opening lines feel like the lock clicking shut. But keep the poem a poem, not an allegory with labels: do not name the concept, do not moralize about conformity or lemmings, and leave the reader holding both facts at once — the outcome is wrong, and everyone in the line was being sensible.
