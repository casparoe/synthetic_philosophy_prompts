One of my university's committees—the Committee on Religious and Spiritual Life—decides which student organizations receive formal standing as religious or spiritual organizations. Recognition carries real consequences: recognized groups get a house on the campus's religious-life corridor, priority access to the interfaith chapel, and an exemption from a policy that restricts non-curricular events on certain holidays. I sit on a student advisory board that the Committee consults, so I have followed its decisions closely and want to work out the rule it is actually applying.

The Committee's recent decisions are these:

- It recognized the Jain Student Association, which runs vegetarian dining programs and holy-day observances and teaches that karma and liberation from the cycle of rebirth are central truths.
- It recognized the Theravada Buddhist Fellowship, which holds meditation sittings and dhamma talks and teaches rebirth and the law of karma.
- It recognized the Shinto Shrine Association, which maintains a small shrine to the kami and holds seasonal festivals; the group has no single founder and no written moral code, but its members organize their year around the shrine's calendar.
- It denied the Campus Skeptics, which hosts lectures on scientific skepticism and critical thinking and has no shared creed, no communal ritual, and no metaphysical commitments.
- It denied the Stoic Fellowship, which reads Stoic authors and runs ethical self-examination exercises but has no communal worship and no shared creed. The Committee explained that studying an ancient philosophy, even one with an ethical program, is not by itself enough.

A new application is now pending from the Confucian Learning Society. It proposes twice-weekly study sessions on the Confucian classics, seasonal rites honoring teachers and ancestors, and a code of filial and civic virtue. In its application it states that members treat the rites as expressions of social memory and moral formation rather than as petitions to any gods or spirits, and it disclaims any belief in an afterlife or in supernatural beings.

I am not asking what you personally think ought to count as a religion. I am asking what decision would best cohere with the Committee's own record. Please lay out the candidate principles that fit all of the Committee's past decisions, state what each principle would say about the Confucian Learning Society, identify the closest precedent under each reading, and then give your recommendation—including a frank statement of what principle the Committee would be adopting under either outcome and, if you can, a further application (real or invented) whose outcome would cleanly separate the principles.
