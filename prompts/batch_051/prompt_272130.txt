I am in a course on classical Chinese philosophy, but my home discipline is economics, and throughout our unit on the debate between Mozi's impartial care (jian'ai) and the Confucian doctrine of graded love I have been mentally translating the arguments into game theory. I would like you to perform that translation rigorously and then tell me what it fails to carry over.

The passage I want the translation anchored in is from the Mozi, chapter "Universal Love II" (兼愛中), in the translation by W. P. Mei available on the Chinese Text Project:

But the gentlemen of the world would say: "So far so good. It is of course very excellent when love becomes universal. But it is only a difficult and distant ideal." Mozi said: This is simply because the gentlemen of the world do not recognize what is to the benefit of the world, or understand what is its calamity. Now, to besiege a city, to fight in the fields, or to achieve a name at the cost of death -- these are what men find difficult. Yet when the superior encourages them, the multitude can do them. Besides, universal love and mutual aid is quite different from these. Whoever loves others is loved by others; whoever benefits others is benefited by others; whoever hates others is hated by others; whoever injures others is injured by others. Then, what difficulty is there with it (universal love)? Only, the ruler fails to embody it in his government and the ordinary man in his conduct.

Set that against the Confucian position we have been reading, according to which care is properly graded: it begins with parents and kin and is extended outward, so a teaching that tells me to regard a stranger's father as my own is not just unrealistic but misdescribes the structure of virtue. My request has two parts. First, restate (a) Mozi's argument in the passage and (b) the Confucian graded-love objection using only the vocabulary I am genuinely fluent in: agents with utility functions, weights placed on others' welfare, discounting by social distance, reciprocity and conditional cooperation, repeated games, folk theorems, equilibrium selection, and the ruler's capacity to shift what is individually rational through incentives or norms. Second, give me the residue report. I want to know exactly which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in that vocabulary at all. In particular: is 'graded love' just a steep discount curve over social distance, or is something lost; is Mozi's claim that whoever loves others is loved by others a claim about equilibrium strategy profiles, about reputations, or about something else; and is the passage's appeal to what the superior encourages an incentive argument or a norm-shaping one? Conclude by telling me what the residue shows: whether my vocabulary has quietly taken Mozi's side, whether it exposes something under-specified in the classical positions, or both.
