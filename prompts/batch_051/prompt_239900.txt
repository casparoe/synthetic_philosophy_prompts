Over the past year I have been thinking about computationalism — roughly, the idea that the brain is a computer, or at least is best understood as one — and I have been trying to write down a test for when a physical system is genuinely computing rather than merely being described as if it were. I am a high school senior; I know some programming and I have read a little about Turing machines, but I have not read the academic literature, so the idea probably exists under a name I do not know. I call it the Rule-in-the-Wiring Test.

The test is this: a physical system counts as computing an algorithm in the strict sense only if two conditions hold. First, the system must contain some part whose role is to determine which transition to make next — a part that specifies, in effect, what the rule is. Second, that rule-specifying part must be separable from the rest of the system in the following way: you could change which rule is specified without rebuilding the hardware that carries the rule out. A laptop passes the test. The same processor can run a sorting program or a chess program because the sequence of operations is itself encoded in memory, in something like the fetch-decode-execute cycle as I understand it, and that sequence can be swapped without rewiring the chip. A waterfall fails the test. I can map the changing water level onto the digits of pi if I choose the mapping cleverly, but nothing in the waterfall specifies pi-computing as the rule, and no part of the waterfall could be replaced so that it computes square roots instead. The computation is in my interpretation, not in the waterfall.

Applying the test to the brain, I think the brain fails it. The brain certainly carries information and transforms inputs into behavior, and in some loose sense it has a program — the organization of its synaptic weights. But that program is not held in a separate and swappable store that directs a general-purpose processor. The structure that would be described as the program is the same structure that does the executing. There is no part of the brain that reads off a next step from one place and then performs it somewhere else, at least not in a way where the stored rule could be switched to an unrelated rule while the rest of the brain stayed the same. So the brain is a computer only in the weak, as-if sense in which a waterfall is one.

I can already see one objection. My laptop is also just electrons following physical law, and no electron knows whether it is data or an instruction. So my test may either exclude the laptop too, or it may secretly assume that some physical states are instructions simply because we intend to treat them that way. I want to avoid that by saying the instruction parts are picked out by their counterfactual role: if the relevant bits in memory had been different in the right way, the machine would have followed the correspondingly different rule. But I am not sure this reply works, since many physical systems have that kind of input-sensitivity for the wrong reason.

What I would like is an honest assessment. Is this test coherent? Is it the same as a known distinction — something about stored programs, special-purpose versus general-purpose hardware, or the debate over whether every physical system trivially implements every computation? Does it capture a real difference, or is it just a definition rigged to exclude brains from computing? What does it get wrong, and which of its problems are fatal rather than fixable? When you answer, please separate what is actually settled among people who work on this from what is still genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
