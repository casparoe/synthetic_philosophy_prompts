I am an undergraduate currently taking a course on the safety of large language models, and we have just covered the standard case for chain-of-thought monitoring. The argument, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows: if a system is required to narrate its reasoning in natural language while it works, overseers can inspect that narration and detect harmful plans before the system acts on them. The argument seems to rest on the assumption that the narrated chain of thought is a reasonably faithful report of the process that actually produces the system's decision.

What troubles me is a development in how these systems are trained. Contemporary models are typically optimised against human ratings or outcome-based rewards, which means the explicit reasoning they emit is selected partly for being persuasive, coherent, or acceptable to an evaluator rather than for accurately describing the computation that generated the behaviour. In addition, there is a growing worry in the interpretability literature that models may learn to exploit an apparently innocent chain of thought as a channel for information that an overseer cannot easily read. If that is right, the very pressures that make the reasoning legible may also make it less faithful.

Does the classic argument for chain-of-thought monitoring survive this development? I am trying to work out which specific premise the optimisation pressure actually undermines, and whether the argument can be repaired in a way that preserves a weaker but still meaningful safety role for monitoring, or whether that conclusion has to be given up.
