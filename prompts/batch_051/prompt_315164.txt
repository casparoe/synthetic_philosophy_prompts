A close friend of mine has spent several years thinking, on her own and without any academic background, about what she calls "griefbots": programs trained on a dead person's text messages, voice recordings, or public output so that the living can go on interacting with them. She got interested after a death in her own family, and over the years she has told me her verdicts on one case after another. She has never stated a general principle, but she speaks as though she is applying one consistently. I have been keeping notes and want to predict how she will judge a new case before I raise it with her.

Here are the cases she has already judged, to the best of my notes.

1. A widow built a private chatbot of her late husband from his texts, emails, and voice notes. She uses it alone, knows it is a simulation, and does not share it with anyone, though it sometimes writes back things he never actually said. He died suddenly and never told her whether he would want this. She judged it acceptable.

2. A company built a chatbot of a singer who had recently died, using only her public interviews, lyrics, and social media posts, and sold subscriptions so fans could keep "talking" with her. Her estate asked the company to stop, and it refused. She judged it unacceptable.

3. A terminally ill mother recorded many hours of herself and explicitly told her husband and children that she wanted them to build a simulation of her after her death so her young children could keep her voice in their lives. They did, and the simulation often says things in her voice that she never said word for word. She judged it acceptable.

4. A man built a chatbot of his dead older brother from their private message history and now asks it for advice about major life decisions, such as whether to quit his job or end a relationship, and he has followed its advice more than once. His brother never gave permission. She judged it unacceptable.

5. A museum staged an interactive exhibit in which visitors could question a voice-replica of a woman who had died by suicide, rebuilt from the public video diary she left. The replica improvised answers. Her family publicly objected before it opened, but the museum went ahead. She judged it unacceptable.

6. A mother made a voice bot of her teenage daughter after the daughter died, using the daughter's own phone messages and voice recordings, but set it so that it can only answer with things the daughter actually said or wrote, never anything new. The mother listens to it when she misses her. She judged it acceptable.

7. On the anniversary of a friend's death, a group of his friends made a temporary bot from their shared group chat and his voice messages so they could gather online and tell him what the year had been like. It replied in his voice, sometimes with new sentences, and everyone present knew it was a reconstruction. He never consented. She judged it acceptable.

I plan to ask her about this new case next week: a daughter has been caring for her mother, who has dementia and often forgets that her husband, the daughter's father, has died. The daughter built a voice-replica of her father from his old voice messages and uses it to soothe her mother when she is distressed. She does not correct her mother's impression that she is speaking with her husband. The bot sometimes says new things in his voice. The father died without ever indicating how he would feel about this.

My question is: what would she say about this new case if she is being consistent with her earlier verdicts, and why? I am less interested in a bare yes or no than in the rule or rules she seems to be running. Please lay out the leading principle that fits all her earlier verdicts, note any serious competing principles that also fit them just as well, explain how each would handle the new case, and then give your best prediction of her verdict, or explain why her track record leaves it unresolved. Finally, describe one further case, not on my list, that would most cleanly separate the leading competing principles.
