I am preparing a graduate seminar on eighteenth-century theories of empire, and we are spending several sessions on Adam Smith's account of colonies in Book IV, Chapter VII, "Of Colonies," in An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations (1776). A student pressed me on the paragraph in which Smith contrasts the motives behind ancient Greek colonization with those of the later European settlements in America and the West Indies, and I would like a careful explication before I return to the text. The passage reads as follows:

"All the different states of ancient Greece possessed, each of them, but a very small territory, and when the people in any one of them multiplied beyond what that territory could easily maintain, a part of them were sent in quest of a new habitation in some remote and distant part of the world; the warlike neighbours who surrounded them on all sides, rendering it difficult for any of them to enlarge very much its territory at home. The colonies of the Dorians resorted chiefly to Italy and Sicily, which, in the times preceding the foundation of Rome, were inhabited by barbarous and uncivilised nations: those of the Ionians and Eolians, the two other great tribes of the Greeks, to Asia Minor and the islands of the Egean Sea, of which the inhabitants seem at that time to have been pretty much in the same state as those of Sicily and Italy. The mother city, though she considered the colony as a child, at all times entitled to great favour and assistance, and owing in return much gratitude and respect, yet considered it as an emancipated child over whom she pretended to claim no direct authority or jurisdiction. The colony settled its own form of government, enacted its own laws, elected its own magistrates, and made peace or war with its neighbours as an independent state, which had no occasion to wait for the approbation or consent of the mother city. Nothing can be more plain and distinct than the interest which directed every such establishment."

What I would like help understanding is the precise work done by the figure of the "emancipated child." On its face, Smith is saying that the Greek mother city retained a moral bond — favour, gratitude, respect — while renouncing any "direct authority or jurisdiction" over the colony. Is this meant as a description of the Greeks' own practice and self-understanding, or is Smith also endorsing it as the sound way to order a relationship between a metropolis and its settlements? And how should this passage be read against the Roman case that immediately follows, in which colonies are proposed, at least in part, as a remedy for domestic inequality and as an outlet for a conquering state? I am trying to see how far this ancient contrast prepares the ground for Smith's later treatment of the modern European empires, where the colonies come to be regulated less as emancipated children than as dependent markets or customers. Please conclude by stating, in a single sentence beginning "Summary:", the main point of the passage as you interpret it within Smith's larger argument.
