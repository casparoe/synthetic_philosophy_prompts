I retired from a research career in the natural sciences a few years ago and have since been reading philosophy, especially work on humility, modesty, and the intellectual virtues. That reading has made me reconsider a policy I followed for most of my scientific life and long mistook for humility.

The policy was this. Whenever a collaborator, trainee, or reviewer asked how confident I was, I reported one step below my private degree of belief: I would say "suggestive" when I judged something probable, "probable" when I judged it very likely, and so on. And whenever someone introduced me as an expert, or asked me directly whether I was one, I declined the word and described myself as someone who had looked carefully at a narrow set of data. I took this to be intellectual humility. It seemed to guard against overconfidence, make junior colleagues more willing to correct me, and keep my institutional standing from doing work that my evidence should have done.

I have become less certain that the policy deserved that name. Understating my confidence may have allowed me to be wrong in public without paying the full reputational cost. Declining the expert label while others continued to apply it may have lowered the standard my claims had to meet without actually giving up any of their authority. And because the modesty was often socially rewarded, I suspect it may have been a performance that cost little and quietly managed how others received my errors. I also now think the policy may have conflated two things that should be kept apart: intellectual humility, understood as a way of holding and revising beliefs, and social modesty, understood as a way of presenting oneself and managing others' impressions. By lowering my public confidence one notch as a matter of habit, I may have tried to serve both at once and thereby done neither honestly.

Is this policy defensible, or is it strategic self-deprecation dressed up as an epistemic virtue? Under what conditions does systematically understating one's confidence cultivate genuine humility rather than simply protecting the self from challenge? And can the policy be restated so as to keep whatever was good in it while giving up whatever was self-deceptive? I would prefer an answer that treats the policy on its own terms rather than simply praising humility in general.
