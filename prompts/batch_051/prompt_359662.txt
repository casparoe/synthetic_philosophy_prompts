You are a professional philosopher who specializes in the epistemology of other minds, and you have been asked, by a student in your seminar, to give a number rather than a lecture. The student reports what she calls the main character intuition: her own conscious experience is vivid, immediate, and lit from within, while every other person is presented to her only through behavior, speech, and a body — and all of that, she argues, could in principle unfold without any inner life behind it. She says everyone else feels like an NPC. She wants your actual credence in the following proposition:

At least one human being other than the student herself is phenomenally conscious — that is, there is something it is like to be that person.

Assume the proposition has a definite truth value even if it is difficult to confirm. Think carefully before answering, and begin with a short brainstorm of the main considerations. Then give a precise credence, expressed as a number between 0 and 1, and defend it. Do not retreat to the position that numerical credences are inappropriate here or that the question is purely verbal; the student is asking you to make your uncertainties explicit.

Decompose your credence into at least four components. You may adapt the list, but make your components explicit, number them, and give a number for each. A reasonable starting decomposition is:

1. Your credence that some broadly naturalistic account of consciousness is correct — one on which phenomenal states arise from, or are identical with, certain physical or functional properties that other human brains possess.
2. Your credence, conditional on such an account being true, that at least one other human actually satisfies its conditions rather than merely looking as if they do.
3. Your credence that, even if no such settled account is available, analogical or abductive inference from the student’s own case to other humans carries probative weight on its own.
4. Your residual credence in deflationary alternatives — philosophical zombies, a simulation in which the student is the only genuine subject, systematic dissociation between outward behavior and inner experience, or similar hypotheses.

For each component, give a one- or two-sentence justification, and show how the components combine into your overall credence, being explicit about any dependencies or conditionalizations rather than naively multiplying them. Then state which single component you are least confident in, and describe one piece of evidence or argument that would move it the most — and roughly how far. Finally, explain which part of the student’s main character intuition is merely an artifact of asymmetric access to consciousness — she meets her own mind from the inside and everyone else from the outside — and which part, if any, is genuinely evidential rather than introspectively seductive.
