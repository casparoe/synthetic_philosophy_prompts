I am the best man at my brother's wedding next month, and I have been staring at a blank page trying to write the toast. My brother is a moral philosopher whose signature view is a fairly strict pessimism about moral testimony: roughly, he holds that you should not form moral beliefs by taking someone else's word for it, because moral knowledge requires working through the reasons yourself rather than holding the right conclusion on credit. He lives this out in ways that are endearing and occasionally inconvenient — he once refused to tell a lost tourist whether it would be wrong to cut through a private garden, and instead walked him through the relevant considerations until the man missed his train.

I would like you to write the toast, about five minutes long (roughly 600–700 words), warm and genuinely funny. The room will include half a dozen of his colleagues who will catch every reference, and a much larger group of family members who will not, so the speech has to work for both without becoming a lecture. The hard constraint is the one I cannot get past: he would consider it a failure of respect if his own best man's toast engaged in moral testimony. That means the speech cannot tell the assembled guests that the couple is good, that the marriage ought to be celebrated, that they should feel joy, that the groom has some virtue or that the bride does — none of the moralizing a toast normally falls back on. It has to be affectionate, occasion-appropriate, and structured by the very view it cannot state: it should show, through memory and description, what it refuses to assert on authority. The humor should come from the predicament of having to praise someone while obeying his own prohibition on moral deference, and it should land as affection rather than as a philosophy lesson in disguise.
