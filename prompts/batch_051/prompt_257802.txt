I'm a science and ideas reporter, and I've spent the last few months following a county historical society that's trying to redo an exhibit about a 1919 labor clash — the kind of local history where the written record (company files, a newspaper item of the day, a trial transcript) says one thing, and the stories families have passed down say something sharply different about who started the violence and who should count as the heroes. The museum's solution is to display the archival account and the remembered account side by side, under a single label saying the museum does not arbitrate between memory and the record. When I asked the director why, she described it as honoring both ways of knowing, and the community has mostly praised the policy while a couple of local historians have grumbled that the museum is dodging it's job.

I want to write a column unpacking what this practice is actually committed to philosophically. What does the side-by-side display presuppose about the relation between collective memory and history — in particular about which one, if either, should give way when they conflict? Does it presuppose that collective memory is a different kind of claim about the past (say, about identity and meaning) rather than a rival factual claim that could simply be wrong? Does the policy commit the museum to a constructivist view of historical truth, or could someone who believes in a single, objective past run the very same display in good faith? And is it internally coherent when the two accounts flatly contradict each other, or does refusing to arbitrate smuggle in a decision it refuses to name? I'd like you to distinguish what the policy strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with, and to flag the places where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.

Please write this as a column-style analysis, in American English. Near the end, tell me how confident you are in the claims you've made and single out the point you're least sure of. Then end with a last line, on its own, that is just a one-sentence summary of the deepest tension in the museum's stance.
