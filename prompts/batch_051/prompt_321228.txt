I am taking a graduate seminar on consciousness, and I have been unable to stop worrying about the conceivability argument against physicalism. As I understand it, the argument runs as follows:

(1) I can conceive of a being that is physically indistinguishable from me in every respect but has no phenomenal consciousness at all — a philosophical zombie.
(2) If I can conceive of a scenario, then that scenario is metaphysically possible.
(3) If zombies are metaphysically possible, then the complete physical truth about the world does not entail the phenomenal truth about the world, and physicalism is therefore false.
(4) So physicalism is false.

My difficulty is that this argument, taken at face value, seems to prove too much. The very same principle in (2) appears to license conclusions I know are false. I can conceive, in some loose sense, of a substance that looks, tastes, and behaves exactly like water but is not H2O: it fills the lakes, falls as rain, quenches thirst, and is nonetheless a different chemical kind. If the principle in (2) is unrestricted, it follows that there is a possible world in which water is not H2O — and therefore that water is only contingently H2O. But that conclusion is false: being H2O is not a feature water merely happens to have, since nothing that failed to be H2O could be water. So the principle that drives the zombie argument also drives a conclusion I cannot accept, and I do not see how to keep one while blocking the other.

I have tried to save the zombie argument by restricting (2): perhaps the relevant kind of conceivability is not mere imagining, but conceivability that survives ideal rational reflection on all the relevant non-phenomenal facts. On that restriction, the water case is excluded because reflection plus chemistry shows that the watery stuff I was imagining would not be water at all; my initial conception rested on a false belief about what water is. But I cannot tell whether this restriction is principled or whether I am simply gerrymandering the principle to protect the conclusion I want. And I cannot show that the zombie conception would survive ideal reflection in a way the water conception would not. Where exactly does the original argument go wrong, and is there a restricted version of (2) that lets the zombie argument through while blocking the water argument? If I must choose, I find myself most willing to give up the unrestricted reading of (2), but I want to know what giving it up costs and whether the zombie argument can survive its loss.
