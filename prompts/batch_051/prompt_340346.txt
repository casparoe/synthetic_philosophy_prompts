My social epistemology tutor keeps marking cases as epistemic injustices or not, and if so which kind. Her rulings so far:

- A care assistant with a thick Sheffield accent reports that a patient hit her; the ward sister says Maria always has a story, so it gets filed as low-level. Tutor: testimonial injustice.
- A woman in the 1970s tries to complain that her boss keeps cornering her and touching her. The panel aren't hostile, but they can't get a grip on what she's describing, so it's written off as a personality clash. Tutor: hermeneutical injustice.
- A new mother in the 1950s tells two doctors she feels nothing for the baby and can't stop crying. They're not dismissive; they just can't make sense of it, and say rest will sort it. Tutor: hermeneutical injustice.
- A Black patient describes chest pain almost word-for-word like the white patient before him; the consultant marks him as exaggerating. Tutor: testimonial injustice.
- A GP disbelieves all patients equally, whatever their background; he dismisses a complaint of fatigue. Tutor: not an epistemic injustice, just a bad doctor.

The case for next week is a student who tells her psychiatrist that her boyfriend keeps denying things he did and saying she's imagining it, and that she thinks she's being gaslit. The psychiatrist writes in the notes that she has a suggestible personality and has picked up concepts from the internet; he's long held that young women pathologise ordinary relationship conflict. What does my tutor call this one — testimonial, hermeneutical, both, or neither? What rule about the testimonial/hermeneutical line do her earlier rulings fit, what would each plausible version of that rule say here, and what extra case would settle which one she's actually running?
