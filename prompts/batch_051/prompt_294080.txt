No single case settles what a theory of naked statistical evidence is for, and I do not want another theory that is built around one example and quietly adds epicycles for the others. My request is upstream of that: give me the specification, the desiderata that any adequate theory of when bare statistical evidence may justify an adverse finding or treatment must satisfy. By bare statistical evidence I mean a base rate or reference-class frequency that raises the probability that the defendant is the party who did the relevant act, unsupported by anything that links the evidence to what this particular defendant did.

Set the theory's target to legal proof first: civil liability under a preponderance standard and criminal guilt under something like proof beyond a reasonable doubt. I also want the list to say whether the same requirements carry over to non-adjudicative reactions such as resentment, exclusion, refusing a transaction, or administrative risk-scoring; where they do not, mark the divergence and explain its source.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a theoretical taste. The list should let the theory take a sharp position on at least the following, though it need not be confined to them:
- the hit-and-run bus case, where the only link between the defendant and the accident is that the defendant operates a large majority of the buses on the route;
- the gatecrasher case, where an event operator can prove that a large majority of attendees did not pay and selects one attendee for a penalty on that basis alone;
- the prison-yard variant for the criminal standard, where a guard is killed in a yard holding many prisoners, the state can prove that all but one participated, but cannot show which one did not;
- the database cold hit, where a search over a large pool turns up exactly one forensic match with a small random-match probability, and nothing else pointed to the person before the search;
- the mixed form, where a base rate falls just short of the applicable threshold but is joined by an eyewitness whose reliability is itself only estimated from group frequencies;
- the opaque-score case, where a learned model outputs a high probability that a particular person breached a contract, and no one can recover which features of that person drove the score.

Throughout, note which distinctions the theory cannot simply take for granted: the distinction between the probability that a piece of evidence is accurate and the probability that the defendant did the act, the distinction between civil preponderance and criminal reasonable doubt, and the distinction between reference-class frequency and a probative link to this defendant's conduct. If one of those distinctions is not a real joint, say why it may be collapsed rather than merely stipulating that it may be.

Then do the analytic work I care about. Mark which criteria are non-negotiable for any theory worth taking seriously and which are only the fingerprints of a particular camp. Flag any pair or subset of the desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say what it would mean if they cannot. Finally, identify the single requirement that does the most eliminative work, the one that rules out the largest share of otherwise attractive theories. Keep the scoring of named positions short or skip it; I am asking for the standard, not the standings.
