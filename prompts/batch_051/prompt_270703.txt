I am a novelist writing a book whose protagonist, a statistically educated man of private means who has begun to lose his fortune, persuades himself that he has found a wager that cannot fail. It matters to the novel that his reasoning be genuinely seductive, so I want to know exactly where it goes wrong before I let another character undo it. The argument I have written for him runs as follows.

(1) A gambler should accept any wager that increases his expected wealth.

(2) Let a fair coin be tossed. On heads the gambler's wealth is multiplied by 3/2; on tails it is multiplied by 3/5.

(3) The expected multiplier on a single toss is (3/2 + 3/5)/2 = 21/20, so each toss raises expected wealth by five per cent over declining to play.

(4) Successive tosses are independent, so the expected multipliers compound: after n tosses, the gambler's expected wealth is (21/20)^n times his original fortune.

(5) As n grows, this expectation grows without bound. The longer he plays, therefore, the better off he is, in the only sense a rational gambler is obliged to respect.

(6) Consequently, he should be willing to pay any per-round fee short of five per cent of his current wealth for the privilege of continuing, and the game is, to rational eyes, an inexhaustible source of money.

(7) This cannot be right. After n tosses the gambler's actual wealth is (3/2)^h (3/5)^(n−h) times his original fortune, where h is the number of heads. By the strong law of large numbers, h/n tends to one half, so along the typical path the nth root of his total multiplier tends to (3/2)^{1/2} (3/5)^{1/2} = √(9/10), which is less than one; his fortune therefore tends to zero, even while the expected wealth after n tosses rises as (21/20)^n without bound.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I had to choose, the character would rather retain (1) and fault something in the move from a single favourable round to an unboundedly favourable sequence, but I do not want to spare him at the cost of the novel's intellectual honesty. I should like to know which step fails and what a coherent repair would look like.
