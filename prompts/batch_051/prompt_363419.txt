I am a student in a formal epistemology course, and we have been working through the problem of updating on conditionals, often introduced as the Judy Benjamin problem: the difficulty that ordinary Bayesian conditionalization does not readily apply when new information arrives as a constraint on a conditional probability rather than as an observed event. For a term project I want to turn this material into a written protocol for the search-and-rescue organization I volunteer with, and I would like you to produce that protocol.

The organization works as follows. The search area is divided into two valleys, West and East, and each valley is divided into an upper sector above a waterfall and a lower sector below it, giving four cells: WU, WL, EU, and EL. At the start of an active search the planning coordinator assigns a probability to each cell using terrain, last known position, weather, and witness reports. During the search, fragmentary information arrives several times a day. Many of these reports are conditional in form: they do not identify a cell, but they constrain a conditional probability, for instance that if the subject is in the West valley, the probability of being above the falls is now 0.2. The coordinator must revise the four-cell distribution and use it to allocate a limited number of searchers to sectors, and the revision must normally be completed within ten minutes by trained volunteers who have had one semester of basic probability but no background in formal epistemology. The operations chief signs off on every revised distribution and will not accept a procedure that is not transparent and reproducible.

Include the following running case as one of the worked examples. The prior distribution is P(W)=0.4, P(E)=0.6, P(U|W)=0.8, and P(U|E)=0.3, so the joint distribution is P(WU)=0.32, P(WL)=0.08, P(EU)=0.18, and P(EL)=0.42. A report then states that if the subject is in the West valley, the probability of being above the falls is 0.2. Show exactly how the procedure revises all four cells.

Please write this as a reusable, step-by-step decision procedure: what the volunteer records from each report, which parts of the distribution the rule allows to change and which it holds fixed, how the arithmetic is performed, and how the result is checked for coherence. Commit to one defensible update rule for conditional information and build the procedure around it, explaining in plain language why that rule is being used rather than merely assumed. Then apply the procedure to at least two further cases: one in which a quantitative report revises the East-valley conditional instead, and one in which the report is only qualitative, such as saying that the subject is more likely than not to be below the falls if in the West valley. Make sure at least one of the worked cases yields an update the operations chief will find counterintuitive, and say which case that is and why the procedure licenses it. Finally, mark the places where the procedure cannot settle the matter and a human must exercise judgment, such as conflicting reports, reports from sources of uncertain reliability, or conditionals whose antecedent currently has probability zero.
