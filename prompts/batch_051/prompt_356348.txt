Write a decision tree for a seminar on the philosophy of mathematical practice, aimed at advanced undergraduates who have completed abstract algebra and point-set topology. The seminar's question is: what makes a mathematical definition the right one, as opposed to merely a possible stipulation? The tree should take a practicing mathematician from a concrete, pre-philosophical answer about a particular definition to a named position about why definitions get chosen and what their correctness consists in. Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of labels. Work first from the axes along which reasonable mathematicians actually divide — for instance, whether a definition can be correct before it proves its worth, whether correctness is fixed by pre-existing facts or by downstream consequences, whether one definition can be strictly better than another, and whether the community's convergence is evidence of correctness or the whole story. Then locate the named positions in that space, and explicitly diagnose combinations of answers that are unoccupied: say whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The branch nodes must be questions a working mathematician can answer on the spot from a displayed case, without knowing which leaf the answer leads to and without any philosophical vocabulary. You must use at least the following cases as node material: (a) the group axioms — in particular whether a set with a merely associative binary operation is missing something, and whether the required identity element and inverses are discoveries or decisions; (b) the open-set axioms for a topology — why arbitrary unions and only finite intersections are permitted, and whether that choice is forced by anything; (c) compactness — why the open-cover definition won out over closed-and-bounded for general spaces, and whether the two are really in competition; (d) continuity — why the epsilon–delta definition replaced "drawable without lifting the pencil"; and (e) the primality of 1 — whether excluding 1 is correcting a mistake, changing the rules, or neither. You may add at most two further cases if the tree needs them.

The named leaves must include at least these six: definition-as-theorem-preserver (a definition is right when it makes the important theorems true and their proofs natural); definition-as-generalization (right when it unifies the largest family of core examples while excluding the agreed counterexamples); definition-as-natural-joint (right when it carves a structure that was already present in the mathematics, independently of our having named it); definition-as-minimal-structure (right when it is the weakest set of conditions that supports the theory built on it); definition-as-productive-convention (right when it is a stipulation justified entirely by the mathematics that follows); and definition-as-community-equilibrium (right when it is what the relevant mathematical community has converged on, so that history and pedagogy, not the object, are the arbiter). Add no more than two further leaves if the logical space requires them.

Throughout, phrase each node so that the leaf is not visible from the question; the sorting must be by beliefs about the cases, not by self-ascription. Flag every pair of leaves the tree cannot separate with the available questions, and state the further question that would separate them. If the tree has a question whose two answers lead to the same leaf, remove it or explain why it is doing structural work. End with a short table or annotated outline of the finished tree — question at each node, possible answers, leaf reached — and a note on which branch any unoccupied combinations hang from. Target 1,000–1,300 words.
