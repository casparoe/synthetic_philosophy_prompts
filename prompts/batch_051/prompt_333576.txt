I have spent the past several months drawing a typeface, and the obstacle that keeps recurring is conceptual rather than aesthetic: what makes every 'a' an 'a' across single-storey and double-storey forms, every weight, and every hand? Before I read any more proposals, I want the desiderata that a theory of letterform identity would have to meet to be adequate to reading, type design, and classification — not the theory itself, but the full specification of what such a theory must deliver. Give me the requirements list: the cases it has to handle (the single-storey and double-storey 'a'; a vertical stroke that can be a lowercase 'l', a capital 'I', or the digit '1'; degraded or partially occluded marks; novel glyphs no reader has seen before; the same visible outline functioning as different letters in different scripts, such as a Cyrillic 'Н' that is not a Latin 'H'); the distinctions it cannot collapse (a letter as an abstract role in the script versus a glyph as a concrete mark; identity versus style; recognizability versus legibility; script-relative identity versus shape-based similarity); and the constraints it cannot contradict (readers identify letters at a glance across enormous shape variation; small changes sometimes change the letter and sometimes do not; a script persists as 'the same letters' through centuries of drift). For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it and say whether it is negotiable or forced. Then flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and tell me which single requirement does the most damage to the usual candidates — a shared shape template, a prototype, a list of defining features, and pure convention. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, not on the scorecard.
