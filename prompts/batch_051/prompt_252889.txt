I was taught that a person counts as an objectionable free rider—not merely a benign outsider—when four conditions all hold: (1) she receives a benefit produced by an ongoing cooperative scheme; (2) she receives that benefit voluntarily, or at least knowingly, rather than having it forced on her; (3) she stands within the circle of people from whom the scheme can fairly demand a contribution; and (4) she withholds a fair-share contribution without an excuse the scheme itself recognizes, such as illness or poverty.

Now consider this case. A coastal neighborhood association maintains a shared seawall and boat dock, funded by member dues and regular workdays. The seawall protects the entire shoreline, including the homes of non-members; the dock is open to any neighbor on equal terms, because the bylaws allow non-members to use it without voting or doing workday labor. Ray refuses to join the association, refuses to pay dues, and calls the whole arrangement a racket, yet he berths his boat at the dock year-round. At the same time, Ray is a retired coastal engineer, and his public critiques of the seawall's design on the neighborhood forum have repeatedly forced the association to adopt repairs that were cheaper and stronger than what its paid consultants proposed. The association never asked for these critiques and finds them uncomfortable, but it keeps acting on them.

Is Ray an objectionable free rider on the association's scheme? Work through the four criteria one by one, indicate exactly where they underdetermine the verdict, and say whether the case shows that the criteria themselves need amending. Respond in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
