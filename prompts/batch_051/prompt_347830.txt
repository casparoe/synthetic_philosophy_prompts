As a novelist, I have spent two decades moving between planning novels in advance and writing them by discovery, and the result is a small theory I would like assessed without flattery. I call it the outlining illusion.

Let me distinguish two activities. Prospective planning is specifying, before drafting, the content and order of scenes, arcs, and revelations. Retrospective planning is the work done after drafting that makes the produced material look intended: cutting, resequencing, re-motivating, and revising earlier scenes so that later discoveries appear to have been foreseen. My claim is that in long-form fiction, most of the planning credited to the first activity is actually the second activity backdating itself. A writer drafts by feel, discovers that a scene was right in ways no outline anticipated, then revises earlier chapters so the discovery reads as a setup. On this view, the outline is not a template for the novel; it is a provisional fiction the writer needs in order to survive the first hundred pages, useful precisely because it can be betrayed.

If that is right, planning is overrated in a specific sense: the structural coherence of a finished novel is manufactured after the fact rather than executed from a prior design. The only prospective planning that truly matters is negative — the few advance constraints that rule out whole directions, not the ones that supposedly determine what the book will be.

I am aware this sounds convenient for a novelist who finds outlining difficult. So I would like an answer that first states the strongest version of my thesis, then tests it. Does it already exist under another name, and if so, what does the standard version say that mine leaves out? Which precise part of the view is false in a way that matters? And where does it fail outside fiction — say, in software design, architecture, or surgery, where backdating one's discoveries is not an available move?
