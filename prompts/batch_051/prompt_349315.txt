Consider the standard proof that every continuous real-valued function on a closed bounded interval is uniformly continuous. In outline: for a given ε, one covers the interval with neighborhoods on which the function varies by less than ε, then uses the fact that the interval is closed and bounded to pass to a finite subcover and extract a uniform δ. Keep this theorem and proof fixed throughout.

Give me a working definition of what it is to understand a proof, as distinct from being procedurally fluent at reproducing or verifying one, that correctly classifies the following six cases.

(1) A machine-checked formalization of the proof, certified correct down to the last inference, with no explanatory text attached.

(2) A student who, a week after the course, can reconstruct the proof from a one-sentence prompt, justify why each step is there, explain why the argument fails on (0,1), supply a small lemma the instructor left out, and transfer the same strategy to a theorem they have not seen proved this way.

(3) A student who can reproduce the proof verbatim and verify that every step follows, but cannot explain its overall strategy, cannot say where the proof uses that the interval is closed and bounded, and cannot adapt the argument to a closely related theorem.

(4) A student who cannot write out the formal estimates, but gives an accurate plain-language account of the strategy, points to the step that passes from an open cover of the interval to a finite selection of neighborhoods as the step that would fail on (0,1), and answers a conceptual follow-up question correctly.

(5) A student who can answer every question asked about the proof in a study session and can verify each line when it is shown, but cannot begin the proof without being given the first sentence.

(6) A mathematician who read the proof once years ago and now remembers none of the details, but can reconstruct the argument's structure with only minor stumbles and can modify it for a problem where they have never seen the technique used.

My initial, untutored sorting is: (1) clearly not understanding and (2) clearly understanding; I am genuinely uncertain about (3) through (6), and I suspect (4) is the hardest for any clean definition. Propose a definition, run it through all six cases, say where it gives a verdict you would reject or no verdict at all, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. End by identifying which case your final definition sorts least comfortably and why.
