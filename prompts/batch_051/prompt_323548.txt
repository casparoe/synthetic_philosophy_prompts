I've been reading Sartre's *Existentialism Is a Humanism* in the Philip Mairet translation, and one passage keeps snagging me:

For at bottom, what is alarming in the doctrine that I am about to try to explain to you is – is it not? – that it confronts man with a possibility of choice.

As a retired physicist, I'm used to keeping descriptive claims and normative claims cleanly seperated. Sartre seems to start with a metaphysical claim — there is no fixed human nature, so we make ourselves through our choices — and then slip into something like an ethical claim that we must own that freedom or act authentically. I don't see what licenses the shift. If existence precedes essence, there's no prior standard left to say that one response to freedom is better than another. Is this a fair version of the standard charge that existentialism is incoherent? Or is Sartre not trying to derive an "ought" from an "is" at all, because the normativity is somehow built into the structure of action or freedom itself?

I'd like help working out what he could actually be claiming in this lecture. If it helps, bring in the distinction between this 1946 lecture and *Being and Nothingness*, but only if it genuinely clarifies rather than just adding jargon. And if I've set the problem up badly — for instance, if Sartre's "authenticity" here isn't functioning as a moral ideal in the way I assume, or if the passage is doing different work than I think — say so plainly instead of trying to make my reading work. I'd also like to know how confident you can be about what Sartre actually held from this text alone.
