Im a junior and I sit on my school's Honor Board, the thing where students hear cheating and other conduct cases and recommend punishments to the dean. We see about fifteen cases a semester and usually get fifteen minutes per case after the facts are read out. Nobody on the board has real training, and the handbook only gives us ranges (zero on the assignment, Saturday detention, suspension, expulsion) without telling us how to choose. In a philosophy unit I read about retributivism and consequentialism: retributivists say the punishment should fit past wrongdoing because the person deserves it, consequentialists say the only point is to prevent future harm. My problem is we keep using both without noticing, so the same board will give one kid mercy because she's clearly embarrassed and says it won't happen again, and another kid a harsh penalty because cheating is cheating and there have to be consequences, with no consistent rule saying which lens to use when.

Give me a reusable checklist, not a one-time ruling. It should be something our board can actually run through in a hearing, with exactly five numbered steps (each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion). The steps should say what facts to establish first, how to apply the retributivist and consequentialist reasons next, when the two theories tell us different things and how to break the tie, and where the checklist honestly hands the decision to the dean as a values call. Also make each step say what a sufficient answer looks like and where it stops with a no. Thread two examples through the steps to show how they work: a kid who copied three sentences of a history paper in a panic, and a senior who ran a cheating ring on discord. Don't tell me which theory is better; I want the procedure to show how far the two theories can be followed before someone has to decide.
