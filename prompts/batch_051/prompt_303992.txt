David Hume, in his essay “Of the Origin of Government” (Essays, Moral and Political, Part I, Essay V), writes:

“Some extraordinary circumstances may happen, in which a man finds his interests to be more promoted by fraud or rapine, than hurt by the breach which his injustice makes in the social union. But much more frequently, he is seduced from his great and important, but distant interests, by the allurement of present, though often very frivolous temptations. This great weakness is incurable in human nature.”

Treat this passage as Hume’s diagnosis of the psychological mechanism underlying what later came to be called the tragedy of the commons: the benefits of a breach of restraint are vivid and present, while the harms are real but remote and diffuse. Now vary the canonical commons thought experiment in one specific way. Instead of a pasture in which each additional animal visibly and continuously diminishes the shared resource, suppose the commons is a threshold resource: the whole resource collapses irreversibly if aggregate use crosses a definite numerical limit, but each additional use only raises the probability that this threshold will be reached, and the collapse, if it occurs, arrives only after a delay of one season. Each actor knows all of this, and no individual act is perceived as the decisive cause. How does this variation interact with Hume’s claim that the remote interest loses out to the present temptation? Does it make the tragedy more or less robust than in the standard continuous-depletion model? And on Hume’s own account, what remedy would be appropriate here, and would the threshold-and-delay structure make that remedy easier or harder to sustain? In answering, say whether the standard analysis of the commons survives this variation, and at what cost to the claim that the weakness is incurable.
