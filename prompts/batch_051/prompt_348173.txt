I keep noticing a pattern in arguments about how much moral weight to give insects, and I want to know whether it has an established name and how to separate it from neighboring ideas.

The pattern has two moves. First, someone observes that insects are extraordinarily numerous — on the order of 10^18 individuals alive at any time. They then argue that this number swamps even a very low probability of sentience: unless the probability is drastically lower than anyone has reason to believe, the expected number of sentient insects is still astronomically large, so insect welfare should dominate any impartial calculation of expected harms. Second, sometimes in the very same body of work, the author dismisses practical proposals to protect insects on the grounds that insect sentience is too uncertain, or that policy should not be built on a small probability. The low probability is first treated as an input to be multiplied through and outweighed by vast numbers, then treated as a reason to set the issue aside.

I am not asking merely for a label. I want to know exactly what the first argument needs in order to work: its assumptions about probability estimates, about comparing harms, and about summing across beings whose very status as subjects is uncertain. I also want to know how it differs from Pascal's wager and from Pascal's mugging, and where the boundary lies between a legitimate use of large numbers and the kind of reasoning that would license absurd conclusions. If the full two-move pattern has no established name, please say so and propose a term, with a definition that distinguishes it from the mere inconsistency of applying one standard to insects and another elsewhere.
