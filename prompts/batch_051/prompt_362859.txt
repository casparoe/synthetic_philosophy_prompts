I recently read an essay on a philosophy-for-the-general-public site that argued there is something distinctively second-best about taking one's moral views from other people. I found it persuasive while reading it, but I don't know how much of it reflects what philosophers working on moral testimony actually think, and I don't trust my ear for where its claims outrun its argument. Here is how I would summarize the essay's argument: "Morality, unlike ordinary empirical matters, cannot be settled by consulting an expert. Even if another person were highly reliable at identifying which actions are right and wrong, there would still be something deficient about adopting their judgement instead of reaching your own. In doing so, you would be buying the conclusion without the reasons, and so you could at most acquire moral knowledge; you could never acquire moral understanding. Understanding is what matters in the moral domain, because it is the capacity to see for oneself why an action is wrong and to be moved by those reasons rather than by the fact that an authority said so. Since understanding cannot be transmitted from one person to another, moral testimony as a source of knowledge is ultimately an illusion. Listening to another person's reasons may still be useful, but only insofar as you critically work through them yourself rather than relying on the person's reliability." Could you go through this summary sentence by sentence and tell me what a philosopher defending the pessimistic view about moral testimony would accept, what they would wince at as an overstatement or a conflation, and what they would simply reject? I am especially unsure whether the conclusion that moral testimony is an illusion as a source of knowledge follows from anything true in the rest of the summary, or whether it slides from a claim about the value of moral understanding into a much stronger claim about the possibility of moral knowledge.
