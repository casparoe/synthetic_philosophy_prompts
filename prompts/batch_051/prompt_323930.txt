Two people I know got into an argument the other night about a third colleague, S, who admitted she felt envious when T got the promotion she had been passed over for. The first, M, said envy is a vice, full stop: T's gain wasn't a loss to S, so the feeling was just disliking someone else's advantage. The second, J, said that's too quick; envy can be a signal that who gets promoted is unfair, especially when the same people keep winning. M came back that Rawls actually cuts the other way: he distinguishes resentment, which is a moral response to an actual wrong, from envy proper, which is a non-moral reaction to another person's good, and he treats even understandable envy as a vice rather than a moral signal. J said sure, but Rawls also allows that envy can be excusable when it grows out of conditions that erode people's self-respect, and that a fairer scheme — like his difference principle — is meant to reduce the occasions for it; so the feeling can carry information about a bad background. M said excusable still doesn't mean justified, and turning envy into a signal of injustice flatters a vice into looking like a virtue. J said in practice people can't always seperate the two: under unfair conditions they may not know whether they hate the advantage itself or the unfairness of it. I couldn't referee them. Can you go through the exchange step by step and say who is right about what, and what both are missing? Please answer in British English.
