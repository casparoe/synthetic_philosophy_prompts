I've got a theory I've been carrying round for a while and I want it honestly assessed rather than just validated. I call it 'collapse as forgetting'. The claim is that what gets called societal collapse — the end of the Late Bronze Age palaces, the Classic Maya, Easter Island, that lot — is not fundamentally a loss of complexity or a material failure. It's a sudden, steep rise in the rate at which a society forgets how to reproduce itself. On this view a society just is a bundle of recipes: how to build a road to standard, who may move grain and when, how to write the language of the courts, how to run the irrigation system, when to perform the rituals. When those recipes stop being transmitted — the few people who carried them die and noone replaces them, or the background conditions they assumed stop holding — the society doesn't 'fall', it simply becomes unable to do tomorrow what it did yesterday. Collapse versus ordinary change is then just the shape of the forgetting curve: collapse is when decades worth of know-how vanish within a single generation; transformation is when it fades slowly enough to be replaced, or at least mourned. Resilience becomes nothing but the number of independent copies of a recipe.

I realise this sounds either trivially true or a little unhinged, and I want to know which. Has someone already worked this out under another name I should know about? Give me the strongest counterexamples: a case that looks like collapse but is not well described as forgetting, and a case of forgetting without collapse. And the harder question: does reframing collapse as an epistemic event — a society stops knowing — rather than a material one — a society stops being — actually do any work, or have I just relabelled the same thing?
