I am a PhD student in comparative political economy, and I have agreed to referee a paper for a graduate-student journal in my field. The paper compares two neighboring provinces that had broadly similar institutions, labor markets, and natural endowments around the turn of the twentieth century, but that have since diverged sharply in self-employment, firm formation, and capital accumulation. The author's central claim is that this divergence is explained by a durable cultural difference. One province, he argues, inherited from a particular dissenting religious movement an ethic that values punctuality, methodical work, and the deferral of consumption, while the other did not, and this difference in values is what does the explanatory work in his account. He is careful to distance himself from crude determinism: culture, he says, operates as a background disposition that shapes how people respond to incentives rather than as a force that overrides them.

My difficulty is that the evidence he offers for the cultural difference consists mostly of contemporary survey measures of work-related attitudes and savings behavior, drawn from the same populations whose diverging economic trajectories he is trying to explain. On his own data, the proposed cause and the predicted outcome look almost like two descriptions of one and the same pattern. I keep wanting to say that he has mistaken an effect for a cause, or that he has selected one element out of a self-reinforcing historical loop and labeled it the driver, but I cannot state the underlying issue precisely enough to be useful in a report. At the same time, I do not want to be the sort of reviewer who dismisses cultural explanations out of hand; I think it is implausible that values never matter for economic behavior.

When I write the referee report, how should I frame the problem? Is there a principled way to tell whether an explanation of this kind, one that assigns causal weight to a cultural value inherited from a religious movement, is a genuine causal explanation rather than a redescription of the outcome, and what would the author need to show to make his cultural story causally credible?
