I am drafting a novel whose central character is a moral theologian, and I want her beliefs about the relation between God and morality to be exact enough that the counsel she gives in the climactic chapters follows from principles she has already endorsed earlier in the book. To keep her from drifting into inconsistency, I have collected her settled judgments on a handful of cases. Asked whether God could command the torture of a child for amusement, she says the suggestion is not a real possibility: it is not that God's power is bounded from outside, but that the notion is internally incoherent, like a round square. Asked whether a woman who has never heard of God nevertheless does wrong when she betrays a friend, she says yes, and adds that the wrong can be known apart from any revelation. Asked whether calling God good merely restates the fact that God commands, she says no, since that would turn the ascription into an empty tautology rather than a substantive claim about God's character. Asked whether some obligations exist only because God has commanded them, she says yes: the choice of a particular fast day or a particular form of worship is indifferent before the command and binding after it.

The scene I am now writing requires her to counsel a young woman who has made a solemn promise of marriage and has become convinced that God is calling her to break the engagement and enter a monastic life. I want to know what the character is committed to saying, not what would merely sound edifying. In particular, is she committed to holding that the call, if genuine, by itself dissolves the promise because divine command constitutes obligation, or that the promise continues to bind unless it was invalid from the outset or is overridden by some independent and weightier duty, with the call serving as evidence at most? Lay out the candidate rules that fit her four settled judgments—the two horns of the Euthyphro dilemma and at least one attempt to soften or dissolve the dilemma—and say what each candidate predicts about the engagement case. Then give your best judgment about which rule she is actually running, identify which of the four earlier judgments does the largest work in excluding alternatives, and propose one further case, not simply a rewording of the engagement case, that would settle whatever ambiguity remains.
