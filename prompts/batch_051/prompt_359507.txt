In a philosophy of mind reading group, one participant defended the possibility of a colony-level mind by way of an analogy. The analogy, as she framed it, descends from Hofstadter's Ant Fugue and its character Aunt Hillary — an ant colony treated as a single thinking being even though its "thoughts" are nothing but ant-level traffic. Her version ran as follows. An ant colony is like a brain. Individual ants are the neurons; the chemical signals by which one ant excites, inhibits, or recruits another are the action potentials and synaptic transmissions; and the colony's large-scale patterns — foraging columns, nest construction, alarm cascades, division of labor — are the information processing. No single neuron is conscious, and no single neuron knows what the brain is doing; likewise, she argued, no single ant needs to know what the colony is doing for the colony as a whole to count as a mind. On the strength of this analogy, she concluded that a colony could in principle possess a mind even though none of its parts does, and that anyone who denies the possibility owes an account of where the analogy breaks.

I would like a systematic audit of exactly that: how far does the analogy hold, and how much of the conclusion survives it?

Please do the following. (1) Set out the full mapping between the two systems, extended beyond what she supplied where you can — for example, nest temperature regulation as homeostasis, colony fission as reproduction, and the queen as reproductive organ rather than controlling brain. A table of correspondences is welcome. (2) Identify the disanalogies and, for each, say whether it is a harmless difference of detail or a difference that matters for the intended conclusion. In particular, address at least these: ants are themselves autonomous, goal-directed agents with individual valence — they explore, fight, assess, and make choices — whereas neurons are not standardly credited with similar agency; the "connections" among ants are not fixed point-to-point synapses but chemical trails broadcast through a shared environment and continuously rewritten by the ants themselves, so the colony's "wiring diagram" is itself part of its behavior rather than a fixed substrate; and the colony, unlike a brain, has a colony-level life history that includes founding, growth, and reproduction by fission. (3) Give a verdict. Does the analogy support the possibility of a colony-level mind, or does it only make that possibility vivid? If it fails, say exactly which features of the source it quietly imports that the target lacks, and whether the conclusion is thereby shown false or merely unsupported by this analogy. (4) If a better analogy is available, give it and state what it would and would not establish about the original question.
