Over the years I have spent covering science and ideas, I have developed a reporting rule I privately call the Gödel quarantine. When a source, a startup manifesto, or an essay insists that Gödel's incompleteness theorems settle a question outside mathematical logic—for example, that artificial intelligence faces an in-principle ceiling, that human reasoning can never be mechanized, that the mind or the universe contains truths no formal system could capture, or that some institution is "undecidable" in the technical sense—I refuse to quote the claim unless I can see how the actual theorem is being applied: which formal system is at issue, how it contains enough arithmetic for the theorem to bite, what consistency assumptions are in play, and whether the conclusion follows from the theorem rather than from the metaphor. In practice, I quarantine the argument instead of amplifying it.

The rule has kept me from repeating a good deal of loose analogy, but I am aware that it is a heuristic, and a blunt one. I may sometimes dismiss careful philosophical arguments because they are hard to paraphrase, or treat my own inability to formalize a claim as evidence that there is nothing to formalize. My question is whether this policy is defensible as an epistemic habit rather than a bias with a sophisticated name. Where do the legitimate transfers of Gödel's theorems beyond pure mathematics actually live, and where do they shade off into misuse? And can someone who is not a working logician run this test reliably, or does the quarantine itself presuppose exactly the expertise I am trying to compensate for?
