In a reading group I attend on forecasting and grand strategy, last week's reading was about reference-class reasoning: using base rates from similar past cases rather than the details of the current case. In the discussion afterward, two members—Marcus and Elena—argued about whether that method can tell us anything useful about how much longer the present American-led international order will last. I was sitting to the side and genuinely could not decide who had the better of it.

Marcus spoke first. "Stop asking how the current order will end by reading the news; that is the inside view—assembling a story from current detail while ignoring base rates. Take the outside view instead. Look at the reference class of comparable great powers and hegemonic orders since antiquity. Their runs of dominance were mostly on the order of a century or two. The present order has already been dominant for roughly eight decades on the usual post-1945 dating, so the base rate alone should keep us from being confident that it is in an early phase; the prior ought to place substantial probability on its being mid-run or later. Anyone who wants to set that prior aside owes us a specific, quantified reason—not a story about resilience." Elena pushed back. "An outside view is only as good as its reference class, and you have not defined one that can do the job. Is Ming China in or out? Is the Soviet Union—a superpower that ended after roughly seven decades without being conquered—in the same class as Rome? Does Rome's run begin with the Republic, with Augustus, or somewhere else? Even the present run's start date is a choice: 1945 versus 1991 differs by half a century. If a handful of coding decisions moves the average by decades, you are not reading a base rate off the historical record; you are choosing categories until they say what you expected. Worse, 'the end of an order' has no agreed operational definition. Empires usually do not die on a single date; they demote, devolve, or transmute, so a lifespan measured without a fixed death event is not a well-defined quantity." Marcus came back. "Those coding worries are real, but they are not an argument for the inside view, because the inside view makes all the same choices silently. Every forecast has a reference class, explicit or implicit; the outside view forces it into the open, where it can be argued about. Fuzzy membership and vague endpoints are reasons to state the class carefully and test how the base rate shifts under alternative codings, not reasons to discard the base rate altogether. And the comparative point stands: the historical record exists to remind us that every dominant power has told itself an exceptional, open-ended story. The base rate is the corrective—it should anchor the prior and let case-specific information adjust the posterior. If you do not like my class, propose a better one; do not conclude that no class can inform us." Elena answered. "That comparative point would be decisive if the units were drawn from a stationary process, like completion times for the same kind of project. But they are not. The reference class is tiny, and its cases differ in the very mechanisms thought to produce decline: technology, the structure of the international system, sources of legitimacy, whether great-power war is suicidal. Ming China and Victorian Britain did not operate under nuclear deterrence or globalized production chains. So the stationarity assumption that makes borrowing base rates rational is exactly what is in doubt. I am not defending a complacent inside story; I am saying this class is too small and too heterogeneous to support a useful prior. Sometimes the honest outside view is that there is no outside view worth using, and one must build an explicit structural model instead." The session ended there, and I still cannot tell who is right.
