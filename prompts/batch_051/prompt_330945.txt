I am the secretary of a university's Mitigating Circumstances Committee. Each term the committee convenes a three-examiner panel to decide individual student cases; the stakes are high, because a finding of mitigating circumstances can change degree classifications, and the chair's written determination must survive appeal. The regulations direct each panel to record a simple yes/no judgement on three questions: (1) Is the supporting evidence genuine? (2) Did the disruption fall within the relevant assessment period and was it serious enough to have materially affected the student's performance? (3) Was the effect one that the student could not reasonably have avoided, for instance by deferring the assessment? The regulations state that mitigation is established exactly when all three answers are yes. The panel then takes a separate vote on the overall question: 'Are mitigating circumstances established?' We keep getting results of the following shape. Examiner A answers yes, yes, yes, and votes 'established'. Examiner B answers yes, no, yes, and votes 'not established'. Examiner C answers no, yes, yes, and votes 'not established'. A majority answers yes to each of the three questions, yet a majority votes 'not established'. Each panellist is applying the regulation logically; the inconsistency sits in the committee, not in any member. The examiners accuse the process of being irrational, and I have been asked to fix it.

Please write a reusable decision procedure for our panels, grounded in what the judgement-aggregation literature has established about the discursive dilemma and the comparative merits of premise-based aggregation (vote on the three questions and let the regulation determine the conclusion), conclusion-based aggregation (vote only on the overall question), and any related mechanisms — for example, giving certain questions priority, or treating the overall vote as decisive whenever it conflicts with the majority on the premises. The procedure must be expressible as a sequence of steps a chair can follow in under fifteen minutes without training in logic or voting theory, and it must specify: the default method and the conditions for switching; what to do about abstentions, ties, and panellists whose own answers are inconsistent; which disagreements are empirical, which are interpretive, and which are value judgements that no aggregation rule should settle; and what the chair may and may not do by way of further deliberation or re-voting to make the numbers come out coherent. Then run the procedure on two worked cases. In the first, use the voting pattern above. In the second, suppose Examiner A answers yes, yes, yes; Examiner B answers yes, no, yes; and Examiner C answers no, yes, no; and all three nonetheless vote 'not established' because they share a worry not named in the regulations — that granting this student's case would be unfair to two other students who submitted no evidence at all. Let the procedure say what should happen in that case, and flag any step at which it delivers an answer the panel will not like; finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do. Please write the whole answer in British English.
