I host a small history-of-ideas podcast, and next month I'm doing an episode on the turn Frederick Douglass made on the U.S. Constitution. For more than a decade he'd been in the Garrisonian camp, treating the Constitution as fundamentally proslavery and something abolitionists should not vote under or hold office under. Then, around 1851, he announced he'd changed his mind and spent the rest of his career arguing that the Constitution, read as its framers intended, is actually a document of freedom and an instrument against slavery. I want the interview to pressure-test that pivot, not just let him restate it. Treat Douglass as the guest, in first person.

Draft eight to ten questions for him, and after each question give the follow-up you would use against the answer he's most likely to give. I want the sequence to build, so each follow-up leads naturaly into the next question. Make sure the questions include at least these pressure points, and phrase them as much as possible in his own idiom — intentions of the framers, the letter and the spirit of the text, strict construction, slavery as a temporary compromise:

- how he handles the clauses that look plainly proslavery one by one: the three-fifths clause, the fugitive slave clause, the insurrection clause, and the twenty-year protection of the slave trade, and how he reads the fact that the word slavery never actually appears in the document;
- what he does with the fact that for the first seventy years the actual courts and Congress read the document the other way, especially after Dred Scott in 1857, and whether his reading requires him to say everyone else was misreading a fairly clear text;
- whether the change was really doctrinal or part of his split with Garrison and his move toward political abolitionism, and what changed his mind at the textual level;
- how his argument is different from what he was clearly borrowing from Spooner, Gerrit Smith, and the radical constitutionalists around them, rather than just adopting a convenient theory from his new allies;
- how much weight the Preamble is carrying, with its claim to speak for the whole people, and whether a preamble can override the specific clauses when they point the other way;
- and how the strong antislavery reading sits next to his 1852 Fourth of July speech, where he indicted the nation for betraying its own principles — does the Constitution get credit for the principles while the nation gets blamed for the practice?

End with a question designed to make him identify the weakest point in his own reading, and a follow-up for if he tries to dodge by appealing to the evils of slavery instead of answering the legal question. The questions should be listenable for a non-specialist audience but written so someone who knows the literature can see the pressure points, and dont just ask him to recaps the two positions.
