I'm trying to pin down my views on value and reasons before I present at a seminar, and I'd rather find the cracks now then in front of people. Here's the package as it stands:

(1) I'm a buck-passer about value, or close enough: for something to be good is just for it to have certain features that give us reasons to favour it—want it, admire it, whatever the fitting pro-attitude is. Goodness isn't some extra property stacked on top of the reason-giving features.

(2) I think the standard demon case is a real problem for the naive version. A demon says it will hurt you unless you admire it; now you clearly have a reason to admire it (you're better of admiring it), but the demon isn't thereby good, or admirable in the value sense.

(3) So I accept the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, and I think the buck-passer survives it by splitting reasons into right-kind ones (grounded in the object's features) and wrong-kind ones (grounded in the benefits or costs of being in the attitude itself).

(4) But I'm also a monist about reasons: I don't think right-kind and wrong-kind mark two different kinds of ought. There's one all-things-considered kind of reason; the split is just a way of sorting reasons by where they come from, not by what they can justify.

(5) And I think a wrong-kind reason can make it the case that you ought all things considered to have the attitude—not merely to take steps to cause yourself to have it.

Does this hang together? I can feel a couple of these tugging at each other but I can't tell which are real incompatibilities and which are just ways of talking.
