I've been arguing with my roommate, who is a programmer, about open-source software, and I can't shake the feeling that the whole community is holding two conflicting ideas at once. On one hand, the entire system runs on licenses that say you can take the code, build a paid product on top of it, and owe the author nothing; the permission is explicit, so nobody is cheating. On the other hand, every few years there's a panic—like that logging library vulnerability a while back, or some tiny utility that half the internet depends on turning out to be maintained by one burned-out volunteer—and suddenly everyone is furious at big companies for being freeloaders and free riders who contribute nothing back.

I want to understand what theory of fairness or obligation is hiding inside that combination of attitudes—what its actually presupposing about when you owe something to other people. Does the license-based side assume that obligations can only arise from explicit promises or contracts? Does the outrage assume something stronger: that you can owe someone simply because you benefit from a practice they maintain, or that open source is really a commons rather than a gift? Reconstruct the implicit view (or the several views) that makes sense of both the permissive-license norm and the periodic free-rider outrage. Be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say what companies and maintainers would actually have to do differently if the rival view were substituted. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
