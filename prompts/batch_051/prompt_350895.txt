I came to philosophy late, after a career in genetics spent mostly on vector control and later on the governance of gene drives, so the case I describe is not professionally exotic to me; what I am less sure about is how to handle a legal text under such conditions. I have been reading about the construction of the category of genocide in the 1940s, and I keep returning to the fact that the Genocide Convention, adopted in December 1948, assumes certain ways of destroying a group, while the available technologies have changed sharply since. Article II defines the crime as follows:

"In the present Convention, genocide means any of the following acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such:
(a) Killing members of the group;
(b) Causing serious bodily or mental harm to members of the group;
(c) Deliberately inflicting on the group conditions of life calculated to bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part;
(d) Imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group;
(e) Forcibly transferring children of the group to another group."

Consider a case its authors could not have had in mind. A government releases a horizontally transmitted gene drive into the territory of a distinct ethnic minority under cover of an agricultural-pest control programme. The construct is engineered so that, over two or three generations, the group's fertility collapses: pregnancies increasingly fail or are never initiated, and the modelling done before release shows that the population will not recover. The design is precise, but the official documents say nothing about the group; the government consistently describes the demographic effect as an unintended side effect it is attempting to mitigate, though no mitigation is ever carried out. No one is killed, and no one is visibly injured.

Does Article II reach this, and if so through which clause? I would welcome a careful treatment of the plain words (does an engineered genetic construct amount to "measures" that are "imposed"? can births be "prevented" before any child exists?), the evident purpose of the Convention (should the mechanism matter if the biological continuity of a group is being destroyed?), what the drafters would have said if asked (would they have seen the device as a new instrument within an old category, or as something outside it?), and what a reasonable reader is entitled to make of the text. The deeper question is whether the Convention's requirement of an "intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such" can be satisfied when the purpose is written into the design parameters of an autonomous system but appears in no document, or whether genocide still requires a human intention attributable to identifiable persons. I am less interested in a brute legal result than in what each interpretive approach would have to show, and which of them ought to bear the most weight.
