I am trying to discover the principle behind my own verdicts about what is physical, and whether any principle can both fit those verdicts and escape Hempel's dilemma. I would like you to state the best candidate account — or a small set of candidate accounts — and to be honest about which of my verdicts each one cannot accommodate.

My verdicts are these.

An electron is physical. The electromagnetic field is physical even though it is not a particle and is not made of particles. Spacetime curvature is physical, not merely a mathematical representation. Gravitational waves are physical.

A hole in a wall is not physical — it is an absence. But an electron hole in a semiconductor is physical: it has an effective mass, it drifts and scatters, and it is needed to explain how diodes and transistors work. I cannot state the difference between the two absences.

A shadow is not physical, even though it has a location, a shape, and a causal history. A rainbow is physical, because it is just sunlight dispersed by water droplets; but I am not sure that is consistent with my shadow verdict rather than merely changing the subject from light to the absence of light.

The information in a DNA sequence is physical when embodied in a particular molecule, while the same sequence considered as an abstract string is not physical. The number 7 is not physical, no matter how indispensable mathematics is to physics.

The quantum state of a single electron is physical — I am drawn to say it is the complete state of a physical system — even though the formalism represents it as a vector in a Hilbert space rather than as a point in spacetime. A virtual particle leaves me undecided: current physics writes it into calculations, and it contributes to the results, but it is often said to be bookkeeping rather than something in the world.

A pain from a burn is physical, because it is a state of a nervous system; but if I became convinced that the felt quality could not be accounted for by any future science of the organism, I would call physicalism false rather than stretch the word "physical" to cover it.

Suppose future physics settled on an entity that lacks mass-energy and momentum, cannot be screened, and acts non-locally to complete the explanation of some quantum or gravitational phenomena. I think I would call it physical, simply because it would be part of physics. But suppose a completed physics counted among its fundamental categories something whose defining role is normative — something that selects among allowed states on grounds of what ought to happen rather than on grounds of forces and probabilities. I would not count that as physical, and I would say physicalism had been abandoned. I am not sure I can justify the asymmetry between these two verdicts about possible future physics.

My tentative principle is that something is physical if and only if it is among the entities countenanced by a completed, ideal physics. But Hempel's dilemma worries me. If "physical" means the ontology of current physics, physicalism is probably false, since past physics got its basic ontology wrong and current physics is by all accounts incomplete. If "physical" means the ontology of an ideal completed physics, then the term does no independent work, since I have no grip on what such a physics will contain except by importing the very distinction I am trying to define.

Is there one account that delivers all or nearly all of these verdicts? Which of my verdicts must I give up, and why? Please also state what the resulting account predicts for a case I have deliberately left open: a software process executing in a computer and considered only as a sequence of physical state transitions, versus the same process considered as an abstract computation.
