I've been circling a disagreement in my philosophy of emotion class and I keep feeling like my professor is papering over the deepest version of it. In the chapter on emotion in William James's The Principles of Psychology, he inverts the common-sense story: you are not afraid and therefore you tremble and run; rather, the body responds directly to the exciting event, and the feeling of those bodily changes just is the fear. Strip away the pounding heart, quick breath, and muscle tension, James says, and what's left is only a cold intellectual perception, not an emotion. In Martha Nussbaum's Upheavals of Thought she defends a quite different kind of view: emotions are evaluative judgments, so grief is the thought that someone of irreplaceable worth has been lost, anger is the thought that I or those I love have been wronged, and the bodily symptoms are at most a frequent accompaniment, not the stuff the emotion is made of. Thats two deeply incompatible pictures, not just two emphases — one makes the emotion bodily, the other makes it a kind of thought, and each says the other has got the arrow of causation wrong.

I'd like a serious essay, not a quick compare-and-contrast. First lay out the conflict as sharply as possible without straw-manning either author. Then work through the candidate reconciliations one by one: that the two are answering different questions (what an emotion feels like from the inside versus what gives it its content); that James still has a cognitive perception of the exciting event doing appraisal work that Nussbaum would call judgment; that bodily changes might themselves be the organism's rough way of appraising a situation, so a feeling of those changes could be a felt evaluation; and the hybrid proposal that emotions are perceptions of bodily changes that carry information about how the world matters to us. For each reconciliation, say which commitments of the original views it preserves, which it quietly surrenders, and whether it strikes you as an honest reconciliation or as special pleading. End by saying which single theory you would defend, one that does justice both to the embodied feel of fear and to the fact that fear says something about danger, and at what cost you hold it.
