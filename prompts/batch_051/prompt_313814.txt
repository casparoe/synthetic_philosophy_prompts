I work as a systems designer on games built around deliberate friction: long progression economies, punishing crafting chains, permadeath runs in which players impose extra constraints on themselves, and reward structures that make the player wait and labor for what they receive. My working vocabulary is the one I design in — intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, reward schedules, flow, mastery, friction versus grind, sunk effort, earned versus purchased outcomes, and the difference between challenge that reads as meaningful and challenge that reads as padding. I have heard enough philosophy to know there is a dispute about whether effort is valuable in itself rather than merely as an instrument to outcomes, but I have never seen that dispute translated into terms I actually use.

Restate the debate for me: the view that struggle, exertion, or difficulty can be good for a person independently of what it produces, against the view that effort is always a cost whose value is exhausted by its results. Do it as systematically as you can, mapping each side onto mechanisms, loops, and player-experience states, and flag the places where the philosophical distinctions cleanly line up with design distinctions — for instance, the difference between a reward being valued more because of the grind that preceded it and the grind itself being the thing the player is actually there for. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto my vocabulary, which did you have to fudge, and which have no counterpart in it at all? I suspect one such residue is the idea that some struggles are worthwhile and others are not, where that is not just a fact about player psychology. Say explicitly whether what fails to survive the translation is a gap in my vocabulary or a sign that the philosophical claim was never clear.
