I've had an argument rattling around my head since a colleague sketched it at a workshop, and I can't tell whether it's a proof of utilitarianism or a sleight of hand. It goes like this.

(1) Each individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so each person i has a cardinal utility function u_i, unique up to positive affine transformation.

(2) Society (or the social planner) also has preferences over lotteries satisfying VNM, so there is a cardinal social utility function W.

(3) Pareto for sure outcomes: if everyone weakly prefers x to y and at least one person strictly prefers x to y, then society strictly prefers x to y.

(4) From (3), W must be a strictly increasing function of the individual utilities: W(x) = F(u_1(x), ..., u_n(x)).

(5) Now the move I find suspicious. Since W is VNM, it is linear in probabilities: W of a lottery that yields x with probability p and y with probability 1−p is pW(x) + (1−p)W(y). Substituting (4) in gives pF(u(x)) + (1−p)F(u(y)). But each individual's expected utility in that lottery is p u_i(x) + (1−p) u_i(y). The argument then says: for W to respect the individuals' evaluations, F of the probability-weighted average of the u_i's must equal the probability-weighted average of F of the u_i's, and the only functions with that property are affine. So W must have the form a_1 u_1(x) + ... + a_n u_n(x) + c.

(6) Therefore any society satisfying VNM and Pareto is committed to a weighted-sum social welfare function, i.e., the utilitarian form up to an irrelevant additive constant.

The conclusion bothers me because it feels far stronger than the premises deserve, and I vaguely remember that Harsanyi's actual theorem needs Pareto over *lotteries*, not just over sure outcomes, but I cant make the diagnosis stick. So: does the argument go through? If not, tell me exactly which step fails, and what single extra premise would repair it.
