I recently started keeping a journal of my beliefs after a difficult year in which I caught myself saying things I did not really accept, and a friend pressed me to read some Wittgenstein. In the Philosophical Investigations, specifically Part II, section x, Wittgenstein discusses G. E. Moore and notes that there is something strange about a person who says of herself that a certain state of affairs obtains but that she does not believe it obtains. He points out that this is not a formal contradiction: the two clauses can both be true at once, since the world’s being a certain way does not require anyone to believe it. What is strange is that the speaker seems to be, so to speak, cancelling her own endorsement of the first clause while still saying it.

The thing I cannot work out is this. If Moore’s paradox is not a logical contradiction but just a clash between two statements, why do philosophers treat it as anything more than a piece of ordinary irrationality—like someone who wants to stop drinking but orders another glass? To me, it looks like the speaker simply has inconsistent beliefs: she believes it is raining and also believes she does not believe it is raining. Is that not just a descriptive fact about a confused person, and if so, what does it have to do with first-person authority or self-knowledge? I would like an explanation of where I am going wrong, and I would prefer it not to presuppose that “believe” has some technical sense I have not learned.
