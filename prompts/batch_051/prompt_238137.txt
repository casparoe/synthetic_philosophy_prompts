Over the past several months I have been working through the philosophy of psychiatry, and the idea I keep turning over is Ian Hacking's looping effect. As I understand it, the thought is that classifying people is unlike classifying rocks or beetles: a person who is classified can become aware of the classification, change their self-understanding and conduct, and be treated differently by institutions and intimates, with the result that the kind of person the classification aims to describe is itself altered — and the category then has to be revised to keep up, setting the loop going. I find this persuasive as an abstract picture, but I am not sure it says anything that could be empirically demonstrated rather than just vividly described.

I would like you to take one psychiatric category — attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, borderline personality disorder, or another you know well — and treat the following claim as the target: for this category, the label is doing genuine looping work, changing the classified people in a way that feeds back into the category itself, rather than merely tracking a condition that would exist in roughly the same shape without the label. Design the strongest empirical test you can of that claim. Be concrete: what would you measure or compare, over what timescale, in which population, and what pattern of results would count as evidence for the looping reading versus for the tracking reading? Say how you would try to distinguish a real loop from (a) nothing more than a change in vocabulary, (b) a change in diagnostic thresholds or awareness that alters who gets counted, and (c) people simply seeking help more. Then spell out what even a clean positive result would not settle — for instance, whether it shows the condition is socially constructed in a strong or damaging sense, whether looping is compatible with the distress and impairment being fully real, and whether the distinction between a label that tracks and a label that changes can ever be made sharp in practice. Please also make clear, as you go, which parts of this are reasonably settled among people working on psychiatric classification and which parts are live or contested, and avoid presenting a contested reading as the consensus.
