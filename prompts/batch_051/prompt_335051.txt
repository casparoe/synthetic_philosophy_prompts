Lately I've been stuck on a tension between how we treat risk in ordinary life and how the regulatory state treats it. In ordinary life we're pretty strict: you can't point a gun at someone even if you're nearly positive it's unloaded, and you can't slip a substance into someone's drink even if you're nearly positive it's harmless. Exposing someone to a risk without their consent is itself seen as a wrong, not just a bad bet. But governments do the policy version of this constantly. The EPA sets tolerances for pesticide residues on food. Drug regulators approve medicines with known side-effect risks for whole patient populations. Traffic engineers set speed limits knowing there will be a predictable number of deaths. Floodplain rules let people build in zones with a 1% annual flood chance. Agencies literally use the phrase "acceptable risk" and sometimes talk about cutoffs like one excess cancer case per million people — risks imposed on people who never individually consented.

That looks like either a flat inconsistency or a case where the two domains presuppose different moral theories of risk. What view of risk, harm, and consent does the regulatory practice actually presuppose? Is it presupposing a consequentialist picture where enough aggregate benefit can license imposing a risk on an individual? Is it presupposing some notion of hypothetical or democratic consent, where living under a government counts as agreeing to its risk tradeoffs? Or is it presupposing a de minimis threshold, the idea that below some level a risk isn't a moral harm at all — and if so, is that threshold a genuine feature of harm, or just pragmatic line-drawing that would collapse under scrutiny? Also, is this practice consistent with the way consent is treated in medicine and torts, where touching someone or knowingly exposing them to a hazardous substance without consent is wrong even before any injury shows up? And if we replaced whatever the practice presupposes with a strict "no risk without individual consent" view, what would concretely have to change in public health, transportation, and environmental policy?
