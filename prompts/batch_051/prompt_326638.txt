I am a third-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of mind course, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a survey. In our seminar, the debate over physicalism about the mental has settled into a frustrating loop. The anti-physicalists keep arguing that no amount of neural or functional detail will capture phenomenal consciousness as it is experienced; the physicalists keep replying that this only shows that current physics is incomplete, and that "physical" should mean whatever an ideal future physics posits. No one's view moves. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is the one usually called Hempel's dilemma. If "physical" is tied to the inventory of current physics, physicalism is almost certainly false, or at least hostage to the next scientific revolution. If "physical" is tied instead to the inventory of a completed future physics, physicalism collapses into the claim that everything is whatever the true final science says it is—which looks either empty or trivially true, since we have no independent fix on what that science would countenance. Each side seems to occupy whichever horn is convenient, so the argument has become less about the mind than about the term "physical." I want you to diagnose the structure of this disagreement. Is it a purely verbal dispute about how to use a word, or is there a substantive metaphysical question hidden inside it—for example, whether the future development of physics will be continuous with current physics, or whether the mental will remain fundamental under any reasonable extension of "physical"? Are the two sides using different standards of evidence for what counts as a physical explanation, or is the question itself ill-posed? And, most importantly, what would actually count as progress? Would splitting the question into separate claims about causal closure, fundamentality, and the continuity of future physics break the stand-off, or would the same dilemma simply reappear inside each sub-question? I want a reasoned diagnosis and an honest account of whether the debate can be disambiguated into progress, not a recitation of positions.
