A government welfare agency is considering replacing its current fraud-detection system—periodic reviews triggered by suspicion, plus a hotline for reports—with the following scheme. Each week, two percent of all benefit recipients are selected at random for a full re-verification of their income, assets, and reported changes in circumstances. Recipients whose re-verification shows everything in order receive a modest bonus, say ten percent of one week's benefit, funded from the agency's existing enforcement budget. Recipients whose re-verification uncovers any unreported income or failure to report a relevant change of circumstances forfeit their next week's benefit and are placed in a fast-track review of the previous twelve months. Recipients may also volunteer for re-verification at any time; volunteers enter the same bonus pool but are audited more quickly. The stated aim is to distinguish the 'undeserving poor' from those who are merely bad at paperwork without imposing sanctions on the latter, and to shift the relationship between claimants and the agency from adversarial suspicion to something more like a compliance lottery.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than a moral position. Analyse who would volunteer and why; how it would change what recipients disclose and when; what the agency would actually learn from the random audits given that the population of audited claims is no longer a representative sample once volunteering is allowed; where the scheme would be gamed first; and whether the failure modes could be fixed without reverting to ordinary suspicion-based fraud detection. What does the scheme select for, and does it make the category of the 'undeserving poor' easier or harder to operationalize in practice?
