You are an expert in signaling theory and in the evolutionary logic of trustworthy communication. I am going to describe a proposed institutional mechanism, and I want you to analyze how it would actually behave in the wild, not just whether its rationale is sound. Please begin by brainstorming the range of actors involved and the strategic moves available to each, before moving to your settled analysis.

A large online platform for policy debate introduces a system of voluntary escrow stakes for public predictions. Here is how it works. Any member may, before publishing an empirical prediction (about an election, a trial outcome, the effect of a law, or anything with a publicly checkable resolution), deposit money into escrow. The member chooses the amount, and the amount is shown next to the prediction. If the prediction is judged true at a pre-registered resolution date by pre-registered criteria, the deposit is returned with a modest bonus from a platform pool. If the prediction is judged false, the deposit is forfeited and distributed among those members who publicly took the opposite position. Staking is optional — members may stake any amount including zero — but the platform's ranking algorithm gives higher visibility to claims associated with larger stakes.

The designers justify the scheme with a handicap-principle logic. A well-calibrated predictor expects to lose the stake only rarely, so the expected cost of a large stake is lower for them than for a badly calibrated or insincere predictor, who expects to forfeit more often. So a large voluntarily chosen stake should be an honest signal of the predictor's own confidence and competence, somewhat as a peacock's tail signals condition, and should make high-stake claims more credible than cheap talk.

Do not settle for the intended effect. Work out how the system would actually play out, from the perspective of the different agents who would use it. In particular, I would like your analysis to address the following, and you should extend beyond them if you see something important:

1. Who would be attracted to making claims under this system, and who would be driven out or deterred?
2. Would stake size function as an honest signal of predictive competence and sincerity in the way the designers intend, or would it primarily signal something else — wealth, risk tolerance, status-seeking, overconfidence?
3. Distinguish the incentive effect from the signaling effect. A larger stake simultaneously makes being wrong more costly and publicly advertises the predictor's confidence. Which is likely to do more of the work in any improvement in the quality of claims, and what would show it?
4. What gaming strategies would emerge once participants understand the resolution rules and the visibility rule? Consider hedging, collusion, disputes about resolution criteria, attempts to manipulate the resolution process, and outside insurance or side bets that would undo the cost.
5. What second-order effects would this have on the community's norms, on the mix of claims that get made, and on how readers interpret high-stake versus low-stake claims?
6. Under what conditions, if any, would the mechanism separate good predictors from bad ones rather than simply selecting for people who can afford, or enjoy, taking large gambles?

Finally, identify the one or two deepest failure modes and assess whether they can be patched without turning the mechanism into an ordinary prediction market or back into a cheap-talk forum.

Throughout, please be precise about your confidence: for each of your main claims, say how confident you are, and at the end explicitly single out the point you are least sure about.
