I am a novelist, currently drafting a book set in a company that has just adopted what it calls a radical meritocracy: every promotion, bonus, and layoff decision is now made by an algorithm that scores employees on measurable output. A central narrative question is whether anyone in the company really deserves what happens to them, and I find I cannot keep the narration morally consistent without a worked-out view of desert. I am asking for a philosophical consultation before I write further.

The term I need defined is desert: what it means to say a person deserves an outcome — a reward, a success, a punishment, or a benefit — as distinct from merely being entitled to it or having a legal right to it. Here are the cases I need the definition to handle.

1. A grocery-store worker who spends his whole shift carefully restocking shelves and is paid his contracted wage. I am confident he deserves the wage.

2. A person who wins a lottery jackpot. I am confident she does not deserve the jackpot, though she is now legally entitled to it.

3. The eldest child who inherits the family firm. I am confident she is entitled to it, but I suspect she does not deserve it. I want a definition that can explain the difference between this case and the first one.

4. A student admitted to an elite program because she got the top exam score, partly owing to an unusually strong innate memory that she did nothing to earn. I lean toward saying she deserves the place, but I cannot say why, if desert requires deserving the basis of one's success.

5. A student of modest talent who studies obsessively and just misses the cutoff, while a gifted student who exerted little effort is admitted. Does the first student deserve the place more than the second? I have no settled view.

6. A surgeon who ignores protocol and performs a risky operation that succeeds against the odds. Does she deserve praise, blame, or something else? I am undecided.

7. A commodities trader whose portfolio doubles because an unexpected hurricane disrupts supply chains in a commodity she happened to hold; she receives an enormous bonus. I lean toward saying she does not deserve the bonus.

8. A bystander who pushes a child out of the path of a car chiefly because he wants to be seen as a hero, and the child is saved. Does he deserve the gratitude and admiration he receives? I am unsure.

9. A person who commits a minor offense that thousands of others also commit but is the only one caught and sentenced. I suspect he deserves the sentence in one sense and not in another; I need help articulating that.

Please propose a single working definition of desert that classifies as many of these cases as possible, then run the definition through all nine cases in order. Tell me where it gives an answer I did not expect or no answer at all, revise it once if it fails on what I take to be a clear case, and then say which of your final verdicts is least secure.

Also, once you have settled on the definition, tell me how confident you are in it overall, and single out the point or case in your answer you are least sure of.

You are a professional philosopher specializing in desert, responsibility, and distributive justice. Think carefully and do not settle for the first definition that occurs to you. Begin by brainstorming two or three candidate accounts of desert — for example, desert as proportional to effort, to contribution, to moral merit, or to what a person has earned through her choices — and briefly say why each falls short before you settle on your working definition.
