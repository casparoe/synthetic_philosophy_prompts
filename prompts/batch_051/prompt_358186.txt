I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar on free will and neuroscience, about 16 students, one 75-minute session. We've just gone through the classic Libet result: the readiness potential starts building up a few hundred milliseconds before the subject's reported time of conscious intention, which freaks people out because it looks like the brain decided before the person did. But I also want them to feel the force of the accumulator-model alternative, where the readiness potential is more like a noisy build-up crossing a threshold than a hidden decision already made in advance.

Design a runnable in-class activity that lets them discover this tension themselves. The idea should come out of what happens in the activity, not from me explaining it first. I have index cards, dice, whiteboards, and the students have phones with timers—no special equipment. The activity should somehow have students first act as Libet-style subjects reporting the moment of their own conscious urge to move, and then act as neuroscientists analyzing the pooled data, so they see how averaging trials time-locked to the movement can manufacture the appearance of a rising signal before the reported urge—even when the underlying process has no hidden decision in it.

Include roles, rules, timeline, exactly what gets recorded by whom, and a debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity to, on the one hand, Libet's original interpretation and, on the other, the stochastic accumulator diagnosis of what the readiness potential is doing. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which part of the actual neuroscience the activity can't reproduce.

I want a committed plan, not a menu of options: make the design choices and tell me why you made them.
