I'm taking a historical linguistics course, and a recurring debate is what it takes for a pattern to count as a genuine rule rather than just a summary of the data. The issue came to a head with Grimm's law, the set of correspondences by which Proto-Indo-European voiceless stops regularly became voiceless fricatives in Germanic. The law is presented as a textbook example of a rule, but it has apparent exceptions: some forms show a voiced obstruent where the law would lead you to expect a voiceless fricative. The class then learned that Verner's law explains many of these by appeal to the position of the old Indo-European accent, which conditioned voicing after Grimm's law had applied. When one student objected that a "law" that needs a second law to cover its exceptions is not much of a law, another shrugged and said, "The exception proves the rule."
