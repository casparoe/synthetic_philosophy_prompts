I am a philosopher of religion working on a paper about whether a translated text can be the vehicle of divine revelation, and before I commit myself on the translation question I want to sharpen the underlying notion. I would like you to propose a working definition of what it is for a text to be divinely inspired — that is, for a text to be such that a religious tradition is right, and not merely psychologically or sociologically disposed, to treat it as a vehicle of revelation. I want the normative or theological property, not an account of how texts come to be received.

The definition should get the following six cases right.

1. The lost original manuscript of a prophetic book, in its original language, received as revelation by the community to which it was first addressed. I take it this is as clear a positive case as there is.

2. A modern academic commentary on that book, in English, explaining its structure and historical background. I take it this is a clear negative case: however useful, it is not itself revelation.

3. The Septuagint, the early Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures, which some ancient Jewish communities and many early Christian writers treated as itself inspired, to the point of defending details of the Greek wording as providentially chosen rather than as mere translation. I have no settled view here.

4. The King James Version as understood in the family of positions called KJV-onlyism: the claim, held by some English-speaking Protestants, that this one English translation is the preserved or restored form of revelation, so that where the Hebrew and Greek manuscript traditions conflict with it, those traditions rather than the English text are to be set aside. I am inclined to say this is not inspired, but I have not found a principled distinction between this case and the Septuagint, and I suspect the definition will have to supply one.

5. A contemporary free paraphrase of the same book, which renders whole clauses by their general sense for the sake of readability. I lean towards saying that this is not inspired, but I do not know whether the distinction between a translation and a paraphrase can carry that much weight, or where the line would fall.

6. A modern critical edition of the book, reconstructed by scholars from many manuscripts and matching no single manuscript ever copied or used in worship. This may be our best approximation of the lost original, but no community has ever received it as revelation. I cannot decide whether inspiration attaches to the reconstruction, the original, or the received text, and I want the definition to force the issue.

Please propose the definition, run it through all six cases, and say explicitly where it gives the wrong answer or no answer at all. Then either revise it in light of those failures or defend the surprising verdicts it forces. The definition should settle the central question — whether a translated text can be divinely inspired, and under what conditions translation preserves, transmits, or fails to transmit inspiration — rather than leaving that as a further question. Finally, say how confident you are in the definition you settle on, and identify the single point about which you are least confident. Please write the entire response in British English.
