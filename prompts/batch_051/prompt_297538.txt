I've been turning over a parallel and I can't tell whether it's deep or just a pun on the word "derivable." In metaethics, reasons internalism of the Williams-ish sort says something can be a reason for an agent only if the agent could reach it by sound deliberation from their current motivational set — their wants, commitments, projects, cares. In logic there's the standard split between proof-theoretic and model-theoretic consequence. Proof-theoretically, a sentence follows if it can be derived from the axioms using the proof rules. Model-theoretically, it follows if it's true in every structure that satisfies the axioms, whether or not a derivation exists. The internalist about reasons sounds a lot like the proof-theorist: a "reason for you" has to be a theorem of your own motivational psychology, derivable by the permitted deliberative rules from your actual desires and projects. The externalist sounds like the model-theorist: some considerations genuinely bear on you — they're true of, or required by, your situation — even if no sound deliberation from where you now are would ever get you to them. Is this a real identity of structure, or just a loose analogy riding on "derivable"? If it's real, what transfers? For instance, does anything like incompleteness make sense for reasons — a consideration that is to you what a model-theoretically valid but unprovable sentence is to a formal system — and does the analogy force the internalist to give a non-circular account of which deliberative rules are "sound"? I'd also like a concrete prediction the analogy seems to make that comes out false, because that's where I suspect it breaks.
