I came to philosophy late, after a career in the natural sciences, and I now run a small interview-based colloquium for an audience that already knows a fair amount of epistemology. Next month I am interviewing a philosopher who defends the credit theory of the value of knowledge: roughly, the view that knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief because it is a true belief creditable to the agent's own intellectual abilities, an intellectual achievement rather than merely a successful outcome. I want the discussion to move past restating the position and to probe the places where the literature has actually put pressure on it. Draft eight to ten questions for this guest. Please include at least one question, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply the defender is most likely to give, on the following pressure points: (1) the swamping problem, where a reliable process seems to add no value to a true belief it has already produced, and how the appeal to achievement is supposed to escape that problem without simply relocating it; (2) cases in which knowledge and achievement appear to come apart, such as testimonial knowledge and ordinary perceptual knowledge, and what the credit theorist should concede or resist there; and (3) the rival view that the distinctive epistemic value attaches not to knowledge but to understanding, and whether the credit theorist should absorb that value, explain it away, or reject the challenge. For each question, the follow-up should anticipate the guest's most likely defensive move rather than merely restating the question. End with one question that asks what kind of evidence or case would most seriously undermine the view, so the conversation closes with the guest specifying where the position is genuinely vulnerable.
