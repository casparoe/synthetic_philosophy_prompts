My cousin is running for a ward council seat back home and I got dragged into a family-dinner argument about whether the envelopes of cash he hands out at funerals and weddings count as corruption. Half the table says it's just how you show respect and take care of people; the other half says it's vote-buying with extra steps. The thing is, nobody likes the idea of importing some NGO-style "no gifts at all" rule, since alot of poor families genuinely rely on those gifts and the politicians loose the one way they actually show up in people's lives.

So I've been trying to think through a compromise someone floated: keep the gifting, but route it through a common pool. During the six months around an election, any candidate who wants to give to a constituent has to give to a community fund instead, run by a rotating committee of retired teachers and religious elders. The fund gives to the same people and for the same purposes—funeral costs, school fees, food baskets—but the candidate isn't present and the recipient isn't told whose money it is; donors are only disclosed in aggregate after the election. The pitch is that you keep the care and solidarity part while killing the "now you owe me your vote" part.

I want you to analyze this as a mechanism, not as a moral argument about whether the gifts were corruption to begin with. What does it incentivize for candidates, for voters, for the committee, and for the local brokers who currently arrange the envelopes and know who gave what? How would people game it, and what second-order effects would show up? Does the whole design secretly assume that a Western-style split between "public" charity and "private" gift-giving is the right frame after all? Finally, flag the place where your analysis is most likely to fail and say which evidence or argument would change your mind.
