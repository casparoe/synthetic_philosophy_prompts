Some things I suspect any honest theory has to deal with, but don't just treat my list as the answer:

- The usual line that tractable = polynomial time (Cobham–Edmonds, right?) fails from both ends: an O(n^100) algorithm is polynomial but useless, and a 2^(n/1000) algorithm is exponential but fine for plenty of real inputs. So 'polynomial' and 'practically solvable' come apart in both directions.
- SAT is NP-complete, yet sat solvers chew through industrial instances with hundreds of thousands of variables. A purely worst-case theory seems to say something false about what working engineers actually experience.
- Reductions are meant to preserve difficulty, but they also preserve blow-ups in hidden constants and exponents. What does a reduction actually earn you if the target problem's algorithm has a monstrous constant?
- Randomised algorithms, approximation (exact TSP vs metric TSP with a 1.5-factor guarantee), parameterised complexity where the blow-up lives only in a small parameter — do all of these count as 'tractable', or does the theory have to draw lines between them?
- The awkward one: we can't currently prove P ≠ NP. Does a theory of difficulty have to be independent of that open problem, or can it just conditionalise on it?
