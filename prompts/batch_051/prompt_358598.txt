I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm putting together an interactive explainer on the Anthropocene — specifically the fight over when it started and who even gets to decide. Could you act as a philosopher of science who also knows the geological politics and build me a decision tree (or flow chart) that sorts a reader into one of the main start-date positions?

Here's the thing. My readers don't know the literature and won't care about stratigraphic procedure until it's explained through questions they can actually answer. So every branch node has to be a concrete question or scenario someone can answer before they've learned the labels. No question should just ask "do you think it started in 1950?" More like: does the Anthropocene need a single, globally synchronous marker you can point to in a rock or lake bed, or is it enough that humans changed the whole Earth system over a long ragged stretch? Or: is the whole point of the date to apportion responsibility, or is it just bookkeeping about rocks? Also a good one: if the International Commission on Stratigraphy or its Anthropocene working group never actually ratifies the term, should we still use it as a historical period?

The leaves I want at minimum: the Ruddiman-style early farming view (starting thousands of years ago), the 1610 Orbis-spike view (a CO2 dip tied to the mass death and land abandonment that came with European contact in the Americas), Crutzen's late-18th-century industrial-revolution framing, the Great Acceleration / mid-20th-century nuclear-and-plastics view, and a leaf for people who think Anthropocene is the wrong category altogether — some kind of Capitalocene or "not a formal epoch at all" position. For each leaf, one sentence on its most awkward commitment, the thing a defender would squirm about.

Because the piece is mostly about the politics of periodization rather than the dates themselves, make sure one branch of the tree is about authority: who should have the final say in naming and defining the epoch — formal stratigraphers, the broader scientific community, historians and social scientists, or the people most affected by the harms. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't cleanly separate, and note any combinations of answers that produce no stable position, saying whether nobody lives there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Before you settle on the tree, start by brainstorming five or six candidate sorting questions, then think carefully about which ones actually do the work of separating the leaves and which ones just sound good. I'd rather have seven sharp nodes than twelve mushy ones. And dont worry about making it printable; I can adapt the flow for the web.
