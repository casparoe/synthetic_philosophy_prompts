I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so go easy but dont pull punches. You're a philosopher of mind who also knows the formal details of Gödel's incompleteness theorems, and I want an honest assessment of a homemade idea I use in my writing.

I read Gödel, Escher, Bach years ago and ever since I've been playing with something I call the "story loop." The idea is: a system becomes self-aware when it starts telling itself a story about itself in wich the telling of the story appears as an event inside the story. Not just a story that mentions the storyteller, but one where the storyteller has to include the act of telling in the list of things the story is about, and that inclusion changes the story, which changes what has to be included, and so on. I think this is what Hofstadter means by a strange loop, only in narrative form: the mind is like the sentence that says of itself that it is saying itself. The self isn't a thing; it's the standing wave you get when a story loops through its own telling.

In my novels, characters start to feel alive to me exactly when I can imagine them noticing the narration they're inside, even if the published book never has them break the fourth wall. So my claim: the felt sense of being a self just is this loop, and Gödel's trick of getting a formula to talk about its own provability is the formal skeleton of it.

Before you answer, brainstorm two or three ways a charitable reader could interpret "story loop," pick the strongest one, and then tell me: is this a real idea or just a metaphor I'm attached to? Is it already out there under another name? Where does it break—what would it predict about consciousness or selfhood that's probably false? And what's the actual difference between a loop and a plain old inconsistency, in a story and in a formal system?
