I've been reading the neuroethics stuff on cognitive liberty, and there's an old liberal argument I can't stop picking at. The line was always that freedom of thought doesn't need to be articulated as a positive right because thought is internal by definition: the law can get at speech, publication, and conduct, but it can't get at a silent thought, and a silent thought can't harm anyone, so there's nothing there to regulate. That argument always struck me as resting on an empirical premise—that the inside of the skull is private by default, not private by principle. But now semantic decoding can reconstruct the gist of what someone is silently thinking or imagining from fMRI, and cruder versions run off consumer EEG. Its not word-for-word, but it pulls out propositional content, like whether the person was silently imagining a violent act or rehearsing a confession. So does the old argument survive? Maybe you can patch it by saying the decoder only gets a noisy reconstruction, not the thought itself, and the law would be regulating the reconstruction. But at that point I'm not sure what's doing the work: if a classifier can reliably report that the subject was silently forming the intention to do X, is the thing it observed still a thought in the sense the old argument needed, or has it become something closer to an act or a trace? And if the argument is dead rather than repairable, should we conclude it was never really a principled argument for cognitive liberty—just a placeholder for the fact that nobody had built a way to get inside the skull yet?
