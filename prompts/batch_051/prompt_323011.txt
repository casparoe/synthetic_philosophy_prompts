I don't have an academic background, but I've been thinking on my own about a position I've come to hold, and I want a rigorous map of where it is vulnerable rather than a defense of it. The view — I believe some people call it the hedonistic imperative — is that the long-term goal of medicine and biotechnology should be to redesign the biological basis of sentient life so that suffering itself is progressively eliminated, not merely managed or treated case by case. The aim would be to move all sentient creatures, including humans, toward a default of well-being, while keeping pain's useful informational role as a signal of injury and danger and, if possible, preserving emotional range and motivation rather than flattening them.

Please act as a professional philosopher who is sympathetic to the ambition but committed to testing it honestly. Begin by brainstorming the strongest possible objections from as many angles as you can. Then tell me what would have to be true — or what evidence, arguments, intuitions, or developments would have to emerge — for this position to be false or importantly incomplete. I especially want you to separate considerations that would be decisive from those that would only force me to qualify the view; to identify which defeaters a committed defender of the position would themselves concede as real vulnerabilities rather than dismiss; to say what this view predicts about actual choices or technologies that its rivals would predict differently; and to assess whether the view, as people actually defend it, has been insulated against counterevidence, and if so whether that insulation is a defect. I would personally find it most persuasive if you could show me a concrete scenario in which the program succeeds on its own terms but still produces something we should rightly reject. I am not asking for a reading list or the history of the idea — just the strongest case against it, organized by how much damage it does.
