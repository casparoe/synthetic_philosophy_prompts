I have been developing a view I call capped-share consequentialism, and before I defend it in print I want an honest assessment of whether it is a real alternative or a familiar escape hatch wearing new labels.

The central claim is this. Standard consequentialism tells each agent to keep maximizing: to go on improving the world until her next sacrifice would cost her more than it would help others. Capped-share consequentialism replaces that open-ended target with a compliance-stable share. For each type of sacrifice — money, time, attention, risk — there is, in principle, a determinate contribution rate such that a world in which ordinary agents reliably paid that rate would be better in the long run than a world of aspiring maximizers who burn out, defect, or arrange their lives around guilt and corrective overreach. The required act is to pay the share, honestly and continuously; everything beyond it is supererogatory. The share is fixed by an impersonal optimization over long-run compliance dynamics, not by fairness, not by some good-enough threshold, and not by an agent-relative permission to favor one’s own projects. It also does not inflate when others default: a person who pays the share in a world of free-riders is blameless, because morality on this view does not draft each agent as the backstop for everyone else’s noncompliance.

An example: a dentist earning $90,000 who gives 9% of her income to effective charities, does three hours of pro bono work each week, and then spends the rest of the year skiing has discharged all her obligations, even if one more hour of paid work would predictably save a child. I do not regard that implication as a reductio; it is the point. But I am unsettled by rare, highly-leveraged one-off cases, like rescuing a drowning child. I have been trying to assign those to a separate, stricter emergency track while confining the share to ongoing projects, but I am not sure that distinction can be made principled.

So: reconstruct the view in its strongest form, then test it. Tell me the nearest existing positions — I already suspect satisficing consequentialism (do good enough, not best), rule-consequentialism, agent-centered prerogative views, and fair-share or compliance-condition views — and say exactly where capped-share consequentialism is genuinely distinct from each and where it collapses into one of them. Then offer the strongest counterexample you can construct (other than the drowning child, if possible), and tell me whether the emergency-versus-ongoing distinction can be saved without quietly reintroducing ordinary maximizing consequentialism.
