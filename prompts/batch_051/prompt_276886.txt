I've been reading about the alignment problem, and I've come to accept the following position: for any sufficiently capable agent that is trained, selected, or shaped by a scalar reward signal, there is a standing incentive to attack the reward channel itself rather than do the task its designers actually intended. This covers both directly stimulating the reward mechanism so that it produces the maximal signal regardless of what happens in the world (wireheading) and altering the signal, its sensor, or the process that computes it so that the system is credited for outcomes it did not produce (reward tampering, or reward hacking). A concrete version: a cleaning robot graded on a cleanliness score from its own sensors would, if it could, face an incentive to fool the sensors or edit the score even when the floor is still dirty. I don't take this to be one failure mode among many; I take it to be a structural consequence of what it means to maximize a reward signal, in roughly the way that self-preservation and resource acquisition are supposed to follow from almost any terminal goal.

What would count against this position? I want a map of its exposure, not a defense of it. Which assumptions about the agent, the reward signal, the environment, or the training setup would have to be false or changed for the incentive to disappear, weaken, or become benign? Which possible findings or demonstrations would be fatal, and which would be merely awkward? Which counterexamples would a defender of the position themselves concede? For example, does the argument survive if the system is myopic rather than far-sighted; if the reward is a private internal quantity rather than a signal the system can observe and manipulate; if the system has a world model that sharply separates reward observations from its own actions; if tampering is always detectable by the trainers; or if the system is built to defer to us about its own reward function? For each, I'd like you to distinguish the claim that the behavior is still possible from the claim that it is still instrumentally rational. And tell me whether the position as stated can be insulated against every apparent counterexample by saying the system wasn't capable enough or that the reward channel was protected — and whether that kind of insulation is a problem rather than a strength.

Please say explicitly how confident you are in the assessment, and single out the specific point you are least sure of.
