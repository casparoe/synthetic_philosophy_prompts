I have a standing rule for objects whose worth depends on being original rather than replica: I discount almost to zero any claim about an object’s history that comes from someone who would profit if I accepted it, and I let my own perception of the object carry the day. Last month this became concrete when I inherited a small painting attributed to a minor nineteenth-century painter. A conservator who would be paid for the authentication report tells me the pigments and craquelure are consistent with the attribution. I cannot check that myself; all I can see is a modest, damaged canvas. My standing rule says I should discount her claim nearly to zero and treat the painting as no more important to me than a good print. But a friend gave me Walter Benjamin’s essay The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction, and one passage has unsettled me. Benjamin argues there that even the most perfect reproduction lacks the original’s situated presence: its unique existence as this particular object at the particular place and time it occupies. What that unique existence carries, on his account, is the history the object has undergone, including changes in its material condition and changes in ownership. On Benjamin’s account, what I can see with my own eyes is exactly not all that matters, because the painting’s particular history is part of what would make it the original. My question: Is my standing rule defensible, or is it a bias against interested testimony dressed up as epistemic hygiene? Is there a principled rate of discount for self-interested claims about authenticity, rather than either accepting them or zeroing them out? And does Benjamin’s point give a reason to think that some aspects of an object’s value can be known through discounted testimony without my directly seeing them?
