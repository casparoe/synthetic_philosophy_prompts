I am preparing a seminar on the justification of deduction and keep returning to a passage in Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles." By the point quoted below, Achilles has recorded two premises of a small Euclidean argument as A and B, the conclusion as Z, and a further conditional, C, stating that if A and B are true then Z must be true. The Tortoise accepts A, B, and C, yet still will not accept Z. The passage reads:

"You should call it D, not Z," said Achilles. "It comes next to the other three. If you accept A and B and C, you must accept Z."
"And why must I?"
"Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?"
"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"
"You might," the candid hero admitted; "though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical."
"Very good.  I'm quite willing to grant it, as soon as you've written it down.  We will call it
     (D)  If A and B and C are true, then Z must be true.

What I want to understand precisely is what this exchange establishes about the relation between a rule of inference and a premise. Achilles tries to force the conclusion by getting the Tortoise to accept yet another conditional, and the Tortoise immediately converts that move into a further step of the same kind. Does the passage show that a rule of inference cannot be represented as an additional premise without generating an infinite regress, or does it show something stronger about the impossibility of justifying deduction by logical means? And is the regress vicious, or merely tedious? I would value an interpretation that stays close to the quoted exchange rather than a general summary of Carroll's point.
