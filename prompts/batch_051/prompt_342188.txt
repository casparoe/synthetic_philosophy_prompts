For this exercise, write a short philosophical dialogue between two equally capable philosophers of language who meet at a departmental seminar on reference and fiction and fall into an argument over three sentences: "Sherlock Holmes does not exist," "Sherlock Holmes is a detective," and "Sherlock Holmes was created by Arthur Conan Doyle." One of them defends an empty-name account: she insists that "Sherlock Holmes" has no referent and that Conan Doyle's stories merely prescribe make-believe; on her view the first sentence is literally true, the second is true only in the sense of being true according to the stories, and the third must be paraphrased as a claim about the stories rather than about any object brought into being. The other defends an artifactualist account: he holds that Sherlock Holmes is a real abstract object created by Conan Doyle, that the second sentence is true of this object in roughly the way a statement about a novel's narrated content is true, and that the third is true because the object really was brought into being; on his view the first is true only under a restricted, concrete-object reading of "exist."

The dialogue should be a genuine exchange, not alternating monologues. Each should press the other on the hardest consequences of their view: the empty-name theorist must say how a sentence using an empty name, even a negative existential, manages to say something true rather than nothing at all, and why the creation sentence does not simply refute her; the artifactualist must say what it could mean for an abstract object to be a detective, and why his "Holmes exists" is not simply a change of subject from the ordinary denial that Holmes exists. Have them respond to each other's actual moves, concede where the other lands a point, and try to determine whether their disagreement is substantive or partly a dispute about the word "exist." Write the dialogue itself, with no preface or commentary; begin directly with the first line of dialogue.
