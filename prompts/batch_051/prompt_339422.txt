I am a novelist working on a book set in a near future in which a company claims to have built a complete map of language — a high-dimensional space in which any two texts can be placed and the distance between them is presented as a measure of how close they are in meaning. To keep the conceit from collapsing into science-fictional boilerplate, I have been rereading Jorge Luis Borges's story The Library of Babel, and I would like a genuinely philosophical reading of that story through the lens of embedding spaces and semantic structure. The obvious starting move is that the Library — the vast, perhaps infinite, complex of hexagonal galleries containing every possible ordering of a finite stock of symbols — is not itself a semantic space but the raw combinatorial space over which an embedding would have to be learned. One can define a distance between any two books by comparing them symbol by symbol, but that metric tracks surface form rather than meaning, and the story's books therefore remain, for its librarians, almost uniformly meaningless noise.

I do not want the reading to stop there. Does the story dramatize the claim that meaning is not intrinsic to symbols and arises only once something imposes a graded, similarity-respecting structure on them? Or is it better read as a skeptical limit on that claim, insisting that total information without a usable geometry is indistinguishable from noise, and that the order the story ultimately entertains — a periodic repetition in which apparent chaos becomes a hidden order, or a single all-explaining Book somewhere on the shelves — is a hope or an act of faith rather than something discoverable? I would also like the reading to be honest about where the analogy breaks. The Library's imagined order tends to be a hidden library-wide order or a single master text, not the distributed, statistical, approximate geometry of actual semantic models; and Borges's persistent religious idiom may suggest that the longing for semantic order is itself something to be examined rather than satisfied. Ideally, the reading would end by saying what the story adds to the philosophical picture that the technical notion of an embedding space leaves out, and what a novelist should take from it about the difference between representing meaning and merely encoding it.
