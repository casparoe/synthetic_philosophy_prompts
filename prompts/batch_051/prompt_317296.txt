I've been unable to settle a question about language, and I would like to see it forced into a credence with its weakest parts exposed. The question is whether the amount of surface ambiguity in natural languages — the homonymy and heavy polysemy that let one form cover bank-as-financial-institution and bank-as-river-edge, or pitch-as-sound-quality and pitch-as-tar — is closer to a communication-theoretic optimum than to a lagging historical accident. By 'closer to an optimum' I mean that a reasonable objective function trading off the cost of longer or more numerous forms, learning load, and residual misunderstanding would select something in the neighborhood of the ambiguity levels we observe; by 'historical accident' I mean that such ambiguity persists mainly because selection against it is too weak, not because it earns its keep.

Please give me a single number between 0 and 1 as your credence for that claim, and then decompose it. I would like explicit numbers for at least these sub-claims: (1) that day-to-day use and transmission of language generates enough selection pressure to prune costly ambiguities over historical time; (2) that if such pressure exists, the savings from reusing short, high-frequency forms roughly balance or exceed the cost of occasional misunderstandings once context, pitch, tone, and world knowledge are available; (3) that contextual disambiguation is reliable enough that residual ambiguity is usually benign rather than a standing tax; and (4) any further factor you think carries weight, such as ambiguity serving social hedging or vagueness functioning as flexibility. Then show how the components combine into your final number, and tell me which sub-claim the number is most sensitive to and what evidence would move it most.
