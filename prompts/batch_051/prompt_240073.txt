I keep coming back to an argument that fairness in algorithmic decision-making is impossible, and I can't tell if it's sound or if I've pulled a fast one somewhere. I'd love a careful verdict. You are an expert on algorithmic fairness and the philosophy of measurement. Please think through it step by step rather than giving the usual "fairness is hard" answer.

Consider two groups, A and B, and a binary classifier that predicts whether someone will default on a loan within two years (for concreteness; it could be recidivism or a medical outcome). For each group, let the base rate p be the proportion of people in that group who actually default. Let FPR be the probability someone is labeled high risk given that they will not default, and FNR be the probability someone is labeled low risk given that they will default. Then the positive predictive value, PPV, is the probability someone labeled high risk actually defaults. And there's an identity:

PPV = p(1 - FNR) / (p(1 - FNR) + (1 - p)FPR).

Now here's the argument.

(1) If an algorithm is fair, its false positive rate must be the same across groups. It's plainly discrimatory if equivalent behavior (not defaulting) is treated differently by group.
(2) Likewise, fairness requires the false negative rate to be the same across groups.
(3) Fairness also requires the positive predictive value to be the same across groups: a "high risk" label should mean the same thing for everyone. If it is less trustworthy for one group, that group is being treated as a second-class evidential subject.
(4) But if group A and group B have different base rates, and the algorithm has the same FPR and the same FNR in both groups, then by the identity their PPVs must differ. For example, with p_A = .2, p_B = .4, FPR = .1, and FNR = .2, the PPVs are about .67 and .84. The only ways around that are degenerate: a false positive rate of zero or a false negative rate of one.
(5) So no classifier can satisfy all three fairness requirements at once when base rates differ. Since base rates differ for basically every interesting outcome, fair classification is impossible; the only question is which fairness criterion to sacrifice.

I'm suspcious of step (4), but the algebra checks out when I do it, and the example comes out as I said. So my questions: Is the whole argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails, and what would the corrected version be? If it is valid, what should we actually conclude—that at least one of the premises isn't really a fairness requirement, or that fairness in this sense is genuinely impossible? Please separate what is mathematically forced from what is genuinely contested among people who work on fairness, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
