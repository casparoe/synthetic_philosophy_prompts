So I've ended up defending a deflationary view of Pearl's three-rung ladder, and I want to know how much trouble I'm in. The view, roughly: rung two (interventions, the do-calculus) is where all the real causal content lives, and rung three (singular counterfactuals, like 'would this patient have survived had she not smoked') is almost always either (a) an interventional question in disguise, or (b) a way of picking one structural causal model over another when the data don't, which is a modelling choice, not a claim the world settles. I think the standard line that rung three information can't be recovered from rung two is true only in the boring sense that you can't derive an answer from evidence you don't have; it isn't evidence that there is a further fact about the world parked at rung three. So the position is: for causal questions that actually matter in science, rung two suffices, and rung-three talk adds no empirical content over and above a chosen model.

What would count against this? I don't mean 'people find counterfactuals natural.' I want to know which formal results, clean examples, or pressures should move me, and how much. What would the world—or a single well-chosen example—have to look like for rung three to earn its keep as something more than model choice? Are there cases where two models share all their observational and interventional distributions yet differ in individual-level counterfactuals in a way that matters for a decision we actually have to make, and where the disagreement can't be shrugged off as 'you're just choosing a model'? Which defeaters would be fatal and which merely awkward? Where do my predictions actually come apart from a rung-three realist's, rather than just our rhetoric? And be honest about whether I've insulated myself—say, by letting 'matters in science' slide around—so much that the view can't fail. If I have, is that a problem for a deflationary view, or just what a deflationary view is supposed to look like?

I suspect the thing that would genuinely move me is a case where the rung-three disagreement survives even after all interventional questions are fixed, and refusing to answer the singular counterfactual would leave a real decision unmade. So tell me if that case exists, or whether I've set the bar too high (or too low).

Give me a commited answer: take a definite line on whether the view survives and which counterexamples, if any, should actually change my mind, with reasons. And write it in British English.
