So there's this clause in our competition code, section 12(2), and it reads: 'A person commits an offence if he or she enters into an agreement with a competitor where the purpose or likely effect of the agreement is to fix, raise, lower, or otherwise stabilise prices.' It was drafted in 1991, plainly with human executives in mind.

Here's the thing that's been bugging me. Two online retailers both buy the same third-party dynamic-pricing agent. Each firm sets the reward signal to 'maximise long-run profit' and lets the thing run; the two instances never share a channel — no messages, no emails, no API calls between them. Over about six weeks they settle into a stable pattern through the public prices anyone can see: firm A's agent nudges a product line up 4% at a particular time, firm B's agent matches about an hour later, and neither ever tries to undercut the other. One engineer at firm A spots this and quietly leaves it alone becuase the margins are great. No one else at either firm knows, and no human ever sat down and agreed to anything.

Is section 12(2) being violated? I want you to actually argue it through four lenses: the plain words of the clause, the purpose behind it, what the 1991 drafters would probably have said if you'd asked them about self-learning pricing agents, and what a reasonable reader today would take the clause to cover. Then say which approach a court or regulator should go with, and — this is the part I care about — what that choice reveals about whether the law is aimed at what people agreed to or at what markets end up doing. Is it even coherent to use the word 'agreement' for two systems that never communicated, or does stretching it that far quietly change the offence?
