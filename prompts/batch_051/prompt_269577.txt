I've been turning over a question about what it means to say that a system that isn't human—a chess engine, a thermostat, a language model—has beliefs or desires. Some time ago I came across a way of sorting predictions about behavior into three kinds: we can predict what a thing will do from its physical parts and the laws they obey, from what it was designed or evolved to do, or by treating it as a rational agent that has beliefs, aims, and expectations and will act to get what it wants. Treating a thing as an agent—what I've seen called the intentional stance—is the one I keep getting stuck on. A friend of mine insists that taking this stance toward a system is justified only when it earns its keep by letting you predict behavior that the other two approaches can't, and that when it does earn its keep, the beliefs and desires it attributes are as real as anything else in science because they pick out real patterns in what the system does. Another friend says no amount of predictive success could show that a system really has beliefs, since a good prediction tells you only that a description is compact and useful, not that it matches anything inside the system. They have argued about this for a year, and I cannot see how any observation would choose between them.

I would like to know how one would test the claim rather than merely argue over it. The claim is this: a system genuinely has beliefs and desires exactly to the extent that the best available way to predict and explain its behavior is to treat it as an agent. Design the strongest test of that claim you can. Choose one or two concrete systems, say what data you would collect, what results would count in favor of the claim and what would count against it, and explain how one would decide that the agent-level description beats the physical and design-based descriptions without rigging the comparison. Then be explicit about what a favorable result would not settle. If treating a system as an agent predicted its behavior better than the alternatives, would that show that the system really has beliefs, or only that belief-talk is a compact way of tracking real patterns in its behavior? Is there any observation that could distinguish those two readings, and if there is not, what does that tell us about the claim: is it partly testable but with a residue, or is the demand for something beyond predictive success asking a question that no test can reach?
