I teach a data-literacy / science-communication class for undergrads, about 20 students, one 75-minute meeting. I want an in-class activity on Simpson's paradox that does not start with a lecture: I want the reversal to happen to them before anyone says the name. The takeaway they should feel is the gap between "each subgroup says treatment A beats treatment B" and "the pooled data says B beats A" — and the vertigo of realizing both are true at once, not a mistake in the arithmetic.

Design a structured small-group activity (four groups of five) where the students themselves produce a two-group dataset with that property. You pick the setting — medical treatment, admissions, batting averages, whatever makes the arithmetic easiest by hand. They should compute the subgroup results first, write down a prediction for the pooled result, then merge and be surprised. Include rules, materials (cards, chips, a worksheet, whatever), a concrete procedure or sample numbers so the reversal reliably shows up no matter how a group shuffles or assigns things, a timeline with minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the paradox. Say what I do in the first two minutes and what I do in the last ten.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it the first time — arithmetic errors, students merging the rows wrong, someone in the class already knowing the trick — and how to preempt or handle each. And be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: I suspect it can't, by itself, teach them why the reversal is legitimate rather than a trick, or how to decide which level to act on. Tell me what I'll have to supply myself after the debrief, with one worked example of the decision problem maybe.

Keep it to at most 1000 words, and make it a run sheet I could hand to a co-instructor without explaining anything else.
