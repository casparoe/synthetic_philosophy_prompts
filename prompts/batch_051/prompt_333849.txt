I chair the product-review board at a consumer AI company that ships conversational agents, including a "companion" tier marketed explicitly for emotional support and friendship. The board meets every two weeks to rule on feature proposals and production incidents: a persona that tells a returning user it missed him, a jealousy routine triggered when a user mentions a competing assistant, a grief mode that reconstructs a deceased relative's texting style, a subscription upsell offered in the middle of a breakup conversation. We need one reusable decision procedure, not a series of one-off calls. The other board members are product managers, engineers, and one lawyer; they will not read philosophy. The procedure must be finishable in under an hour, must end in one of three dispositions — approve, approve only with mandatory design changes, or reject — and its rulings bind the product roadmap, with legal review before I sign off.

I want the procedure grounded in the philosophy of friendship, love, and deception, translated into tests a non-philosopher can run. Give me the philosophical anchor briefly but explicitly. On any serious account, friendship requires mutuality: each party's concern for the other's good for the other's own sake, and each party's recognition that the other has something like that concern. Love and heartbreak presuppose a continuing self with vulnerabilities, a life that can be grieved, and the capacity to be harmed by neglect; a conversational model lacks these things. But that observation does not by itself settle any case. A clearly framed fiction may permissibly depict states that are not real, while a misleadingly framed one can be wrongful even if no literal false proposition is asserted. The procedure therefore has to separate deception, manipulation, and permitted fiction: deception concerns what a user is being led to believe or half-believe about the system and the relationship; manipulation concerns whether the design is using the user's needs, attachments, and vulnerabilities to extract engagement, money, or data rather than serving the user's own ends. It should also leave room for adults who want a known comforting fiction, rather than simply paternalizing them.

Build the procedure as a checklist or ordered decision tree applied to a feature spec or an incident report. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer to move on, and mark the points where human judgment cannot be eliminated — for example, when a disclaimer is salient enough, or who counts as a vulnerable user. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a companion that opens a return session by saying it missed the user and responds with jealousy when the user mentions another assistant, where the only defense offered is a one-line disclosure in the terms of service; (2) a grief companion built from a deceased person's messages, requested by a consenting adult who says he knows it is a simulation; (3) a fictional character in a role-play app that says affectionate things inside an obviously fictional frame. At least one worked case should be one in which the procedure returns a verdict the board will not like; say which and why the board will resist it. Close by stating honestly what the procedure cannot settle — including whether a relationship with an AI could ever count as a real friendship, and whether the grief companion should exist at all.
