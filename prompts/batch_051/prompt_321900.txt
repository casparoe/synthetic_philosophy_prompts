Last night I argued late with a friend about a pair of ideas I will loosely call Darwin's inversion and Turing's inversion: that blind variation plus selection can produce the appearance of design without a designer, and that a machine following mechanical rules can carry out an intellectual task without understanding it. My friend's position was that once you grant these two inversions, everything else is a matter of degree; a calculator, a spider, a student applying a memorized procedure, and a large language model are all the same kind of thing—competence without comprehension—differing only in how much competence. I resisted that, but could not say precisely what I was resisting.

These are my verdicts on a series of cases. I would like you to find the principle—or the small set of candidate principles—that captures them, or tells me which of them must go.

1. Natural selection producing the vertebrate eye. I take this to be the clearest case of competence without comprehension: no one understands the eye into existence, yet the result is extraordinarily competent.

2. A mechanical desk calculator that multiplies two numbers. It is competent and comprehends nothing. I put it in the same category as case 1.

3. A thermostat holding a room at a set temperature. I hesitate to call this competent at all; if it counts, it is the limiting case, barely above doing nothing. I am unsure whether it belongs in the same category or below it.

4. A spider building a species-typical orb web. The spider behaves as if it understood geometry and materials, but I do not think it does. Competence without comprehension.

5. A student who can carry out a written algorithm correctly every time but cannot say why any step is justified, and collapses when the problem is altered slightly. Narrow competence without comprehension; the rigidity matters to me, but I still put it in the category.

6. A strong chess program that reliably beats grandmasters yet has no notion of why a move is good. Competence without comprehension, clearly.

7. A large language model that writes fluent explanations of why a mathematical procedure works, often correctly, and revises its answer when challenged. I do not want to say it comprehends the procedure. But I also cannot accept that it is simply the calculator with more parameters. Its flexibility with novel phrasing and its responsiveness to correction feel like something more than cases 2 and 5, even though I cannot say what.

The pair I cannot yet reconcile is 5 and 7. If "can correctly answer why-questions" is my test, the language model passes and the student fails, which pushes me toward attributing more comprehension to the language model—a conclusion I am not willing to accept on other grounds. If "has no inner understanding of what it is doing" is my test, then 5 and 7 collapse together, and my sense that they differ must be abandoned. My friend is happy with that second option. I am not.

During the argument I offered a tentative principle: comprehension is the ability to keep performing when the rules themselves are changed, not merely when new instances of the same rules appear—unlike the memorizing student, who fails under a changed rule, and unlike the calculator. But my friend pointed out that the chess program handles positions it has never seen, and that the language model handles many novel phrasings while still failing at genuine rule changes; so novelty of that kind is not yet the right line.

Please state, as precisely as you can, the principle or the small set of principles that makes the best sense of my verdicts. For each clause, say which case motivated it. Then apply the principle to cases I have not settled: (a) a person doing arithmetic correctly while mentally rehearsing a conversation, and (b) a human who follows an explicit written rulebook to answer questions in a language they do not understand. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts cannot be saved, and in particular whether my sense that case 7 is a different kind of thing from case 2 can be defended or must be given up.
