The handicap principle is often used to explain why certain displays are honest: a signal is credible only if it is costly enough that a low-quality sender cannot easily afford it, so the cost itself carries information. On this view, a carefully argued essay, a technically accomplished artwork, or a fluent intellectual performance functions as a reliable signal of ability partly because producing it once required scarce time, skill, or effort. That premise has now been destabilized by generative models, which allow almost anyone to produce polished prose, images, video, and code at near-zero marginal cost. Does the handicap principle survive this development? More precisely: which premise of the usual argument does cheap generative production undercut, and does the principle predict that these displays collapse as honest signals, or does it instead predict that receivers will discount them and senders will migrate to signals that remain expensive—live unscripted performance, verified physical feats, costly material expenditure, or staking a reputation on public claims? Say what the handicap principle must assume about the relationship between cost and quality for the standard argument to go through, and whether that assumption fails in this setting or merely relocates to a different currency, such as access to scarce compute, control of a known identity, or willingness to be corrected in public.
