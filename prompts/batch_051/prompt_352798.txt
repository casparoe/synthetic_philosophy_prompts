I'm in a reading group on moral responsibility, and someone came in last week with an argument that seems to show Strawson's whole reactive-attitudes move quietly gives away the store. I keep flip-flopping on it and I'd rather get a second opinion from someone who will actually commit.

Here's the argument, tidied up:

(1) Strawson's strategy is to describe the practice: we hold people responsible through reactive attitudes like resentment and indignation, and we withdraw those attitudes when particular sorts of conditions are cited — accident, coercion, immaturity, mental disorder — but not merely when someone cites the general fact that the universe is deterministic.
(2) So whether a particular act warrants resentment is settled by the considered operation of the practice, not by some independent metaphysical fact about free will.
(3) The practice is open-textured. There's no sharp line in it between background causes that don't undermine the reactive attitudes and conditions that do: the persons exhausted, they had a brutal childhood, they've got an undiagnosed something — these shade into each other.
(4) So for any act you resent, there's some true story about the act's causal history, told in the same language our practice already recognizes as excusing or exempting, that would lead a sensible practitioner to withdraw the resentment.
(5) And the practice itself doesn't supply a fixed stopping point along that story; how much history you tell decides whether the person looks like a participant or an object of management.
(6) But if the only thing separating warranted from unwarranted resentment is where you stop the story, then no resentment is robustly warranted — any one is hostage to further facts of exactly the kind the practice treats as relevant.
(7) Determinism — the claim that every act has a complete causal story — just is the endpoint of that story-telling. So the practice, extended along its own lines, would end up withdrawing all the reactive attitudes. That's the incompatibilist conclusion, reached with no appeal to anything outside the practice.

Now, that conclusion seems wrong, and worse, it looks like exactly the conclusion Strawson set out to block. I'd hate to have to pretend the practice has sharp edges it obviously doesn't. Give me a committed diagnosis: which step do you reject, why did it look right, and what does giving it up cost? Don't just lay out the options and tell me reasonable people disagree; I want a verdict.
