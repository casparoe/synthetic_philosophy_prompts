I been turning over an argument that seems to prove something I don't believe, and I'd like to know which step to be suspicious of. It goes like this.

(1) Suppose someone offers to sell me a share for $10. That offer is itself information: if the seller's information made them think the share is worth more than $10, they wouldn't be selling it to me at $10.

(2) So a rational buyer, seeing a sell offer at $10, should update their estimate of the share's value downward. The seller is telling me, with money on the line, that the share isn't worth $10 to them, given what they know.

(3) Same in reverse. My willingness to buy at $10 is information for the seller, so they should update their estimate upward: this eager buyer must know something that makes the share worth more than $10.

(4) If we both update like this, the gap between my estimate and the seller's estimate should shrink. And once each of us sees that the other still wants to trade, we each factor that in again, until the trade no longer seems good for both of us at the same price.

(5) So if we're both rational and trading purely on our own information, we shouldn't trade with each other at all.

(6) But actual stock markets have huge volume every day, and the story about markets being smart is exactly that they aggregate a lot of people's private information through this kind of buying and selling.

(7) So either that story is wrong, or a lot of real trading is irrational, or one of the steps above is bad.

I'd most like to keep the idea that prices reflect private information, and also the idea that most traders aren't idiots. What does this argument force me to give up, and is the flaw in a particular step or in a background assumption I'm not noticing?
