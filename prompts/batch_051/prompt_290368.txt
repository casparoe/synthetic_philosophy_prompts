After a long argument with a friend, I found myself returning to the chapter on chance in Henri Poincaré’s *Science and Method*, specifically the passage in which he observes that a very small cause that escapes our notice can produce a considerable effect, and that if we knew exactly the laws of nature and the state of the universe at an initial moment we could predict the later state exactly, but since this is never fully possible for us, we attribute the effect to chance. We disagreed about what the passage is doing. Here are the readings we produced:

(1) It is a purely epistemic remark: determinism is untouched, and Poincaré is only saying that our ignorance of small causes is what makes outcomes look chancy.

(2) It is an early statement of sensitive dependence: some systems amplify unmeasurably small differences so quickly that determinism, while true in principle, is predictionally empty.

(3) It is a claim about the semantics of “chance”: we call an event chance not because it is uncaused, but because its cause is too small for us to hold in view, so chance talk is really a report on our cognitive situation rather than on the world.

(4) It is a methodological lesson for science: where small causes can produce large effects, the right scientific response is not to search for ever-finer initial conditions but to study stable regularities and averages, because that is where prediction can succeed.

I feel there is at least one more reading we missed, one grounded in the same passage but genuinely different from these four—not a restatement, special case, or combination of any of them. What is it? Explain the reading, and then say whether it is any good as a reading of Poincaré, or whether it is just an idea the passage happens to suggest.
