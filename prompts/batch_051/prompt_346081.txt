In my early modern political economy seminar we are working through Hume’s economic essays, especially Of Money and Of the Balance of Trade, and I keep getting tangled in an argument I half-constructed from his remarks. The lecturer paraphrased Hume as saying that money is not itself a wheel of trade but only the oil that makes the wheels move more smoothly; that the greater or lesser plenty of money is of no great consequence once prices have adjusted; and that during the interval before adjustment, the new money passes through many hands and enlivens some kinds of industry more than others, depending on where it first enters. I tried to turn this into a tight argument, but I worry it proves that Hume cannot consistently hold all three claims. Here is what I have:

(1) In the long run, an increase in a nation’s money supply, with no change in its real resources or labour, does not increase its real commerce; it only changes the nominal prices attached to the same real exchanges.
(2) If the long-run effect of more money is only nominal, then the real condition of every person and every trade after the adjustment must be exactly what it would have been without the increase.
(3) The real condition of every person and every trade after the adjustment is what determines whether they have genuinely gained or lost from the arrival of the new money.
(4) So no person or trade can genuinely gain from being among the first to receive and spend new money before prices have adjusted.
(5) Therefore Hume’s claim that the entry point of new money can temporarily stimulate particular trades must be false.

I am fairly sure the problem is somewhere in (2) or (4), but I cannot say exactly why, and my study group disagrees: one insists it is valid, another says (1) is false because Hume allows short-run real effects. Identify the first step that fails, explain why it fails on the most charitable reading of Hume, and state the corrected version of that step that would make the argument consistent with Hume’s entry-point remark. Give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
