Since retiring from a career in systems neuroscience, I have been reading moral philosophy, and I keep returning to a resemblance that I cannot tell is a real isomorphism or merely verbal. Bernard Williams's integrity objection concerns an agent whose motivating thought must pass through an impartial principle before he can act. In his well-known example, a man who can save only one of two people, one of them his wife, has added, as Williams sees it, an extra thought—the thought that a general principle permits him to prefer his wife—and that extra thought alienates him from the attachment that should have moved him directly. The rescue should issue from the attachment itself, not from the attachment together with a theory licensing it. A standard reply is that the theory need not be consulted at the moment of action: it can serve as a criterion of rightness while the agent acts from settled dispositions and particular attachments.

This seems to me the same shape as an older debate in motor control. One research program held that skilled movement is produced by the nervous system's continuously minimizing an objective function—minimum jerk, minimum torque change, metabolic cost, and so on—while its rival argued that the trained nervous system mostly executes an acquired policy, a direct mapping from estimated state to motor command, and that the optimizing description is true of the behavior without being a computation carried out inside the agent at the moment of movement. Indeed, inserting a real-time optimization loop is often the wrong account of fluent, overlearned action; an athlete mid-movement would be degraded by recomputing the cost landscape. That looks to me like Williams's point retold in engineering language: the justifying calculation is an extra step in just the way that online cost minimization is an extra computation for the trained reach.

What I cannot decide is whether this is deep or superficial. Is Williams's integrity objection really the claim that the moral cost function must be embedded in something like a policy and not evaluated during action, so that the ethical counterpart of the motor system's acquired policy is simply good character or a set of settled dispositions? Or does the analogy come apart at the crucial point, because Williams is objecting to the source and content of justification—to what makes an action one's own—rather than to the computational economy of acting? If the parallel is genuine, what would it lead one to predict in the moral case, and is there something it predicts that turns out false?
