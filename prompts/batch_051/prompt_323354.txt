Last night a friend and I spent a long time arguing about whether self-deception is even possible, and we kept talking past each other: one of us treated it as a plain fact of everyday life, the other as a contradiction in terms. I have since agreed to lead a discussion on this for first-year undergraduates with no background in philosophy. I do not want to give them a survey of positions, and I do not want to resolve the argument for them; I want to build up the question itself so that it genuinely bites. Plan the session's explanatory arc: the distinction or prior idea that has to be in place before the question even makes sense, the one example or case that should carry the whole session and what it must have and must lack, the misconceptions students will arrive with or form along the way and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off, and a final check for whether they have understood the problem rather than just learned to repeat it. Also tell me what to leave out at this level and why. Throughout, separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
