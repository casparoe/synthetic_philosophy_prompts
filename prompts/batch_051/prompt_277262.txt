I'm prepping a philosophy of language unit on Davidson's radical interpretation and I want to use the film Arrival (Amy Adams as the linguist Louise Banks) as the running example instead of the standard textbook cases, but I keep going back and forth about how well it actually fits. My rough sense is that the early contact scenes are a decent dramatization of radical interpretation: Banks has no translation manual, no shared history, no independent grip on heptapod psychology, so she has to attribute a mostly shared world and rationally coherent beliefs just to get interpretation off the ground, and the film is pretty good about how much of that is assumption rather than discovery. But then the movie leans hard into a strong Whorfian idea, where learning the heptapod language doesn't just add a new vocabulary but actually changes how she experiences time. Davidson, as I read him, would treat that as the very thing that can't be made sense of: you only ever identify another scheme as different against a massive background of shared beliefs, and a language that supposedly rewires how you experience time is either a local difference intelligible within a shared world or it isn't really a different scheme at all. So I'm not sure how to pitch this too undergrads. Where does Arrival genuinely dramatize radical interpretation, where does it depart from Davidson in a philosophically interesting way rather than just as a plot device, and is the Whorfian ending best read as an objection to Davidson or as a backhanded confirmation of the principle of charity? I'd also like a specific scene as the hinge where film and theory come apart, and an honest note on where the movie resists the philosophical reading so I'm not flattening it into an illustration just to make the theory look good.
