In my philosophy of language class we just finished a unit on vagueness, and my professor, who's a supervaluationist, keeps saying "vagueness is semantic indecision" like that settles the whole debate. Honestly it's doing to much work. It came up three times in one afternoon. First, someone asked whether there's a sharp cut-off for "heap" (like, is there a number of grains n where n grains is a heap and n-1 isn't) and the prof said there's no fact of the matter, stop looking for a boundary, it's just semantic indecision. Second, someone asked whether the law of excluded middle fails for vague predicates, and the prof said yes, at least in borderline cases "this is a heap" ends up neither true nor false, because the language hasn't decided. Third, a friend from a metaphysics seminar said "great, so reality itself is vague and the world has no sharp joints" and the prof shut that down: no, the words are indecisive, the world doesn't have to be.

The problem is a grad student I talked to says the slogan isn't even obviously anti-epistemicist. An epistemicist can grant that we never made any decision and still hold that our total usage plus facts about the world fixes a sharp boundary anyway, so the only "indecision" is in what we know, not in what's true. So which precise claim inside "vagueness is semantic indecision" is doing the real work against that?

Could you lay out the different precise things that slogan could be claiming, from the boring to the radical, and for each say: is it trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false? And does it actually support each of the three moves my professor made — the no-sharp-boundary move, the excluded-middle-failure/neither-true-nor-false move, and the vagueness-is-only-in-language-not-the-world move? I mostly want to know which one is the weakest reading that an epistemicist genuinely has to deny, because I want to go to office hours and pin down which version the prof means.
