I've been going in circles between a Dennett-ish line and a Caruso-ish line on punishment, and I want help figuring out wich part of me to give up. On reflection I'm drawn to the Caruso-style, public-health/quarantine model: no one ever fundamentally deserves to suffer for what they did, because every bad act is the causal upshot of genes, brain wiring, and circumstances nobody chose. On this view blame and punishment are only tools—incapacitation, deterrence, moral training—and they're justified iff they make the future better, the way you quarantine a typhoid carrier without hating them. It's clean, it's unified, and it hands me a tidy decision procedure: punish when and only when the expected future benefits outweigh the harms, and no amount of extra 'wickedness' ever licenses extra pain for its own sake.

But I also have a conviction I can't shake. When I imagine a man who rapes a child and then brags about it, or a CEO who drains pensioners' savings and sleeps fine, I don't just think 'stop him, deter others, move on.' I think it would be wrong—a real injustice—if he ended his days comfortably on a beach. I think something is deeply askew in the world if he doesn't suffer in proportion to what he chose to do. And the intuition isn't that his suffering would be useful; it's that he merits it, even if it deters no one.

So this is a straight clash between a theory I find compelling and an intuition I find firm, and I don't want a boring 'it's partly both' answer. Give me the strongest case that the desert intuition is doing real work—tracking something the forward-looking story leaves out—and the strongest case that it's just the punitiveness module misfiring, no more trustworthy than wanting to kick a vending machine that ate your dollar. Then tell me wich should yield, and what would justify each move: debunking and dropping the intuition, amending or abandoning the theory, or finding a reading of 'deserves' on which both survive. Say what each option costs, not just which side you'd take.
