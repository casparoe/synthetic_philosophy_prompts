I chair the investment committee of a family foundation with an effectively perpetual horizon and no near-term liquidity needs. Before us is a proposal from an outside manager to put the entire endowment into a recurring strategy: each quarter, stake the whole current endowment on a binary position that gains 50% if the trade succeeds and loses 40% if it fails, with even odds. The manager emphasizes that each quarter the expected return is positive — five percent of the amount at risk — and argues that repeated exposure is therefore unambiguously in our long-run interest. A faction on the committee objects in the language of ergodicity economics. Because the foundation lives through exactly one realized history, they argue, the relevant quantity is the time-average growth rate of a single wealth path, not the average across a large ensemble of parallel foundations; and for the full-stake policy described, that time-average growth rate is negative, so the strategy enriches the ensemble of possible foundations on average while making the typical foundation poorer over time. Others reply that this only shows we should not stake the entire endowment each quarter, and that a smaller fixed-fraction policy with the same per-round edge may be defensible under ordinary expected-utility reasoning. Draft the short decision memo I will circulate before next Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — approve the full-stake policy, reject the strategy outright, or approve a modified version with an explicit maximum position size and rebalancing rule — and it should give the committee a reasoned basis for acting, including a serious treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side. In the memo, please separate the parts of the dispute that are mathematically settled from the parts that remain genuinely contested among people who work on this material, and do not present the ergodicity-based critique of expected-value reasoning as if it were the consensus.
