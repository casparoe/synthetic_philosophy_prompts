I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, so please treat this as a question from a practitioner rather than a specialist. I work on systems that personalise content and make recommendations for users, and I have watched the same argument run through my field for years without either side moving.

One camp holds that a system should aim at the user's considered preference: what she would choose if she knew all the relevant facts about the options and their likely effects, had time to deliberate, and was not being driven by fatigue, fear, or impulse. The other camp holds that this is just second-guessing the user with extra steps, because the only preferences we actually have access to are the messy ones she expresses in practice. On this view, the informed, calm version of her is a model we ourselves have built, and the choice of model quietly does the moral work while presenting itself as neutral.

The dispute recurs in concrete design cases. A user states a goal, such as saving money or reading more substantive material, and then repeatedly chooses things that undermine it. One camp treats the stated goal as the real preference and says the system should steer towards it. The other camp says no: the stream of actual choices simply is the preference, and the stated goal is just one more utterance, produced in a particular context and no more authoritative than the rest. Each side has a debunking story about the other. The first side says that deferring to whatever a person happened to click on at two o'clock in the morning is not respecting her but treating a recording of her mistakes as a constitution. The second side replies that the idealising side must specify which distortions to remove, ignorance but what about caution, fatigue but what about ambition, fear but what about prudence, and that every such specification is itself a normative choice smuggled in as though it were a technical detail.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides agree on roughly all the facts: that expressed preferences are unstable, that different idealisation procedures can return different answers, and that there is no uniquely neutral way to idealise. What they appear to disagree about is whether there is any fact of the matter about what a user would choose under idealised conditions, or whether a considered preference is always a construction whose construction-choices are themselves value judgements. If that is right, this is a disagreement about what counts as the user's own preference rather than an empirical disagreement about what users are like.

I would like a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than a survey of the two positions and rather than a verdict on which side is correct. Is this a verbal disagreement? Do the sides differ over matters of fact, over standards of evidence, over which cases count as data, or over starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the real work, such as whether preferences stabilise under particular kinds of reflection, and would answering it actually settle anything? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change in how the question is asked, or should I conclude that the choice between the two camps is itself a value commitment and that no amount of argument should be expected to close it?
