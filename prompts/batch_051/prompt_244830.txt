Coming to this question from the natural sciences, I have been struck by the standard argument in historiography that presentism is unavoidable. As I understand it, the argument runs as follows: a historian must choose which evidence to consult, which questions to put to it, and which concepts to apply to past actors, and each of these choices is inevitably governed by the standards and concerns of the historian's own time; therefore no historical account can be written from a standpoint free of present-day commitments. I would like to know what becomes of this argument if one of its assumptions is changed. Suppose that a substantial part of historical work came to be done algorithmically: large digitised corpora, topic modelling, and language models trained on the archive select, collate, and describe patterns across sources without first being instructed by a historian about what is presently relevant or morally significant. Does the unavoidability argument survive this development, does it now show something weaker or different, or does it simply become inapplicable? I am particularly interested in which premise the development bears on, and whether the argument can be restored in another form once that premise is made explicit.
