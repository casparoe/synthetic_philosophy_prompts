Im doing a philosophy-for-kids unit on fairness and free riding, and the story that keeps coming up is The Little Red Hen. You know the beats: the hen finds the wheat and asks the other animals to help plant it, harvest it, thresh it, mill it, and bake it, and each time they all say "not I"; but when the bread comes out of the oven they all volunteer to help eat it, and the hen eats it herself (or with her chicks, depending on the version). The moral everyone takes away is that it's wrong to enjoy what others produced without contributing, and the hen is right to withhold the bread.

Im trying to get my kids past the stock moral, and I realized I don't actually know how the story sits with a more careful account of free riding. It seems to me the animals never really free rode on the hen — they declined an offer, with full information, and then were denied the benefit, which sounds like plain voluntary non-participation rather than parasitism. Or is their last-minute willingness to eat the bread the free ride? Does the fact that nobody needed the bread matter — would the hen's refusal be less righteous if one of the animals were starving? I'm also wondering whether the story's clean "no work, no reward" logic carries over to the cases where free riding actually worries us, like public goods you can't exclude anyone from, or cases where someone can't contribute at all. Basically: is The Little Red Hen a good way to teach what's wrong with taking without contributing, or does it teach a narrower lesson about desert, and maybe even mislead about what makes free riding bad? I'd like a reading that's faithful to the actual story and to the philosophy, and that says honestly where the two come apart.
