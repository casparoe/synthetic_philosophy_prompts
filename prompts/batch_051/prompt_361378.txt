I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm developing a piece on a premise that now runs through a great deal of AI safety and alignment work: that a system can recover what a person genuinely wants by watching what they do. This is the conceptual core of techniques like inverse reinforcement learning and preference learning, but it also revives an older problem about the distance between revealed choice and endorsed value. Before I interview researchers, I want to be able to say with some precision what any adequate account of this inference would have to accomplish — not what current algorithms happen to do, but what the standard of success ought to be.

Write the desiderata. Specifically: what must a theory of inferring human preferences from behavior be able to explain or handle in order to count as adequate? For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it — for example, the fact that many different reward functions can rationalize the same observed behavior, self-defeating or time-inconsistent choices, or actions done from habit, compliance, or impression management. Say which requirements are non-negotiable and which are demands one camp would make that another could reasonably reject. Then make a judgment: which two or three requirements do the most to separate a serious account from a naive one, and is there any pair of requirements that probably cannot be jointly satisfied? Close by telling me which issue I should press the researchers on most. Please write the answer in American English.
