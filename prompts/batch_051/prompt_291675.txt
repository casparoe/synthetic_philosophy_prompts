Here's a parallel I can't decide about. Ergodicity economics distinguishes the time average — what happens to one agent who takes a gamble over and over — from the ensemble average — what happens across many agents who each take it once. For multiplicative, compounding payoffs the two can diverge: a bet can have a positive expected value and still ruin the person who takes it repeatedly, because losses compound. I started wondering whether Kant's universalization test has the same two readings. Asking what would happen if everyone adopted my maxim looks like an ensemble question: all agents act on it at once, and I check whether the resulting world is even coherent. But I could also ask what happens to one person who adopts the maxim as a standing policy over a whole life: a time-average question. And the two can apparently come apart. Petty free-riding looks fine, maybe even prudent, as a one-person policy over a life; it collapses if everyone does it at once. A hyper-scrupulous maxim might be fine if everyone followed it but slowly ruins the one person who treats it as a life-policy. Is this a real structural parallel — non-ergodicity showing up in the moral domain — or just a verbal analogy, since Kant's test isn't aggregating payoffs or outcomes at all? If the structure is real, what would have to be true for the results to transfer, and what would the parallel predict that turns out false?
