I'm translating a set of oral histories from a language with obligatory evidentiality — the verb itself marks whether the speaker witnessed the event, inferred it, or only heard about it from someone else. The code of conduct I work under says: "A translator shall render the meaning, tone, and style of the source text as faithfully as the target language permits; where no equivalent is attainable, the translator shall choose the closest attainable rendering and shall not knowingly allow the loss to pass unmarked." English doesn't have that category, so I keep flattening every distinction into the plain past tense. To keep them I'd have to scatter "apparently," "reportedly," "I saw it myself" through every sentence, which wrecks the oral style, or write it clean and put a translator's note at the head of the chapter saying the distinctions are untranslatable.

The chapter in question is built on exactly this: the narrator carefully separates what she witnessed from the half-remembered stories she was told, and in my draft its basically invisible. The people who drafted the code were clearly imagining puns, tone, and local gaps, not a whole grammatical category the target language has no room for. What does the clause actually require of me there? Walk through a literal reading, a purposive reading, and what the drafters would probably have said if this case had been put to them, then say which should govern and why.
