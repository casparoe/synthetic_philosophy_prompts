For a magazine piece in progress, I am trying to develop an argument about Adam Smith’s treatment of economic ambition in Part IV, Chapter I of The Theory of Moral Sentiments. Here is the working sketch.

Smith describes the poor man’s son being drawn toward wealth and rank and argues that, if he reaches them, he finds their real advantages over a modest working life are slender: the difference is mainly bodily ease and convenience, not lasting happiness. Yet Smith also says this restless pursuit, driven by the imagination, is not simply regrettable, because it rouses people to cultivate land, build cities, and create order. On my reading, the pivotal idea is that objects of wealth and greatness are fitted to excite the mind because they appear as beautiful systems of adaptation. Prosperity thus has a moral worth that is not reducible to the private satisfactions it buys, but also not independent of the useful order it tends to produce. It has worth because the appearance of a well-ordered, abundant system of means and ends can recruit effort and admiration even before the agent fully grasps its real effects.

The question I would like pressure on is whether this position is stable. If the value of prosperity is located in the appearance of utility, or in the aesthetic satisfaction of contemplating grand systems, then it seems the moral worth of wealth could survive even when actual welfare is not improved. But if it is only a useful illusion, then prosperity’s worth collapses into pure instrumental value, and the deception starts to look ethically suspect. I want to defend a third option: prosperity has a real but conditional moral value as a socially visible form of order, one that is good only when joined to genuinely useful ends. If that works, it gives a reason to reject both a flat instrumentalism about wealth and any claim that abundance is a final good in itself.

I would appreciate comments on whether this reading of Smith is fair, where the third option is most likely to break apart, and whether the broader argument about wealth’s non-instrumental value can survive if the aesthetic justification is removed. In your answer, please say how confident you are in your claims and single out the point you are least sure of.
