I am enrolled in a graduate seminar on the moral limits of parental authority, and in our most recent session we discussed a proposal to require prospective parents to obtain a license before raising children: a short training course, home visits, and an assessment of income and housing stability. One of my classmates objected that this would violate a fundamental liberty and would give the state disturbing power to decide who may form a family. Our professor replied, almost as if that closed the matter, that we were overlooking the child's right to an open future, and that the licensing proposal is justified precisely because it protects that right. I want to respond carefully, but I have realized that I do not know what the phrase actually commits him to. It circulates in debates about genetic selection, religious upbringing, compulsory schooling, and more recently in arguments about children's digital lives, and in each setting it seems to be doing somewhat different work.

What are the distinct precise claims the child's right to an open future could be expressing, once it is disambiguated? Please set out a menu of readings, from the most minimal to the most demanding, and for each indicate whether it is trivial or substantive, whether it is plausibly defensible, and whether it would actually underwrite the professor's conclusion about parental licensing. I am not looking for a single reconstruction with one long argument behind it; I need a survey of the options with verdicts, so that I can ask him in office hours which reading his argument requires and whether that reading survives scrutiny. Keep it crisp enough to bring straight into that conversation.
