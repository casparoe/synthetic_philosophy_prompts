In my philosophy of mind class we were given a list of markers for attributing consciousness to a non-human animal: (1) centralized integration of sensory information, something like a brain; (2) associative learning and memory; (3) behavioral flexibility in novel situations rather than fixed reflexes; and (4) at least the machinery for pain, not necessarily felt pain. The instructor said each marker is defeasable but that together they point at there being something it is like to be that animal.

The case I keep coming back to seems to split the list down the middle: box jellyfish. From what I understand, they have a bunch of eyes, including lensed camera-type eyes, but no brain. Instead they have a nerve ring plus four little clusters of neurons near their sensory structures, and the processing is pretty local. In a recent experiment, a box jellyfish learned to associate a low-contrast stripe pattern (meant to look like the mangrove roots it keeps bumping into) with a mild collision, and after a handful of trials it started avoiding the stripes. It also steers around roots and swims toward gaps in the canopy in the wild, so its behavior isn't all stereotyped. But there is no central integrator, and I've seen nothing suggesting it feels pain; damaged ones seem to just keep swimming.

So is a box jellyfish conscious on the criteria we were taught? Or is the "centralized brain" criterion just vertebrate chauvinism, and does this case show the list itself needs revising? Walk through it criterion by criterion, and say where the criteria underdetermine the verdict.
