In Section X (“Of Miracles”) of his *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Hume writes:

“One, who in our climate, should expect better weather in any week of June than in one of December, would reason justly, and conformably to experience; but it is certain, that he may happen, in the event, to find himself mistaken.”

I read this as an early, compact statement of what rationalist circles would call the outside view: the wise reasoner lets accumulated, aggregate experience set a base rate, and treats the particular reasons in front of them as a perturbation that can lose in any single case without thereby refuting the method. Hume immediately turns the point into a maxim about testimony and miracles, but I do not want the standard miracle debate. I want the general epistemic principle.

Treat the outside-view principle expressed in that passage as a position, and tell me what would count against it. I am not asking for a defense or the usual catalogue of biases; I want a disciplined map of its exposure. What would have to be true, or what cases would have to be described, for this principle to be false or importantly incomplete as a general account of how we should form expectations about matters of fact? Distinguish the defeaters that would be decisive from those that would merely force qualifications. In particular, address at least these candidate problems: the selection of the reference class (which base rate is “the” outside view?), nonstationary environments where the aggregate past is a misleading guide to a changed present, and cases where an inside view contains causal-structural information that the base rate averages away. Which of these, if any, would a committed outside-view theorist be forced to concede counts against the principle as stated, and which would they dismiss as local refinements? End with an honest judgment about whether the principle is well insulated, and whether that insulation is a feature or a problem.
