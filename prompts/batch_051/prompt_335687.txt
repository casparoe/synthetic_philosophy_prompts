Consider the thesis that for any human person, there is no possible world in which that very person exists but develops from a different pair of gametes than the sperm and egg that actually produced them. Call this gametic origin essentialism about persons, understood as a claim of metaphysical necessity rather than a merely epistemic limitation on our ability to reidentify people across scenarios. I would like your actual credence that this thesis is true, stated as a number, together with the reasoning that produces it.

Please decompose the question into the main sub-questions your credence rides on, and address at least the following:

1. How likely is it that persons have any essential origin at all — that is, that some feature of their actual material or causal origin partly fixes their identity across possible worlds — rather than identity being fixed by something else, such as psychological continuity, a role in an unfolding history, or nothing beyond stipulation? Give a rough probability that some form of origin essentialism holds.

2. Conditional on some form of origin essentialism's being true, how likely is it that the essential item is specifically the pair of gametes, as opposed to the zygote, the genome, or the broader causal-historical chain? In particular, say how you handle the fact that monozygotic twins arise from the same zygote (and the same sperm and egg) yet are two persons, and whether that bears on the necessity claim or only on a sufficiency claim.

3. How much weight do you give to unusual cases — natural chimerism from the fusion of fraternal twin embryos, IVF mix-ups, reproductive cloning from a somatic cell, and other departures from ordinary reproduction — either as counterevidence to gametic essentialism or as cases our concept of identity simply does not settle?

4. How likely is it that the proposition is indeterminate — that "could have developed from different gametes" lacks a well-defined truth value once metaphysical and epistemic readings of "could" are separated and we look at how identity boundaries are actually drawn? If you allow a nontrivial probability of indeterminacy, say so and show how you combine it with the truth-apt alternatives.

Then show how these components yield your overall credence — for example, by multiplying conditionals, or by explaining where the components are correlated — and identify two things: the sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and one concrete thought experiment or empirical finding whose outcome would move your credence more than any other. Please resist dissolving the question into "it depends on the correct theory of personal identity"; the point of the decomposition is to put probabilities on the rival theories themselves.
