I am a machine learning researcher, and I have just come out of a long argument with a friend who works in epistemology. The question was whether testimony — roughly, forming beliefs on the word of others — is a basic source of justification, more like perception, or whether whatever justification testimony gives must reduce to evidence about the reliability of the speaker and the general correlation between reports and the world. She spoke of reasons, entitlements, and default warrant; I kept reaching for the vocabulary I actually work in: pretraining and fine-tuning distributions, self-supervision, grounded versus ungrounded training signals, hallucination and confabulation, calibration, in-context learning, source reliability, out-of-distribution generalization, sim-to-real transfer, and reward models.

Act as a philosopher of epistemology who is fully fluent in ML research. Do not give me analogies; give me a systematic translation of the reductionist and anti-reductionist positions about testimony into that ML vocabulary, as faithfully as you can and without letting the translation quietly settle the debate in favor of one side. For each position, say which claims translate cleanly, which translate only under strain, and which have no counterpart at all. Pay attention to the global-versus-local split within reductionism and to the anti-reductionist idea that a speaker can have default but defeasible credibility. Then give me a residue report: where the translation fails, is my vocabulary missing a distinction the philosophers are right to insist on, or is the original disagreement less substantive than its terminology suggests? Before writing the translation, map each side's core commitments onto the nearest ML concepts and flag the mappings that are tempting but misleading.
