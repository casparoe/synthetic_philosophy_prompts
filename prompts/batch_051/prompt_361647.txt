I help run a residents' association in a block of flats, and we are about to vote on a change to our standing orders. The problem is familiar: the same dozen or so members dominate the monthly meetings with sweeping pronouncements about how other people should behave — noise, shared repairs, the bins, subletting — while several of them are privately among the worst offenders. I have been reading about the Ming-dynasty Confucian thinker Wang Yangming, and I found his idea of the unity of knowledge and action striking: he held that genuine knowledge is not a separate mental step that may or may not be followed by a separate behavioural step, but that action is already part of what it is to know something, so a person who can recite a moral principle but never acts on it does not truly know it. A neighbour, a retired civil engineer, has proposed the following rule in response: at any general meeting, no member may urge a change to the association's rules or criticise another member's conduct unless they first state at least one specific, dated instance from the past twelve months in which they themselves acted in accordance with the principle they are invoking. Alternatively, they may record that they have been failing to act on that principle and state concretely what they will now do differently. These declarations would be entered into a public practice ledger kept by the association, and any appeal to a general principle without such a declaration would be ruled out of order.

Please approach this as an expert on institutional design and moral psychology, and think carefully before answering. I would like you first to brainstorm the likely first- and second-order effects of the rule, and then walk through how different kinds of members would actually respond: the habitual lecturers, the quiet rule-followers, the skilled self-justifiers, the people whose good conduct is genuine but undocumented, and the members who will simply stop turning up. Pay particular attention to how the rule would be gamed — for example, by retroactively selecting flattering instances, by manufacturing small virtues so they can be cited later, or by turning public self-confession into a performance that silences others — and say whether the ledger would do genuine work or become a contest of its own. Finally, give your judgement on whether the rule would select for the unity of knowledge and action that Wang Yangming described, or for something else such as superior record-keeping, hindsight narration, and self-presentation, and whether any amendment, perhaps requiring witnesses rather than self-reports or restricting declarations to recent failures, would fix the worst failure modes without hollowing the rule out. Please write your answer in British English.
