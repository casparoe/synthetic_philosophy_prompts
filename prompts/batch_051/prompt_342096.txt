I have been trying to get clear about how a language can carry so much homonymy and polysemy without breaking down, and I have found that my own judgments about where one word ends and another begins are far more confident than anything I can extract from the standard polysemy/homonymy distinction. Here are my cases and verdicts.

Bank in the financial-institution sense and bank in the riverside sense are, I am sure, two different words that happen to sound alike. If a dictionary tells me they share a history, that does not move me: ordinary speakers do not command that history, so it cannot be part of what they know when they use the word.

Pitch in pitch a baseball, musical pitch, pitch a tent, and pitch meaning the black tar: whatever the philology says, I refuse to count these as one word with a family of senses. They are several words wearing the same sound.

Run in run a mile, the water is running, the engine is running, run a company, run a program, a stocking has a run, and a candidate is running: here the opposite. One word, with a sprawling but genuine family of senses.

Tone in tone of voice, the tone of an essay, the tone of a school, muscle tone, and a musical tone: again one word, even though the uses reach into social register, physiology, and acoustics.

Pay in pay a bill, pay a visit, pay attention, and crime does not pay: one word, with something systematic going on, as if a cost metaphor were extending into domains that no longer involve money.

So my verdicts split these cases into one-word-with-many-senses and several-words-with-one-sound, and I can report the split without being able to state a principle that draws the line without quietly consulting etymology or a dictionary. Is there one account of lexical individuation that delivers all five verdicts? If so, what does it say about cases I have not listed: crane for the bird and the machine, seal for the animal and the act of closing, shower for rain and for washing, hard for a hard surface, a hard problem, and hard water, and stock for inventory, shares, broth, and family line? And if no single principle delivers them all, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and why? Since my underlying interest is ambiguity tolerance: whatever principle is right, which features of context, frequency, or meaning structure do the real work in letting communication proceed so smoothly despite all of this?
