You are an expert in social choice theory and formal epistemology. I have been working through some material on group decision-making and would like you to think through this carefully with me. Do not simply answer the surface question: if my framing rests on a mistake, begin by identifying and correcting that mistake, and only then give your considered view. Before settling on an answer, take some time to brainstorm the strongest objections to the position I sketch.

Imagine a three-member hiring committee that must decide whether to make an offer to a candidate. The committee's standing rule is to hire if and only if two conditions both hold: the candidate's research is excellent, and the candidate's teaching is excellent. Each member's individual judgments are perfectly consistent with this rule. Member A thinks the research is excellent but the teaching is not, so she votes against hiring. Member B thinks the teaching is excellent but the research is not, so he votes against hiring. Member C thinks both are excellent, so she votes to hire. Now suppose the committee aggregates its views. On the final question of whether to hire, a majority of two to one says no. But on the underlying premises, a majority of two to one says the research is excellent, and a majority of two to one says the teaching is excellent — which, under the committee's own rule, entails that they should hire. So the majority's positions on the premises point one way while the majority's position on the conclusion points the other.

I do not see why this is treated as a deep problem rather than an elementary procedural mistake. The obvious fix is to aggregate premise by premise and then let the standing rule determine the conclusion. If that yields an outcome which most members did not individually support, so much the worse for the conclusion vote: the collective decision would at least be well grounded in shared reasons, which is what committee deliberation is supposed to secure. So what exactly is lost by always aggregating premises first and treating the conclusion vote as irrelevant? Is there any genuine reason for a collective body to aggregate conclusions rather than reasons, or is this dilemma just an artifact of using the wrong aggregation procedure?
