I am at work on a novel in which a neuroscientist character has a habit of ending arguments by saying that, in the end, everything is physical. In a scene I am drafting he uses this to dismiss an interlocutor's worry about whether a molecule-for-molecule simulation of a brain would be conscious: the brain is physical, he says, and since everything is physical, a faithful simulation of all the relevant physical facts simply is a mind. I want the scene to have real weight, and I want the other character to be able to probe the slogan rather than be flattened by it. What are the distinct precise claims that "everything is physical" could be used to assert—claims, for example, about what exists, about what properties there are, about what forms of explanation are legitimate, or about what the word "physical" refers to, whether now or at the end of inquiry? For each candidate reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or probably false; say which meaning people who repeat the slogan most likely intend; and then say which of the readings would actually entitle my neuroscientist to his conclusion about the simulated brain. Please keep the answer to no more than 500 words.
