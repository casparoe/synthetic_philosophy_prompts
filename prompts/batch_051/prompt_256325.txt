Hey, I'm a political philosophy PhD student writing a paper on critiques of effective altruism, and I've hit the point where everything new I read turns out to be a version of something I've already got. My list so far: (1) the institutional critique — EA treats poverty and health as problems for individual donors and mostly ignores the political and economic structures that produce them; (2) the earning-to-give critique — taking a high-paying job in a dubious industry so you can donate more is self-defeating or corrupting and rationalizes the harm you do day to day; (3) the measurability-bias critique — the demand for quantifiable, scalable interventions privileges bednets and deworming and quietly discounts hard-to-measure work like organizing, advocacy, and institutional reform; (4) the demandingness/special-obligations critique — impartial maximizing treats friends and family as interchangeable with strangers and asks for sacrifices that are either psychologically unrealistic or morally objectionable; (5) the longtermism/x-risk critique — the field's turn toward speculative far-future outcomes rests on fragile expected-value reasoning and pulls money away from present suffering. I think thats basically the standard menu. Give me one more objection to EA that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or combination of any of these — something with a genuinely different structure. Then tell me honestly whether it's any good or just a clever restatement.
