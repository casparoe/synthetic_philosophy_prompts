I am a student in a seminar on Hume's account of causation, and I am writing a term paper that requires me to state and defend a definition of what it is for one event to cause another before I can assess whether Hume's own analysis — roughly, constant conjunction together with the idea of necessary connection that the mind projects onto the world — survives the standard hard cases. My difficulty is that any definition I formulate in the abstract keeps giving me the wrong verdict when I press it against particular examples.

Here are the cases I want the definition to handle, with my provisional verdicts. (1) A match is struck and lights: I am confident the striking caused the lighting. (2) A rooster crows every morning just before sunrise: I am confident the crowing does not cause the sunrise, even though it is an impeccable regular precursor. (3) A barometer falls shortly before every storm: I am confident the fall does not cause the storm. (4) While I am away, nobody waters my plant and it dies: I lean toward saying the omission caused the death, even though nothing occurred during the relevant interval. (5) A is about to shoot B when C disarms A; D shoves C aside and A shoots B dead: I lean toward saying D's shove caused B's death, but this looks like causation by the removal of a prevention, and I do not know how to make room for that. (6) Two gunmen fire at a victim at once, either bullet alone would have been fatal, and both hit: I have no firm view about whether each shot caused the death. (7) A throws a rock at a window and it shatters; B's rock would have shattered it a moment later, arriving through the now-empty frame: I am confident A's throw caused the shattering, and I am unsure whether B's throw caused anything.

Please propose a working definition of cause, run it through all seven cases, and say exactly where it gives the wrong answer or no answer; where it conflicts with my provisional verdicts, either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. Then, in light of the definition you settle on, tell me whether a purely regularity-based account can be salvaged or whether the mature view must include something like a necessary connection between cause and effect. I know the course will eventually reach the dispute between the New Hume reading, on which Hume is a kind of causal realist about necessary connection, and the traditional regularity reading, and I want a definition that helps me take a side rather than one that quietly assumes one.
