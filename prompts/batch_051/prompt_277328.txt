I've been developing a three-way distinction among laws that are almost never enforced, and I want an honest assessment of whether it earns its keep. I call them dead-letter laws, sleeper laws, and ornamental laws. Dead-letter laws are statutes routinely violated with public tolerance, which no one seriously expects to see enforced again; their persistence is mostly legislative inertia, like an old Sunday-trading ban that no official would now invoke. Sleeper laws are dormant but not dead: they are almost never enforced, yet a serious attempt to invoke one in a particular case is generally treated as legitimate, which means their dormancy effectively delegates enforcement discretion, like an anti-adultery statute charged only opportunistically. Ornamental laws are kept primarily to express a public commitment, with non-enforcement built into their point rather than a failure of it, like a ceremonial declaration of a national language or symbol. My working claim is that each kind raises a distinct rule-of-law problem: notice and consistency for dead-letter laws, the arbitrariness of selective enforcement for sleeper laws, and the use of law as insincere or purely expressive commitment for ornamental laws. The desuetude debate, as I understand it, tends to run these together under the single question whether long non-enforcement changes a law's status. I would like you to tell me whether the taxonomy is coherent and does real work; whether it, or something near it, already exists in the literature on desuetude, dead-letter statutes, or expressive law; and where it breaks. Are these differences in kind or only in enforcement context, and what would the taxonomy wrongly classify? Give me the three strongest objections and say which, if any, is fatal rather than repairable. Please separate what is settled about desuetude and the rule of law from what is genuinely contested among legal philosophers, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
