You are a legal philosopher specialising in jurisprudence, legal personality, and the private-law treatment of non-human actors. Work slowly and carefully. Begin by brainstorming a larger set of candidate requirements than you will finally keep, and for each candidate you reject, say in one sentence why it is not a genuine desideratum.

Before I read any further arguments about whether AI systems should be granted legal personhood, I want the specification of what an adequate theory of legal personhood has to get right. Write the desiderata, with the motivating case or consideration behind each, and build the list so that it can adjudicate the familiar analogy: business corporations are already legal persons, so some AI systems should be legal persons too. I am interested in the criteria themselves more than in scorekeeping, so spend most of your answer on the list and its justification.

The theory must, at minimum, be put in a position to explain the following. Mark for each whether it is a non-negotiable requirement or a preference of a particular theoretical camp, and where two requirements cannot obviously be jointly satisfied, say so explicitly.

First, the paradigm inclusions: natural persons, including children and adults under guardianship, and business corporations. Second, the paradigm exclusions and contested inclusions: ordinary objects, unorganised collections of people, and the more difficult cases such as rivers granted legal personality in some jurisdictions, which some theorists treat as a reductio and others as evidence of how flexible the concept is. The principle doing the excluding should be stated, not assumed. Third, the functional core: holding rights, bearing duties, owning property, contracting, suing and being sued, and being liable. The theory should say which of these capacities are necessary, which are sufficient, and whether they can be held separately. Fourth, the relationship between legal personhood and moral personhood: the theory should say whether conferring the status is a purely technical device or whether it carries normative meaning, and why. Fifth, the true basis of the corporate analogy: corporations are legal persons in virtue of features such as asset partitioning, limited liability, and continuity through changes of membership, not in virtue of minds or interests in the human sense. The theory must specify which of these features ground corporate personhood and whether any AI system could share them. Sixth, the agency problem: non-persons already perform legally effective acts because automated systems and employees bind their principals. The theory must explain what legal personhood adds over and above a developed law of agency and representation, and why we should not simply model AI systems as agents of human principals. Seventh, the question of liability: the theory must accommodate blame, punishment, and responsibility attaching to non-human persons, as with corporate liability, and say what this means for whether an AI system could be a defendant. Eighth, the unitary or bundle question: the theory should say whether legal personhood is a single status or a cluster of incidents that can be granted partially or for limited purposes.

I suspect two desiderata may be in tension: corporations are persons for exactly the functional reasons of asset partitioning and liability shielding, yet we may want personhood for AI to require something more than functional convenience, so as to avoid sliding into treating any structured software as a person. Check whether this suspicion is right, and state precisely what would have to be given up to resolve the tension either way. Finally, briefly score the main contenders, such as capacity-based, interest-based, bundle-of-rights, fiction or concession, and realist group-person theories, against the list, noting which single requirement excludes the most candidates.
