I've been going back and forth with two friends about whether "better than" has to be transitive, and one of them, who takes the issue to be settled, sent me the following summary of what he thinks the spectrum argument establishes. Before I accept it, I want to know how fairly it represents the dialectic, since I've heard that the interesting part is exactly where the chain of intuitions gets converted into a conclusion about rational choice.

His summary reads:

"The classic spectrum argument shows that 'better than' is not transitive. Start with a short and intensely painful experience — say, a week of agonizing pain. Now move along a spectrum of alternatives: at each step, the pain becomes very slightly less intense but lasts significantly longer, so the next alternative seems, all things considered, better than the previous one. A chain of small improvements therefore leads from a week of agonizing pain to a barely noticeable headache that lasts for a century. Yet when we compare the two ends of the chain directly, the week of agonizing pain is clearly better than the century-long headache. So we reach the final alternative by a string of 'better than' steps, while also judging the starting point better than the final point. That means 'better than' is intransitive. It follows that rational preference can be cyclic, which is a decisive problem for expected utility theory. The only way to preserve transitivity is to insist on a sharp, arbitrary cutoff somewhere on the spectrum, where one more small increase in duration suddenly flips the comparison — and nobody who thinks about it seriously can accept that."

I'm inclined to accept the stepwise comparisons and the endpoint comparison myself, but I'm suspicious of the last two moves — the inference to cyclic rational preferences and the claim that a sharp cutoff is the only alternative. I'd like you to go through this paragraph sentence by sentence and tell me which parts someone working on this issue would accept, which parts they'd call misleading or overstated, and whether the conclusions about cyclic preference and expected utility theory actually follow from the parts that survive correction.
