I run a fortnightly policy reading group, about 18 people, mostly political-science grad students and a few historians, and I have one 90-minute session to spend on lessons-of-history arguments. I want an activity where the group does foreign-policy advice under three rival analogical frames — Munich (don't appease an expanding autocrat), Vietnam (don't get dragged into an unwinnable intervention), and the Thucydides trap (a rising power makes war likely even if neither side wants it) — without me ever naming those analogies up front. The goal is for them to feel how much of the argument is done by the choice of analogy before any of the facts get debated, and especially how the same facts can be slotted into any of the frames without contradiction. So the activity should get the three groups to produce their recommendations seperately, in role, from the exact same one-page briefing, so they can't later blame the disagreement on having had different information.

Design it as a structured deliberation, not a lecture. There should be a real decision at the end: the President has to decide whether to commit security assistance, send troops, or do nothing — or some better menu if you have one. Use a fictional or historically-mixed scenario so pre-loaded beliefs about a real country don't dominate. Stakes can be narrative (an advice memo, a public statement, an annotated map); no real money is needed, but each group has to write its advice down and face questions in character from the President's staff. Keep the analogue names inside the group briefings, not in the plenary, and let each group discover during the debrief that the others were working from a different template. After the groups present, the plenary should try to construct one merged recommendation. That's where the real feeling should hit, because the templates will keep pulling the merged recommendation in opposite directions while every fact stays the same.

For the debrief, I want specific moments from the run mapped to these points: analogies select and weight features; they supply a narrative arc and an emotional tilt; they make disanalogies hard to notice; and talk about the lessons of history often means taking the most available precedent with the strongest mood and treating it as a structure. Also have the debrief cover why the Thucydides trap is odd as a historical lesson, since it purports to be a predictive, structural claim rather than a policy lesson at all.

Then give me the whole design as JSON with this schema:

- title: string, a name for the activity
- participants: string, assumed group and size
- time_minutes: number, total session length
- learning_goal: string, one or two sentences on what participants should come away seeing or feeling, not a list of content
- materials: array of strings, everything I need with counts
- briefing_notes: string, the one-page scenario and the three rival analogical framings condensed so I can write up the actual handouts myself
- phases: array of objects with name, minutes, what_participants_do, facilitator_role
- debrief: array of objects with observation_to_look_for, concept_it_maps_to, prompt_to_launch_it
- likely_failures: array of objects with symptom, cause, remedy
- what_it_cannot_reproduce: string, the part of the idea the activity can't deliver by itself and I'll need to supply
- adaptation_for_small_groups: string, a note for running it with 8 to 10 people

Make sure likely_failures covers the predictable case where one group wins the merge just because it has the strongest talker rather than the best analogical mapping, and make sure what_it_cannot_reproduce admits that real policymakers usually bring their analogies with them from years of prior experience, so the activity overestimates how easy it is to see the analogies as optional once you're inside one.
