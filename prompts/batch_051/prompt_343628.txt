I am taking a course this term on how the past is transmitted, and in a recent seminar a classmate repeatedly invoked the children's game of telephone in order to argue that traditions about the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus can scarcely preserve any genuine historical kernel. As he presented it, each retelling is a link in a chain of whispers, errors compound at every step, and after enough steps the original message is unrecoverable; he took this to show that centuries of oral transmission are enough to dissolve whatever historical events may once have stood behind the stories. The analogy has a good deal of rhetorical force, and I want to know how much argumentative work it can actually bear.

Could you audit the telephone-game picture as a model of oral tradition? In particular, I would like an explicit mapping: what is supposed to correspond to the original utterance, the chain of players, the noise introduced at each relay, and the final report; and then a careful account of where the mapping breaks once we consider how traditions like these are actually performed and transmitted — for example, formulaic composition, meter and recurring type-scenes, specialist performers such as bards, multiple parallel lines of transmission rather than a single chain, and audiences that can correct, reject, or sanction a performer. I am especially interested in which disanalogies matter for the conclusion the analogy is being used to support — that no historical core could plausibly survive — and which are merely incidental. Does that skeptical conclusion survive once the disanalogies are put on the table, or was the telephone game carrying the argument the whole time? If the analogy fails for this purpose, what would a better analogy be, and what would even the better analogy fail to capture?
