I keep noticing a pair of cases that are treated as the same thing, and I want to know whether the difference between them has been identified and named, and exactly where the line between them falls.

In the first kind of case, a single person anticipates that his own later self will choose badly. A man knows that at midnight he will want to break a streak he has decided to maintain, so before the temptation arrives he puts the thing out of reach, or arranges a penalty that makes yielding self-defeating. This is presented as a picture of self-rule: the earlier person constrains the later person, and the two are, in the relevant sense, the same agent. The binding is one's own. Reading around rationality forums, I have seen this kind of case treated as coherent and even admirable.

In the second kind of case, a constitutional assembly entrenches a rule so that a future majority cannot change it except through some deliberately heavy procedure — a two-thirds vote, successive legislatures, a referendum — and it does so explicitly because it expects future majorities to act foolishly in a moment of panic or enthusiasm. The friends of this arrangement describe it with the same grammar of self-rule: the people bind themselves; the polity ties its own hands; the nation makes a promise to itself. But the people whose hands are actually tied at the later moment are mostly not the people who tied them. Some were not born then; many were born and lost; and later persons often experience the entrenched rule not as their own earlier selves reaching forward but as an arrangement imposed by people who are now dead. I have seen entrenched rules criticized as letting the dead govern the living, but that is usually offered as a complaint, not as an analysis of the borrowed justification.

What I want to know is whether this mismatch has been carved up under a name of its own. In particular, is there an established concept for the specific move of borrowing a one-person-across-time justification when the binding party and the bound party are different agents? I am not principally after a term; a bare name will not satisfy me. What I need is a criterion: what has to be true for an entrenched constitutional rule to count as self-binding in the first-personal sense rather than as one group of people binding another? Do the cases of an individual, a club that makes its own dissolution require unanimity, a majority that entrenches a rule to constrain a present minority, and a founding generation that entrenches a rule for generations unborn lie along one spectrum — and if so, what is the variable that does the work: continuity of the same persons, consent, retrospective endorsement, something else? And does the justification fail gradually as the overlap between the binding and the bound changes, or does it change its character all at once?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
