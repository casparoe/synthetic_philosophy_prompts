After a long argument with a friend about Adam Smith's account of moral judgment, I wrote down the position I found myself defending, and I want a consistency check on it before I build on it. The argument left me fairly convinced that the commitments below form a single coherent picture, but my friend insisted I was papering over at least one crack, and I no longer trust my own read. Here is what I defended:

1. Sympathy — understood as imaginatively projecting into another person's situation and finding that I do or do not come to share their sentiment — is the basic mechanism of approval and disapproval.

2. The impartial spectator supplies the standard of correctness: a sentiment, and the action that expresses it, is proper just in case a fully informed and impartial spectator would sympathize with it.

3. The impartial spectator is socially acquired. We come to see ourselves through the eyes of actual others, and the internal judge whose approval and disapproval we heed is built up out of repeated social experience of what is praised and blamed.

4. Even so, the impartial spectator can be a critic of consensus: a fully informed and impartial spectator could condemn a practice that an entire society endorses, so the view is not a crude conventionalism.

5. Self-command is a virtue explained by the same machinery: a passion is proper when it is moderated down to a pitch with which the spectator can go along.

6. Propriety and merit can come apart. I can admit that a con artist shows real courage and composure in the middle of a scam, and even admire the self-command as a quality, while condemning the scam itself without qualification.

Please audit the set: which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with one another, and which only appear to be? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what the least costly repair would be. I would also like to know whether any single revision would do the most to unify the rest. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
