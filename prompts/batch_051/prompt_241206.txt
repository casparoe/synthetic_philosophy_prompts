Last night a friend and I argued for hours about a proposal to replace several single-family houses on our street with a modest apartment building. My friend, who opposes it, kept returning to one analogy: a neighborhood is like a household. The people already inside have built and maintained the life of the place, they alone bear the costs of having it rearranged, and newcomers are at most guests; therefore, the settled residents ought to hold a strong presumption of control over whether and how the neighborhood changes. I found this intuitively weighty but could not tell whether the analogy was doing genuine argumentative work or smuggling in the conclusion.

Please audit that analogy as it is used in housing and zoning disputes. Begin with the correspondence table: what corresponds to the household's members, its shared history, the rooms, the invitation, the right to keep strangers out. Then show where the mapping breaks. In particular, identify which features of a household the analogy quietly imports — intimacy, common ownership, broadly aligned interests, a right to exclude outsiders, the fact that household decisions mostly fall only on the household — and determine which of those a neighborhood or city possesses, which it lacks, and which it has only partially. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion that incumbents' preferences should be presumptively decisive from the ones that are incidental. Finally, say what survives the audit: is there a defensible residue, a legitimate interest of incumbent residents that the analogy points to even if it fails as a full justification, and is there a better analogy for the moral geography of a city's housing?
