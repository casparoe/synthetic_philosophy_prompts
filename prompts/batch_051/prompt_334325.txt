I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas desk, and I've caught myself using a policy I can't tell if it's smart or self-serving. Whenever a source's argument only pays off if you take the far future dead seriously — a thousand years, a million years, whatever — I quietly run the simulation argument on it. Roughly: if we're more likely to be in a simulation than not, the far future is out of our hands, because the simulators could shut us down, tweak the parameters, or just not care about simulated descendants. So any argument whose whole weight sits on far-future stakes gets discounted by me almost to zero, unless it works on near-term stakes too. I don't have a principled rate; in practice it's almost total. My editor calls this a non sequitur: if we're in a simulation, my choices inside it matter exactly as much as if it were base reality, and a simulation could run to its own heat death, so the far future is still there. I don't actually have the metaphysics worked out; the habit just does the work of keeping fanatical math from driving my coverage. Is this a defensible filter, or a bias dressed up as a rule? And if its not defensible as I've framed it, what's the version that keeps whatever is right about the simulation argument while not letting me write off far-future claims wholesale?
