In my seminar on self-reference we've been working through Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, and a student came to office hours with what he called the strict version of the analogy between Gödel's self-referential formulas and self-aware minds. After talking it through, I cleaned his argument up into the following steps. (1) For any consistent formal system strong enough to do elementary arithmetic, Gödel's first incompleteness theorem gives a sentence—call it G—that says, in effect, that G is not provable in that system. (2) Since the system is consistent, G is not provable in it; so G is true, and we humans can see this truth from outside the system. (3) Therefore, for every consistent formal system of arithmetic, the human mind can recognize an arithmetical truth that the system cannot prove. (4) Now suppose, for reductio, that the human mind (or at least its arithmetical capacities) just is a consistent formal system of the relevant kind; call it M. (5) Applying (3) to M, there is a sentence G_M that M cannot prove but the human mind can recognize as true. (6) But if the mind just is M, then anything the mind recognizes as true is something M can prove, or at least formally register as true. (7) Contradiction; so the mind is not a formal system, and no digital computer—which is just the implementation of one—could have a mind. I keep thinking the trouble is with step (2), because it looks like we can only be sure G is true if we've already assumed the system is consistent, and when the system is supposedly our own mind that assumption is doing an awful lot of work. But I can't make the objection precise, and I honestly am not sure the argument isn't valid. So: does the argument go through? If not, tell me exactly which step fails and what the corrected version of that step would have to be. If it does go through, tell me why we should accept the conclusion, strong as it is. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
