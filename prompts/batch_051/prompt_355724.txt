I'm on the exam committee for a state clinical licensure board, and we're in the middle of a fight about the diagnostic-reasoning section of the qualifying exam, which is currently all multiple-choice. A proposal on the table would bring back a "justify your diagnosis" component, where candidates have to say in a few sentences why they picked the answer they picked. We tried this about a decade ago and killed it, mostly because grading was expensive and the reliabilty between scorers was lousy. The opponents of the proposal say the exam's only job is to certify that people get the right answer under time pressure. Their line is that a clinician who reliably lands on the correct diagnosis has true beliefs, and true beliefs are all a patient or a hospital can ask for; making people explain themselves just filters for English fluency, confidence, and a certain kind of test-taking polish. I think this is really the Meno problem wearing scrubs: the pro-justification side says a right answer without an account is just true belief, not knowledge, and that difference is exactly what licensure is supposed to be about. But I can't shake the fairness worry, and the other side is going to call the proposal ableist or at least bad measurement.

I have to circulate a short memo before Thursday's meeting. Please draft it for me: recommend a concrete course of action (reinstate, don't reinstate, or reinstate with conditions), say in roughly what form any justification requirement should take, and give reasons the committee can actually vote on. Most importantly, engage the fairness/disparate-impact objection seriously instead of just restating that knowledge is better than true belief. Keep it actionable, not a philosophy essay, but I do want the epistemology to do real work in the reasoning rather than just decorate the recommendation.
