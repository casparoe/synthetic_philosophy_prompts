I'm a software engineer by training, so I keep trying to refactor my moral intuitions into one rule instead of a pile of special cases. The place where this keeps failing is reparations for historical injustice. I've written down my verdicts on seven cases, and they feel almost right but not quite consistent. I want to know whether there is a single principle — or at most a small set — that captures them, and if there isn't, which verdict I should overturn.

Here are the cases.

1. The forged deed. In 1921 a county bank used a forged deed to foreclose on a Black family's farm. Through mergers that bank still exists, and the same parcel is now the site of one of its branch offices. The family's great-grandchildren are identifiable. I think restitution is owed to them, and I feel no real hesitation.

2. The innocent buyer. As in case 1, except that in 1970 the bank sold the land, without any cloud over the title, to a farming family that knew nothing about the forgery and has worked the land since. I still think the land, or its present value, should go to the original family's great-grandchildren. But I don't think the current family has done anything wrong; the obligation tracks the asset, while the blame doesn't. I'd probably want the innocent buyers to be made whole separately.

3. The survivors. In the 1950s a state-run children's home forced the children in its care to work without pay. About a hundred of those children are still alive, and the state that ran the home still exists. I think they are owed compensation; this feels like the easiest case on the list.

4. The underpaid ancestor. A man spent thirty years being paid roughly half the going rate because segregated labor markets shut him out of better-paying work; he died in 1960. No particular asset can be traced to the lost wages. His grandchildren are identifiable and about average by present-day standards. I lean toward saying something is owed to them, but I'm far less certain than in case 3, and I can't say how large the payment should be or who exactly should make it.

5. The painting with no heirs. A museum holds a painting seized from a Jewish family in 1938. The family was later murdered and left no identifiable heirs. I don't think the museum should keep the painting as though nothing happened, but "to whom" seems unanswerable, and "the public" doesn't feel like the right answer either. My best guess is that the proceeds should go to a fund serving the community the family belonged to, but I can't justify that step very well.

6. The aggregate case. From the 1930s into the 1960s, the U.S. federal government systematically excluded Black Americans from mortgage insurance and related housing programs. Today Black Americans have lower median wealth than the rest of the population, but nobody can trace a specific dollar from a specific government office to a specific family. When I ask whether the government owes something to the group, my gut says yes. When I ask how much, to whom precisely, and by what mechanism, my gut goes silent.

7. The prosperous descendants. In 1900 a mob destroyed a family's successful business while local officials looked on. The family rebuilt within a generation, and the present descendants are wealthier than the national median. I feel the claim is weaker than it would be if the family had stayed poor, but I don't think it vanishes outright: the original owners still bore the loss. What I can't say is what remains to be compensated once the descendants are no longer, in any ordinary sense, worse off.

The asymmetry that bothers me runs between cases 1 and 6. Case 6 involves a vastly larger wrong, yet case 1 is the one where I can name the payer, the recipient, and the amount without hesitation. I'd like to know: is there a principle that explains both my confidence in case 1 and my collapse of intuition in case 6, or are those two verdicts pointing at different principles that I'm trying to run under one variable name?

So the task is this: state the principle, or the small set of principles, that best captures these verdicts. Put it precisely, and say which clause does the work in each of the seven cases. Then tell me what your account predicts for two cases I haven't listed.

- A corporation that never itself stole anything, but in 1950 knowingly financed a stolen-asset purchase and took a cut; the victims are dead, and no stolen asset remains identifiable today.
- A country that wronged an immigrant community 150 years ago and now wants to settle with the descendants, some of whom are poor and some of whom are wealthy.

Finally, say honestly which of my seven verdicts your principle cannot keep without becoming ad hoc, and what you would replace it with.
