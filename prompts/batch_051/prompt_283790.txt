I am preparing a seminar that pairs philosophical work on asylum with fiction and film, and I intend to use Casablanca as the film component. I would like a careful reading of the film through the question of who counts as someone owed refuge and on whom the duty to take such people in falls. The film is usually remembered as a romance, but it is set in a way station crowded with people who have left danger behind and cannot move on without permission controlled by others. I want to know what the film actually does with that material. Does it reduce these refugees to a moral backdrop for the central love triangle, or does it grant any of them the standing of genuine claimants with demands on particular people and institutions? Does the storyline make passage something achieved through personal virtue, sacrifice, or luck, and what would it imply about the view that refuge is owed on the basis of need rather than desert? Which scenes would you draw attention to as revealing the film's assumptions about who may cross a border and who decides, and where should an honest reading concede that the film resists this lens because its deepest interest is private redemption rather than collective obligation? I want a reading that works in both directions: one that lets the ethical question expose the film's unexamined assumptions, and one that lets the film show what a purely institutional account of asylum tends to miss.
