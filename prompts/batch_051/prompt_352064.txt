Consider the standard liberal argument against autarky: deep trade interdependence makes coercion self-defeating, because a state that disrupts a mutually beneficial relationship damages itself at least as much as its target, and therefore can safely specialize and import rather than trying to produce everything at home. The argument quietly assumes that interdependence is roughly symmetric and grows out of ordinary commerce rather than being engineered as a trap.

Now suppose a state can deliberately shape the interdependence before any dispute arises. It can make itself nearly self-sufficient in the goods that matter while keeping its partners deeply exposed; it can monopolize a chokepoint such as a critical input, a transit route, or a clearing system; it can ensure that partners have no usable alternatives for years; and it can starve or buy up the redundant capacity that would let partners exit. When a dispute begins, the mutual-hostage logic no longer binds: one side can absorb the cost of rupture while the other cannot.

Does the anti-autarky argument survive this development? Identify the precise premise that fails first—symmetry, the assumption that dependency always costs the coercer, the assumption that exit remains available, or something else. Then say whether the argument can be repaired, and what the repaired version actually licenses. Does it still support blanket commercial openness, or does it shift the autarky debate from all-or-nothing self-sufficiency to something like a standing resilience floor in chokepoint sectors, maintained not to replace trade but to make coercive ruptures too expensive for others to attempt?
