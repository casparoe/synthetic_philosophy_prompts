You are advising a small private foundation that will fund one three-year postdoctoral fellowship in the metaphysics of individuality. The fellowship topic is fixed: unresolved problems about the identity of indiscernibles, especially as they bear on cases like Black’s two-sphere universe. The foundation’s funding covers one postdoc and normal academic travel; it does not fund laboratories, experiments, or archival work. The foundation wants a defended research agenda, not a survey of the literature and not a reading list.

Please organize your response into the following sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths.

1. The payoff space (about 150 words). Identify the downstream debates in which progress on the identity of indiscernibles would most change what people think, such as the metaphysics of elementary particles, spacetime points, persistence, or modal metaphysics. Say which one or two the foundation should care about most, and why.

2. Candidate problems (about 300 words). Present up to five unresolved problems that a single researcher could plausibly make progress on in three years. For each, state in one or two sentences what a successful treatment would have to establish. The problems should differ in kind: at least one conceptual, at least one driven by physics, and at least one about the principle’s modal status. You may draw from the following starting points, improve them, or replace them:
   (a) whether weakly discerning relations genuinely individuate quantum many-particle systems or merely presuppose distinctness;
   (b) how Black’s two-sphere universe bears on bundle theory, given that every feature of one sphere appears to be a feature of the other;
   (c) whether the principle is best understood as a claim about the world, about our evidence, or about a regulative norm governing theory choice;
   (d) whether the principle should be regarded as necessary, contingent, or something weaker, and whether conceivability or physics is the right arbiter of that modal status;
   (e) what grounds numerical diversity in perfectly symmetric worlds, such as haecceities, bare particularity, primitive identity facts, or nothing at all.

3. The ranking (about 250 words). Rank the candidate problems from best to worst as targets for the fellowship. Defend the ordering with explicit criteria: importance, tractability, neglectedness, and how many other problems depend on the answer. This ranking is the centerpiece, so give a reasoned ordering rather than a list.

4. Dependency structure (about 200 words). Explain which candidate problems are upstream of which and whether there is a single crux problem. If there is no robust crux, say which two or three problems are joint prerequisites for real progress and why.

5. Objections to the top-ranked problem (about 150 words). Give the strongest objection to funding your top choice — for example, that it is ill-posed, too insular, or unlikely to produce decisive results — and explain why it does not overturn the ranking.

6. Recommended project (about 150 words). Concretely specify the project to fund: its research question, its method, and an intermediate result that would show the project is on track by the end of year two.

Do not include citations or reading lists; justify each decision by argument.
