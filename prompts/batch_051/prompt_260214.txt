I've had a belief-forming policy for the last couple of years and I can't tell any more whether it's a genuine rationality win or just a bias with a maths-y story attached. The policy: whenever someone claims that some rule, intervention, or predictor works — a supplement, a hiring change, a trading strategy, whatever — I ask them what class of cases it's supposed to work in. If they can't tell me the prior or the set of environments under which it should be expected to succeed, I discount their evidence almost to zero. In ML terms I'm demanding to know the inductive bias before I'll grant that the evidence has any weight. My justification is basically the no-free-lunch theorem, which I've been treating as the problem of induction with the mathematics finally done: averaged across all possible worlds, every learner has the same out-of-sample performance, so "it worked for me" is only evidence once you've located the thing in a space where it isn't just fitting noise. I apply this to ordinary inductions too, not only ML stuff, and it drives my friends up the wall.

The obvious objection is that I don't do this to my own everyday inductions — I trust bread to nourish me without stating a prior over nutrient-environment pairings — so the policy looks selectively applied, probably at claims I already dislike. My question: is this policy defensible as stated, or can it be restated into something that keeps whatever the no-free-lunch theorem actually licenses without the double standard? And is there a prinicpled amount of prior-specification to demand before updating, rather than either zeroing out the anecdote or shrugging and accepting it?
