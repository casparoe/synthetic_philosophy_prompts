I'm a software engineer, and I've been trying to formalize a puzzle about preference cycles, because it reminded me of a replay attack in a multi-step protocol. I wrote the following argument down, and I'd like the kind of review I'd give to code: is it valid, and if not, exactly which step breaks?

An agent's strict preferences over three holdings A, B, C are cyclic: A is strictly preferred to B, B to C, and C to A. Let ε be a small transaction fee, small enough that none of the strict comparisons invoked below gets reversed. Define four states:

S0 = A  
S1 = C − ε  
S2 = B − 2ε  
S3 = A − 3ε  

Assume the agent has the following strict preferences over these states:

S1 is preferred to S0,  
S2 is preferred to S1,  
S3 is preferred to S2,  
S0 is preferred to S3,  
and S1 is preferred to S3.

A trader offers, in order: S0 → S1, S1 → S2, S2 → S3.

Consider the following argument that this agent, if forward-looking, cannot be money-pumped.

(1) A money pump succeeds only if the target voluntarily completes each trade in the chain.  
(2) By construction, each offered trade S_i → S_{i+1} is preferred to the current state at the moment it is offered; so a target who decides each offer by the immediate pair alone will complete the chain and end in S3.  
(3) The target regards S3 as worse than S0, so the immediate-pair target has been pumped.  
(4) But now suppose the target is forward-looking: she knows her own preferences and the trader's script, and at each node she chooses among the available courses of action by their terminal states rather than by the immediate pair alone.  
(5) At S0, the forward-looking target sees that if she accepts S0 → S1, she will accept S1 → S2 and then S2 → S3, since both are strict pairwise improvements; so accepting the first offer terminates in S3.  
(6) Since S0 is preferred to S3, she compares the accept strategy (terminal S3) with the decline strategy (terminal S0) and declines the first offer.  
(7) Therefore a forward-looking target cannot be led through the money pump.  
(8) Therefore the money-pump argument fails to show that cyclic preferences are irrational.

I can't tell whether this is sound or whether step (5) is quietly assuming the myopia it claims to eliminate. Is the argument valid? If not, which step or steps fail, and why? In particular, run the correct backwards-induction calculation for this exact setup, and then say what is left of the money-pump charge: does the corrected reasoning still get the target out of the pump, and what is the strongest move available to someone defending the money-pump objection after the correction?
