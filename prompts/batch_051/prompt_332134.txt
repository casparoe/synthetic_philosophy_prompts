I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been reasoning about a problem the way I reason about system design: as a dependency graph in which some things have to be worked out before others can be. The problem is this. Before anyone builds a machine capable of transformative output — a system that could eventually do most cognitive work better than the people who built it — it may be possible to make agreements, contingent contracts, and commitments now that are meant to bind or shape behavior after such a system exists. Examples would include contracts between developers that make payouts conditional on safety conditions being met, a joint agreement to keep certain verification systems running even when one side could profitably defect, or an individual's commitment to cooperate with a project only if it meets some outcome criterion. I will call these pre-AGI bargains.

My budget for thinking about this is a few hours a week on evenings and weekends, and the endpoint I am working toward is a practical decision about whether pre-AGI bargains deserve any of my attention in the first place, and if so which kind. I want to direct the budget at the question whose answer unblocks the most other questions, not the question that is merely the most interesting. The candidate questions I keep returning to are:

1. Credibility. Once a transformative system exists, the power difference between the parties and any would-be enforcer will be enormous. What could make a bargain written before that transition more than cheap talk?

2. Specification. If the counterparty, or its successors, are much better than the drafters at interpreting terms, can terms like "beneficial," "share of power," or "in everyone's interest" be written down so they cannot be trivially gamed?

3. The right negotiating unit. Should these bargains be made by individuals, by labs, or by states, and does the fact that some relevant actor will always have an incentive to defect settle whether the whole idea is workable before any of the other questions matter?

4. The actual objective. Should a pre-AGI bargain aim to secure a better share of the gains for one group, or to reduce the global incentive to race, and can one agreement plausibly do both?

5. The null case. If a transformative system can renegotiate, evade, or reinterpret anything written now anyway, is the whole category of pre-AGI bargains closer to planning fiction than to a real lever?

I want you to treat this list the way I would treat a module dependency graph. Rank these questions by which is most worth settling first, and give the criteria behind the ranking. For each one, say whether it is upstream of most other questions or downstream of them, whether different answers would actually change what I would do later, and how far it can be pushed by someone who can reason carefully but cannot do empirical work. Also tell me which questions to deprioritize because their answer would not change much downstream, and whether there is some question I have not listed that is more upstream than everything here. I am not asking for a survey of what others have written; I am asking for a worked judgment about the sequence of the thinking itself.
