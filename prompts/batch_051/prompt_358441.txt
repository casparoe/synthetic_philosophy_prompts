I have collected my own reactions to a set of choices and policies that all involve time, and I want to know whether any single principle of discounting captures them. Please take my verdicts as data, propose the principle you think best fits them, then tell me where my verdicts and the principle part ways.

Here are the cases.

1. I am offered $1,000 today or an inflation-adjusted $1,000 ten years from now. I take the money today, but I am confident this is not because I value the later dollars less: I can invest, and I might not be alive to receive them. I would not cite this case as evidence that the future matters less.

2. I am told I will have a moderately painful headache either this afternoon or one year from now, with the surrounding day otherwise identical. I genuinely do not care which. Timing alone does not seem to me to make one option worse.

3. If I know I will have a bad flu next month, I would pay $500 to prevent it. If the exact same illness is thirty years away, I would pay only $50. Looking at the two side by side, the difference strikes me as partly reasonable—the distant illness is less certain, and my future self will be a somewhat different person—but I do not believe those considerations account for all of it. There is a leftover pull toward the present that I can feel.

4. I think it is a moral mistake for a government to count benefits to people living 150 years from now as nearly worthless, the way a 3 percent annual discount does. The fact that the beneficiaries are far in the future does not, on reflection, make their welfare worth less.

5. But if I had to choose between spending $100 to relieve one person's severe pain today and spending $100 to relieve one person's severe pain three hundred years from now, I would choose the person today. I cannot honestly say this is only about uncertainty. This is the verdict that bothers me most, because it seems flatly at odds with the fourth.

6. In January I plan to start exercising in March; when March arrives I sincerely prefer to postpone to May. I regard the March preference as a bias, not as one more legitimate data point. So I do not think every time-based preference I happen to have is authoritative.

7. I care intensely that my children and grandchildren have good lives, but beyond about three generations my concern thins out. I think the thinness is a limitation in my sympathies rather than an insight about the value of later lives.

Is there one principle—for example, a zero pure time preference with all apparent discounting explained by uncertainty, opportunity cost, and the weakening of personal identity—that gets all seven, and if not, which verdict should go? What does your principle say about a case I have not listed: choosing between a certainty of preventing one death this year and a one-in-a-thousand chance of preventing a catastrophe that would otherwise kill one million people in the year 3000?

Please begin with one sentence stating your bottom line—the principle, or your verdict that no single principle fits—and then reason toward it, without preamble or restating the question. Then say which of my verdicts your account cannot accommodate and what it predicts for the unlisted case. Finally, tell me what would most change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different principle, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
