For a 75-minute session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar on metaphysical explanation, with 14 students, design a structured game or activity that makes participants feel the difference between a vicious and a benign infinite regress. They should construct the regresses themselves—chains of justification, explanation, definition, or ontological dependence—and should discover through the rules and incentives, before the distinction is named, that some chains can continue indefinitely without undermining the item at the head of the chain while others can never get started or never deliver what each step promised. Include concrete rules, materials such as cards or tokens, a point-based payoff scheme that does not use real money, a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened in the game onto candidate criteria for viciousness (for example, that each step merely defers the needed explanation, or that the whole chain would have to be completed before its first step is available). Also anticipate what is likely to go wrong the first time it is run and how to handle it, and be explicit about which feature of the vicious/benign distinction the activity cannot reproduce on its own and the facilitator must supply.
