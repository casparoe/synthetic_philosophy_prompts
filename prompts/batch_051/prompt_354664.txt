I recently joined the rules committee of my housing cooperative — about forty voting members, electing a board from several candidates — and I have been thrown into a literature I did not expect to find so frozen. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem is the center of gravity, and my sense is that the debate about what it means for real institutions has not moved in decades.

Everyone I read agrees on the theorem in its standard form: for deterministic rules that take complete strict rankings and can actually elect any of at least three alternatives, no non-dictatorial rule makes sincere ranking a dominant strategy; somewhere in the space of preference profiles, a voter gets a better result by misreporting. No one disputes that. What they dispute is how much it should matter for institutions like my co-op.

One side treats strategy-proofness as the absent ideal. Because any non-dictatorial rule is manipulable somewhere, design becomes damage control: choose among imperfect rules by asking where manipulation is least likely, hardest to pull off, or least consequential. The axiom is the standard; the theorem forces a compromise. The other side treats the theorem as a worst-case curiosity. Successful manipulation, they argue, requires an unusual combination of accurate beliefs about how others will vote, willingness to vote against one's sincere ranking, and often coordination; many realistic profiles are not manipulable for many rules, and voters have expressive and social reasons to stay sincere. On this view the theorem tells us almost nothing about which rule actual institutions should pick, and design should be driven by ordinary-case properties — simplicity, representativeness, robustness to obvious pathologies.

The two camps share the formal results and, as far as I can tell, the same interpretation of every concrete example, yet they never move. I am not asking for a survey of voting rules or a verdict on which rule to adopt. I am asking for a diagnosis of the disagreement itself.

My working hypothesis is that it is a hidden empirical disagreement about which profiles are the relevant ones when judging a rule: the worst-case profiles the theorem guarantees versus the typical profiles in a small, stable electorate like a co-op. If so, the frequency and feasibility of manipulation in our particular electorate should be the decisive question, and simulation or data should settle it — or at least shrink it. But I can also see it being a difference about what a voting rule is for: a fail-safe mechanism that must not reward manipulation even in rare cases, versus a practical tool that should work well in ordinary use. And I can see a verbal ambiguity in “manipulation,” meaning mathematically possible on one side and realistically available on the other.

Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, engaging with what each side characteristically says. Tell me whether my empirical-question hypothesis captures it, partly captures it, or misses the point, and what would actually count as progress given your diagnosis — a simulation, a formal distinction, a change of question, or an honest acknowledgment that the two camps would not be moved by any such thing. Also say how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the point you are least sure of.

Think of yourself as an expert in social choice theory and the methodology of formal models in political science. Before settling on a diagnosis, brainstorm two or three candidate diagnoses and compare them against the specific arguments each camp gives; then argue for the one you find best and explain why the others don't fit as well.
