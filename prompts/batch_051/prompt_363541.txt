I teach piano and help coach a small archery club, and I have been noticing a pattern that I do not have a precise word for. At the range, a beginner improves when I talk him through the sequence—stance, draw, anchor, release—and he can later manage the same steps by thinking them through deliberately. But an experienced shooter often shoots measurably worse if I ask her, just before she draws, to walk herself through that same sequence; the instruction that educates the novice seems to disrupt the expert. I saw the same thing in a piano lesson recently. A student had a piece memorized and was playing it smoothly until I asked her what her left hand was doing in a rapid passage, and as soon as she tried to answer she could no longer play it at all. Yet when she went home and practiced the passage slowly, hands separate, before putting it back together, her performance of the whole piece improved.

The puzzling thing is that the same act—turning attention onto a component of a skill—seems to help in some circumstances and harm in others, and the difference is not simply how skilled the performer is. Reflecting between attempts helps the very performer who is harmed by reflecting during the attempt, and in reasoning tasks, such as working through a logic puzzle, the same kind of deliberate self-questioning in the moment seems to help rather than hurt. It also does not look like ordinary performance anxiety: the decline comes when the person is calm and focused on the steps, and telling someone simply to relax does not fix it. Does this specific failure mode have a name, and is there an accepted account of where the boundary between helpful and harmful self-directed attention falls? I would rather have the phenomenon carved precisely than have a one-word label: what is going wrong during execution, how does it differ from mere distraction or nerves, and why is the same kind of attention a tool in one setting and a liability in another?
