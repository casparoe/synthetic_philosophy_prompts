I'm putting together a lecture on legal epistemology and came across a passage in one of those breezy 'philosophy of law in a weekend' books — I won't name it, it was a library copy I've since returned — that summarises the blue bus and gatecrasher cases like this:

'Take the blue bus case. A pedestrian is hit by a bus on a route that is 80% served by the Blue Bus Company, and there is no other evidence. The balance of probabilities is plainly satisfied, since it is four times more likely that a Blue bus caused the injury. Yet the law would not let her recover against the company on that statistic alone, and most philosophers of law approve: proof must be individualized, not merely statistical. The gatecrasher case is the criminal version of the point. If 501 of 1,000 spectators at a rodeo crashed the gate, a randomly chosen spectator is still more likely than not to be a gatecrasher, but no court would convict him beyond reasonable doubt. The moral drawn is that statistical evidence, however strong, is never sufficient for legal proof; the law demands something closer to certainty — evidence that directly ties this defendant to this event — before it will attach liability or guilt.'

The author presents all this as settled, but the whole thing feels too neat to me, and I'd rather not pass on a distorion to my students. My plan is to have them think through what, if anything, the cases actually show. Could you seperate which parts of that summary are fair, which are common simplifications or mistakes, and what a careful defender of the view that naked statistical evidence cannot by itself support a verdict would actually be committed to? A couple of things already smell wrong to me — the slide from the balance of probabilities to 'beyond reasonable doubt', and the idea that the law never uses statistics, which can't be right if DNA evidence does real work in court — but I don't want to bias you.
