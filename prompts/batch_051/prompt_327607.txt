Consider a one-dollar auction run as follows. Two bidders may make ascending bids of at least five cents; when the bidding stops, the two highest bidders each pay their final bid, the highest receives the one-dollar note, and the second-highest receives nothing. Alice has bid ninety-five cents and Bob now leads at one dollar. Alice reasons:

(1) My ninety-five cents is already lost whatever I do next. If I quit now, I end up at −95¢. If I raise to $1.05 and Bob then quits, I pay $1.05 and receive one dollar, ending at −5¢ — a ninety-cent improvement over quitting.
(2) So raising is better than quitting unless Bob bids again. But if Bob does bid again, I will once more be the second-highest bidder with a sunk bid, and the same reasoning will recommend raising again.
(3) By applying this reasoning at every stage, there is never a point at which the second-highest bidder should quit rather than raise one more increment.
(4) Parity of reasoning gives the same conclusion to Bob whenever he is second-highest. Therefore, once the auction has begun, neither bidder can rationally stop.
(5) Hence the auction must escalate without limit, and the only rational policy is never to enter it.

This conclusion strikes me as far too strong, but I cannot pin down why. Is the argument valid? If not, identify the exact step that fails, state the strongest reading on which that step is true, and give the corrected version of the step — then say whether the corrected argument reaches the same conclusion.
