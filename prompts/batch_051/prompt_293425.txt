I keep going back and forth about what deepfakes should do to my evidential practices, and I don't think I've seen the two sides put together in a way that doesn't stack the deck. On one side, I'm attracted to a clean view: video and audio have been doing a job they can no longer do, namely acting as a kind of epistemic backstop. Testimony floats around and gets distorted, and the recording was supposed to be the thing that anchors it. But now that a recording can be manufactured cheaply, there's no epistemic category for 'the thing that can't lie.' The backstop is just more testimony with extra steps. The clean view says: stop giving visual or recorded evidence any special privilege; score every piece of evidence the way you'd score the same claim in a text message—by base rates, priors, and how surprising it is on each hypothesis. It's a single rule, it doesn't need a new patch each time the generators improve, and it never has to be rebuilt.

But I also have an intuition that won't move. A dashcam video of a crash, or a phone video of a cop beating someone who's handcuffed, is stronger evidence than the same account typed out, and it should be. There's somethign about the causal path from the event to the photons to the sensor to the file—even if that path is spoofable in principle—that carries information a paraphrase doesn't. If I did what the clean view tells me, I'd feel like I was throwing away real knowledge in the overwhelmingly ordinary cases where nothing was faked. 'It can be faked' is not 'this one probably was.'

I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for each side to give. Give me the strongest version of both sides first. For the clean view: would its defenders say my intuition is just a lagging prior from a pre-deepfake world, and if so, what would have to be true for it to be the intuition, not the theory, that goes? For the intuition: would its defenders say the clean view conflates forgery-in-principle with evidence-of-forgery, and can that survive in a world where fakes are genuinely cheap and perfect? Is there a reading on which both survive—say, video stays high-grade but defeasible, and the 'backstop' becomes a social and institutional practice (chain of custody, provenance, signing) rather than an intrinsic property of images? Tell me which pieces of this are settled among people who actually work on it and which pieces are genuinely contested, and don't dress up a contested take as the consensus. Mark where you're confident and where you're just guessing. I'd rather have a sharp, uncertain answer than a smooth one.
