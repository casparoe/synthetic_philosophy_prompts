I host a recorded conversation series on the philosophy of history, and next week's guest is a scholar who has recently argued that the taboo on comparing historical atrocities is a form of evasion. Her positive claim is that the major historical mass atrocities can be placed in a stable ordinal ranking of severity, and that only such a ranking lets people who work on prevention allocate attention among cases honestly rather than pretending that all cases are equally urgent. Prepare eight to ten interview questions that force her to do more than restate the ranking-and-prevention argument. Distribute the questions across these pressure points, with at least one question for each: the choice of metric — deaths, suffering, avoidability, downstream consequences, or some composite — and whether changing the metric reorders the cases; the counterfactual baseline problem, meaning the question of against what alternative an atrocity's severity is to be measured; whether the internal heterogeneity of a single event makes it better represented as a vector of harms than as a point on a one-dimensional scale; the political and memorial risks of publishing an ordinal list, including its likely use by denialists and by groups seeking competitive victimhood; the uniqueness objection from Holocaust historiography, and whether her view can grant uniqueness in one sense while denying it in another; and the status of the middle of the ranking, where adjacent cases may be effectively incomparable. For each question, state the reply her position most naturally supplies and then give the follow-up that presses on the weakest part of that reply. End with one question asking her to specify, in advance, an empirical or moral discovery that would make her lower her confidence in the possibility of a defensible ranking.
