You are an expert in climate policy and the ethics of technological intervention in the climate system. Please reason carefully; the goal is not a general list of pros and cons but a precise, move-by-move assessment of a specific disagreement.

At a workshop, two colleagues argued about whether a serious public research program into solar geoengineering—specifically stratospheric aerosol injection, the idea of dispersing reflective particles such as sulfates in the upper atmosphere to hold down global average temperature—should be funded. For the sake of argument they stipulated that large-scale injection would likely cool the planet substantially, with uneven regional effects and side effects. Their disagreement was about the indirect effects of research.

Marta opened:

(1) Research normalizes the technology. The more seriously solar geoengineering is studied and publicly funded, the more deployment becomes thinkable and the more pressure comes off emissions cuts. That moral hazard is the strongest argument in this debate, and it is decisive.

Jon replied:

(2) Moral hazard is an empirical claim, and the evidence for it is weak. There is little sign that nations ease up on mitigation because a speculative technofix sits on a distant shelf. And if mitigation keeps failing, being deliberately ignorant about an emergency option is not a virtue.

Marta:

(3) The termination-shock problem shows this is a bad backup. If injection is maintained and then interrupted by war, economic collapse, or political breakdown, the warming it had been masking comes back all at once, faster than ecosystems and agriculture can adapt. A technology whose interruption is catastrophic should not be researched as if it were a harmless insurance policy.

Jon:

(4) Termination shock is an argument about how deployment would have to be governed, not about research. And it cuts the other way: if some actor might deploy anyway, unilaterally and badly, the world is better off understanding the system beforehand. Research is exactly how you learn about the risks and design safeguards.

Marta:

(5) But the governance does not exist, and there is no plausible route to it, because a single wealthy state could plausibly manage deployment on its own. So the choice is between research with global governance, which is fantasy, and research without governance, which is de facto normalization. The real question is not whether injection would work but who decides. Today the answer is nobody, so the box should stay closed.

Jon:

(6) Refusing to research does not keep the box closed; it just makes it more likely that whoever opens it does so without shared knowledge. You cannot run a responsible governance strategy on a policy of deliberate ignorance.

They asked me to referee, and I genuinely could not. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. Before the ratings, briefly state the two or three criteria you are using to evaluate whether a research program of this kind should be funded. Then rate each numbered move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify each rating in a sentence or two. Finally, say which side has the better overall position and identify the most important consideration that neither side handled well.
