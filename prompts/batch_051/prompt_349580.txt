I am a sixth-form student writing an essay on personal identity for my philosophy course. In class we discussed the view that a self is not a thing inside the skull but an abstract object, a kind of centre of narrative gravity. Just as a centre of gravity in physics is not a material part of an object but a useful abstraction for predicting how the object will move, a self is something we posit to make sense of a person's words and actions, especially the stories they tell about themselves and that others tell about them. I want a working definition of "a self" in the counting sense — the sense in which we ask whether someone has one self, or whether anything besides a human being could have one.

Please propose a definition of "a self" (you may refine the narrative-gravity view rather than start from scratch) that classifies the following cases:

(a) an ordinary adult human, awake and telling a friend about their day;
(b) a granite rock;
(c) a person in dreamless sleep;
(d) a well-drawn character in a novel whose thoughts and history the narrator recounts;
(e) a person with advanced dementia whose family continues to tell the story of the person they once were;
(f) a split-brain patient whose two hemispheres give conflicting verbal and manual reports;
(g) a large language model sustaining a coherent persona over a long conversation;
(h) a business corporation.

I am fairly confident that (a) is a self and (b) is not, and I am inclined to say that (c) is still a self, though I cannot yet defend that properly. I genuinely do not know what to say about (d) through (h).

Please run the definition through all eight cases, say where it yields no clear verdict or a verdict you would have to defend against common sense, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. Finally, tell me which of your verdicts is least secure, and say whether your definition separates being a self from merely being the object of a narrative that others sustain.

Please write your answer in British English.
