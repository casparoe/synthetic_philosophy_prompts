A cofounder of mine has this bit he does in every strategy meeting: the company is an organism. He's not using it as loose metaphor, either — employees are cells, teams are tissues, departments are organs, and the hiring practices and culture are the genome. Then he pivots to the conclusion he actually wants: a body doesn't have the CEO send memos to each cell, so we should stop setting top-down targets and let the parts do what their specialized for; our only job is to keep the thing alive. I took one philosophy of biology course and remember there's a whole debate about whether groups can be units of selection — genes vs. individuals vs. groups, replicators vs. vehicles, and what it takes for a bunch of organisms to count as one organism rather than just a population. How far does his analogy actually hold? I want the correspondence table — what's the gene, the cell, the organism, the environment, the selection pressures — then the places it breaks, and which features of real organisms he's quietly importing that a company might not have. Then tell me whether his anti-top-down conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the work the whole time.

Format: exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no intro or conclusion — roughly (1) the best version of the mapping, (2) what the analogy quietly imports, (3) the disanalogy that matters most for his conclusion, (4) whether the conclusion survives or the analogy was carrying it, (5) the point you're least sure about and what would flip your verdict.
