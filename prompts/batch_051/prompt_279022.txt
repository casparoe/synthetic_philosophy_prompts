Someone at a complexity conference is trying to convince me that assembly theory's assembly index is finally an objective measure of how complex an object is. The idea, roughly, is: choose some elementary building blocks, then score an object by the shortest number of recursive assembly steps it takes to build it, where at each step you can reuse any subassembly you've already made. So a crystal or a pile of sand scores low because its the same move repeated, and a protein or a hand axe scores high because you'd need many distinct nested substructures. They claim this isn't just another information-theory statistic; it actually captures something like made-by-a-process versus could-have-happened-by-accident, and they'd like to use it to spot alien life in chemistry data.

I'm unconvinced that a single scalar can be a measure of complexity in any ordinary sense of the word, as opposed to a measure of one particular kind of constructional overhead. But arguing about definitions won't settle it. Design the strongest test you can of the claim that the assembly index measures complexity, rather than some nearby quantity like compressibility or internal repetition. Be concrete: what objects or datasets would you compare, what would you measure alongside the index, and what pattern of results would count for the claim and what would count against. I keep thinking of awkward cases: a snowflake is elaborately structured but probably gets a low score, while a short but very specific molecule gets a high one. Then be honest about what a positive result would still leave unproven, and whether any empirical test could close that gap or whether its a conceptual gap no amount of data can fill. Also flag where the operationalisation is doing hidden work.

Answer in British English, please.
