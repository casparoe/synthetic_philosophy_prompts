So I'm in an argument with a friend who works on corporate governance, and his whole position rests on the idea that a group can be a person in a substantive sense: the hiring committee concluded the candidate was qualified, the central bank believes inflation is transitory, the court found the defendant liable. His move is that once a collectivity has beliefs, intentions, and maybe even a kind of character, you can hold it responsible as a unit instead of spreading blame over the people inside it. I think this group-as-a-person analogy starts to fall apart exactly where it's doing the most work, and I want to use the discursive dilemma (or the older doctrinal paradox) to pin down why.

My understanding of the paradox: take three judges deciding whether a defendant is liable. Liability requires two premises, breach of duty and causation. Judge 1 says breach yes, causation no, so not liable. Judge 2 says breach no, causation yes, so not liable. Judge 3 says yes and yes, so liable. Majority on breach: yes. Majority on causation: yes. Majority on the final conclusion: no, not liable. So the "group judgment" produced by ordinary majority voting is a set of commitments that the majority of individual judges would reject. If that's right, a group's beliefs aren't just a summary of what the members believe—they're partly an artifact of the aggregation rule, and they can be inconsistent in ways an individual mind usually isn't.

Help me audit this group-as-person analogy. Give me the explicit correspondence table—what is the group's belief, memory, inference, intention, conscience supposed to map onto?—then walk through where the mapping holds, where it bends, and where it breaks, using the discursive dilemma as the stress test. Seperate the disanalogies that matter for the corporate-responsibility use from the ones that are just cosmetic. And tell me whether the conclusion survives once we admit that a committee's judgments are partly a byproduct of the voting rule, or whether the analogy was carrying the whole argument. If there's a better analogy—a group as something other than a person—what would it be?
