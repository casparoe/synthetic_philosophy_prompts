People in my discussion group keep leaning on the corporate-person analogy to settle the AI legal personhood question, and I cant tell how much weight it should actually bear. The move they make is: we already treat corporations as legal persons, and nobody thinks a corporation has a mind, so legal personhood was never about consciousness — therefore the objection that an AI isnt conscious is simply beside the point. It feels right for about five seconds, and then I start wondering what the analogy is quietly importing.

I want an audit. How far does the analogy hold? First give me the correspondence table: what maps to what? Shareholders to whoever owns or deploys the model, the board and executives to whoever sets its objective function or operates it, employees or agents to the model actually taking the actions, corporate assets to the model's... what, exactly, some escrow account? Then separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the specific conclusion being argued from the decorative ones.

The one I keep coming back to is that a corporation always bottoms out in humans somewhere — humans put up the capital, humans make the decisions in the end, humans lose their money or liberty when it goes wrong, and the law can usually find one if it has to. An LLM agent that can enter a contract or make a representation without any human authoring that particular action seems to lack that footing, or at least the chain looks like it terminates in a model checkpoint rather than a person. Does the analogy break exactly where it's doing the work, or can it be shored up? And after all that, does the conclusion survive — is the consciousness objection really dead, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If it is broken for that purpose, is there a better analogy, or should the whole analogy be dropped?
