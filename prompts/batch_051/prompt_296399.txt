I'm a PhD student working on responsibility and autonomous systems, and I've been sitting in on our university hospital's safety review board meetings for about a year. The cases are anonymised, but I've kept notes on how the board assigns responsibility after an incident involving an AI or a robot. Their past decisions go something like this:

1. A closed-loop insulin pump gave a patient a dangerous overdose after a glucose-sensor fault that nobody could have foreseen. The board blamed the nurse who had programmed and started the pump, saying there has to be a responsible human in the loop somewhere.
2. A warehouse robot hit a picker who had stepped into a marked exclusion zone. The robot's obstacle detection had been switched off by a manufacturer software update without the warehouse being told. The board blamed the picker for ignoring the signs.
3. A driver on the motorway in a semi-autonomous car hit a pedestrian in heavy rain. The car was marketed as having full self-driving, but the manual says to stay alert with your hands on the wheel at all times; the driver had looked down for about four seconds. The board blamed the driver.
4. A radiology AI missed a tumour. The board blamed the radiologist for not catching the machine's error, even though the hospital's own protocol says not to second-guess the AI when it's confident, and the board accepted that on average, radiologists who override confident AI calls make things worse.
5. A military drone in a training exercise misidentified a marked friendly vehicle and fired on it. The board found no individual responsible and called it a systemic failure. This is the only decision on my list that didn't land blame on one person.

Now a new case has come up. A surgical robot injured a patient during a routine procedure. A pre-op software check had flagged a possible fault in a fresh update, and the IT team had been sent the flag in an automated queue but nobody opened the ticket; the surgeon, meanwhile, assumed IT would deal with it. The surgeon was supervising throughout and could in principle have hit a manual override, but she'd had no training on the new update's failure modes.

I honestly can't tell whether the board is running a "blame the nearest human" principle, a fault-based principle, or something about who was best placed to prevent the harm, because the first four decisions look one way and the drone decision looks the other. So: if I want to predict how they'll rule on the surgical robot case — and I'm less interested in the label they give the decision then in the underlying rule — what should I expect? Reconstruct the implicit theory of responsibility the board seems to be running: control, fault, foreseeability, role obligation, or something else. Say which of the five earlier cases is the closest analog to the new one and why, and what decision each candidate rule would require. If their record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say what principle they'd be adopting under each choice, and give me a best guess with reasons. I need this written in British English for a journal I'm putting together, please.
