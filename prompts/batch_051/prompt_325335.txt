I'm a philosopher of mathematics, and I keep returning to a tidy view about what it is to understand a proof: understanding a proof is just being able to verify each inference and reconstruct the proof yourself, give or take memory. The view has a lot going for it—it makes understanding testable, gradable, and non-mysterious, with no ghostly 'seeing' above the mechanics. But I've got a stubborn intuition that I can sit with a proof, check every line as correct, and still not understand it; and conversely, a colleague can sketch a proof on a napkin and I'll get it immediately even though I couldn't fill in half the details. I don't want someone to just tell me which side wins. I want you to walk me through what it costs to say that intuition is only a confusion between understanding and the feeling of understanding, what it costs to amend the theory instead (say, by adding something about grasping the idea or the overal structure), and whether there's a reading on which both can survive—perhaps understanding has both a local and a global component. Which of these moves is most justified, and why? Please answer in British English.
