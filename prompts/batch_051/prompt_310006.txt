I have no formal background in philosophy, but I have been thinking on my own about whether doubt is something we owe to other people, and I have reached a conflict I cannot resolve.

On one side, I am pulled toward a principle that seems, when I state it plainly, hard to deny: because my beliefs guide what I do, and what I do affects others, I owe it to others to hold a belief only as strongly as the evidence supports it, and to go on doubting wherever the evidence falls short. If I believe a home remedy is safe on thin evidence and give it to my child, or believe a neighbor is dangerous on the strength of a rumor and warn people against him, I have done wrong even if I happen to be right. The appeal of this principle is that it is simple, impartial, and gives me a clear test; it also explains why gullibility is not merely foolish but a moral failing, since the gullible person risks other people's lives on a guess.

On the other side, I cannot shake a conviction that cuts the other way. If my brother were accused of stealing from his employer and the circumstantial evidence against him were genuinely strong, I would still feel that I ought to believe him when he tells me he did not do it — not only stand by him, and not only act as though he were innocent, but believe him. A person who said "the evidence makes it seventy percent likely he is innocent, so I will support him while reserving judgment" does not seem to me to be doing what a brother should do. I admire the person who says "I know she did not do it" when the case against her is strong, and I would not want to live in a world where every spouse, parent, and friend treated a loved one as a hypothesis to be updated. I am aware that this looks like exactly the partiality the first principle forbids.

The usual answer I hear is that loyalty requires action and feeling but not belief. That strikes me as an evasion, because the belief itself is what feels required, and the person who privately suspends judgment while publicly showing support seems to be holding something back. I have also tried to dissolve the conflict by distinguishing "believing my brother" from "believing the proposition that he is innocent," and confining the evidential principle to the second. But I cannot tell whether this is a real difference or a trick I am playing on myself, because if I believe my brother, I act on the proposition, and if I doubt the proposition, I am not really believing him.

So I want to know which conviction should yield. Should the loyalty-intuition be debunked and dropped, as a sentimental mistake that the evidential principle correctly corrects? Should the evidential principle be amended or abandoned, and if so, what remains of the idea that careless belief wrongs other people? Or is there a reading of "belief," "doubt," or "owing" on which both can be kept, and is that reading honest rather than special pleading? I am not looking for a survey of positions. Present each side at its strongest, say what it would take for either one to give way, and then give me your own verdict.
