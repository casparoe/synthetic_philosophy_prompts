A popular article on language I read recently offers this summary of the poverty of the stimulus argument:

"Children master their first language in a few years with little explicit teaching, drawing on a finite, disorganized sample of the speech they happen to overhear. That sample, by itself, cannot tell them which sentences their grammar excludes: adults hardly ever correct children's syntax, and children do not go around producing the errors it would take for correction to teach them anything. Even so, every normal child converges on essentially the same adult grammar. The only explanation, the article concludes, is that the child is born with a substantial stock of innate linguistic knowledge—a universal grammar—that narrows down the possible languages it can learn. The poverty of the stimulus argument has therefore been the standard refutation of the idea that language is acquired from experience by general-purpose intelligence."

This account strikes me as more confident than its own reasoning supports, but I lack the linguistic training to identify where the inflation happens. Please give me an accuracy audit rather than a debate of the underlying issue. Which elements of this characterization would a careful defender of the argument accept as faithful? Which are conventional oversimplifications? Are there claims a defender would reject outright, and if so, why? Finally, tell me whether the last sentence survives once the preceding corrections are made.
