Below is a passage from the second chapter of the first edition of Darwin's _On the Origin of Species_ (1859):

"Practically, when a naturalist can unite two forms together by others having intermediate characters, he treats the one as a variety of the other, ranking the most common, but sometimes the one first described, as the species, and the other as the variety."

You are a philosopher of biology who works on the species problem. Before answering, set out briefly the different ways this rule might be read, then reason through the case.

The case: in 2026 a taxonomic working group reviews two forms of European whitefish, long ranked as separate species on the strength of a few skeletal and gill-raker differences and largely separate spawning habitats. Whole-genome resequencing finds no separate genetic clusters: the two forms are connected by a continuous gradient of intermediate genotypes wherever their ranges meet, the intermediates are fertile, and gene flow between the forms has been recurrent since the last glaciation. No naturalist has ever assembled a chain of morphologically intermediate museum specimens uniting the two forms.

Does Darwin's rule, applied as literally as possible, reach this case? Does it apply if "intermediate characters" are read as covering genomic data, or did the rule depend on visible, specimen-based characters in a way that cannot be extended? Is there a version of the rule, closer to its underlying point about gradation and the arbitrariness of the species–variety distinction, that would catch this case without collapsing every pair of hybridising forms into one species? Finally, state which consideration would be most likely to change your verdict, and where your reasoning is least certain. Please answer in British English.
