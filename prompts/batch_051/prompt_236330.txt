A few weeks ago I described to a friend something that had unsettled me: in a single week in a city I had never visited before, I ran into three people from three disconnected chapters of my life — a former colleague, a childhood neighbor, and a one-time thesis examiner. My friend cut me off with the lottery analogy. Somebody wins the lottery; it is overwhelmingly likely that somebody will; but the winner would be wrong to conclude that the draw was rigged or that fate selected them. In a world with enough people and enough chances for meetings, astonishing coincidences are likewise guaranteed to happen, so any particular coincidence carries no evidential weight and should not move your beliefs. He applies the same line to near-misses, repeated numbers, and dreams that appear to come true.

I would like a genuine audit of this analogy, not a gesture. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the lottery, the ticket, the draw, the huge field of losing tickets, and the winner's after-the-fact astonishment. Then say where the mapping breaks, and which features of the lottery the analogy quietly imports that a reported coincidence may lack. I suspect the analogy is doing two separate jobs at once, and I want them pulled apart. The first is the claim that some coincidence or other was bound to occur — the birthday paradox makes this vivid, since in a room of twenty-three people a shared birthday is already more likely than not. The second, much stronger claim is that this particular coincidence was bound to occur, or at least that learning of it should not raise anyone's estimate of a non-chance explanation such as a common cause, a hidden selection process, selective retelling, or deliberate arrangement. Does the lottery analogy support the second claim, or does it quietly assume that the event was noticed only after the fact and never specified in advance? Under what conditions would a reported coincidence legitimately raise the posterior probability of a non-chance explanation? Close by telling me whether the lottery dismissal is usually warranted, sometimes warranted, or rarely warranted, and what a better analogy would be, if there is one.
