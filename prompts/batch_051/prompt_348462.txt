I'm a legal officer at a small country's law reform commission, and I do a quick rule-of-law screen on draft bills before first reading. I see three or four drafts a month and have about half a day each, and my note goes to the Attorney General, who uses it to answer MPs who ask whether a bill is compatible with the rule of law. Nobody here is a philosopher, and I want something more defensible than my instincts. Please think carefully as a philosopher of law and begin by briefly brainstorming which strands of the actual debate can realistically be turned into an afternoon checklist for a non-specialist rather than a seminar point — Fuller's internal criteria (generality, promulgation, clarity, no contradictory rules, no impossible demands, relative stability, congruence between declared rule and official practice), Raz's more formal list (prospective, open and clear, stable, independent courts, natural justice, accessible), and Waldron's argument that the rule of law is centrally about procedure and participation in argument. Then give me the checklist as an ordered set of questions: what counts as a pass, where it stops with a no so I can tell the AG this is not marginal, and where it should hand the decision back to politicians as a values question rather than dressing a policy disagreement up as a rule-of-law defect. Run it on three drafts: a retrospective tax provision that is otherwise clean; an emergency bill that was debated but is retrospective; and a law that cuts benefits for a named minority, drafted with perfect clarity, stability, and procedure. Make sure at least one worked example gives an answer I won't like, and at least one shows the procedure's limit. Be honest about which steps are judgement calls wearing a checklist's clothes, and finish by telling me which consideration would most likely lead you to redesign the procedure and where you think your own reasoning is softest.
