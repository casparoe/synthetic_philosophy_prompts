I'm pulling together a seminar on the evolution of cooperation, and before we evaluate the usual candidate mechanisms one at a time, I want to settle what any adequate theory in this area has to achieve. My working frame is the prisoner's dilemma and its n-player generalization, but the desiderata should be stated generally enough to apply to any account of how cooperation can arise and persist without centralized enforcement. The problem I keep running into is that each camp seems to carry its own local list of demands: kin-selection theorists want the theory to respect relatedness and inclusive-fitness accounting; direct-reciprocity theorists want it to explain the shadow of the future and the stability of strategies like tit-for-tat and its forgiving variants; indirect-reciprocity theorists want it to explain reputation, image scoring, and standing; network and spatial-structure people want it to explain assortment so that cooperators cluster; and group- or multilevel-selection people want it to explain between-group variation. I need to know which of these are genuine constraints on any serious theory and which are just commitments of one research program.

Please write the full requirements list. It should cover (i) the cases a theory must get right — a one-shot anonymous prisoner's dilemma in which defection strictly dominates and yet cooperation is observed once the interaction structure is changed; an iterated dilemma with an uncertain horizon where the future casts weight back on the present; an n-player public-goods or commons problem where the temptation to free-ride is distributed across many beneficiaries; noisy interactions where errors in implementation or perception occur and a single mistake can unravel a relationship; finite populations where drift and stochastic payoffs matter; and second-order dilemmas such as why anyone pays the cost of punishing or monitoring; (ii) the distinctions a theory cannot collapse — between a mechanism that selects for cooperation and one that merely maintains a cooperative convention once it already exists, between direct and indirect fitness benefits, between the different sources of assortment (shared relatedness, shared tags or greenbeards, spatial proximity, or prior behavior), and between the one-shot logic in which defection dominates and the repeated-game logic in which reciprocity becomes possible; and (iii) the formal and empirical results a theory must be consistent with — dominance of defection in the one-shot game, the folk-theorem observation that repeated play permits too many equilibria so that the mere existence of a cooperative equilibrium is not yet an explanation, and the cost–benefit threshold conditions such as Hamilton's rb > c condition and the analogous thresholds for direct reciprocity, indirect reciprocity, and structured populations. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp.

Then do the part I most want to see: flag any subsets of these desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. In particular, I suspect there is a real tension between the demand that a theory explain cooperation in a one-shot anonymous interaction with strictly dominant defection and the demand that it do so with random mixing, no reputation or punishment, no correlated interaction, and selection operating only at the individual level — and a related tension between the folk-theorem result that many equilibria exist in repeated games and the demand that a theory single out cooperative equilibria as the ones that will actually obtain. If some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say which requirement has to give and what the usual candidate positions do about it. Finally, briefly score the main contenders — kin selection, direct reciprocity, indirect reciprocity, network reciprocity or assortment, and group or multilevel selection — against the list, and tell me which single requirement does the most work excluding the popular accounts. You can present the brief scoring however you like, including as a table, but the criteria should carry the weight.
