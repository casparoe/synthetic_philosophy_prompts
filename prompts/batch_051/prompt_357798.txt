I'm a PhD student in political theory, working on nineteenth-century critiques of mass society, and this term our seminar read Kierkegaard's Two Ages alongside some of the later pieces from his final attack on Christendom. Our instructor gave a compact characterization of the position that the rest of us have been treating as the settled reading, and I want to know how much of it I should actually carry into my own work. Her summary ran, more or less, as follows. The modern daily press is the root cause of leveling, the process by which an age erases qualitative distinctions and assimilates everyone into a mediocre sameness. The press does this by manufacturing the public — an aggregate of private readers whose opinions the press then presents as the voice of the people — and because this public has no definite membership, no one can be held responsible for what is said in its name. Once leveling is underway, it becomes almost impossible for an individual to stand out, take a decisive stand, or be answerable for anything. According to our instructor, Kierkegaard's conclusion is therefore that the remedy for this pathology is better public communication: a more responsible and accountable press that would give the public a real rather than a phantom voice.

I admit this is an elegant summary, and the first two-thirds sound right, but the final step makes me uneasy. From what I remember of the way Two Ages ends, Kierkegaard seems to move toward the single individual, inwardness, and a religious category like the eternal, not toward institutional reforms of journalism. I also suspect that calling the press the root cause of leveling may be too strong, as if leveling were a condition of the age that the press expresses and accelerates rather than originates. But our instructor knows this material far better than I do, so I do not trust my misgivings.

Assume the role of a philosopher who specializes in Kierkegaard and in nineteenth-century social criticism. Before you write, take some time to think through each element of the summary, and then give me a committed accuracy audit rather than a menu of possible readings: go clause by clause, say what is accurate, what is subtly distorted, what a sympathetic Kierkegaard scholar would object to, and whether the conclusion about a responsible press follows from anything true in the summary. I am not looking for citations or a reading list; I want the conceptual diagnosis, because I need to decide whether to reproduce this summary in a field exam.
