I run a monthly 90-minute workshop for senior engineers at my company — usually 10 to 14 people — on decision-making traps that show up in real project work; we have done base-rate neglect and the planning fallacy so far. Next month I want to tackle escalation of commitment: the thing where a rewrite, a migration, or a tooling bet keeps getting staffed long after its expected value goes negative, because people cannot walk away from what has already been invested, or because stopping means saying in front of the team that the original bet was bad. What I want participants to come away with is not a vocabulary lesson but a felt sense that escalation has at least two separate engines, plus a quick heuristic for noticing, in a live decision, which engine is doing the work.

The obvious move would be to run the standard dollar auction, but we tried that once and it landed badly: people saw the trap coming, a few refused to bid, and the session collapsed into a discussion of how predictable the game was. So I want an activity that does not announce its topic in advance, where participants walk into the escalation themselves, and that cleanly separates (1) the sunk-cost / self-justification engine — you keep going because you cannot admit the earlier investment was wasted — from (2) competitive arousal — the bidding or the race itself becomes the reason to continue because you are one increment behind someone else. I want the game to make participants run into each engine at a different moment, and I want the debrief to map specific moments in the activity onto the two engines and onto the point where they interact.

Concretely: give me rules, materials, and a payoff scheme that does not require real money but still creates real second-order stakes — status, a prize, elimination from a follow-up round, having to present the losing position. Include a 90-minute timeline with the debrief built in, a list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle each failure in the moment, and an honest note on which feature of real-world escalation the activity cannot reproduce — I suspect the months-long delay between a decision and its consequences, or the role of a single accountable sponsor — and that I will therefore have to supply myself in the closing discussion.
