I'm a machine-learning engineer, not a philosopher, and my working vocabulary is the one I actually reason in: token vectors, contextualized embeddings, cosine similarity and nearest-neighbor structure, vector arithmetic, training distributions, representation geometry, polysemy, and the type-level/occurrence-level distinction. I want you to take a long-standing debate in the philosophy of language and restate it in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, without quietly substituting my framework's assumptions for the philosophers' claims.

Here is the debate. Semantic holism, as I understand it, says that a word's meaning is not a self-standing unit. It is fixed by the word's total role in the language — the whole web of sentences, inferences, and uses into which it enters — so that there is no fact of the matter about what a word means "by itself," apart from that network, and no strict synonymy that survives a change in the network around it. Semantic atomism replies that words (or concepts) must have determinate, independently fixed content, and that the meanings of complex expressions are built up compositionally from those independently meaningful parts; otherwise we could not explain how novel sentences mean anything at all.

Please translate each position into embedding-space terms as precisely as you can, including: what holism amounts to when meanings are vectors trained over a corpus; what atomism would have to look like in that setting; how the type-level/occurrence-level distinction maps onto static versus contextualized embeddings; whether the holist's denial of context-independent synonymy has a natural counterpart in the fact that all pairwise similarities are artifacts of a training distribution; and whether vector arithmetic or composition functions ever constitute genuine semantic composition or only a statistical summary of it.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of each position translate cleanly, which parts require distortion, and which parts have no embedding-space expression at all? I am especially interested in whether normativity — the difference between a correct and an incorrect use — has any counterpart here, and whether reference and truth show up as anything beyond a downstream probe's success.

Finally, I want you to be committed rather than even-handed. Give a definite verdict on where the residue points. Is it pointing to something real that the embedding vocabulary is missing and could in principle be extended to capture, or is it pointing to parts of the philosophical debate that were never precise enough to constrain a computational model in the first place? Take the side you think is better supported and defend it.
