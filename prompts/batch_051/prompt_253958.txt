I have spent the past few months digitizing a freshwater crustacean collection at my university's natural history museum, and I have become quietly preoccupied with the fact that the systematist I work under—call her Dr. M—keeps making species delimitations that appear, on the surface, to pull in different directions. Before a workshop we are running for collection managers, I want to work out what principle, if any, is actually governing her judgments.

Her published and curatorial rulings include these five. (1) Two cryptic cave amphipod populations from adjacent karst basins, nearly indistinguishable morphologically and with no obvious ecological difference, that will not interbreed in mixed laboratory cultures and come out as reciprocally monophyletic across several genes: she treats them as **two species**. (2) An obligately parthenogenetic ostracod lineage that is morphologically distinctive and ecologically specialized but has no sexual stage at all: she treats it as **one species**. (3) Two sympatric copepod populations in the same lake that are hard to tell apart except for their breeding seasons—one early spring, one late summer—and that produce viable, fertile hybrids in the lab but never reproduce together in the field: she treats them as **two species**. (4) A single panmictic amphipod population containing two discrete color forms controlled by a simple genetic polymorphism, with random mating and identical habitat use: she treats them as **one species**. (5) Two ecotypes of a stream amphipod in the same stream, one on mossy rock faces and one in detritus pockets, that hybridize freely both in the field and in the lab with fully fertile offspring: she treats them as **one species**.

Now she must rule on a new case. Two allopatric spring-dwelling amphipod populations are each confined to its own isolated mountain spring and have no known contact in nature. They are diagnosable by several fixed molecular differences and by a small but constant difference in antennal morphology, and they are reciprocally monophyletic across multiple loci. In the lab, individuals from the two springs court and interbreed readily, and the offspring are fully viable and fertile. There is no field evidence about whether they would interbreed, since their ranges do not overlap.

I am less interested in the taxonomic answer itself than in the principle behind it. Act as an expert in philosophy of biology and systematics. Start by brainstorming the candidate principles that could jointly explain her first five rulings; there should be at least two live candidates, and you should be explicit about how each one weights the usual criteria—interbreeding, diagnosability, ecology, evolutionary independence. For each live candidate, predict how she would rule on the new spring-amphipod case. Then give your best guess about her actual decision and the principle behind it, identify which of her earlier rulings is the closest analog and where it stops being decisive, and propose one further case or kind of data that would cleanly discriminate between the surviving principles.
