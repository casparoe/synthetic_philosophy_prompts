Lately I've been going back and forth about the common prior assumption. On one hand, the formal arguments behind it feel almost inescapable: if two people are ideal Bayesian reasoners, start with the same priors, and both know how the other updates on shared evidence, then they shouldn't be able to agree to disagree. Any remaining difference has to come down to private information or some failure of rationality (Aumann's agreement theorem is the usual place people point). I find the tidiness genuinely attractive — instead of shrugging at persistent disagreement, you get a standing explanation: different evidence or different priors. It makes disagreement into something you can audit rather than just tolerate.

On the other hand, I also have a really firm intuition that two smart, honest, careful people can look at exactly the same public evidence about, say, whether a social policy is just, or how to interpret a batch of statistical results, or whether there's a fact of the matter about moral questions, and still legitimately end up on opposite sides. I don't just mean cases where one of them is biased or sitting on private information. It feels like they could share absolutely everything public and still rationally disagree, without either one being irrational. i can't shake that, and none of the standard moves — "they must be using different priors," "they're not really both rational," "wait long enough and they'll converge" — has actually dissolved it for me.

Lay out the strongest version of each side. Is the intuition just a failure to appreciate how demanding "same prior" and "common knowledge of each other's posterior" really are, or is it tracking something the formal picture leaves out — permissive rationality, value pluralism, idealization, whatever? Is there an honest reading where both can stay standing, and if not, which one should give way, and what kind of consideration would justify that concession instead of just talking oneself out of the intuition?
