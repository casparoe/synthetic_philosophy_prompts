While reading Mozi's *Universal Love III* in W. P. Mei's translation, I came across this passage:

> Mozi said: Partiality is to be replaced by universality. But how is it that partiality can be replaced by universality? Now, when every one regards the states of others as he regards his own, who would attack the others' states? Others are regarded like self. When every one regards the capitals of others as he regards his own, who would seize the others' capitals? Others are regarded like self. When every one regards the houses of others as he regards his own, who would disturb the others' houses? Others are regarded like self. Now, when the states and cities do not attack and seize each other and when the clans and individuals do not disturb and harm one another -- is this a calamity or a benefit to the world? Of course it is a benefit.

I keep noticing a specific argumentative pattern in this and a few other things I have read. The pattern is: a moral ideal is defended by asking us to imagine what things would be like if everyone actually felt and acted from it, not merely occasionally, but as a settled public standard. The argument then treats the fact that this imagined world would be free of attack, seizure, and disturbance—and full of care for the old, the orphaned, and the weak—as decisive evidence that the ideal is right. It is not directed at what some particular person should do in an isolated choice; it is an everyone-adopts-the-attitude test whose force comes from the total state of the world that results. I have seen a similar move in everyday debates about whether parents may give their own children special priority: someone imagines a world in which every parent treats every child with the same urgency, insists that this world would be better, and then concludes that favouring one's own child is mistaken.

I am not after a broad label for judging things by their results. I want the precise carving of this argumentative move. Does it have an established name? Where exactly are its boundaries? For example, is it the same thing as testing an action by asking what would happen if everyone did it, or is there something distinct because the attitude being tested—impartial care—is itself part of the description of the imagined world? And I would especially like to know how a defender of Confucian graded love should respond: does the fact that a world of universal impartial regard would be harmonious actually show that graded love is wrong, or does the argument beg the question by describing the impartial world as already containing the reliable care that graded love claims only intimate relationships can supply?
