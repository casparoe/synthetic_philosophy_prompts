Milton Friedman's old argument about unrealistic assumptions in economics—the one critics later called the F-twist—always seemed a little too neat to me: assumptions can be as unrealistic as you like, so long as the model predicts well. But the argument seems to run on the idea that you can actually check predictions against outcomes, over and over, under more or less stable conditions.

Here's the case I keep chewing on. A government is deciding whether to phase in a steep carbon tax, and the main evidence comes from an integrated assessment model that couples a climate model to an economic model whose assumptions everyone admits are false: a single representative agent with infinite horizon, perfect foresight, no endogenous technical change of the sort that matters, and so on. The policy has never been tried at anything like this scale, so there just isnt any historical record of this model's predictions for this kind of intervention. Whatever predictive success the model has is mostly for other variables, under other conditions, at other times.

Does Friedman's argument still tell us to ignore the false assumptions in this kind of case? If not, say exactly which premise of the F-twist is under pressure in the new situation, and whether there's a repaired version that tells us when idealized assumptions should actually bother us. Work it through rather than just taking a side. Answer in American English.
