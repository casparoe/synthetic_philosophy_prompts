Consider the following counterfactual. It is 2030, and a safe, cheap, fully reversible neurotechnological procedure has been cleared for elective use across the OECD and is spreading globally. In a large majority of volunteers, the procedure is followed by about one week in which they spontaneously describe their experience in exactly the terms of the classic inverted-spectrum thought experiment: red things now present the qualitative character that green things used to present, and vice versa, while the rest of experience feels unchanged. After a short recalibration, color-naming, sorting, and discrimination return to baseline and become statistically indistinguishable from normal; no physiological or imaging measure available in 2030 can tell whether a subject is currently in the inverted state except by asking them. Subjects' later, post-reversal memory of the state is as detailed and stable as an ordinary memory of a distinctive week.

The dispute at stake concerns qualia. On one side are realists, who hold that experience contains intrinsic, private, ineffable qualitative properties and that inverted-spectrum cases show how such properties can float free of any behavioral or functional role. On the other side are those who follow the line of argument usually called quining qualia, developed by Dennett. On their view, the posit of a qualitative property with no functional consequences is systematically confused: once every functional and dispositional difference has been stripped away, the first-person reports invoked to establish the property no longer license any definite claim about what it is, whether it is present, or whether it has changed. The point is standardly illustrated by Dennett's two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn. Both now dislike the coffee they once approved. One reports that the coffee tastes exactly as it always did and that it is his standards that have risen; the other reports that his standards are unchanged and that it is the taste itself that has altered. Dennett argues that the totality of evidence is consistent with either self-description, and with others besides, so the question of what has really changed may have no determinate answer.

Now trace, with a visible chain of reasoning, how you expect this dispute to develop over the three decades from 2030 to 2060. Address all of the following.

(1) What would each side predict the inversion procedure would show, and which of those predictions do you expect to be treated as confirmed, half-confirmed, or refuted as the reports and studies accumulate?

(2) Which further experiments, protocols, or conceptual arguments would each side demand, and are they likely to converge or instead to harden around different standards of evidence?

(3) How would the availability of the procedure change the institutional and rhetorical equilibrium of the debate: what gets published, taught, funded, and absorbed into public understanding, and how does the language in which reductionist and non-reductionist positions are stated change?

(4) By 2060, is the concept of qualia retired, domesticated (kept but reinterpreted), or entrenched?

(5) Most importantly: does the Chase–Sanborn style of underdetermination survive the existence of a technology that seems to make the inverted state directly and reversibly introspectable, or will the two sides continue to dispute whether that appearance is itself just another pattern of self-reports?

For each main prediction, state your degree of confidence, the direction of error you most fear, and what observation or development would most change your mind. Where a prediction depends on assumptions about how fast institutions, training practices, or funding priorities adapt, identify those assumptions and say what you would expect to see if they failed.
