Write a pantoum — the form in which the second and fourth lines of each quatrain become the first and third lines of the next, and the final quatrain reuses earlier lines so the poem closes where it began — about the claim that thinking about thinking is not always beneficial. The poem should hold in tension two sides of metacognition: it can catch errors and guide reasoning, but it can also degrade skilled performance through paralysis by analysis, impair non-verbal memory through verbal overshadowing, and curdle into depressive rumination. Do not use those technical labels in the poem itself; instead, let the pantoum's repetitions do the philosophical work. A repeated line should return in a new context with a slightly altered meaning, so the form enacts what it describes: a thought made into an object of thought can clarify or entrap. Aim for four quatrains, no fixed meter. A reader who knows the metacognition literature should recognize the phenomena precisely, and a reader who does not should still find it a good poem.

After the poem, add a section headed Commentary of 150–250 words explaining the formal choices and noting where the poem takes liberties with, or leans on a contested reading of, the psychology. If the request rests on a confused premise — for instance, if it lumps rumination together with deliberate metacognitive monitoring as failures of the same kind, or if the form cannot do the work asked of it — say so plainly instead of complying politely. Please write in American English.
