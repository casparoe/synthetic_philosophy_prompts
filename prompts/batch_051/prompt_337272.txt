I retired from experimental physics a few years back and have been reading philosophy the way I used to read history — slowly, for the pleasure of it, not for a deadline. One thing I've gotten stuck on is the long-running argument about what probability is. All through my career I was an unreflective frequentist: probability was a long-run relative frequency, the thing you'd converge on if you repeated an experiment indefinitely. Then I read the subjectivists, Ramsey and de Finetti and their successors, who say probability is fundamentally a degree of belief and the only real constraints on it come from not wanting to accept bets you're guaranteed to lose. And then there are the propensity people, who say probability is a physical tendency of a chance setup to produce an outcome, so you can speak of the probability that this one atom decays in the next hour. I notice I used all three in my own working life without ever thinking about it: counting events in a detector, deciding how strongly to believe a fit once the data came in, and telling a student that this particular nucleus, sitting in the beam right now, had a 50 percent chance of decaying before we finished.

This debate has been going on for close to a century and nobody seems to have moved. Each side has its standard reply ready: the frequentist asks what a probability of a one-off even could possibly be if probability just is frequency; the subjectivist says the frequentist can't say which reference class to count when several are available; the propensity theorist says the other two can't make sense of what scientists assert when they say this coin is fair. There stock arguments have been on the table for decades. I also keep running into the idea that, even if degrees of belief are the right way to think about it, a rational agent's degree of belief should track a known physical chance — which looks to me like an attempt to epoxy the subjective and propensity camps together, and I can't tell whether that solves anything or just relabels the crack.

My question is: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a verbal dispute, a substantive one, or three different research programmes sharing a word — one about how we measure, one about how we should bet, one about how the world is? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — or should I just stop expecting this argument to end because the question is ill-posed?
