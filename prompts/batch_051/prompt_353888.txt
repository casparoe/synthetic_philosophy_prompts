Here's the argument.

(1) When I reach for my mug while reading, the mug is not something I first locate by constructing an internal representation of its position and then plan a path to it. The mug shows up as reachable, and my arm organizes itself around it. Merleau-Ponty's name for this is motor intentionality: the body's own way of being directed toward a meaningful world beneath explicit thought.

(2) This is not a fringe case. Skilled action generally -- typing, driving, catching a ball, playing an instrument -- seems to have the same structure. The situation solicits movements directly, and inserting an inner model between body and world feels phenomenologically wrong and explanatory idle.

(3) So we have a central case of intentionality that works without internal representations. If the world is genuinely present to me in reaching, then reaching is intentional in the fullest sense, just as much as believing or judging.

(4) But if intentionality can work without representations in these basic cases, then the idea that representations are required to explain how mind hooks onto world is not a conceptual truth. It is at most an empirical convenience for some cases.

(5) And once that is granted, the representationalist research program in cognitive science loses its basis. The honest move would be to give up internal representations across the board and describe cognition as a family of ways the body is directly in touch with situations.

(6) That conclusion is too much for me. Thinking about a city I've never visited, wondering whether the Fed will raise rates next quarter, or believing that some number is prime -- these do not look like bodily skills, and they don't reduce, as far as I can tell, to dispositions to move.

So either one of these steps is bad, or I have to accept that all cognition is really a form of skilled coping with the world, which I can't quite make myself believe.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which step should I reject, and what do I lose by rejecting it? I'd most like to keep (1) and (3), but I'm prepared to be told they are the problem.
