The American legal system holds two commitments at once. For serious crimes it treats the guilty mind as the heart of blame: the prosecution must prove the defendant acted purposely, knowingly, recklessly, or at least negligently, and a reasonable mistake about the facts can defeat liability. Yet the same system maintains a large periphery of strict liability. Parking and speeding fines issue without any inquiry into intention, diligence, or mistake. In many states, statutory rape admits no defense of reasonable mistake about the partner's age. Under the responsible corporate officer doctrine, a corporate official with no actual knowledge of a violation of federal food-and-drug law can be convicted of a crime on the strength of his formal responsibility and authority to prevent or correct the violation, even if he delegated the relevant task in good faith. And in many felony-murder jurisdictions, a participant in a robbery can be convicted of murder when a confederate kills someone, although the participant neither intended nor anticipated the killing.

I am trying to understand what theory of responsibility this mixed practice presupposes. The system's official rhetoric treats mens rea as the indispensable marker of culpability, yet these exceptions are not marginal quirks; they structure whole areas of traffic, food, drug, environmental, and public-welfare regulation. Reconstruct the implicit view of fault — or the family of views — that makes the general mens rea requirement and the strict-liability periphery intelligible together. In particular, does the practice strictly presuppose a consequentialist justification of punishment, in which fault is at most a proxy for dangerousness or deterrability? Or can it be read as presupposing an objective, role-based conception of fault, in which undertaking an activity or position of authority can itself be culpable when harm results? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely tolerates or fits well with, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Then spell out what would have to change in the actual doctrines if the presupposed view were replaced, first by a thoroughgoing retributivism that punishes only culpable wrongdoing, and then by a thoroughgoing consequentialism that treats mens rea as nothing more than evidence about future dangerousness. Please write the answer in American English.
