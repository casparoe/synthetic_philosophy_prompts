Ok so in my probability and philosophy of science class we've been on Bertrand's paradox and I'm confused about what the actual upshot is supposed to be. The setup: you want the probability that a 'random chord' of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Depending on how you pick the chord — choose a random radius and a random point along it, pick two random points on the circumference, or pick a random midpoint in the disk — you get 1/2, 1/3, or 1/4. I understand why the three procedures give different numbers.

But here's what I don't get. The standard line is that this shows the principle of indifference gives contradictory answers, so it's underdetermined or maybe inconsistent. But to me the actual problem just seems to be that 'random chord' is underspecified. If you specify a physical chance mechanism — like you actually spin a long straw over the circle or drop a needle at random — the ambiguity disapears and there's one definite answer. So why do people treat this as an objection to the principle of indifference rather than just an objection to saying 'random' sloppily? Is there a version of the paradox where the physical procedure is fully specified and the principle of indifference still manages to gives two different probabilities? Or is the principle only in trouble because it says to distribute credence evenly across 'possible cases,' and there's no fact of the matter about which way of carving the possibilities into cases is right? I guess I want an explanation of where exactly the principle, as opposed to the word 'random,' goes wrong (if it does). Also, is there a real argument that one of the three answers is *the* right one, or is picking 1/2 just a convention?
