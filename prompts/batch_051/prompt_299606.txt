I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about weakness of will, and whether the "will" in the name is doing real work or whether these are all just cases of hyperbolic discounting — steep up close, flatter further out — so I flip-flop as the smaller-sooner reward gets near. If that's the whole story, then akrasia should be capturable by one story about time preferences and discount curves, and I want to know if my gut verdicts below actually add up to that or to something messier.

Here are the cases and how I'd call them. (1) I firmly decide in the afternoon not to order dessert tonight, and at the restaurant I genuinely think "one slice won't matter, I've been good this week," and I order it; verdict: weakness of will, mild, because I broke a plan, and the rationalization feels like it got manufactured by the dessert being right there. (2) Lying in bed with a 9am meeting I keep thinking "I should get up, this is going to cost me," with the judgment never changing, while I stay put; verdict: the purest akrasia, since judgment and action split at the very same time. (3) I pay upfront for a year's gym membership because I know I'll quit by February, and when I quit in February I feel no conflict at all; verdict: the signup is sensible self-management, and the quitting isnt weakness of will, because by then there's no judgment to act against. (4) I agree to help a friend move Saturday partly to avoid the awkward phone call, knowing Friday night that a better offer will probably come and I'll bail; Saturday I text that I'm sick and go to the lake; verdict: weakness of will, and worse than (1), because I'd already planned around my own unreliability and made it someone else's problem. (5) I sell a stock I still believe is the right long-term holding because I can't stand watching it drop, while telling myself I'll buy back later, probably higher; verdict: not akrasia, it feels more like finding out my real risk tolerance under stress. (6) I ask my partner to ration my cigarettes, three a day, to quit, and by evening I'm nagging her until she caves and hands me a fourth; verdict: the nagging is akrasia, since I still judge while doing it that she shouldn't give in, but the precommitment also feels like it was made in bad faith. (7) I simply don't want to climb on Saturday anymore, though I was excited Wednesday, and I feel no pull against the new preference; verdict: not akrasia, just a preference shifting.

Is there one principle — about the shape of preferences over time, about what the agent judges at the moment of action, or about what they identify with — that gets all seven? I keep noticing that (1) and (2) feel like the same failure even though in (1) the judgment changed and in (2) it didn't, and that (3) and (4) look structurally identical even though I rank one as fine and one as clearly wrong. I'd prefer a priniciple that overturns a verdict or two over a baroque rule with a clause for each case. Please state the principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and why, and tell me what it predicts about a case I keep going back and forth on: someone who books a non-refundable cabin in the woods to force themselves to finish a thesis, and then packs a laptop loaded with downloaded movies.
