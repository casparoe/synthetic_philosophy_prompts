Been reading Sartre for a seminar and got into an argument with a friend about whether existentialism is even internally consistent. He laid out this argument and I can't find the exact step to resist:

(1) The claim that existence precedes essence just is the denial that there is a human nature, where a human nature would be a set of defining properties that every human has simply in virtue of being human.
(2) But existentialists also make universal claims about human beings: for instance, Sartre says something like that we are radically free, that choice is inescapable for us, and that a person is nothing other than what they make of themselves.
(3) A universal claim about what every human is, in virtue of being human, is exactly a claim about human nature.
(4) So existentialism both denies and affirms a human nature.
(5) A position that affirms what its central thesis denies is incoherent.
(6) So existentialism is incoherent.

I suspect the issue is somewhere around (3)—maybe there's a difference between a human condition and a human nature—but I can't tell wether that's a real distinction or just a relabeling to dodge the objection. So my question: is this argument valid as stated? If not, which step fails, and on what reading? If it is valid, is the conclusion just something existentialists have to bite? And when you answer, please keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know existentialism well—I don't want a contested reading presented as if it were the consensus.
