Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of the wrongness of free riding, where free riding means benefiting from a cooperative scheme, shared resource, or public good while failing to bear an appropriate share of its costs. I want the list to function as a prespecification: the tests a theory must pass before I trust it on hard cases, not after. For each item, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement.

Cover at least these three groups.

First, the cases a theory must get right, either by delivering the correct verdict or by explaining convincingly why the apparent case is misdescribed. Include: (1) the large-group case in which one person's noncontribution has no perceptible effect on whether the good is provided; (2) the threshold case in which a good appears only if enough people chip in, and no one's contribution is pivotal until the threshold is met; (3) the person who would have voted against the scheme, cannot avoid the benefit, and refuses to pay; (4) the person who is unable to contribute even though willing; (5) the person who benefits only as an incidental by-product of actions others would have performed anyway; (6) the overuse of a rival, exhaustible common-pool resource such as a fishery or shared pasture, as against the under-provision of a non-rival public good such as clean air or flood control; and (7) the small-group case in which contributions are visible, mutually conditional, and individually consequential. Add any further cases you regard as load-bearing.

Second, the distinctions a theory must respect rather than collapse. I especially want these drawn carefully: free riding versus parasitism versus exploitation; the wrongness of an act versus the blameworthiness of the agent who performs it; refusing to contribute because you reject the scheme versus refusing because you expect others to cover your share; non-rival versus rival resources; excludable versus non-excludable benefits; fair share understood as equal division, as ability to pay, or as benefit received; and a one-off taking versus participating in a standing practice.

Third, the formal or game-theoretic results a theory cannot contradict—or, if it proposes to set one aside, it must say explicitly why. These should include the dominance of defection in a prisoner's dilemma; the dependence of cooperation on mutual expectation in assurance or stag-hunt interactions; the fact that noncontribution is often individually rational in large anonymous groups because no single contribution changes the outcome; the logic of step-level and threshold goods, including cases where one or a few actors can single-handedly secure provision; and the claim that the tragedy of the commons concerns rival, exhaustible, open-access resources rather than non-rival public goods.

After laying out the list, give your overall verdict: which desiderata are non-negotiable, which are merely preferences of particular camps, and whether any pair of them cannot be jointly satisfied. I suspect there is a real tension between condemning free riding in cases where no individual contribution makes a difference and refusing to condemn harmless acts, and I want to know whether that tension can be resolved or has to be admitted. Also say which single requirement does the most work in eliminating the main candidate theories. Finally, add a short paragraph on how the leading families of theories—harm-based, fairness-based, consent- or contract-based, reciprocity-based, and rule- or generalization-based accounts—fare against the list, but keep the weight on the desiderata themselves rather than on the scorecard.

Please write the answer in American English. Also indicate how confident you are in the overall list, and single out the individual point you are least sure of.
