I am currently taking a course on the moral psychology of prejudice, and I would like the distinction I have been building all term to be assessed on its merits rather than graded gently. I have been trying to separate preferences from biases with two criteria. A disposition is closer to a preference insofar as its holder, when challenged, defends it by describing a state of affairs to be brought about; it is closer to a bias insofar as the defense is secretly carried by a description of how some group of people is, even when that description is never asserted outright. The second criterion is what I call the avowal test: ask the person to state the load-bearing description as their own claim. A preference, I want to say, becomes more determinate when this happens; a bias stalls, hedges, or retreats to something acceptable that no longer does the work. For example, someone who avoids pork can state the target state without strain, while someone who repeatedly passes over older applicants usually falls back on ad hoc claims about team fit while the real work is done by an age claim they would not defend in a conversation about age alone. I know this sits near the Anscombean direction-of-fit distinction and near transparency accounts, and I suspect my two criteria are really two different tests glued together. I want a committed verdict, not a survey: is this a real distinction, has it already been drawn cleanly under a name I should know, and what is the clearest case that breaks the avowal test? Tell me which criterion is doing the real work and whether the distinction survives the break or should be dismantled.
