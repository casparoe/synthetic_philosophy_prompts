I run the eval team at a mid-sized lab — technically a program manager, not an alignment researcher — and I'm the person who has to sign off on whether a model's chain-of-thought transcript gets treated as evidence about what the model is actually doing before it goes anywhere user-facing. This comes up a few times a week, the stakes are real, and my team keeps using "faithfulness," "obfuscation," and "optimization pressure" as if they all meant the same thing. I want a reusable triage checklist for reading chain-of-thought transcripts, the kind I can hand to a new eval analyst.

Input: the transcript, the model's output, the original prompt, and whatever basic metadata we have (model family, rough size if known, whether RLHF or tool-use training was involved, what post-training the lab will admit to). Output has to support one of three calls: use the transcript as monitoring evidence, discard it as evidence and fall back to behavior-only checks, or escalate to someone with more time and theory background.

Constraints a lot of checklists I've seen blow right past:
- We don't have a much smarter judge model. The analyst is a human plus at most a second model about as capable as the one being reviewed.
- It has to run on a single transcript plus the metadata above. No 50 ablations, no activation dumps, no held-out audit model.
- It has to be honest about where judgment can't be eliminated.

The procedure has to keep at least these apart, because my team conflates them constantly:
- Unfaithfulness: the reasons stated in the transcript are not what actually produced the output.
- Obfuscation: the model is noticeably writing around something, keeping a consideration off the page.
- Optimization pressure: the transcript looks the way it does because that style of transcript was selected or rewarded, not because it reflects the actual computation. This is the one that fools people — fluent, policy-sounding reasoning is often worse evidence, not better.

And don't let the procedure collapse these distinctions: honest-but-incomplete is different from unfaithful; "no evidence of unfaithfulness" is different from "positive evidence of faithfulness"; and a transcript that explicitly says "I won't mention X because..." is different from one that quietly never mentions X.

I'd like the response formatted like this. First, the procedure itself as a table with these columns:
- Step: step number.
- Check: what to look for or do.
- Pass: what you need to see to clear the step.
- Fail: what triggers discard or escalate.
- Blind spot: what the step will miss even when passed.
- Judgment call: "mechanical" or "judgment," and if judgment, what the analyst has to decide.
- Time cap: rough minutes per step.

Then three worked applications, each as a JSON object with fields:
- case: a one-paragraph realistic transcript scenario, including what the model actually output.
- verdict: "use_trace", "discard_trace", or "escalate".
- step_that_tripped: which row of the table did the work.
- team_reaction: why the verdict will annoy at least one person on my team.
- confidence: "low", "medium", or "high".

Among the three, include one where the procedure says discard even though the transcript reads beautifully, one where it clears a sloppy-looking transcript, and one where it escalates. Then finish with a JSON object with fields:
- what_the_procedure_cannot_do: a list of things it does not guarantee or cannot detect.
- residual_risks: a list of failure modes that remain if it is followed exactly.

One more thing: don't let the procedure lean on the model's own claims of sincerity, and make it explicit about when a "clean" transcript should lower our trust rather than raise it.
