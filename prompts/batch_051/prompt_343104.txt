I'm a resilience planner for a county council, and every other week someone sends my team a scenario described as 'possible' — a sustained outage at a substation, a reservoir breach under rainfall the records don't have, a fuel supply interruption. We have to either put a risk on the register or leave it off, and the register decides what gets funded, so we can't register everything. The snag is that 'possible' gets used to mean at least three things: not ruled out by physics, not ruled out by our current models, and likely enough to matter. I have a feeling the real issue is how possibility claims relate to probability estimates, but I can't make that precise without a philosopher's toolkit.

I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in whatever the philosophy of probability and modality actually has to say, for separating those three. It has to be something a non-philosopher can run in a two-hour meeting, often with no defensible number for the probability. It should say which questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. My line manager will only sign off if it is auditable and does not smuggle value choices in as if they were probability estimates.

Then run the procedure on two proposals: (1) planning for cascading failures in the grid during a heatwave, where the models disagree wildly, and (2) planning for a breach of an upstream reservoir under a combination of conditions each individually plausible but never observed together, with a nominal estimate of 1 in 10 million per year. Include one case where the procedure gives an answer the team will not like, and say what the procedure still cannot do. Finally, tell me which part of your reasoning is most likely to fail and what would change your mind about the main verdicts.
