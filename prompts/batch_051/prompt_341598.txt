I've been trying to make up my mind about something, and I keep getting pulled in two directions. The theory I find compelling is clean: once a kind of evidence gets cheap to fake and you can't reliably spot the fakes, it can't carry weight on its own anymore, because for any particular item there's now a live chance that it was manufactured. That seems exactly right about video in the age of deepfakes, and I like that it gives me a tidy, non-arbitrary rule instead of making me decide case by case on vibe. But I also have a pretty firm intuition that some videos still work as evidence: footage of an incident that matches twenty eyewitness accounts, a livestream I watched unfold in real time, a clip my brother sends me of his kid's first steps. The theory seems to say I should file all of those under "possibly fake, so suspend judgment," and I don't think a reasonable person could actually do that. I used to think of video as the epistemic backstop you fall back on when testimony and memory conflict; now I'm not sure whether the backstop is broken, or whether it was never what I imagined. So which should give? Is the intuition just a lag in updating to a changed base rate — like people who went on treating heavily retouched magazine photos as plain photographs long after Photoshop became cheap? Should the theory be amended to say that video can still justify belief when it arrives embedded in a corroborating network of other testimony, provenance, and cost signals — and would that be an amendment or a quiet surrender of the original rule? Or is the actual lesson that video never was a standalone backstop, only one strand in a larger fabric, so the theory and the intuition both survive once you stop picturing any source as doing the work alone? Give me the strongest version of each move, and tell me which one really costs the least.
