I've been rereading Jowett's introduction to his translation of Plato's *Timaeus*, and I was struck by this sentence:

"Of all the writings of Plato the Timaeus is the most obscure and repulsive to the modern reader, and has nevertheless had the greatest influence over the ancient and mediaeval world."

I take Jowett to be gesturing at a general phenomenon: obscurity does not reduce a text's historical influence; it changes its causal pathway, because later readers can project their own commitments into it with less friction. That seems relevant to demarcating pseudohistory, especially with Atlantis, ancient aliens, and Fomenko's new chronology.

I'm compiling a list of mechanisms by which an ancient text or chronographic hypothesis gets immunised against disconfirmation. So far I have:

(1) textual lacunae and translation flexibility: where the source is fragmentary or multivalent, proponents can always appeal to 'correct' readings that support their claims;
(2) secret-or-destroyed evidence: Atlantis is real but vanished; the Tartarian empire left traces that were deliberately erased; the Library of Alexandria or a post-cataclysm dark age destroyed the records;
(3) privileged access to a hidden pattern: the ancients encoded their knowledge in measurements, alignments, or numerological correspondences that only a practised eye can recover;
(4) the reclassification of counter-evidence as folly of the 'official' chronology: any conflicting document is forged, misdated, or produced by the orthodox academic establishment.

I want one more mechanism that is genuinely different from these four. It should not be a restatement or special case of any of them. It should be a distinct way that a pseudohistorical programme can absorb or pre-empt contrary evidence, not merely another example of the same move. Then tell me honestly whether the new mechanism is any good, and whether it maps onto anything Jowett is saying about the Timaeus.
