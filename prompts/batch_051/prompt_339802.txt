ok, here's one I'd like. I'm teaching an upper-level undergrad metaphysics seminar, about 14 students, one 80-minute session, and I want an in-class activity on the statue and the clay — the material constitution puzzle. You know the setup: Monday there's a lump of clay on the table; Tuesday someone shapes that very clay into a statue. We want to say the lump was there all week and survived the shaping, but the statue only came into being on Tuesday, and yet there seems to be just one material object sitting there. (If you like the cutesy names: Lumpl and Goliath.)

Design me something runnable that makes students feel both pulls before we name anything. I've got modeling clay, a simple humanish mold, index cards, a kitchen scale, a whiteboard, space to push tables around, and I can hand out a small bag of candy as "court awards" if that helps. The hard part: I dont want constitution vs identity, or coincident objects, or sortal to be introduced by me until the debrief. The setup itself should force students to make the call — is it one thing or two? — and feel what hangs on the call.

Things to include:
- rules or roles, with a timeline;
- at least one point where students commit publicly, before discussion, to a verdict about whether the shaped statue is the same object as the pre-shaping lump;
- a later twist where the statue gets squashed or destroyed some other way and they have to live with their earlier verdict — that's where I want the puzzle to really bite;
- a debrief mapping specific moments in the activity to the actual moves in the literature (different histories, different persistence conditions, two things in the same place, constitution vs identity, maybe sortal-relative identity) without just listing tags;
- an honest note on which part of the philosophical problem the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply;
- and a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it.

Also, please separate what's basically settled (like the intuitive starting points people tend to have) from what's genuinely contested among people who know this material. Don't write as if the constitution view, or the monist response, or any one resolution is obviously the consensus — I'd rather you lay the disagreement out cleanly than flatten it.
