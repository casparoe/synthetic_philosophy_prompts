You are a professional philosopher whose research specialty is the metaphysics of causation, and you serve on the review committee for the Meridian Foundation's Causation in Scientific Practice grant program. The committee can fund only one of the three finalist projects described below. Draft the recommendation memo the Foundation director will circulate to the board.

Before writing, work through a private brainstorming step: construct the strongest possible version of each proposal, then formulate the strongest objection to whichever proposal you end up recommending. Do not include the brainstorming in the final output; present only the polished memo and the structured summary requested at the end.

The memo should open with an actionable bottom line: name the proposal to fund, or fund with conditions, and give your primary reason. It should then defend that recommendation and include a section stating the best case for the strongest runner-up and explaining why it does not prevail. The Foundation has asked the committee not to lean on outside reading: no bibliography, no citation chasing, and no references. Evaluate the proposals as described.

The three finalists are:

1. Causal Singularism. This proposal holds that causation is a fundamental, unanalyzable relation between token events, not reducible to regularities, counterfactual dependence, or inference rules. It aims to give a formal framework for legitimate singular causal claims in sciences that cannot run interventions or gather large regularities, such as geology, evolutionary history, and parts of macroeconomics, by treating causal production as a primitive relation with its own logic. Deliverable: a formal semantics plus a user-oriented guide for researchers in the earth and historical sciences.

2. Counterfactual Reduction. This proposal holds that causal claims reduce to facts about what would happen under specified interventions. It proposes to extend structural causal models to settings with feedback, interference, and spillover, and to produce open-source software that lets epidemiologists estimate policy-relevant causal quantities from observational data. Deliverable: tested software and two worked case studies.

3. Pragmatic Eliminativism. This proposal holds that causation is not a well-defined scientific relation and, in the spirit of Russell's deflationary remarks about the concept, should be replaced in practice by predictive models that say what would be observed under specified manipulations without asserting that one thing produces another. It proposes to build a causality-free prediction suite for a clinical research group and compare its recommendations with a standard causal-inference pipeline on benchmark data. Deliverable: software and a methodological report.

Use these scoring criteria: philosophical depth (does the work clarify what causal language commits us to?); practical payoff for scientific inference (does it improve how real inferences are made or interpreted?); and tractability (can the deliverable plausibly be completed within the grant period?).

Throughout the memo, distinguish what is settled within the philosophy of causation from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested metaphysical thesis as the field's consensus.

After the memo text, provide a one-page assessment table with these exact columns: Project; Core thesis; Philosophical depth (1-5, 5 highest); Practical payoff (1-5, 5 highest); Tractability risk (Low/Medium/High); Recommended action (Fund / Fund with conditions / Do not fund). Use one row per project.

Write in American English.
