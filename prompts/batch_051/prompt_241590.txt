I teach a seminar on punishment, and there's a conflict I keep coming back to that I can't get out of. The theory I'm drawn to is this: the wrongness of an act doesn't decay with time. If a theft was wrong in 1995, it is exactly as wrong in 2025; the victim's desert, or the wrongdoers guilt, is not the kind of thing the calendar can erode. Time is a do-nothing factor—it just measures how long the act has been sitting there, equally wrong. The view is attractively simple: it refuses to treat years as a hidden solvent, it fits the widespread practice of not letting the worst crimes lapse, and it avoids drawing some arbitrary line where a wrong supposedly stops mattering. If ten years erases desert, someone must explain why nine doesn't and eleven does, and every line looks arbitrary.

And yet I also have a firm intuition that a statute of limitations is not merely a pragmatic compromise about fading memories and dead witnesses. I think the state genuinely shouldn't prosecute the corner-store shoplifter from twenty-five years ago, even if we somehow had clean video and a confession. Not because the evidence is stale, but because the passage of time itself changes what is owed. If the person has lived those decades and become, in the relevant sense, someone else, chasing them now feels less like justice than like a grudge wearing the costume of a court.

I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know: if I keep the theory, what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes—what would I have to discover about it to debunk it? And if I keep the intuition, what does the theory get amended to, and does it lose its simplicity in the process? Or is there a reading on which both survive: the wrongness doesn't decay, but something else—standing, the continuity of the person, the state's role—does? Work out what would justify each move, not just pick a side.
