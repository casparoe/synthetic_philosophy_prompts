Consider the paradox of tolerance, but with the following modification. A society maintains an entrenched norm of unrestricted public expression. Eight percent of the population supports a movement that rejects this norm: the movement openly states that, upon taking power, it will ban the expression of all views it does not endorse and dismantle the institutions that permit such expression. Assume, as the standard argument does, that argument and exposure will not liberalize the movement, so that tolerating it indefinitely will eventually end the tolerant order. The canonical response holds that the community may suppress the movement in defense of tolerance without betraying tolerance itself.

Now vary the case in a direction the canonical response tends to leave unexamined. The movement is internally divided. Sixty percent is a hard core committed to the entire program. Forty percent is a soft wing that is intolerant only in the sense of deferring to the hard core; the soft wing would return to ordinary democratic politics if the community made a small face-saving concession, such as creating a purely advisory body with no coercive authority, but it would not do so under outright suppression, which it would read as proof that the community's tolerance was never genuine. The community cannot suppress only the hard core.

Stipulate the following probabilities. Under unconditional tolerance, the movement's chance of taking power within one generation is 0.4. Under outright suppression, that chance falls to 0.05, but there is a 0.25 probability that the soft wing radicalizes and merges with the hard core; if that happens, the movement doubles and its one-generation takeover probability becomes 0.12. Independently, there is a 0.10 probability that a currently tolerant faction, observing the suppression, begins pressing to suppress a view it dislikes in the next cycle. Under the concession policy, the soft wing peels away and the movement shrinks, so the one-generation takeover probability is 0.15; but with probability 0.20 the concession legitimizes the movement enough to double its recruitment from the remaining 92 percent of the population over the course of the generation.

Work through what the canonical analysis says in this modified case. If the community's only objective is to maximize the probability that a broadly tolerant order still exists one generation later, compare the three policies — unconditional tolerance, outright suppression, and concession — and say which dominates, or whether the stipulated numbers leave the choice indeterminate, and on which parameter the verdict most sensitively turns. Then give the philosophical diagnosis. Does the familiar conclusion that tolerance need not tolerate intolerance depend on treating intolerance as a fixed attribute of an agent that the tolerant community merely observes, rather than as something partially produced by the community's own response? If the modified case shows the latter, is the correct conclusion that the paradox dissolves, or that it reappears at a higher level as a choice between suppressing intolerance and manufacturing it?
