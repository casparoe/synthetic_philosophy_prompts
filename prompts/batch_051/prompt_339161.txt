In the philosophy-and-film seminar I co-run, the same argument has been running for two years without moving. Whenever someone proposes a biopic or a historical drama, one side treats the historical person as someone to whom the writer owes fidelity: if the record establishes that an event happened a certain way, or that a person did or did not say a certain thing, then collapsing timelines, merging two historical figures into a composite, or inventing scenes that never occurred is a failure of respect, no matter how much it helps the drama. The other side treats the work primarily as a story: a biopic is not a legal deposition or a scholarly monograph, and the writer's real obligations run to the living audience and to something like thematic or emotional truth, which a factually faithful sequence of events can fail to capture. Everyone in the room agrees about what the record shows, and everyone agrees that the record and the drama can pull apart; nonetheless, no one has shifted. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement has stalled because the two sides are answering different questions under the same word, owe: one side is asking what respect for the dead requires, while the other is asking what kind of truth narrative art is answerable to, and neither question is settled by any fact about what actually happened. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than a verdict or a survey of the positions. Is my hypothesis right, or is the disagreement better understood as a substantive moral dispute about whether the dead can still be wronged by how they are depicted, or a dispute about the purpose of historical drama, or something else? Given the diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction that splits the apparent dispute, a test case in which the two commitments come apart, a reframing of the question, or an argument that we should not expect the two sides to converge?
