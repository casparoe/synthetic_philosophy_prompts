I've been going back and forth with a friend in linguistics about indicative and counterfactual conditionals, and she keeps insisting the difference isn't just surface grammar — the two kinds of if make you do different things with what you already know. Her go-to example is a burned house: an investigator in the morning can accept that if the faulty wiring didn't start the fire, something else did, while rejecting that if the faulty wiring hadn't started the fire, something else would have. Same facts, same antecedent and consequent, different mood, different truth value. I want a poem that actually does the work of showing why they come apart.

Write a Petrarchan sonnet in the voice of that fire investigator walking through the house. The octave should use only indicative conditionals — if-clauses in the simple past, results in the simple past (did, was, started) — and the sestet should use only counterfactual conditionals — if-clauses in the past perfect, results in would have. The volta between lines 8 and 9 should be exactly where the investigator stops holding fixed that the house burned and instead un-fixes one thing, the wiring as the cause, and watches the rest of the night go differently. In the octave he can add and eliminate causes while keeping the fire itself fixed; in the sestet every conditional should be a thought about a world where the wiring was fine, and most of them should end in the house never burning.

Don't let the poem name "indicative" or "counterfactual" anywhere; the grammatical mood has to carry the distinction on its own. The tell I'm looking for is that a specialist can locate the volta just from the grammar, and someone encountering the poem cold still feels the epistemic ground shift under them halfway through. I wont mind if the iambic pentameter gets a bit lumpy around all the past-perfect and would-have phrases, so long as the Petrarchan rhyme scheme stays exact.
