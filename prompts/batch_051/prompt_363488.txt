I'm putting together a handout for a reading group on the philosophy of gauge theories, whose members are comfortable with classical electrodynamics but have not read the specialist literature on the realism question. I'd like you to build a decision tree that takes someone from an initial hunch about what gauge freedom means to a named interpretative position about what, if anything, is real in a gauge theory such as classical electromagnetism. Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of labels: give the branching claims first, then locate the positions in the space they define, and phrase every branch as a question someone can answer without already knowing which leaf it would lead to. The main branches should be something like the following. (i) Are two gauge-equivalent potentials, such as A and A + ∇λ, two descriptions of one physical situation or two distinct but observationally equivalent situations? (ii) If they are one situation, can the physical content be exhausted by local gauge-invariant quantities such as the field strength, or does the Aharonov–Bohm effect force you to count non-local gauge-invariant objects such as holonomies or Wilson loops as part of the content? (iii) If non-local objects must be admitted, is the underlying ontology best described as a structure or relation, as a loop-like object, or as a situation in which ontological questions should simply be declined?

The leaves should include at least: a strict local field-strength realism; a holonomy or loop view; an overdescription view on which the potential is real but gauge-equivalent potentials stand for distinct, empirically inaccessible situations; a structuralist or quotient-space view; and a deflationary or instrumentalist view. For each leaf give either its standard name (if you are confident one is standard) or a descriptive label, and add one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The audience is a seminar handout, so aim for roughly five or six leaves. Explicitly flag any pairs of positions your tree cannot separate and explain why; also note any combinations of answers that your tree allows but no one seems to occupy, and say briefly whether each is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

In the final text, please separate clearly what is settled empirically, such as what the Aharonov–Bohm phase shift measures, from what is genuinely contested among people working on the topic, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus. Avoid citations, bibliographies, and reading suggestions throughout, since I will use this as a self-contained handout. Write the handout in British English.
