In the introduction to The Invention of Tradition, there’s a passage where Hobsbawm argues that traditions which present themselves as ancient are often quite recent in origin and deliberately constructed, using the Highland kilt and the rituals of the monarchy among his examples. What I keep hearing in politics here is a different claim: that the nation is like a family, that it inherits its traditions from its ancestors, and that this inheritance is what binds the present generation to the past and to each other. People use this to argue we shouldn’t lightly change national rituals, since a family doesn’t vote on whether to keep its inherited customs. I want to know how far that analogy actually holds once Hobsbawm’s passage is on the table. Give me a proper correspondence table—nation/family, tradition/inheritance, ancestors/founding figures, shared memory/family memory—and then the places where it breaks. In particular, does the analogy quietly import the idea that inheritance is involuntary and unbroken, when Hobsbawm’s point is that the chain was often manufactured quite recently? And after the disanalogies are in, does the conclusion that national rituals deserve special protection survive, or was the family analogy doing all the work? If there’s a better analogy, what is it, and what does it preserve about the original argument?
