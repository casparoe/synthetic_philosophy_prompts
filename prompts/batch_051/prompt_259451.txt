I teach a seminar on European integration, and the discussion of the democratic deficit keeps stalling on the same abstract question: whether the EU's legitimacy problem is a defect that institutional change could fix, or a property of the polity itself — in particular, the absence of a common demos. Rather than let the argument stay at that level, I want to pose a concrete constitutional counterfactual and ask for a reasoned, mechanism-level prediction.

Suppose a new treaty enters into force in 2035 with the following changes, everything else about the Union's competences, budget, membership, and enlargement policy remaining the same. First, the President of the European Commission and the College of Commissioners are elected on a single Europe-wide party-list ballot every five years; the executive therefore owes its mandate to a transnational electorate rather than to national governments and the European Parliament's approval. Second, the Council of the European Union ceases to be a co-legislator. It becomes a chamber of national governments that can refer a proposal back once and delay it for up to six months, but cannot amend or block it; the Commission proposes legislation, the European Parliament adopts it by majority, and the Parliament also gains the right of initiative. Third, qualified-majority voting now extends to every remaining Council decision, including taxation and foreign policy, and all residual national vetoes are abolished.

Your task is to predict how politics in the Union will look in 2050, making the chain of reasoning explicit. I care less about whether you think the reform is a success than about which effects are robust, which depend on assumptions about voters, parties, or Member State constitutional courts, and what would have to hold for your prediction to be wrong. One issue worth addressing is whether a directly elected, partisan College can still function as the neutral enforcer of the treaties, and what happens if it cannot. Please structure your answer as follows:

1. "The first five years" (one paragraph): how parties, national governments, and the newly elected Commission adapt to the changed incentives.
2. "The case that the democratic deficit is genuinely reduced" (one to two paragraphs): address input legitimacy, output legitimacy, and the no-demos objection.
3. "The case that the deficit is relocated or deepened" (one to two paragraphs): identify where the old pathologies might reappear in new form.
4. "Long-run effects on sovereignty and ever-closer union" (two paragraphs): national constitutional courts, exit threats, and the pace and direction of further integration.
5. "Confidence and falsifiers" (one paragraph): which predictions you hold with high confidence, which are speculative, and what future evidence would change your mind.
