I've gradually settled on a view about moral disagreement that I keep reaching for in arguments, but I've realised I've never properly asked what could count against it. The view is this: when two people or groups are working with different value systems, there is almost always a compromise available that leaves each side better off by its own standards, and when no compromise is found, that usually reflects a failure of imagination, patience, or goodwill rather than a conflict of values that was genuinely beyond negotiation. Put differently, moral conflicts are rarely zero-sum: there are gains from trade to be had even between people who do not share a common morality, because each side cares about different things with different intensities.

What I want is a map of the view's exposure. What would have to be true for this to be wrong, or far narrower than I think? I am not asking for the standard objection that some people bargain in bad faith or make unreasonable demands. I mean the deeper cases: situations where the values themselves make trade impossible or impermissible, where a compromise would itself be a moral wrong, where both sides sincerely want a resolution and still could not honestly accept one, or where the search for mutual benefit distorts something important. Which real or imagined cases would show that my view is false, and which would merely show that it needs qualifying? I would like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I would like you to distinguish what a defender of the view should concede counts against it from what they can reasonably set aside. I would also like to know what my view predicts that a more pessimistic view of moral conflict would not predict — what would the world have to look like for the pessimistic view to be right and mine wrong.

I would also like you to be direct about whether the view, as I have stated it, risks anything at all. If it is so flexible that no outcome could count against it, I want to hear that, and whether that flexibility is a serious problem for a view of this kind or just the normal shape of a practical rule of thumb.

For what it is worth, I would be most moved by cases in which both parties enter the disagreement in good faith, with a genuine desire to resolve it, and still cannot find an honest compromise, since those are the cases my view is meant to handle best. Please write the answer in British English.
