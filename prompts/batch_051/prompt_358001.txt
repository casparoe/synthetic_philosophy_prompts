I'm TA-ing a course on science and religion and I keep coming back to Galileo, so here's what I'd like you to write. A dialogue, set in a garden outside Florence around 1615. One speaker is Galileo, and he should defend the position he actually laid out in his Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina: nature and scripture cannot really contradict each other because both come from God, but scripture is written for our salvation and often speaks according to how things appear to ordinary people, so when a genuinely demonstrated physical result seems to conflict with a literal reading, it is the reading that has to give way, not the demonstration. The other speaker should be an invented but fair-minded churchman—well read in the Fathers and councils, not a caricature. He should press the genuinely hard objections: that the Fathers and the Council of Trent reserve the interpretation of scripture to the Church and the Fathers rather than to private mathematicians, that Joshua commanded the sun to stand still and the Psalmist says the earth shall not be moved, and that if a private mathematician can reinterpret verses every time his telescope disagrees with them, the whole fabric of scriptural authority comes apart.

I want a real back-and-forth in which each person responds to the other's actual objection and even concedes a point here or there; I don't want the churchman to be a pushover who just gets demolished, and I don't want Galileo to "win" by simply asserting that science trumps theology. Aim for about 800 words, keep the language natural rather than stilted, and make sure they argue from eachother's actual premises instead of talking past one another. Oh, and please write it in American English.
