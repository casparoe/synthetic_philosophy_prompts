I've been going back and forth with a sociologist friend about whether generational categories—Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, that sort of thing—pick out real kinds or are just journalistic conveniences, and she's pretty dismissive: she says the whole thing is astrology with spreadsheets. I'm sympathetic, but I don't want to just assert that the categories are fake. So here's the claim I'd like you to help me pressure-test: that a generation like "Gen Z" is a real kind in at least the minimal sense that there are robust, durable cohort-level properties distinguishing it from adjacent generations, properties that wouldn't simply dissapear once you controlled for how old people are and what year it is.

The obvious test would be to compare 20-year-olds in 2024 with 40-year-olds in 2024 and chalk any differences up to cohort. But that's exactly where my friend pounces: a difference could be an age effect (20-year-olds are at a different life stage), a period effect (2024 is unusual in a way hitting everyone), or a genuine cohort effect, and since cohort plus age equals period the three are collinear—you can't put all three in a regression and identify them separately. So I want you to design the best test you can of the "real kind" claim under that constraint. Tell me precisely what data or comparison you'd use, what pattern of results would make you think there's a genuine cohort-level property at work rather than age or period noise, what pattern would make you think generational talk is doing no real work, and—crucially—what even your best test would fail to settle. I don't just mean practical limitations; I mean what gets left out when you operationalize "real kind" in terms of measurable between-cohort differences. No need to cite anything—I'm after the design itself.
