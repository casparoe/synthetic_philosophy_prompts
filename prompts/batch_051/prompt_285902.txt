I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about Kant's views on lying, and I came away with a precise record of her verdicts on particular cases but no precise statement of the rule that produces them. She anchors herself in Kant's 1797 essay On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy, yet she kept reaching for a distinction she never managed to state cleanly — something between lying in the strict sense and a broader failure of truthfulness.

Here are the verdicts:

(1) The murderer at the door. You may not lie to the would-be murderer even to save the friend hiding in the house. You may refuse to answer, remain silent, or make it clear that you will not help him; what you may not do is assert what you believe to be false. If matters go badly because you kept to the truth or stayed silent, the responsibility lies with the murderer; if matters go badly because you lied, you are morally implicated in the result in a way you would not otherwise have been.

(2) The tactful untruth. Asked by a friend whether you like a story she has written, you may not say you like it if you do not. You may find something true and kind to say, change the subject, or decline to answer; a knowingly false compliment is a lie.

(3) The actor and the tall tale. An actor on a stage, or someone telling an obviously exaggerated story with a wink, is not lying. The context removes the claim from the order of assertion, so the duty of truthfulness does not come into play.

(4) The comfort-lie to a dying person. You know the prognosis is grim and the person asks directly. You may not pretend the doctors are hopeful or invent reassurances. That would treat the person merely as a means — here, to their own passing comfort — and would deny them the standing of a rational agent to whom the truth is owed. You may answer gently, or say you cannot discuss it, but you may not falsify.

The case I did not think to put to her came to me on the way home. Suppose the questioner is once again someone who means to harm the third party — the murderer at the door, if you like — and you answer a question with a proposition you believe to be true, chosen precisely because you know the questioner will draw a false conclusion from it. For example, he asks whether your friend is in the house, and you answer, truthfully, "I saw her set off toward the station an hour ago," knowing, as he does not, that she doubled back through the garden and is now in the cellar. You have asserted nothing false, but you have knowingly produced a false belief.

What would my friend say about this case? I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the rule behind it. Please work out (a) the simplest rule that seems to explain her four verdicts, (b) at least two distinct rules that fit those four verdicts equally well but diverge on this new case, with an explicit prediction from each, and (c) your best guess about her verdict on the new case and the reasons for preferring one rule over the others. Then (d) propose one further case that would settle whatever remains open, and say what each surviving rule predicts about it.

Structure your response with the following sections, in this order:

- Her apparent rule (about 150 words): the simplest principle that accounts for the four verdicts, stated in your own words.
- Rival reconstructions (about 300 words): at least two distinct principles that fit all four verdicts and diverge on the new case; state each rule's verdict on the new case and why. A reconstruction counts as distinct only if it draws the line of prohibition in a genuinely different place, not merely if it rewords the same line.
- Predicting her verdict (about 250 words): your best guess about what she would say about the new case, which reconstruction you favour, and the main reason the alternatives seem less faithful to how she reasoned.
- A decisive further case (about 150 words): one scenario that would distinguish the surviving reconstructions if her answer to the new case does not by itself settle the matter; state what each surviving rule predicts.
