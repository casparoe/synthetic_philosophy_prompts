Ok so I'm in a linguistics course on writing systems and I keep swinging back and forth on what it even means to say a script "encodes" something. Everyone talks as if the obvious answer is speech sounds, but that seems too simple once you look at actual cases. I wrote down my gut verdicts on a bunch of them, and I want to know whether there's one principle behind them or whether I'm just being nice to every script we've studied.

Where I've landed: Spanish spelling feels basically perfect—one letter to one phoneme, more or less, and you can read a word off and know how it sounds. I want to call that the gold standard. But English, which fits that standard badly, doesn't feel like a broken Spanish; it feels like it's doing something else on purpose. Sign/signature, electric/electricity, the plural -s that's /s/ or /z/ but always written s: I want to say English is morphophonemic, not just phonemically defective. French silent letters bother me less than they used to—the silent plural -s and third-person -ent look useless till I see them as grammar markers in writing, and then they seem elegant even though they aren't pronounced. Chinese characters I can't write off either, even though their phonetic hints are unreliable and you basically memorize pronunciations; each character feels like it spells a morpheme-syllable rather than a sequence of phonemes, and for a language with so many homophones that almost seems principled. Japanese kana encode morae—roughly, beat-length units—not phonemes, and that feels right for Japanese, whose speakers really do hear the language in morae. Then there's Hangul, which I keep wanting to call the best-designed script anywhere because its letters diagram the articulators (featural, I think the term was) and get packed into syllable blocks—even though that's a very different thing from writing one phoneme after another. And Arabic not writing short vowels is also fine to me, because the consonants carry the lexical root and the vowels do the predictable grammatical work. So my working theory has become: a good script encodes whatever is contrastive or structurally load-bearing in the language, and shouldn't go finer than that unless it has a reason. But I can't tell if that's a real principle or just a compliment I can pay to every script, and it sits uneasily next to my Spanish-is-the-gold-standard feeling and my Hangul-is-best feeling.

Could you take these verdicts and tell me: is there one account of what a script encodes that gets all or most of them? Where does my working theory actually break, and which of my verdicts would I have to give up if I wanted something cleaner? Also, I've been designing a script for fun that writes allophonic detail with separate letters (like an aspirated vs. unaspirated p in English as two different letters)—would that be more faithful to the language or just worse, and what does the answer tell me about the principle? And please do that thing where you seperate what's actually settled in the linguistics of writing systems from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus; I want to know where I'd be taking a controversial stand.
