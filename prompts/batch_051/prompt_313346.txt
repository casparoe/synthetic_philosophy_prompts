At work I run a small team, and I have a policy: I dont do premortems. You know, the exercise where before a launch you imagine the project already failed and list the reasons why. My lead keeps suggesting them, and I've started participating only in a fake way — I write down the obvious, boring risks so nobody can say I skipped it. The honest version, when I've actually done it, doesn't stay hypothetical for me: once I've pictured the launch failing in detail, my felt probability of failure goes up alot, more than the exercise can justify, and then I get cautious, cut scope, push dates, and sometimes kill things that — soberly, after the fact — were still worth shipping. So my rule is: don't rehearse the failure; keep some hope in the system and ship.

I've also got a half-observed impression that my projects since adopting the rule ship faster and don't fail more, though I haven't tracked it rigorously, and a friend says that's exactly the loophole optimism bias needs.

So my question is: is this an epistemically defensible policy, or am I just refusing to imagine bad outcomes because I dont want to know, and then filing it under 'that technique doesn't work for me'? Is there a form of the don't-rehearse-failure policy that keeps whatever motivation hope gives me without making my beliefs worse? And if I keep the policy, what would I have to measure to tell whether I'm running on hope or on evidence?
