I am trying to get clear, before I read further defenses and critiques of Hume on miracles, about what a good account of testimonial evidence for a miracle would actually have to deliver. I do not want a summary of Hume's argument, and I do not want a verdict on whether it succeeds. What I want is the specification: what must any adequate theory of the evidential force of miracle reports be able to do, which cases must it get right, which distinctions must it respect, and which demands made in the literature are really just local prejudices of one camp rather than genuine requirements. My anchor is this passage from David Hume, An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, Section X, "Of Miracles":

"All probability, then, supposes an opposition of experiments and observations, where the one side is found to overbalance the other, and to produce a degree of evidence, proportioned to the superiority. A hundred instances or experiments on one side, and fifty on another, afford a doubtful expectation of any event; though a hundred uniform experiments, with only one that is contradictory, reasonably beget a pretty strong degree of assurance. In all cases, we must balance the opposite experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the smaller number from the greater, in order to know the exact force of the superior evidence."

I want you to write the desiderata for a theory of testimonial evidence about reported miracles that treats that passage as a serious constraint rather than as an all-purpose conclusion. Organize the response in exactly these sections. First, "The fixed point": in no more than 180 words, say what conditions the passage lays down for how evidence from opposing observations must be combined, and what must be true of any theory that treats it as a starting point. Second, "Desiderata": a numbered list of nine to twelve requirements an adequate theory of miracle testimony must meet, each stated in a single sentence in bold, followed by two or three sentences giving the motivating case or puzzle that makes it a requirement, and an explicit label of non-negotiable or negotiable. Third, "Tensions and joint satisfiability": in about 400 words, say which combinations of these desiderata look hardest to satisfy together, whether any pair or subset is plausibly inconsistent, and where the pressure would fall if one were given up. Fourth, "The most exclusionary desideratum": in about 250 words, identify the single requirement that does the most to rule out popular ways of treating miracle reports, and explain what it costs to reject it.
