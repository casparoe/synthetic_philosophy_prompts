So I've been in this argument on an AI forum for months and I've noticed it isn't actually going anywhere. One camp says we're obviously drifting toward agent AI — systems you hand a broad goal to and they break it into subgoals, send emails, run code, maybe move money, and keep going until the job's done — and the only real question is how to make them safe enough. The other camp says that whole drift is the mistake, and we should deliberately build tool AI instead: systems that do one bounded thing you asked for and then stop, so they never have the kind of open-ended 'wanting' that can go sideways. Both sides agree on the basic facts about how current models work, both agree the systems we have are unreliable, and both seem to agree that a genuinely capable agent with a badly specified goal would be dangerous. Nobody moves anyway. And it gets stranger because both sides will happily agree we should use tools now; the fight is over whether treating that as the resting place is prudence or just denial about where the technology is going.

I keep suspecting the argument is secretly about something else — about what 'agent' even means (acts in the world at all? has preferences? picks subgoals?), or whether the scary future is one where the AI is to good at its goals or to sloppy to be stopped, or maybe one side is answering a question about what products people will actually pay for while the other is answering a question about what could go wrong in principle. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict: is this disagreement substantive, verbal, two different questions sharing one name, or a hidden empirical bet about what gets built first? And if you can diagnose it, what would actually count as progress — a demonstration, a definition, a real-world incident — or should I stop expecting this one to resolve?
