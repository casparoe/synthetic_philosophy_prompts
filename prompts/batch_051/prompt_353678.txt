So I've got this recurring argument with a friend and I want a careful walkthrough instead of vibes. A few years ago I submitted a patch to a fairly popular open-source routing library, the sort of thing that does fast nearest-neighbor lookups on map data and shows up in ride apps, disaster-response tools, whatever. My patch was a generic optimization to the quadtree traversal code, nothing domain-specific. It recently came out that a private surveillance company builds its geofencing product on top of this library, and several police departments use that product to monitor people on probation without telling them. My patch is in their product and probably makes it a little faster, but if you reverted it nothing would break and the tracking would proceed exactly the same.

The way I've always half-remembered complicity, from some law-and-philosophy podcast, is four moving parts: there's a wrong being done by somebody else; your action made the wrong easier, more likely, or worse; you knew your action would help; and you either intended to help or were indifferent. The odd part, to me, is that on this picture causation seems optional: I didn't cause the tracking, the police department and the company did, and my commit sits a few layers down the dependency tree. I did know, when I submitted the patch, that this library is widely used by location-analytics companies, some of which I'd seen pitch to law enforcement, but I didn't know about this company or this product. So I'm struggling with whether I'm complicit, and my friends split clean down the middle.

I want you to go through the criteria one at a time and say where I stand, and be honest about where the criteria themselves are underspecified or doing no real work. How specific does the knowledge have to be? Does it matter that my contribution is swamped by everyone else's, or that they'd proceed without me? Does continuing to maintain the library after finding out change the verdict? If the answer turns on a contested reading of one of the criteria, tell me which readings are on the table and which is the mainstream one. And please keep clear what's actually settled among people who think about complicity for a living versus what's a philosophical faction's pet view, because I don't want some one professor's line presented as if its consensus. The thing I most want out of this is whether complicity is doing real analytical work in a case like mine, or is just a word we use after the fact to mark someone's contribution as tainted.
