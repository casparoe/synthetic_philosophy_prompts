So I've been building a knowledge graph at work and I think I stumbled into a genuine infinite regress, and I've got a homemade fix I want someone to stess-test. The setup: I was treating edges as first-class objects. A triple like (Alice, knows, Bob) means there's an edge-object "knows" with a source pointer to Alice and a target pointer to Bob. But then I realized the source pointer is itself a relation between the edge-object and Alice. So if I'm being strict, I need an edge between "knows" and Alice, and then an edge between that edge and "knows," and so on forever. The edge never actually connects its endpoints; it just spawns more edges. My coworker said that's just what a pointer is and I should stop thinking about it, but it bugs me.

My fix: stop treating relations as objects at all. In code, a function isn't the same kind of thing as its arguments — you apply it, and application just happens; you don't need a third thing to glue the function to its arguments. So I've started saying a relation is an evaluator, not an entity. It's a rule that takes n terms and returns whether it holds. The connection between the relation and its terms isn't another relation; it's the act of evaluation, which is a different type of thing entirely. I've been calling this the call-site view of relations, as opposed to the edge-object view. It seems to kill the regress dead.

I'm sure actual philosophers hit this exact problem, since it feels too basic not to have a name, but I don't have the background to know what they said. Is the call-site view coherent? Is evaluation really not a relation, or did I just hide the same problem one level down — like, what connects the evaluator to the act of evaluating? And does this actually explain how a bunch of parts add up to one fact, or did I just rename the glue?
