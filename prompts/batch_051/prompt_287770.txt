Since I began helping to raise my sister's four-year-old twins, I have noticed an asymmetry in how the adults around them explain spontaneous behavior. When one of the boys unprompted splits his snack with the other, or runs to comfort a child who has fallen at the playground, the family says this is who he really is — his nature showing through, the actual child beneath the tantrums. When the same boy, again without deliberation, snatches a toy from a smaller child and hurls it over the fence, or shoves a child who reaches for his plate, the same adults reach for an external explanation: he is overtired, he is imitating a classmate, it is a phase, he did not mean it. The generous burst is read as nature; the cruel burst is read as interference with nature.

That division strikes me as a folk version of an old philosophical quarrel. Mencius is standardly read as holding that our moral tendencies are already present in us as native sprouts that need cultivation, not insertion from outside; Xunzi is standardly read as replying that the raw material we are born with is self-seeking and unruly, and that virtue is something built on top of it by ritual, teaching, and deliberate effort. I am less interested in a summary of the texts than in whether anything justifies the underlying asymmetry of judgment itself.

Work through this as a philosopher of moral psychology who also knows the Mencian and Xunzian alternatives. Think carefully, and before settling on an answer, brainstorm the strongest candidate justifications for treating our immediate attractive impulses as revelatory of our nature while treating our immediate ugly impulses as distortion or external noise, and also the strongest reasons for suspecting the asymmetry is mere flattery of the species or of the individual. Then pick or reject a principle.

Test whatever principle you propose against a hard case: two equally unpremeditated acts by the same child in one afternoon — a spontaneous act of comfort toward a stranger child who has just fallen, and, later, a spontaneous act of gleeful mockery toward that same child when he gets up and limps. If a single principle licenses calling the first act the child's real self and the second an external imposition, state that principle exactly; if it does not, say which of the two judgments should be revised.

End by saying whether you would keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up, and whether your answer should differ for young children as against adults, in whom spontaneous kindness is likewise often credited to character while spontaneous cruelty is explained away by stress or pathology.
