I've mostly come around to the view that the paradox of blackmail isn't actually a paradox. The way I'd put it: there's no deep, unified moral principle that makes pay me or I reveal wrong while leaving I reveal and you pay me, taken separately, fine. The combination is wrong (or criminal) for institutional and incentive reasons. A society that lets people sell silence about damaging truths ends up running a racket where everyone with a secret is a mark and everyone with knowledge is a potential extortionist. So the wrongness tracks the social institution, not some special wrong-making feature of the speech act itself. The appearance of a paradox is just an artifact of the law individuating acts in a way morality doesn't have to.

What I want from you: tell me what would count against this view. Not the standard objections in the abstract. I want a ranked map of defeaters. Which observations, edge cases, or arguments would be fatal, which would be serious but containable, and which are just awkward. I'm espically curious about things like: cases where the blackmailer credibly never intended to reveal, cases where the secret is already about to be exposed by a third party so the blackmailer's silence changes nothing, cases where the victim is powerful and the secret is trivial but the demand isn't, blackmail of third parties (pay me or I tell your spouse), and small-scale settings where there's no legal system around to produce the artifact. Also whether the view overgenerates by making too many conditional offers look like blackmail. What would defenders of my sort of view honestly concede counts against it, and which of their moves would they admit are ad hoc? And tell me straight whether the view is starting to look unfalsifiable because every counterexample can just be blamed on incentives. End by saying what would change your mind: which one of these should move me most, and where is this whole no-deep-paradox line most likely to fall apart.
