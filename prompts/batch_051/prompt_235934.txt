For the past year, the political philosophy club I run at school has kept returning to a question that never moves: setting feasibility aside entirely, what would make communism desirable? We agree on the record of twentieth-century communist states and we still talk past each other. One side argues that the ideal is unattractive even on its best version, because removing market competition and private control removes the setting in which ambition, distinctiveness, and self-direction can be expressed, and no amount of equality or security would compensate for that. The other side answers that this is a caricature: the ideal is not equality plus comfort but the end of economic coercion, so the first side is refusing to imagine the thing under discussion. My hypothesis is that we are not having one disagreement but two: a substantive one about which features are essential to the ideal, and a methodological one about whether an ideal may be evaluated apart from the mechanisms that would supposedly produce it. Is that the right diagnosis? What kind of disagreement is this really — verbal, factual, methodological, or a clash of bedrock intuitions — and what would count as actual progress?
