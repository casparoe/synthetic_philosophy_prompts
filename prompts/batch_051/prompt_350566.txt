Take Feinberg's idea that a child has a right to an open future—a bundle of future autonomous options held in trust by caregivers, who shouldn't irrevocably foreclose options the child might later want to exercise—and use it to analyze the Ashley treatment at Seattle Children's Hospital. The facts as I know them: in 2004 a six-year-old with profound static encephalopathy, whose development was expected to plateau at the level of an infant, was given high-dose estrogen to cap her growth, plus a hysterectomy and removal of her breast buds. Her parents and doctors argued this kept her small enough to be cared for at home and spared her the discomfort of breasts and menstruation; critics called it mutilation that served the caregivers more than the child. The question isn't whether it was legal. I want the mapping: what the open-future framework says about each intervention, what it helps you see, and where it breaks down or reveals that the framework secretly assumes a child who will eventually grow into a chooser. Then tell me whether the case cuts for or against the proposal that the state should license parents, or whether it shows that both sides of the licensing debate are leaning on a picture of a normal child's future that Ashley just doesn't fit.
