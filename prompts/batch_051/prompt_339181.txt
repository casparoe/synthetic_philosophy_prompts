After a long argument with a friend about whether evolution is a "stupid optimizer," I came away defending a view I want pressure-tested. I call it the moving-threshold view. Its core claim is that natural selection is not an optimizer at all, because an optimizer needs an objective function that stays fixed across iterations, while what persists across generations is only a pass/fail threshold supplied anew by the current environment. Fitness, on this view, is not a quantity selection maximizes; it is a retrospective summary of how far above that moving threshold a lineage happened to sit. Cumulative adaptation is the residue left by a filter whose bar is ratcheted upward, not a gradient's approach to a pre-existing optimum. Example: antibiotic resistance looks like a targeted climb toward resistance, but the population is not estimating any direction; cells below the drug's threshold fail to reproduce and the distribution shifts as a side effect. Withdraw the drug and the apparent objective reverses, not because a gradient reversed but because the threshold moved. I know this overlaps with familiar population-genetic results about selection increasing mean fitness under simplifying assumptions, and with the idea that selection is just covariance between fitness and trait. But I want to know whether the stronger claim can be sustained: the analogy between natural selection and gradient descent is not merely imperfect but categorically wrong, because only one of them has a fixed loss. Give me a candid assessment: is this view coherent, has it already been defended under a name I should know, what does it get right, and where does it first break down?
