Recently I have been thinking about a double standard in the way conceivability is used in modal reasoning, and I would like a careful assessment of whether it can be defended. The positive inference — from conceivability to possibility — is a familiar and respected form of argument. If one can conceive of a mind existing without any body, or of a creature indistinguishable from a human being in every physical respect yet entirely lacking consciousness, many philosophers will grant, at least provisionally, that such things are possible. The reverse inference, by contrast, is treated as dramatically weaker or as no evidence at all. The fact that a scenario cannot be conceived is taken to show little about whether it is possible: for a long time competent inquirers could not conceive of a geometry in which the parallel postulate fails, and we ourselves may be unable to conceive of how a particular scientific discovery was made, but no one thinks such inconceivability rules those things out.

The result is an asymmetric evidential policy: conceivability points toward possibility, while inconceivability is not allowed to point toward impossibility. I would like you to examine whether this asymmetry is principled or merely convenient. Please give the strongest justification you can find for trusting the positive inference more than the negative one, and then test that justification against cases in which the positive inference seems to misfire — for example, cases in which a person finds something conceivable only through ignorance of a relevant fact, or in which a scenario is called conceivable while hiding an unnoticed contradiction.

Does the asymmetry survive such tests, or should we either take inconceivability more seriously as a guide to impossibility, or abandon the positive inference's evidential force as well? Please write the response in British English.
