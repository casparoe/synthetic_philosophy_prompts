i keep coming back to the part in Mill's Autobiography, chapter V, "A Crisis in My Mental History," where he's trying to explain what caused the depression he fell into in his early twenties. He says something like the habit of analysis has a way of wearing away the feelings, because it keeps pulling things apart and nothing new grows in the place of what it destroys. And he also says that when he asked himself directly whether he was happy, the very asking made him unhappy. I don't have the book with me, so this is paraphrase, not word-for-word.

The thing I want help understanding is what he actually thinks the mechanism is. Is he blaming self-examination itself, or is he blaming a particular style of self-examination that he'd been trained into? His solution seems to have been to stop treating happiness as a direct object of introspection and to let himself be affected by poetry and nature, which sounds like he didn't give up on examining himself—he just changed what he attended to. But then why does he describe the problem as analytic habit rather than as a misplaced target? I'm trying to figure out whether his account supports the idea that rumination is bad for you in general, or whether it's only bad when it takes the wrong form, and what that wrong form would be exactly.
