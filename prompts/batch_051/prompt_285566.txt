For most of my working life I was a physical chemist, and since retiring I have been reading experimental philosophy with something like the patience I once gave to instrumental data. The Knobe effect has struck me as more relevant to real industrial misconduct than most textbook treatments suggest, and I would like a careful case analysis rather than a survey.

Take the Volkswagen diesel emissions scandal. The salient facts as I understand them: Volkswagen installed engine-control software in certain diesel vehicles that could detect when the car was on an emissions test cycle; during testing the emissions controls operated fully, while in ordinary driving they were dialed back, so on-road nitrogen oxide emissions were many times the legal limit. Roughly eleven million vehicles were affected. The company's first public response blamed a small group of engineers below senior management, then the account widened; the chief executive resigned, and in the United States Volkswagen eventually pleaded guilty to criminal charges. I am not asking for additional facts or for citations; I want the facts interpreted.

Analyze this episode through the lens of the Knobe effect from experimental philosophy. By the Knobe effect I mean the asymmetry found in the chairman vignettes: people are substantially more willing to say that a foreseen side effect was brought about intentionally when the side effect is morally bad than when it is morally good, even when the decision-maker's stated attitude — caring only about the main objective and being indifferent to the side effect — is held fixed across the two cases. Give a disciplined mapping. Who plays the chairman: the individual engineers who wrote and approved the defeat-device code, the managers who assigned emissions and fuel-economy targets, the chief executive, or Volkswagen as a corporate agent? What is the main objective and what is the side effect, and how does that distinction shift across roles — emissions cheating as the primary aim of some work-streams versus a side effect of performance, cost, and compliance pressure in others? Does the Knobe asymmetry illuminate why the public, regulators, and courts so readily treated the environmental harm as intentional and culpable despite Volkswagen's suggestion that it was collateral to commercial and engineering goals rather than the point of the scheme? Then be candid about where the model breaks. The chairman vignettes isolate a single decision-maker, a clear moral badness, and a stipulated state of mind; Dieselgate involves decisions distributed across years and teams, partially contradictory goals, detailed automotive-engineering considerations, and executives with strong legal incentives to describe their own intentions in a favorable way. Say which features of the episode the Knobe framework explains well, which it misdescribes or cannot reach, and whether you read the case as a real-world confirmation of the asymmetry, an anomaly for it, or a phenomenon experimental philosophy was never designed to handle.
