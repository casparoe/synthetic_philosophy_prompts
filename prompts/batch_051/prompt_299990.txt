After three decades in experimental geochemistry, I retired and began reading moral philosophy, and I have been struck by how easily debates about belief assume that epistemology is a matter of good method rather than of obligation to other people. The position I now want to defend is roughly this: whenever someone acts on, asserts, or otherwise puts a belief into the world in a way that could bear on others, they owe those others a standing moral duty to doubt it. By doubt I do not mean suspending judgment, and certainly not Descartes's project of demolishing everything; I mean a specific, ongoing obligation to keep the question of what would count against the belief genuinely open, and to search for such counterevidence with effort scaled not to how confident the believer happens to be, but to the vulnerability of the people who would be harmed if the belief were false. I treat this as a moral duty on a par with honesty, not merely as an epistemic ideal.

Could you tell me what would count against that position? I am not asking only for the familiar objections — that belief is rarely under direct voluntary control, that the duty may be self-undermining when applied to itself, that it threatens to collapse into a demand to suspend judgment about everything. I want a fuller account of the view's vulnerability. Which cases would a committed defender have to concede create real trouble: beliefs we owe to friends against the evidence, the way demands for evidence can constitute or compound testimonial injustice, the place of trust in collective inquiry and in close relationships, the thought that some confidence is an intellectual virtue rather than a vice, faith-shaped commitments, or findings about how inquiry actually works from the social sciences? For each, I want to know whether it is fatal, merely awkward, or absorbable by a careful but non-ad-hoc qualification, and which of these a defender of the duty-to-doubt should themselves treat as weighing against the view rather than dismiss.

Finally, I would value an honest appraisal of whether the principle, as I have stated it, makes predictions that could conceivably go wrong, or whether it is in practice shielded from refutation by its own flexibility. If it is shielded, is that a mark against it, or is a moral principle of this kind legitimately harder to dislodge than a scientific hypothesis? Please make the points self-contained; I will not be able to chase references.
