I retired from experimental physics a few years ago, and I have lately been reading the philosophical literature on self-locating belief and cosmology. There is an argument in that neighborhood that I have now written out more strictly than I usually see it stated, and I find its conclusion unacceptable even though I cannot see which step to surrender. I would like a diagnosis.

The argument runs this way. (1) On the cosmological picture I find best supported, the universe eventually settles into a finite but extremely long-lived de Sitter-like phase, in which thermal fluctuations generate observers vastly more often than ordinary astrophysical and biological processes ever did. Among them are Boltzmann brains: momentary configurations that realize the very neural state of an ordinary observer at an instant, together with apparent memories of a past that never happened; for any particular experience an ordinary observer can have, fluctuation-produced instantiations of that experience vastly outnumber ordinary instantiations. (2) I should apportion my credence about what kind of observer-moment I currently am by treating myself as a random sample from the class of all observer-moments that share my current total evidence. (3) My current total evidence is nothing over and above the intrinsic character of my present experience: its phenomenal content, its apparent memories, and the thoughts I am now thinking. (4) By (3), a Boltzmann brain that instantiates this very experience and an ordinary observer who instantiates it have exactly the same total evidence; by (1), Boltzmann brains dominate that evidential class. (5) Therefore, by (2) and (4), I ought to be nearly certain that I am a Boltzmann brain. But then my apparent memories — including the memories of the astronomical observations and thermodynamic arguments that make (1) credible in the first place — are delusive, and I have no entitlement to the premise on which the whole chain rests. So the argument proves too much: it directs me to accept a conclusion whose acceptance destroys its own evidential basis.

I should very much like to keep (1), since it is simply the physics of a universe that has reached equilibrium, and I am prepared to pay a price elsewhere. What I need to know is exactly which premise or inferential step fails, and what I have to give up in exchange. If the sampling principle in (2) is the weak point, what restriction on it would rule out the Boltzmann brains without being contrived only for that purpose? If the difficulty lies elsewhere, what is it, and why did it look innocuous?
