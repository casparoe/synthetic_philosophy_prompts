I keep finding myself with strong verdicts about gene drives and de-extinction, but I can't articulate the priniciple tying them together. Here are my verdicts; tell me what, if anything, they add up to.

1. Releasing self-limiting GM mosquitoes in a city to suppress dengue (the construct doesn't persist in the wild; the population just rebounds when you stop releasing). I'm fine with it. Child mortality is at stake and the intervention unwinds itself. I actually think this one is under-used.

2. A self-sustaining suppression drive meant to eradicate the main African malaria mosquitoes across sub-Saharan Africa. I flinch. Enormous upside, but it's continent-wide, it's permanent, we can't fully predict the ecosystem effects, and there's no off switch once it's out. I'd want it phased and locally consented at minimum, and maybe I'd still say no if it can't be spatially contained.

3. A suppression drive to wipe out rats on an island to save ground-nesting seabirds. Yes, obviously. The rats are there because people put them there; the birds were there first; the island bounds the whole thing naturally. I don't even mind that it's deliberate local extinction.

4. A daisy-chain drive—one engineered to lose its drive machinery after a fixed number of generations, so it burns itself out after doing a local job—deployed in a single valley against a disease vector. Yes. The built-in expiration is what makes it better than 2.

5. A threshold-dependent drive (one that fizzles if you release below a threshold) for the same valley. Also yes, for basically the same reason, and I honestly couldn't say whether I prefer it to 4.

6. De-extincting the Pyrenean ibex—human-caused extinction, habitat still basically intact—by cloning, if it could be made to survive. Yes. That's repair, like putting back a painting you knocked off a wall.

7. Reconstructing the passenger pigeon genome and hatching birds. No. Extinct for a century, but the species was its flocking behavior and the eastern forests aren't what they were. You'd get a pigeon, not a passenger pigeon. I'd fund the exhibit; I'd object to calling it de-extinction.

8. Releasing "proxy" mammoths into Siberia to restore mammoth steppe and slow permafrost thaw. No. The ecosystem hasn't been that way for thousands of years; the carbon claims strike me as shaky; this is ecological authorship, not repair, with elephants inside it.

9. A drive whose explicit aim is to drive one native mosquito species extinct, when the disease in question is already reasonably controlled by drugs and nets. I'm uneasy in a way I wasn't in 3, even though 3 is also deliberate extinction. The mosquito is native; the rats weren't. But I can't yet say whether "native" should be doing that much work.

10. A replacement drive that doesn't kill the vector but makes it refractory to a pathogen, with a later reversal drive available as a crude undo. Tentatively yes: the species persists and the intervention is partly revisable, though the "undo" is itself another release.

What principle, if any, captures all of this? I can feel a view behind it—prefer reversible to irreversible, and prefer repair of recent human damage to new ecological authorship—but that view probably can't say yes to 3 and no to 9, or distinguish 4 from 2 in a principled way. Please state the best principle you can, precisely enough that I can run new cases through it, say which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon and why, and give me one further case you think the resulting view gets right. End by saying how confident you are in that principle and which single step or verdict you're least sure about.
