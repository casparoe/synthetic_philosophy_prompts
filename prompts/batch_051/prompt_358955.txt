Someone showed me the standard twin-prime heuristic and now I'm suspicious of the whole "primes are random" business. Walk me through this argument, because every step seems fine and the conclusion can't be right:

(1) The usual move is to treat the event "n is prime" as having probability 1/log n, and to treat "n is prime" and "n+2 is prime" as independent — except you multiply by local correction factors at each prime p to account for the correlations that come from divisibility by p. Those corrections are where the twin-prime constant comes from, and the result is the well-known guess that the number of twin primes up to x is about C·x/(log x)².

(2) Now do the same thing for adjacent primes, n and n+1. If primality of n and of n+1 behaved independently, you'd likewise expect about x/(log x)² pairs of consecutive primes up to x.

(3) But there's exactly one such pair, (2,3), since one of any two consecutive integers is even.

(4) A defender of the heuristic says: not a problem, the local correction handles that. The pattern {0,1} occupies both residue classes mod 2, so the correction factor at p = 2 is 0, and the corrected heuristic predicts essentially no such pairs. Fine, no disaster.

(5) But this is exactly what bothers me. In the n+1 case we already know the answer (finitely many, in fact one), and the correction comes out 0. In the n+2 case we don't know the answer, and the correction comes out positive. If the correction factor is allowed to cancel the prediction when the answer is known to be finite and to inflate it when we believe there are infinitely many, then "independence plus corrections" never actually constrains anything. It's just notation for what we already expect.

(6) So either the twin-prime heuristic is worthless, or the correction procedure has to be principled in a way that isn't just reverse-engineering the known answer. But is it? We compute the correction from the residues the pattern hits mod p — sure — but we choose to compute exactly that because we know divisibility by small primes correlates. Why is that the principled place to stop, rather than one more ad hoc fix?

Where does the argument go wrong? I want to keep (1), since the twin heuristic has a decent track record, but if rejecting (5) or (6) is the price, tell me what it costs. In particular: is there any real reason to think the local corrections are all the corrections, or is that itself just a conjecture wearing the clothes of a method? And please separate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this — don't present a contested view as the consensus.
