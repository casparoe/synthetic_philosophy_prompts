I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm three drafts into a book where the hinge scene is a family dinner: the youngest sibling, Ana, asks her older brother to forgive their father for something he did thirty years ago, and the whole table splits on whether that's even a coherant thing to ask—whether forgiveness is something you can owe someone, whether it can be given when the father hasn't asked for it and won't, whether it requires the wrong to be over and not repeated. The problem is every time I write these arguments I can feel all the characters sliding toward my own mush: everyone sort of agrees forgiveness is good but not always, and the scene dies on the page. I want to fix the characters to actual, distinct views and then test them against concrete cases.

So I'd like a decision tree (or a sorting quiz, whichever you think will do the work better) for the positions on whether forgiveness can be obligatory and/or unconditional. The audience is just me, a novelist with no formal philosophy, so the diagnostic questions need to be answerable from inside a character's head without knowing the jargon—concrete cases, not "do you hold a Butlerian view?". Aim for about eight questions with two or three options each, and a tree that sorts into at least five but no more than eight leaves. Include at least these three views I know I'm working with, as leaves or near them: one character thinks forgiving without repentance is just self-abasement; another thinks forgiveness is a gift and making it a duty would destroy the thing that makes it forgiveness; a third thinks there are cases where you genuinely ought to forgive even if the person will never ask. Don't force those three to be the only leaves—build the logical space from the questions first and place the real positions in it, flagging unoccupied combinations. One distinction I especially need drawn cleanly: forgiveness versus excusing, condoning, and reconciling, because my characters use them interchangably and I need to know where the line actually falls.

Format the response in four sections, in this order: "The diagnostic questions" (around 800 words, each question with the concrete case and a sentence on which two positions it's meant to pull apart); "The tree" (around 900 words, each branch labeled with the claim that splits it, each leaf labeled with the position's name or "unnamed," plus a plain-language gloss if the name is technical and one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment); "Unoccupied combinations" (around 300 words on which answer patterns nobody defends and whether that's because they're incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored); and "Where the tree fails" (around 250 words on what it can't separate or gets wrong at the margins).
