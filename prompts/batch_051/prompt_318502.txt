Last night my roommate and I argued for like two hours about Locke's labour theory of property. He kept attacking the mixing-labour idea—the old tomato-juice-in-the-ocean example, though neither of us could remember who's example it was—and after he went to bed I started turning over a parallel I can't shake.

Roman law had this doctrine called specificatio. Roughly: if you take someone else's materials and make a new thing out of them, ownership depends on whether the new thing can be reduced back to the old materials without too much trouble. Turn someone's grapes into wine and it can't be reduced, so the wine is yours; make a gold cup out of someone's gold and it can be melted back down, so the cup stays theirs. The Justinianic middle-way compromise said the maker owns only when the new thing is irreducible, and good faith matters for compensation, not for who owns it.

Dosen't that look like basically the same structure as Locke? Labour plus pre-existing matter creates new property, with some kind of protection for the prior owner. In Locke the material starts out unowned and the constraint is the proviso (leave enough and as good for others); in specificatio the material starts out owned by someone else and the constraint is reducibility plus compensation. The parallel would even predict a Lockean version of the reducibility test: maybe you own a thing only when your labour can't be disentangled from it, and if your labour is reversible you haven't really appropriated it. Is that prediction true, or does it give away exactly where the analogy breaks? I can't tell if I've found something deep or just two stories that both happen to use the word labour.

Say whether this is a real structural match, a loose analogy, or a category mistake, and if it is not deep, point to at least one thing the parallel would predict that is actually false.
