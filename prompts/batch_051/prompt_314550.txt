I'm working through Augustine's *On Grace and Free Will* for a seminar paper on how the early Augustine compares to the later anti-Pelagian Augustine, and this passage keeps tripping me up:

"Now He has revealed to us, through His Holy Scriptures, that there is in a man a free choice of will. But how He has revealed this I do not recount in human language, but in divine. There is, to begin with, the fact that God's precepts themselves would be of no use to a man unless he had free choice of will, so that by performing them he might obtain the promised rewards."

So, he seems to be saying the very existence of divine commands proves the will can obey them, otherwise the commands would be pointless. But I thought the whole point of his later position was that the will is bound by sin and can only obey if grace first operates on it. If that's true, aren't the precepts "of no use" to the unregenerate person anyway, since he can't perform them without grace? Am I just missing his definition of "free choice" here, or is there a real tension with, say, *On Rebuke and Grace*? Treat me like someone who knows the broad outlines of Augustine's predestinarianism but hasn't read these texts side by side. Walk me through what he's actually arguing in this passage, what work "free choice" is doing, and whether the later Augustine would have to qualify this argument. Don't flatten the tension too fast—I want to see if it's only verbal or if something substantive changes.
