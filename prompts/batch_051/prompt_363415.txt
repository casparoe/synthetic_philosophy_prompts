I'm a PhD student in philosophy of probability and I'm trying to reverse-engineer my advisor's views on Bayesian convergence before I write a chapter that leans on her unpublished position. Over the years she's run these little vignettes by the reading group about whether two perfectly rational agents with different priors will end up agreeing once their shared evidence grows — ie whether the priors 'wash out.' Her verdicts feel consistent but I can't quite state the rule, and I don't want to put words in her mouth. You're an expert on the washing out of priors and the convergence of subjective probabilities; think carefully, and start by brainstorming the candidate rules and any further cases that would tell them apart before you commit.

Here are the vignettes I remember.

(1) Two agents observe flips of a bent coin. They agree the bias is one of three values: 0.4, 0.5, or 0.7 heads. Their priors over these three are very different, but all three get positive probability from both. After 2,000 flips, 1,380 come up heads, and both agents are effectively certain its the 0.7 coin. Her verdict: yes, the priors washed out.

(2) Same three hypotheses, except one agent has a physical reason to assign zero prior to the 0.7 coin from the start. The coin really is the 0.7 one. After the same data, the unrestricted agent converges on 0.7, but the restricted agent just shifts all her mass to the 0.5 alternative and never reaches the truth. Her verdict: no, the prior didn't wash out, because the true hypothesis was ruled out from the start.

(3) Two agents estimate a coin's bias over the whole continuum from 0 to 1. Both have positive density everywhere but with very different shapes. After a long sequence of flips, both posteriors pile up tightly around the observed frequency. Her verdict: yes, the priors washed out, even though there's a continuum of hypotheses rather than a finite list.

New case for you to predict: two agents both consider only two rival hypotheses, A and B, and both give positive prior to each. It turns out A and B are observationally equivalent — they assign exactly the same probabilities to every possible sequence of observations, so no amount of future data can ever discriminate between them. The true hypothesis is A. One agent starts with 80% on A and 20% on B; the other starts with 20% on A and 80% on B. After as much shared data as you like, do their priors wash out — that is, do they converge on A?

Given her earlier verdicts, how would my advisor most likely rule on the new case if she wants to be consistent with herself? Which earlier vignette is the closest analog, and what principle would she be committing to under each possible answer? If her old verdicts genuinely underdetermine her answer, say what further case would cleanly separate the candidate rules. Give me her likely verdict, the reasoning behind it, and the clearest statement of the rule she seems to have been applying all along.
