I retired from a career in organic chemistry a few years ago, and have since been working through philosophy of language on my own. Over the same period I have kept up a correspondence with a retired lexicographer, who spent her career editing a large historical dictionary. Each month I send her a small batch of sentences built around a single word, and she rules on whether the word is being used in one sense or in two, giving the ground of her ruling in a sentence or two. She refuses to consult usage notes or corpora while answering my letters; she treats the question as one of principle. Her record so far is as follows.

1. Book. 'The book weighs five pounds' and 'The book is bewildering.' Two senses of one word: the physical object and the text it carries. Not homonyms, because the object-to-content relation is regular and transparent.

2. Chicken. 'The chicken escaped the coop' and 'The chicken was overcooked.' Two senses: the bird and its flesh as food. She added that the same animal-to-meat pattern covers duck, rabbit, and lamb, which suggests she treats it as a living pattern rather than an accident of the vocabulary.

3. Kettle. 'The kettle is boiling.' One sense only. The word continues to name the vessel; the water is supplied by context, and there is no kettle-as-water sense.

4. Ham sandwich. 'The ham sandwich at table six wants his check.' One sense only. The phrase is a local, on-the-spot metonymy for a customer; no speaker stores a customer-sense of 'ham sandwich.'

5. Balzac. 'Balzac is on the top shelf' and 'Balzac is difficult.' Two senses: the man and the works. The author-to-work pattern is productive, she says, and the two are not homonyms.

6. Newspaper. 'The newspaper is soaked,' 'The newspaper is dull,' and 'The newspaper fired three editors.' Three senses: the printed copy, the content it carries, and the organization that produces both. Systematically related but distinct.

7. Door. 'We painted the door' and 'We walked through the door.' One sense with two facets. The panel and the opening are not distinct concepts but aspects of a single door-concept, so she declines to split them.

8. Port. 'The port was excellent' and 'The ship entered port.' Two different words, not two senses of one word. The resemblance is historical accident, and no living pattern links the wine to the harbor.

The case I mean to send her next is this: 'The children watched the gazelle at the waterhole' alongside 'After the hunt, they roasted the gazelle over the fire, and the gazelle was excellent.' In the second sentence the word seems to be used for the meat, exactly along the animal-to-meat pattern she recognized in chicken, duck, rabbit, and lamb. But, unlike chicken, I do not think 'gazelle'-as-meat is a stored use in the English I ordinarily speak or read; it feels more like the boiling kettle, something the context supplies rather than something the word itself carries.

If she is to remain consistent with her previous eight rulings, what should I expect her to say about the gazelle case, and on what principle? In particular: (a) Which earlier ruling is the closest analog, and which earlier ruling, if any, points the other way? (b) Is she best described as holding an enumerative principle — a word has exactly those senses that the community stores and reproduces — a generative principle — a word has a base sense plus productive patterns such as animal-to-meat and author-to-works that yield further senses whether or not the community has separately stored them — a single-core principle — all apparent multiplicity is contextual modulation of one underspecified sense — or something else? (c) If more than one principle can rationalize the record, what does each live principle predict for the gazelle case, which prediction do you favor, and why? (d) What single further case should I send her after this one that would most cleanly settle which principle she actually holds, and what would each of her possible rulings indicate?
