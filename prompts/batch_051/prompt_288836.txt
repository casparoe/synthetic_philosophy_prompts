I'm putting together a lecture on whether "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" has been flattened by its standard classroom use, and I want help working through a specific comparison. Le Guin's story sets up a utopian city whose entire happiness depends on one child being kept in abject misery in a cellar; the citizens learn of this when they come of age, most accept it as the necessary price, and a few silently walk away. The story is usually read as a parable against the utilitarian willingness to sacrifice one for the many. But I keep thinking it might be the mirror image of Nozick's utility monster: the imagined being whose utility from consuming each unit of a resource so dwarfs everyone else's that a consistent utilitarian would have to keep feeding it at the expense of all others. In Omelas, the aggregate happiness of the many occupies the place the monster's utility occupies, and the one is sacrificed to it; in Nozick, the one's outsized utility occupies that place and the many are sacrificed to it. I want to know whether reading the city of Omelas itself as a collective utility monster is faithful to both the story and the thought experiment, or whether the inversion papers over a difference that matters.

Give me a careful reading that stays honest in both directions. Where does the utility-monster lens genuinely illuminate what Le Guin is doing, and where does it distort either the story or Nozick's argument? What would a fair critic have to concede about the analogy before being entitled to keep it?
