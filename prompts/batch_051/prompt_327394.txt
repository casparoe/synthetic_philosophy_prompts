Longtime rationalist-blog reader here. At a party last weekend I ended up refereeing (badly) an argument between two of my friends and I want a proper adjudication, because I kept going back and forth and my half-remembered decision theory is too rusty to settle it. It started when Dan said he was "about 80% sure" he'd go to the gym after work tomorrow. Maya said that's not a real probability, and it escalated as follows.

(1) Maya: A probability has to be the kind of thing that can be wrong. If Dan says 80% and then doesn't go, he hasn't mispredicted anything; he just decided otherwise. His "80%" is a feeling, like being tempted, not a forecast. You can only have probabilities about matters of fact, and your own future choice isn't a fact to be discovered, it's a fact you make.

(2) Dan: But people forecast their own behavior accurately all the time. I know I'm way more likely to hit snooze than get up with the first alarm; I have base rates on myself. "80%" just summarizes my track record in this exact kind of situation, the same way I'd predict a friend. If I couldn't do that, self-control and precommitment wouldn't make sense either, since both depend on predicting my future self.

(3) Maya: Predicting someone else leaves the prediction outside the system, but predicting yourself puts the prediction inside the very choice. Once Dan assigns 80%, tomorrow's decision isn't "go or don't go" anymore; it's "go, or skip and thereby make my earlier number wrong, or go partly to vindicate the number." The forecast changes what it forecasts. That's the sense in which deliberation crowds out prediction: to deliberate between two options you have to treat them as open, which means you can't simultaneously be settled on probabilities for them.

(4) Dan: That's just reflexivity, not incoherence. Polls move voters and market prices move markets; self-referential forecasts are hard but still well-defined. There's a real question: what would a well-informed outside forecaster say about me? I've sometimes written down a prediction about my own behavior and then acted so as to make it self-fulfilling, which was useful. Treating the number as just a confused intention would throw that away.

(5) Maya: The outside-forecaster move doesn't work, because an outside forecaster predicts Dan-before-seeing-the-prediction, not Dan-after-seeing-it. There's no fixed object being predicted. And if Dan precommits to fulfilling the number, then the "probability" is just his intention wearing a costume. What would it even mean for 80% to be right and 60% wrong? If no possible observation could prove the number wrong, it isn't a probability.

Please adjudicate move by move. Give me a table with three columns: Move, Rating, Justification. In Move, put the move number and speaker (e.g., "1 — Maya"). In Rating, use exactly one of: clearly mistaken, mostly mistaken, mixed, mostly right, clearly right. In Justification, give one or two sentences. After the table, add a two-sentence bottom line saying who is more right overall and what the most important thing the other person got right is.
