A colleague from the computer science department and I have been arguing about whether theoretical results in machine learning have anything to contribute to the philosophical problem of induction. He is convinced they do, and he recently sent me a paragraph summarising what he takes to be the decisive lesson of the no-free-lunch theorems. As best I can render it, his summary runs:

'The no-free-lunch results settle the problem of induction. The theorems show that if you average over every possible sequence of observations a learner might face, no extrapolation rule does better than any other: a rule that expects the future to resemble the past and a rule that expects it to reverse have exactly the same expected success, because any finite run of data is compatible with infinitely many continuations and no algorithm can favour one continuation over another without smuggling in assumptions. So it has been mathematically proven that induction has no rational basis, and Hume's original point is now a theorem rather than a mere worry. The spectacular success of science, and of present-day machine learning, is just an artefact of the particular, friendly environments we happen to inhabit; across the space of all possible worlds, every learner is as good as random guessing.'

I am inclined to think he is overstating something, since I cannot see how a theorem about averages over all possible functions could by itself tell us that induction is irrational in this world, with its stable regularities and background knowledge. But I do not know the material well enough to say exactly where the slide occurs. Could you go through it and tell me which parts a careful philosopher of induction — and a careful learning theorist — would accept, which parts are overstatements or conflations, and whether the conclusion about induction's rationality actually follows from anything true in the summary? In particular, I would like to know whether it is right to present the no-free-lunch theorems as results about induction at all, and whether 'no better than random across all possible worlds' is a fair way to put what they show.
