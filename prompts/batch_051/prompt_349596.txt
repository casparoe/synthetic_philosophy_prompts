I am a first-year graduate fellow at a law and technology center, and I have been asked to help draft a position paper on whether advanced AI systems should have legal personality of some kind. Most of the people in my group keep arguing either from moral status — can the system suffer? — or from liability — who pays when the system harms? — but reading Blackstone has pushed me toward a different and, I think, less exciting thought. In Book I, Chapter 18 of his Commentaries on the Laws of England, after explaining that corporations exist so that particular rights may be kept on foot and continued, Blackstone writes:

"all the individual members that have existed from the foundation to the present time, or that shall ever hereafter exist, are but one person in law, a person that never dies: in like manner as the river Thames is still the same river, though the parts which compose it are changing every instant."

What I have started calling — perhaps infelicitously — "personhood as continuity grant" is the view that legal personality, at least in the corporate tradition, has never primarily been about treating an entity as a morally responsible individual. It is a device the law uses to guarantee that the same legal subject remains standing while its contents change. The corporation is a way of solving an accounting problem across time, not a claim about an inner life. I think this explains why the AI personhood debate keeps misfiring: when people attack the idea by saying that a language model is not a person, they assume the paradigm is a human individual, while the more natural paradigm is the college, the king, or the parson — Blackstone's corporations sole and aggregate. On my view, the strongest case for giving some AI systems a limited juridical personality is not that they deserve rights but that we already need a single continuing entity to hold responsibilities, permissions, audit records, and accumulated constraints across updates, retirements, and redeployments. My theory's central claim is that, for an AI system, legal personhood would be justified exactly when the law is better able to manage change over time by positing one unchanging legal subject than by naming the developers, users, and owners afresh each time. That seems to yield a test: ask whether the system is being run like a river, with a definite continuing channel despite changing parts, or like a succession of separate ponds with different owners. But I worry I have merely rediscovered the corporate-person analogy and added a temporal gloss, or that the whole distinction collapses when you ask which updates preserve the same AI and which create a new one — something the law of corporations seems to settle by the brute fact of incorporation rather than by any real identity across time. I would like an honest assessment. Is this a genuine concept or just a metaphor? Has it already been worked out under another name in the legal-personality literature? And where does it break: what would it wrongly count as the same legal person, or wrongly count as needing one?
