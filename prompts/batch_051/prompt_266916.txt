There's this old puzzle in philosophy of law: if a legal system has a rule that says how rules get amended, can that rule be used to amend itself? The standard argument says no, or at least that doing so is fishy: no rule can be the source of its own validity, so an amendment rule that amended itself would be circular—it would have to validate itself before it could validate its own amendment, or something like that. The worry is that if you allow it, the whole system becomes bootstrappy: the amendment clause could make itself unamendable, or repeal itself, or rewrite the quorum rule that made its own passage possible. Now take a DAO, where the governance rules are just smart-contract code. When a proposal passes, the contract literally rewrites itself, including, in principle, the function that does the rewriting and the clause that says how many votes count as passage. Suppose it does this cleanly, no bugs. Does the old argument survive? Is a contract rewriting its own amendment clause a working counterexample to the circularity worry, or is it just a physical event that doesn't touch the validity question? I care about the argument, not the tech. Which premise of the standard argument is actually under pressure here, and is there a repaired version that still captures the original worry?
