An older mentor of mine died last year, and I've been asked to write a long remeberance for our community newsletter and maybe give a short memorial talk about him. He genuinely changed my life: he taught me how to think through problems, how to take a setback without falling apart, and half of my own style as a mentor now is just me imitating him. But he also had a serious mean streak. Later in life he got mixed up in some ugly business dealings and screwed over a couple of people I know personally. He could be casually cruel to his own family. Nothing criminal, but genuinely bad.

My plan was to write about the good parts—the turning points, the lessons, the version of him that made me who I am. A friend who read the draft told me I was whitewashing him, and said if people only remember the sanitized version they'll end up imitating the whole package without knowing what came with it. Another friend said the opposite: a remembrance isn't history, it's job is to pass on what was worth imitating, and dragging in every failure just makes the lesson muddier. The uncomfortable thing is that the same bluntness that made him a great mentor is what later made him cruel, so I can't neatly separate the lesson from the flaw.

Who's right, and is there something inherently dishonest about turning a life into a lesson even when the lesson is true and useful?
