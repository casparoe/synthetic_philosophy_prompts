Political theorists sometimes assess whether a government is democratically legitimate by checking it against five criteria. (a) Popular authorization: the power at the top can be traced, through a chain the public can inspect, to an election or some comparable act of collective choice. (b) Rule-governed succession: each transfer of power followed pre-existing public rules rather than being improvised in a crisis. (c) Recognition: those subject to the government generally treat its commands as binding, rather than obeying only because disobedience is too costly. (d) Accountability: those who govern remain answerable to the governed and can be removed through regular procedures. (e) Capacity: the government can preserve public order and keep the basic machinery of the state functioning.

Now consider this case. The Republic of Asteria has a continuity-of-government statute. Because the constitution requires the president, vice president, and almost all legislators to gather for the annual opening of the legislature, the statute provides for a constitutional stand-in: each year the president secretly selects one cabinet secretary, who is taken to an undisclosed command post during the ceremony. The statute authorizes this person to exercise the powers of the presidency for any period in which, in the stand-in's judgment, the constitutional successors are unable to discharge the duties of the office.

During this year's opening session, a coordinated attack destroys the assembly hall. The president and vice president are killed. About two-fifths of the legislators survive, but for several days they are dispersed, in hiding, and unreachable. The stand-in receives reliable reports of the two deaths but conflicting reports about the legislature's condition; after three days she concludes that the constitutional successors cannot act. She takes the oath before a military notary and announces that she is assuming the powers of the presidency. Her name has never appeared on a ballot, and it was kept secret until her announcement. Over the next month, her decrees restore electrical power, halt a run on the banks, and deploy troops to stop looting. She also postpones the emergency election required by the constitution, arguing that the state needs a single executive and that parties cannot campaign safely. When surviving legislators reconvene after two weeks in an undamaged provincial chamber and ask her to transfer power to an interim speaker, she refuses. The chamber votes to ratify her initial decrees but not her continuation in office. Opinion in the country is split: many people accept her orders as necessary, while others call her a usurper and comply only because soldiers are deployed.

At the one-month mark, does the stand-in's rule qualify as a democratically legitimate government of Asteria under the checklist? Evaluate the five criteria in turn, identify where they conflict or leave the verdict underdetermined, and then say whether the case reveals a defect in the checklist itself.
