I've been chewing on something that seems off to me. If a guy gets wrongfully convicted and locked up for twenty years, the state ends up paying him, and if he dies before the claim closes, his kids collect. But let the original wrong be from, say, 1865—someone's family enslaved and then cheated out of land by the same government that now says 'too long ago, you weren't personally harmed.' I dont get what actually changes. What picture of 'who owes who' across generations is hiding underneath that line? It seems to commit you to something about whether an injury can run in a family, whether a debt to a dead person follow the bloodline, and whether time itself shrinks what is owed. Tease out the assumptions that are silently doing the work, mark which ones the practice genuinely forces on you and which are just extras, and say what would have to shift if we treated obligations as passing down the way property and debts already do.
