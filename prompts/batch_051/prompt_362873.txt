I am a novelist, and a recent detour into the Kelly criterion has left me with a parallel I cannot decide about. The Kelly insight, as I understand it, is that when wealth compounds multiplicatively, expected wealth is the wrong objective: the arithmetic mean flatters bets that carry ruinous variance, while expected log wealth tracks long-run growth, so a rational bettor will often take a smaller stake in a favorable bet than naive expected-value reasoning recommends, or decline a bet that risks ruin. I was struck that this is, almost point for point, a craft rule I absorbed as a writer: a novel is closer to a product of its scenes than a sum of them, because a scene that breaks the reader's trust or the book's internal logic multiplies through everything that follows, and a single dazzling set piece cannot multiply it back. The old maxim against letting a scene be brilliant at the cost of the whole looks to me like the Kelly rule against overbetting even when the bet is favorable. Is this a genuine identity of structure, or is the word compounding doing all the work? If it is genuine, what would have to be true of readerly experience for the transfer to hold—for instance, that scene-level effects are multiplicative and approximately independent—and does anything from the Kelly side, such as volatility drag, transfer back to fiction? If it is superficial, where exactly does the mapping fail?
