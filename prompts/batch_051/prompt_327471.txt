I keep running into the same standoff in animal ethics and I want help breaking it, not just watching it. Coming from biology, I keep looking for the structural difference that would justify a line, and I can't find one. The principle that pain is bad wherever it occurs seems to me close to self-evident: if an aphid poisoned by insecticide has even a faint aversive state, that's a bad thing in the world, and being small or strange or not cute can't remove it from the ledger. I know the insect-suffering science is unsettled — nociception, some behavioral signs of valence — but the resulting view is beautifully unified: count pain where you find it, and lots of small bads add up to one large bad. Yet I spray the aphids on my roses and bait the ants in the pantry without a twinge, and I can't make myself think a gardener doing the same is seriously wrong, even if thousands of insects die badly. If the principle is right, ordinary pest control looks like a small massacre; if my reaction is right, the principle owes me an explanation of why all those bad deaths come to nearly nothing. Which should give way? What would justify deciding the intuition is the thing to debunk, what would justify amending or dropping the principle, and is there a reading of 'counts' on which both survive?
