I am helping edit a commemorative volume on the origins of the First World War, and I have been asked to turn a long-running feud between two historians into a document both could actually sign. The question became personal for me recently: while settling my late father's estate I found a packet of letters from my great-grandfather written in July and August 1914, moving from ordinary family news into something between dread and exaltation, and since then I have wanted the old argument about responsibility to become more precise rather than merely rituallistic. I would like you to draft the document these two camps should have produced if they had conducted a disciplined adversarial collaboration.

The two positions are these. One side is the German-primary camp, associated with the Fischer controversy, which holds that Imperial Germany deliberately turned the July Crisis into a general war, pushed Vienna forward with the assurance now known as the blank cheque, and entered the war with expansive aims; on this view Germany bears the principal burden of responsibility. The other side is the systemic camp, which holds that the war emerged from the interaction of the alliance system, Great Power rivalries, rigid mobilization timetables, and miscalculations in several capitals, so that responsibility is genuinely distributed and the search for one guilty nation distorts the history.

Please structure the document as follows. First, a numbered list of claims both camps can sign: what the chronology of the July Crisis establishes, what the surviving evidence does and does not show, and the distinctions they share—in particular among causal responsibility, moral culpability, and legal war guilt. Second, a disagreements table, with each residual disagreement stated once in neutral terms and then in wording each side would accept for its own position. Third, a set of cruxes: the sub-questions that, if settled, would move at least one side. Include at least one crux about the weight of the Berlin war-aims memorandum of September 1914, one about whether Russian general mobilization closed off German alternatives or was itself a foreseeable response to prior moves, and one about whether a nation can be held morally responsible in a way not reducible to the responsibility of its government or leaders. Fourth, for each crux, the evidence or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. End by saying where, if anywhere, the dispute is partly verbal—for example because primary responsibility is being used in a causal, moral, or legal sense—and where it is genuinely substantive.
