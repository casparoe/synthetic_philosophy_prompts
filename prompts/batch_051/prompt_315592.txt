I'm a retired physical chemist, and for the last year or so I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group. Lately we've been doing social choice theory, and last week two of the regulars, Elena and Ravi, stayed after to fight about Arrow's impossibility theorem. I'd like an adjudication of the argument as it actually went. (Apologies if I've mangled any of the technical terms; I'm still new to this, and philosophy arguements never seem to settle the way experiments do.)

As near as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Elena said Arrow had shown once and for all that no voting system can be fair, so ongoing arguments about which system to use are a bit pointless: you're just choosing which fairness condition to betray.

(2) Ravi said that's stronger than the theorem actually is. Arrow shows that you can't have all four of the following at once: a complete, transitive social ranking built from any possible profile of individual rankings; Pareto; independence of irrelevant alternatives; and non-dictatorship. So it doesn't say every system is unfair. It says four reasonable-sounding demands are jointly incompatible, and at least one has to give.

(3) Elena came back: Pareto and non-dictatorship are close to non-negotiable, so the real fight is about IIA. And IIA says something minimal — if nobody changes their mind about A versus B, the social verdict between A and B shouldn't flip just because a third candidate C enters or withdraws. Any method that lets that happen is letting the candidate list, rather than the voters, decide.

(4) Ravi said IIA isn't as innocent as it sounds, because it forbids using where the other candidates sit on a voter's ballot — the only intensity-like information an ordinal ballot carries. Consider a candidate who is every voter's second choice versus a candidate who is the first choice of a bare majority and the last choice of a large minority. IIA forces the comparison to be made strictly head-to-head, so it throws away real information.

(5) Elena said that's what makes Borda count, which violates IIA, so easy to game: voters can bury a serious rival under candidates they don't actually prefer in order to inflate the gap, and parties can introduce clones or decoys that change the field and swing the result without changing anyone's genuine A-versus-B preference. IIA is what stops the candidate list from deciding the outcome between the serious candidates.

(6) Ravi said she was describing real costs, but keeping IIA doesn't buy what she wants either. Majority rule satisfies IIA, but it can produce Condorcet cycles and no complete transitive ranking. And once you insist on a complete transitive ranking over an unrestricted domain, Arrow's result says that keeping Pareto and IIA pushes you into a dictator. As for manipulation, there's the Gibbard–Satterthwaite result: under Arrow-style assumptions (deterministic, non-imposed, three or more candidates), any non-dictatorial single-winner rule can be gamed, so the manipulation objection cuts against everyone, not just Borda.

Go through this move by move and adjudicate. For each numbered move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify the rating in a sentence or two; then say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and which side's reading of Arrow's theorem is the more honest one. Keep the whole answer under 800 words.
