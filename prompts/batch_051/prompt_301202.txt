I run a small architecture firm, and a client will renew our contract only on one condition: everyone on the account must wear a headband during billable hours that reads electrical activity at the scalp. The headband does not record words or images of what anyone is thinking; it logs a second-by-second engagement index and uploads it to the client's productivity platform. The client says the data will be used only to confirm focused work, never to infer what anyone was actually thinking or feeling about. Employees may refuse, but anyone who refuses will be reassigned to other accounts, which would gut our team.

My colleagues argue this is simply an intensification of monitoring we already accept: keystroke logging, time-tracking software, a badge that records where we are. I find myself resisting, but I cannot articulate exactly what the new intrusion is. If the device captures only the outward, physical correlates of my mental activity and cannot decode its content, is monitoring the brain different in kind from exhaustive behavioral tracking, or only different in degree? Is there a distinct value at stake in keeping my unshared mental activity my own—and would that value survive if the device grew better at decoding? I want a definite decision about whether to sign, with the strongest reason for it, not a survey of positions. Answer in American English.
