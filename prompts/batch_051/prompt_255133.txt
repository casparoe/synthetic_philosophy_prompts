Draft the joint statement that a committed proponent of the earning-to-give strategy and a committed institutional critic of effective altruism could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. By earning to give I mean the strategy of taking a high-paying job—management consulting, software engineering, finance, corporate law—in order to donate a large fraction of the income to effective charities, on the grounds that the donations purchase more expected good than the person's direct labor would and that the job would be filled by someone less altruistic anyway (replaceability). The proponent replies to the institutional critique that the alleged harms are rarely quantified and that rejecting a high-earning career often substitutes a visible personal role for greater impartial impact. The institutional critic holds that this reasoning treats the job as morally inert except for the salary it yields, ignoring the ways a career entrenches harmful industries, legitimates their practices, captures talent and attention, and shapes the person's values; the critic also holds that effective altruism's preference for interventions with legible, quantifiable outcomes—measurability bias—systematically undervalues systemic, political, and movement-building work, so earning to give looks better than it is mainly because its benefits show up as a number while its harms do not.

The deliverable is the document such a collaboration would produce, written so both could put their names to it. Include (1) a numbered section of agreed claims: the shared moral and epistemic framework, the empirical concessions each side makes, and the strongest version of the other side's position each can sign. Then include (2) a disagreements table. For each row, state the disagreement in wording each side would accept for its own view; identify the crux—the sub-question whose resolution would move at least one side; and specify the case, argument, or evidence both sides agree bears on that crux, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Work through concrete cases rather than abstractions—compare, for instance, petroleum engineering, management consulting, a direct-delivery nonprofit role, and a political or movement-building role—and distinguish the claim that institutional effects are small from the claim that they are large but already priced into the salary from the claim that they are large and systematically omitted. Where a disagreement turns out to be verbal, or to reduce to a difference in modeling choices or discounting, say which part and why.
