So a friend and I got into it last night over Nietzsche’s On the Genealogy of Morals, specifically the second essay where he treats the creditor–debtor relation as the seedbed of law and punishment. He says — I’m paraphrasing because I don’t remember the exact wording, and I don’t want to misquote — that in the earliest legal arrangements the creditor could compensate himself by taking a kind of satisfaction out of the debtor’s body: pain, labor, liberty, even life. My friend thinks Nietzsche’s whole story about the internalization of cruelty and the bad conscience depends on that specific bodily practice. I think that overreads the passage.

Anyway, here’s the counterfactual we kept circling. Suppose that in the early communities Nietzsche is describing, the custom never develops of letting a creditor take satisfaction out of the debtor’s body. No flogging, no mutilation, no enslaving the debtor, no killing them as forfeit. A creditor can seize goods, livestock, and land, and if the debtor has nothing left the debt just stays on the books as an unenforced obligation, maybe a stain on their reputation. Everything else stays the same: contracts and barter exist, breaches happen, feuds happen, and eventually chieftains or councils begin adjudicating disputes and imposing their own penalties.

Given Nietzsche’s actual genealogical mechanism, predict how things develop over the next several generations. I want the chain of reasoning to be visible. Which parts of the story are robust, which are guesses, and what would have to hold for your prediction to be wrong? In particular: does bad conscience still arise? Does the instinct for cruelty turn inward anyway through some other channel? Does the moralization of debt into guilt still happen? Or do we get a recognizably different shape of morality? And does the absence of early bodily punishment matter more at the private-creditor stage, or later when the state centralizes punishment?

My friend also kept insisting there’s a passage where Nietzsche almost says the state steps in to protect the debtor from the creditor’s cruelty, and that that’s the real pivot. Maybe I’m garbling it. But if that’s the relevant mechanism, tell me how it interacts with my counterfactual. I want a reasoned prediction, not just a verdict on who won the argument.
