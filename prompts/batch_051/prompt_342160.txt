I cover defense tech and the philosophy of technology for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about whether "meaningful human control" over autonomous weapons can be turned into a workable review standard. I got my hands on a set of redacted summaries from our country's Autonomous Systems Review Board, and I want to figure out what principle is actually doing the work in them. Here's the track record, simplified (and I've changed the systems' names):

1. **Harrier** — approved. A loitering munition that flies in a pre-authorized "kill box," detects vehicle-sized objects, and cues a human operator who must approve each individual engagement before weapons release. If the datalink drops, the thing self-destructs.

2. **Mosaic** — approved. A drone swarm in which a human officer designates the exact target set ahead of time (say, those fourteen vehicles and those two buildings), and the swarm autonomously assigns individual targets to individual drones within that set. A human can veto any strike before impact, but nobody approves each aircraft-to-target pairing.

3. **Night Sceptre** — rejected. A fixed border sentry that uses gait and size to classify any adult intruder in a declared strip at night and fires a lethal round with no human authorization. An operator can watch on a feed, but the public summary says the manual override has failed in bad-weather testing.

4. **Quill** — approved. A ship-defense interceptor that automatically fires at incoming missiles on trajectories into a human-preauthorized defended volume; per-shot approval is impossible at those speeds, and a human can disarm or re-scope the volume at any time.

5. **Long Dark** — rejected. A submarine-launched strategic system that, after 72 hours without contact and a detected nuclear detonation, would launch on its own with no human decision anywhere in the final sequence.

Next month the board is supposed to rule on a new one, **Cordon**, and I want to think it through before the hearing. Cordon is a non-lethal crowd-control platform for urban checkpoints. Its vision system picks out individuals in a crowd who are throwing rocks or molotovs and automatically fires a non-lethal kinetic round at that individual. A human console operator watches each engagement live and has a real-time veto before and during any shot, but nobody has to identify the rock-thrower first — the machine does that — and nobody approves individual targets.

If the board wants to be consistent with it's own precedents, what should it do with Cordon, and which earlier ruling is the closest analog? I want the honest version: which two or three rules fit all five old rulings, where they split on Cordon, and what principle the board would be committing to either way.

Please structure the answer in these sections, in order:

- **The track record** (~120 words). One sentence per ruling, no editorializing.
- **What fits** (~250 words). The candidate rules that can each account for all five old rulings, without pretending the rulings force one of them.
- **Cordon under each rule** (~250 words). Run Cordon through each candidate rule, and say which old case it most resembles under each.
- **My verdict** (~150 words). The decision you'd recommend if I forced you, and which precedent is the closest analog.
- **The commitment** (~150 words). What principle the board adopts with that verdict, and one further test case that would best expose whether the board actually holds it.
