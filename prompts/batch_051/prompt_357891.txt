I recently read a feature article in a general-interest magazine about the case for antinatalism, and the author spent several paragraphs summarizing David Benatar's so-called asymmetry argument for the view that coming into existence is always a harm. The summary I have in mind goes roughly as follows: "Benatar begins from an asymmetry between pleasure and pain. For a person who exists, pain is bad and pleasure is good. For a person who never exists, however, the absence of pain is good, while the absence of pleasure is not bad, because there is nobody there to be deprived of it. So when we compare a possible life with never existing, the suffering in that life counts against bringing the person into being, but the pleasures cannot count in favor of bringing them into being in the same way. Since every human life contains at least some pain, every life is a net harm, however happy it is on the whole; non-existence is therefore better for the person who is never born. That, the author says, is why Benatar concludes that having children is always wrong." The author then dismisses the whole position as absurd, on the grounds that a life can plainly be worth living even if it includes some suffering, and that it is meaningless to say something is better for a person who does not exist.

I have a nagging sense that the author is attacking a coarser version of the argument than the one Benatar actually gives, but I do not know the terrain well enough to say exactly where the distortion enters. Could you go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful defender of the asymmetry argument would accept, which parts they would correct, and why? In particular, I would like to know whether the step from "every life contains some pain" to "every life is a net harm" is a fair rendering, whether "better for the person who is never born" captures how defenders understand the asymmetry or whether it smuggles in a notion of welfare for nonexistent people, and whether the author's conclusion—that the whole position is absurd—survives once the corrections are made.
