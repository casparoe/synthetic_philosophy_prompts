I am a retired physicist who has recently started reading philosophy of mind, and I would value a professional philosopher's judgment on something I cannot quite assess myself. A popular-science magazine recently reviewed a collection of essays by Daniel Dennett, and it summarized the idea behind the essay usually called The Self as a Center of Narrative Gravity roughly as follows. Dennett is said to argue that the self is not a thing inside the head but an abstract object, on the model of an object's center of gravity. A center of gravity is not a material particle; it is a point we posit in order to make an object's behavior intelligible. Similarly, the self is not a part of the brain but a posit we use to unify the stories a person tells about their own life and the stories others tell about that person. Since the self is posited rather than discovered, the summary continues, it is a useful fiction rather than a real entity. It follows that asking whether the self is identical with some brain process is a category mistake, and that any view which treats the self as real in the way neurons or brain states are real has missed the point. I find the first steps persuasive, but the slide from not a material part of the brain, to not a real entity, to category mistake feels as though a stronger conclusion is being smuggled in, and I suspect the key term is fiction. Please act as a specialist in philosophy of mind and cognitive science. Think the matter through carefully, and begin by reconstructing, in your own words, Dennett's actual position and the work the center-of-gravity analogy is meant to do. Then audit the magazine summary point by point, distinguishing what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, what is subtly wrong or would be rejected by a careful defender, and how the conclusion needs to be restated once the errors are corrected. In particular, before you settle on a verdict, distinguish several senses in which something might be called a fiction or an abstract object here, since I suspect the fairness of the summary hangs on that. I am not asking for citations or further reading; I want the conceptual distinctions that would let me judge the summary for myself.
