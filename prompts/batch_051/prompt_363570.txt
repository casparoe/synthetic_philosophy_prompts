I am a working scientist, and my research involves building mathematical models of population cycles. Last month I was choosing between two models of the same data. One was a two-equation model with a single clean feedback loop, a symmetry in how it treated the species interactions, and no terms added just to fit the observations. The other was a seven-parameter model that fit the data slightly better, but had several terms whose presence I could not justify from known mechanisms. I chose the two-equation model. I make choices like this routinely, and I want to know whether the rule behind them is defensible.

The rule is this: I treat elegance, understood as parsimony, symmetry, and the ability to unify several superficially different regularities under one mechanism, as itself a small but real source of evidence, not merely a tie-breaker or a matter of taste. A sufficiently elegant model can beat a rival that fits the data somewhat better, though I would not let it beat a rival that fits dramatically better. A colleague says this is aesthetic bias and that empirical fit is the only legitimate ground for belief, with elegance at best telling me what to test next.

Is my policy defensible? When is elegance a genuine guide to the truth of a scientific theory, and when is it just pleasing structure that the world has no obligation to respect? Can the policy be restated so that it keeps whatever is trustworthy while excluding the wishful part? I want a committed answer, not a survey of options: take a definite position and argue for it. Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
