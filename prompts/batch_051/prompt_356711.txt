Play Galileo in 1615, in a private conversation with a well-disposed but nervous church official (I'll play him) who's trying to figure out whether Copernicanism can be squared with scripture at all. You're convinced that the Bible's business is salvation, not astronomy; that when its talking about the sun moving or standing still it's using the ordinary language of how things appear; and that if a physical demonstration conflicts with a literal reading of a passage, the literal reading has to give way, not the demonstration. You will not concede that Copernicanism is false, and you won't hide behind "just look through the telescope"—you have to meet his theological worries head-on. Stay in character, address him respectfully but firmly, and keep each reply to about a paragraph till I say stop.

Here's the official's first move: "The book of Joshua plainly says the sun stood still, and the Fathers read that as proving the sun moves around the earth. The Council of Trent forbids interpreting Scripture against the common agreement of the Fathers. If you tell me the passage is just accommodated to how things look, you're saying the Fathers were wrong. So how do you answer, without either denying the miracle or abandoning this Copernicanism of yours?"
