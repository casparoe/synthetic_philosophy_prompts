I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a story about self-confirming instruments: the question of when a measuring device, a perceptual ability, or an artificial-intelligence system has earned our trust, and when it is merely vouching for its own accuracy. For several weeks I have been interviewing an epistemologist who works on what she calls the bootstrapping problem, and she has given me verdicts on a series of thought experiments. I want to be sure I have identified the governing principle correctly before I describe her position in print.

Her verdicts so far are the following.

First, she says that if a driver's fuel gauge is in fact perfectly reliable, and the driver sees that it reads full and on that basis believes the tank is full, then the driver knows the tank is full, even though she has no other way of checking at that moment.

Second, she considers the same driver attempting to test the gauge without any external check. Every morning for a month, the driver reads the gauge, forms a belief about the amount of fuel in the tank solely on the strength of that reading, and records that the gauge read a certain amount and that the tank did indeed contain that amount. At the end of the month the driver infers that the gauge has been accurate and is reliable. Her verdict: the driver does not know the gauge is reliable on this basis. The log entries, she points out, take for granted the accuracy of the very instrument they are supposed to test.

Third, she endorses independent calibration. A driver adds fuel to a nearly empty tank from a graduated container of known capacity and observes that the gauge rises through the corresponding increments. After several checks of this kind, the driver concludes that the gauge is accurate. Her verdict: the driver knows the gauge is reliable on this basis.

Fourth, she refuses to let an instrument certify itself. A hiker's altimeter runs a built-in self-diagnostic routine, after which the device reports that all of its sensors are functioning and that its readings are reliable. The hiker has no map, no known reference elevations, and no second instrument. On the strength of the self-test alone, the hiker concludes that the altimeter is reliable. Her verdict: no. A report generated by the device is just another output of the device; it cannot supply independent confirmation.

Fifth, she accepts calibration against well-established physical anchors. A technician immerses a digital thermometer in ice water and in boiling water at standard atmospheric pressure, and the instrument reads 0 degrees Celsius and 100 degrees Celsius respectively. The technician concludes that the thermometer is reliable. Her verdict: yes.

I need to know how she will rule on the following case, which I plan to raise with her next week. A driver installs two fuel gauges on the same tank: a conventional float gauge and an ultrasonic level sensor, made by different manufacturers and relying on different physical principles. Each morning for a month, the driver records both readings. He never dips the tank, never consults the odometer or fuel-consumption calculations, and never checks the readings against a pump. The two instruments agree on every reading. The driver concludes, on the strength of their agreement, that both are reliable. What will my source say?

I want more than a one-word prediction. I want your identification of the rules that could be governing her earlier verdicts, since more than one seems available to me; what each candidate rule predicts in the two-gauge case; your best guess about her actual ruling and the reasons that favor it; and a further case I could describe to her that would cleanly distinguish the remaining candidates if the two-gauge case leaves them unresolved. Please write the response in American English.
