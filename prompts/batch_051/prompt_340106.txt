I am running a two-hour reading seminar for advanced mathematics undergraduates, many of whom are formalism-trained and mildly skeptical that philosophy of mathematics has anything to teach them about the practice of proof. The text is Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations, and the session is built around the question whether mathematics is, in any nontrivial sense, quasi-empirical. I do not want to lecture the thesis at them; I want them to reach it by working through the polyhedron dialogue. Plan the teaching sequence rather than the explanation itself. What must be established first—for instance, the gap between a formal proof and the informal definitions and heuristics under which it operates—before a counterexample to the Euler formula can do the work Lakatos needs it to do? Which single episode from the dialogue, such as monster-barring, exception-barring, lemma-incorporation, or proof-generated concepts, should carry the session, and what exactly must it demonstrate if the quasi-empirical conclusion is to feel forced rather than imported from philosophy of science? Specify the one concrete example the whole session should be built around and what it must have and lack.

Then map the misconceptions these students are likely to form: that monster-barring is simply dishonest or ad hoc, that Lakatos's account is only about informal heuristics while formalized mathematics remains a priori and unaffected, or that "quasi-empirical" amounts to enumerative induction from examples. For each misconception, say where in the sequence it is likely to form and how to head it off there rather than later. End with one question I can ask to check that they have grasped the specifically Lakatosian thesis, as opposed to a generic slogan about mathematics being fallible, and state clearly what a first two-hour treatment should leave out and how to flag the omission honestly.
