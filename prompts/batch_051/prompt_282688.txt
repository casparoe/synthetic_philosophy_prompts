I've been arguing with a friend who works in statistics about what probability even is, and at this point I've realized my gut calls on cases don't line up into anything I can name. A perfectly symmetric die is about to be rolled once and then destroyed: I think it's true, before the roll, that the probability it lands 6 is 1/6, even though there will never be a long run of rolls of that die. A slightly bent coin sits in a drawer and will never be flipped: I still want to say it has some probability of heads, fixed by its shape and mass distribution, even if nobody ever knows it. The weather service says there's a 70% chance of rain tomorrow in my city: tomorrow is a one-off, but the number seems meaningful, and I also think the forecaster can be right or wrong in a way that isnt just about whether it actually rains — someone who says 70% on days like this, and it rains on 70% of them, is doing something right. A radioactive atom of some isotope with a known half-life: I think there's an objective probability it decays in the next hour, even though the atom only gets to do this once.

But then I have a bunch of cases that pull the other way. If I've measured everything about a coin toss — the exact force, angle, air currents, all of it — I think the probability it lands heads switches to 1 or 0. Measuring didn't change the coin, so probability starts to look like it tracks my information, which sounds subjective and I dont like that. At a horse race I say the favorite has a 25% chance; that feels like a claim about the world, not just my confidence, but I can't say what in the world it is, since the race won't be rerun and the horse isn't a symmetric die. And when a jury is deciding whether a defendant committed a crime, I'm comfortable saying the probability is 0.8 on the evidence, but that feels like yet another kind of probability, because the crime is already settled one way or the other in the past.

My friend says I'm just flip-flopping between frequentism, subjectivism, and propensity without noticing they're rival answers. Is there one interpretation of probability — or a principled hybrid that isn't just a label for each case — that captures all of these? If not, which of my verdicts have to go? Lay out the candidate interpretations and which of my cases each one saves or kills before you tell me where you land, and then say what that account predicts about a case I keep going back and forth on: the probability that this particular patient, with all her individual quirks, is helped by a surgery. If the answer is a hybrid, spell out exactly how its parts fit together, because pasting frequencies and degrees of belief together sounds to me like changing the subject rather than answering. Lead with your bottom line, and keep the whole thing under 850 words.
