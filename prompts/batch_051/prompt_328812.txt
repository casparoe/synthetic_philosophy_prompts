I got roped into giving the opening remarks at a small two-day workshop next month called 'Evidence and the Unseen.' The room will be genuinely mixed: about half historians and philosophers of science, a quarter working researchers in parapsychology and anomalous psychology, and the rest statisticians and psychologists who think the whole field is best understood as a case study in motivated reasoning. I've been asked to open with ten minutes on Henry Sidgwick, since the workshop is loosely built around him: he wrote The Methods of Ethics, was a founder and the first president of the Society for Psychical Research, and spent decades sitting in on séances, investigating mediums, and helping design early telepathy experiments. The angle I want the speech to take is that his psychical research wasn't a weird footnote or a hobby but grew directly out of his ethics. The dualism of practical reason he kept running into — the claim that we have reason to seek our own good and also reason to seek the good of all, and that the two can pull apart — looked to him like the kind of problem that could in principle be fixed by empirical evidence. If it turned out that people survived death, and that something like a cosmic moral order was actually in place, then the apparent conflict between doing what's right and looking after one's own good would be a local, temporary thing rather than a final feature of the universe. He didn't think he could just assume that, so he tried to find evidence for it, and he tried to find it by the same standards he'd demand anywhere else.

What I need from you is the speech itself, not a plan for it. I want it to use Sidgwick's actual stance to set ground rules for the workshop. The rule should come out as neither 'the prior is too low to bother' nor 'low priors excuse sloppy method,' but something more demanding about what rigor looks like when you are personally hoping for a result, and what fairness to the evidence looks like when the result would be momentous if true. It has to land for the parapsychologists (tired of being treated as cranks), for the statisticians (tired of being treated as closed-minded), and for the one person in the room who has read everything Sidgwick published in the Proceedings of the SPR — accurate but not pedantic. There's also a real constraint on the speech: whatever Sidgwick's private hopes were, the public stance the workshop is named after was methodological, and the speech should keep it that way instead of trying to convert anyone to either view. Please begin directly with the speech — no preface, no framing, no commentary before it.
