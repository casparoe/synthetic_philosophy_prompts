I'm in a high school philosophy class and we've been reading Mill's On Liberty, and me and my friend have been arguing for like two weeks about one passage in chapter 3. It says: "The despotism of custom is everywhere the standing hindrance to human advancement, being in unceasing antagonism to that disposition to aim at something better than customary, which is called, according to circumstances, the spirit of liberty, or that of progress or improvement." I basically think this is an argument that societies should be looser, like not so many unwritten rules and not so much social punishment for people who deviate. My friend says no, Mill isn't against social rules in general, he's only against custom when it shuts down "experiments in living." He says you can have a society that's strict about harm and basic duties but still loose about lifestyles, and Mill would be fine with that. I say he's watering down the quote. He says I'm ignoring the rest of the chapter and acting like Mill is some kind of absolute anti-conformist, which he isn't. We've basically stalemated.

The thing is, I notice the same stall happening when people talk about tight and loose cultures today. Like one side says tight cultures are good for coordination, handling disasters, social trust, whatever, and the other side says they crush individuality and innovation. And then both sides just cite different studies at each other. So I'm wondering if my Mill argument is actually the same shape as that bigger debate, and maybe neither side knows what kind of disagreement it's having. Could you act as an expert on political philosophy and cultural psychology and diagnose what's really going on here? Is it a verbal dispute about the word "custom," an empirical disagreement about what loose or tight norms actually do, a normative disagreement about which tradeoffs are acceptable, or maybe two different questions being asked under one heading? And what would progress look like? Not necessarily which of us is right, but like what would actually have to change in the argument for one of us to move.
