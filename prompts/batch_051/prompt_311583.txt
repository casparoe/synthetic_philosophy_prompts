I am the lead methodologist for a state Medicaid agency's high-cost therapeutics unit. Each quarter the unit scores about sixty applications to fund interventions for patients with severe neurological conditions — chiefly prolonged disorders of consciousness (the vegetative state, also called unresponsive wakefulness syndrome, and the minimally conscious state) and intractable chronic pain. For each application, analysts predict change on two dimensions: conscious capacity (from no awareness to reliable interactive awareness) and affective well-being (pain, distress, happiness). The dimensions are normalized to a common scale and currently combined by a weighted sum into a single benefit score that drives funding priority.

The unit's medical advisory committee is split. One camp argues that the dimensions should be multiplied — benefit is consciousness times happiness — because more consciousness with no positive experience is no benefit at all. Another camp argues that the relief of severe, otherwise intractable pain is lexically prior: no amount of mild happiness or added consciousness can outweigh even one such case. A third camp defends the present additive score as continuous, transparent, and easy to audit.

Write a decision procedure the unit's analysts can actually run. The procedure should determine, for each application, which combination rule — additive, multiplicative, or lexical — is defensible, and it should be operational: questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a refusal to score, and where it hands the decision to a human because the issue is one of values rather than measurement. Keep the procedure usable by analysts with no formal philosophical training and no more than 45 minutes per application, and make it leave a trail that a reviewing physician and a patient advocate can audit. In converting the theory into a checklist, state which distinctions among the three views must survive and which can safely be dropped.

Then apply the procedure to three illustrative applications:

1. A deep brain stimulation protocol for a patient in a minimally conscious state, expected to restore reliable interactive awareness but leave severe, treatment-resistant depression in place.

2. A medication for a patient with intact consciousness and otherwise intractable severe pain, expected to sharply reduce pain while modestly and persistently reducing alertness.

3. A sensory stimulation protocol for a patient in a vegetative state (unresponsive wakefulness syndrome), expected to produce intermittent wakefulness without awareness, with weak evidence that minimal awareness might occasionally occur.

For each application, show what the procedure instructs and what score or escalation results. Include at least one worked application where the procedure yields an outcome the agency will not like. Conclude by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do: where the choice among additive, multiplicative, and lexical combination remains a value judgment that no decision rule can eliminate.

Please write the entire answer in American English.
