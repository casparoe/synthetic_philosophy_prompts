I have been working on a position I call the finite-resolution account of experience, and I would like an honest assessment of it.

The core claim is this: any embodied finite mind has only finitely many pairwise discriminable experiential states. A finite physical system is coarse-grained at some scale, so below some threshold two distinct inputs will register identically for it. It follows that the set of experiences a given subject can tell apart is finite, even if the world presented to it is indefinitely rich. From this I draw the consequence that interests me: there is a last genuinely new experience for any finite subject. Eventually every further experience is, at the resolution the subject can actually access, a repetition of a type already had. I do not claim we are anywhere near that cap; only that the space is finite.

This makes the ideal of endless novelty constitutively impossible. There would be, for each subject, a most beautiful chord progression they can ever hear, a longest novel they can read while still registering all its internal differentiations, a final thought that is not a recombination-plus-recognition of earlier thoughts. I think recombination of a finite stock of elements does not escape this: notes, words, and colours are finite sets that can be recombined without end, but a finite mind can still only tell apart finitely many of the resulting recombinations. Infinite combinatorics upstream does not yield infinite discriminable experience downstream.

I am aware of one serious-looking objection. Discriminability is not transitive: a subject may find A and B identical, B and C identical, and yet A and C different. So the relation of being indistinguishable for a subject may not sort experience-space into clean equivalence classes, which threatens the finiteness count. My reply is that the finiteness claim should be restated at the level of useful resolutions: for any chosen tolerance, there are finitely many bins, even if those bins overlap at the edges. I am not sure that reply actually saves the conclusion about a last novel experience.

I cannot tell whether all this is trivially true or importantly false. Please give me the strongest defensible version of the principle, then the three strongest objections, and say which, if any, are fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. Also tell me where the principle stops being distinctive and becomes a restatement of some familiar idea.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning. No preamble and no restating of the question.
