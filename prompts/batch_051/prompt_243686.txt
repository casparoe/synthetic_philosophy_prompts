I have been trying to work out what, if anything, a cluster of art-world practices jointly commits someone to. The market for visual art attaches enormous weight to authenticity, but the weight falls in ways that sit oddly together. A painting that sold for eight figures would be worth almost nothing if it were discovered to be a perfect copy, even if no expert could tell the two apart by examining the object alone; the law, like the art world, treats passing off a forgery as a serious wrong even when the fake is visually indistinguishable from the genuine article and satisfied every perceptual expectation the buyer had. Yet a heavily restored original—one in which a large fraction of the painted surface is the restorer's work—can retain most of its value. And in much of the sculpture market, a bronze cast pulled from the sculptor's own mold after the sculptor's death is worth far less than a cast pulled during the sculptor's lifetime, although the two are physically indistinguishable and come from the same mold. These practices seem to presuppose something about where the value of an artwork resides: not simply in its perceptible form, and not simply in the fraction of the object physically made by the artist.

What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of aesthetic value, authorship, or authenticity that this pattern presupposes, and an assessment of whether the pattern is coherent. Does it presuppose that an artwork's history—who made it, when, and under what authority—is part of the work itself, so that two perceptually identical objects can be different works with different values? If it presupposes that the artist's act or intention is the locus of value, why doesn't restoration dilute value as much as copying does? Or is the pattern better explained by something other than a theory of the work—scarcity, legal title, the economics of certification, or social signaling? Please distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit with, say where they are underdetermined by rival theories, and then tell me what would have to change in pricing, conservation, and forgery law if the underlying view were replaced by a purely perceptual theory of aesthetic value. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
