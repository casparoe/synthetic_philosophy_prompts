One of my colleagues has been running a reading group on Abelard's ethics this term, and she's gotten into the habit of handing down verdicts on little moral cases. She says she's working out what she calls the consent view: on her reading of Abelard, what makes something a sin is what you consent to, and the outward deed is, as she puts it, morally inert. Her track record so far: a sudden urge to steal that the person immediately shakes off — not a sin. A woman who consents in her heart to an affair and would have gone through with it, but the would-be partner dies before anything happens — sin. A man who consents to a revenge killing and carries it out — sin, though she's never said whether the killing itself adds anything. A sleepwalker who breaks a valuable vase with no prior thought of it — not a sin. A woman who gives a large donation to a shelter purely because she wants to be admired — sin, because what she actually consents to is vainglory rather than charity.

Now I have two cases for next week, and I'd like to know what she'd say and why. First: two men both consent to defraud their employer. One is caught before he can touch any money; the other succeeds and spends the money for a decade. Would she say the second is more guilty than the first, or that they're equally guilty? Second: a woman spends an evening each week elaborately savoring a fantasy of an affair, but she never consents to doing anything and arranges her life so she's never alone with the person. Is that a sin on her view? I'm less interested in the bare answers than in the rule she's actually running, the other rules that fit her verdicts just as well, and which further case would seperate them.
