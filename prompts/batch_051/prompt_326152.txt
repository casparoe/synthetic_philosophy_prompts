There's this weird double standard I keep seeing in interpretability work, and I'd like you to sort out what it presupposes. The standard move is: train a linear probe to read a concept off a layer's activations, or find a steering vector by contrasting prompts, and then the paper starts talking about that direction as though it just is the model's representation of truthfulness or sycophancy or whatever. Then someone ablates the direction, behavior changes, and that's taken as confirmation the concept was really encoded there all along. But the same people will, in the limitations section, admit that the direction depends on the method, that a different probe gives a different direction that does roughly the same behavioral work, and that there's no uniquely correct decomposition of the activations. So what view of representation does this practice actually commit you to? Does using a direction to steer or ablate require the feature to be causally real in the model, or just that the activation space supports a useful coordinate system? If many directions do the same downstream work, does that show features are made rather than found, or only that they're relative to a decomposition? And is there any experimental result that would count as evidence the model does not have a particular feature, rather than evidence that you found a bad probe? I keep sliding between "this direction causes the output" and "this direction represents the concept" as if they were the same claim, so feel free to tell me where the conflation is doing the work.
