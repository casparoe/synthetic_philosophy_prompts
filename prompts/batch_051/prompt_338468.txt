I'm a PhD student in environmental law and administrative regulation; I follow the Anthropocene debate only as a well-read outsider. I've been working through Paul Crutzen and Eugene Stoermer's 2000 article The Anthropocene, published in IGBP Newsletter 41, and one passage in it has been nagging at me. In that passage they argue that human activity has become a force operating on the Earth system at a scale comparable to major geological processes, so the present epoch warrants a new name. They propose the closing decades of the eighteenth century as the appropriate onset, citing the ice-core record of carbon dioxide and methane beginning to climb and the industrial revolution as a convenient marker, and they acknowledge that any more precise boundary date is unavoidably somewhat arbitrary, yet treat that arbitrariness as no real objection to the proposal. I am paraphrasing the passage rather than quoting it, because I don't trust my memory of their exact wording.

I want you to translate that argument into the vocabulary I work in most fluently: administrative law's treatment of agency determinations. Use precisely the distinctions among findings of fact, policy judgments, and mixed questions of law and policy; the arbitrary-and-capricious standard of review; and the account of procedural legitimacy under which an agency's determination becomes authoritative only through notice and comment, a reasoned explanation, and judicial review. Don't define these terms; just use them as a specialist would.

Take their proposal point by point: the claim that humans have become a geological force; the selection of the late eighteenth century as the onset; the concession that a more precise date would be arbitrary; and the implicit assumption that scientific bodies such as stratigraphic commissions are the relevant decision makers. Restate each point in the administrative-law vocabulary, saying what kind of determination it would be classified as, what standard of review it would face, and what procedural episode it most closely resembles. Then give me a residue report: which parts translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no expression in the administrative-law vocabulary at all. Finally, tell me whether the residue points to a defect in the administrative-law framework, to a gap in Crutzen and Stoermer's periodization argument, or to a substantive difference between scientific periodization and legal decision-making — and whether the "who decides" question in the Anthropocene debate becomes clearer or murkier after the translation.
