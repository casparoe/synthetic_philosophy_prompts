I am a professor of moral philosophy, and next month I am giving a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on character, adversity, and the virtues. The paper's thesis is that the contemporary constructs of grit and resilience, as used in psychology and public policy, are not neutral rediscoveries of the classical virtue of fortitude; they are partly ideological concepts that individualize the burdens of unjust or malfunctioning institutions. My argument has three moves. First, I claim that fortitude, in its premodern sense, includes a normative judgment about which hardships are worth enduring and for what ends, whereas grit and resilience are typically defined in terms of persistence and adaptive recovery regardless of the worth of the goal or the justice of the conditions producing the adversity. Second, I argue that this difference is not merely theoretical: invoking grit or resilience in educational, workplace, or public-health settings can function to demand that people adapt to conditions they ought instead to criticize or change. Third, I allow that resilience-talk can be genuinely useful in clinical contexts, but I deny that this rehabilitates it as a virtue concept, because its practical recommendations become normative only when a substantive account of worthy ends is smuggled in from elsewhere.

The audience will be roughly two dozen faculty and graduate students. About half are virtue ethicists and historians of philosophy who know Aristotle and Aquinas well; a third are empirical psychologists who study self-regulation and stress and who regard resilience research as broadly vindicated; the remainder are political philosophers and critical theorists who are disposed to see psychological constructs as ideology but may find my paper insufficiently structural. The psychologists are likely to resist the suggestion that their constructs are normative or ideological; the virtue ethicists will press on whether I have defined fortitude too thinly or treated grit too much as a caricature; and the critical theorists will press on whether I have offered a genuine account of the social function of resilience discourse rather than a merely conceptual complaint.
