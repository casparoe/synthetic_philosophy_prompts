I follow EU politics from the outside, not as a scholar, and I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a word for. Officially, the Union only has the powers the member states gave it, and any real expansion is supposed to happen through treaty change—which is noisy, slow, and sometimes fails in a referendum. But in practice the EU seems to take on whole new areas through much quieter routes: the Commission finds a legal hook in an old treaty article that was written for something else; a crisis hits and creates a precedent (vaccine procurement, the banking union after the financial crisis, the rule-of-law conditions attached to budget money); and by the time anyone looks up, the EU is doing something nobody remembers ever being asked to agree to. Each individual step looks defensible, even necessary, but the cumulative effect is that member states loose room for maneuver without ever losing a single treaty battle. Because it never shows up as a line item in a manifesto, voters can't exactly punish anyone for it either.

I know this is adjacent to spillover and to the whole ever-closer-union language, but spillover, as I understand it, is mostly about economic integration generating pressure for more integration, and the ever-closer-union idea is a goal, not a description of how the thing actually happens. So what I want to know is whether this quieter pattern has its own name. If it does, what does the term pick out exactly, and where do its edges run—does a crisis-driven jump count, or is the concept reserved for slower, everyday drift? If it doesn't have a name, what would you call it, and how would you distinguish it from spillover and from ordinary treaty interpretation working as intended?
