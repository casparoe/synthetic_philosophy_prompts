William James writes in "What is an Emotion?" (Mind, vol. 9, 1884):

"Without the bodily states following on the perception, the latter would be purely cognitive in form, pale, colourless, destitute of emotional warmth. We might then see the bear, and judge it best to run, receive the insult and deem it right to strike, but we could not actually feel afraid or angry."

My sister has a rare neurological condition that, for the purposes of this question, you may take to have eliminated every one of the felt bodily changes James describes: her pulse stays steady and unfelt, her face does not flush, her throat does not tighten, and her stomach and limbs remain still. Last year, when she learned that a colleague she trusted had been quietly undermining her work for months, she insisted that she was not merely judging the colleague's conduct to be a betrayal; she was genuinely angry, and the anger, she said, was "in her head" rather than anywhere in her body.

James plainly did not have my sister in mind. Treat the quoted passage as a rule for identifying the standard emotions and her case as one its author did not foresee. What does the rule require us to say about whether her anger is really anger? Argue the question from the wording of the passage, from the purpose of the essay, from what James would most plausibly have said if asked about a case like hers, and from what a reasonable reader would take the passage to commit him to. Where these readings diverge, say which should govern here and why. Then step back and say what the case does to James's theory: does the theory survive, require a friendly amendment, or fail on its own terms?
