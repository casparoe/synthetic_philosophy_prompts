When a novel by a writer from a socially dominant group quietly takes the existing order for granted—treating its hierarchies as natural background—critics tend to praise it as realistic, subtle, and universal. When a novel by a marginalized writer openly challenges that order, the same critics are apt to call it a tract, propaganda, not quite art. W. E. B. Du Bois refused this asymmetry: he held that all art is propaganda, and that the only real difference is whether the politics are visible enough to be named. Give the critic's distinction its strongest defense. One candidate principle is that propaganda deliberately subordinates aesthetic choices to a predetermined persuasive aim, whereas art leaves its meaning open; you may replace that principle if you can find a better one. Then test your principle against the case in which a novel's politics are hidden in what it treats as natural, and say whether you would keep the art/propaganda distinction, narrow it, or abandon it.
