I've been collecting my gut reactions to a bunch of "if" sentences, because I keep feeling that indicative and counterfactual conditionals are two different species but I cant state the difference. Here are my verdicts:

(1) "If Oswald didn't pull the trigger on Kennedy, somebody else did." Fine, feels true.
(2) "If Oswald hadn't pulled the trigger on Kennedy, somebody else would have." Not fine — now it sounds like a claim about how history had to go, and I won't sign up for that.
(3) "If the butler didn't kill him, the gardener did." Fine, in a whodunit where those two are the only live suspects.
(4) "If the butler hadn't killed him, the gardener would have." Not fine — the gardener wasn't lying in wait; he's just second on my suspect list.
(5) "If it rained last night, the streets will be wet this morning." Fine.
(6) "If it had rained last night, the streets would have been wet this morning." Also fine, but clearly a different act: I'm not inferring from a forecast I missed, I'm imagining the difference it would've made.
(7) "If Mary is at the party, she is with Dave." Fine, because those two are joined at the hip right now.
(8) "If Mary were at the party, she would be with Dave." Weird. I don't know whether she's at the party, so why jump into alternative-history mode? It doesn't say what I'm trying to say.
(9) "If I were you, I'd take the offer." Totally normal advice.
(10) "If I am you, I will take the offer." Absurd, almost ungrammatical.
(11) "If the sun doesn't rise tomorrow, I'll eat my hat." Fine as an exaggerated bet.
(12) "If the sun didn't rise tomorrow, I would eat my hat." Weird enough that I'd correct somebody who said it.

My working hunch is: indicatives are inferences from what I know about the actual world, counterfactuals are claims about what would be true under an alternative history. That handles (1)–(4) cleanly. But it makes a mess of (6) vs (12): both are alternative-history claims, yet (6) is fine and (12) isn't. And it has nothing to say about (9)/(10), where the counterfactual is a fixed idiom and the indicative is nonsense. So the hunch is too simple, but I don't know in which direction to fix it.

Is there a single principle — semantic, pragmatic, or some mix — that gets all or nearly all of these? What does it say about sentences like "If you're so smart, why aren't you rich?", where the if doesn't seem to be doing either job but I still hear it as an if? And what does it predict for a fresh pair like "If the patient's condition worsened overnight, we're already too late" vs "If the patient's condition had worsened overnight, we would already be too late"? If some of my verdicts have to go to get a clean principle, tell me which ones and what the principle would predict instead.
