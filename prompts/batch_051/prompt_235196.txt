I'm a novelist, and the book I'm outlining has a Han Feizi-style minister running a last-ditch reform of a disintegrating kingdom. His whole conviction is that the ruler's private virtue is irrelevant to ruling, and that the only thing that actually produces order is a system people can see coming: fixed standards, automatic reward for compliance, automatic punishment for deviation, and no exceptions even for the ruler's freinds. I keep going back and forth on whether to write him as the one clear-eyed person in the court or as the novel's slow catastrophe, and my editor and I argue about it too. I'm at maybe .55 that he's mostly right; my editor is at .85.

So here's what I want. Give me your actual credence for this proposition: that for a large impersonal state, over a generation or two, governing through a predictable system of incentives and punishments produces more reliable order — lower corruption, steadier taxes and military service, fewer internal revolts, longer survival of the state — than governing by trying to cultivate genuine virtue in the officials and the population at large. I want a number, not it depends, plus the decomposition. Give me the sub-questions your credence rests on, with a rough probability for each and how they combine: how much rides on whether people's behavior is mostly driven by incentives and fear of punishment rather than internalized norms; how much on whether a fixed incentive system can be designed without getting gamed into a tool of the powerful; how much on whether virtue can be cultivated at the scale of a whole state anyway; and whatever else belongs. Then tell me which term you're least confident in, where the gap between my .55 and my editor's .85 most likely lives, and what evidence or argument would move your number the most.
