After a long argument with a friend last night about generics — sentences such as "ducks lay eggs," which attribute a property to a kind rather than to particular individuals — I came away with several judgments that feel settled but no single principle that unifies them. What I want to know is whether one exists, or whether I am keeping my judgments together only by quietly switching between principles as the cases change.

Here are the settled judgments, each about whether the generic sentence is true:

"Ducks lay eggs" is true. "Ducks are female" is false. I feel the awkwardness of this pair sharply, because both predicates are tied to sex and neither is true of the species across the board.

"Dogs have four legs" is true, even though some dogs have lost a leg. "Mosquitoes carry malaria" is true, even though only a tiny fraction of mosquitoes carry the parasite. "Mosquitoes don't carry malaria" is false, even though, taking mosquitoes one by one, the overwhelming majority do not carry it. "Sharks attack bathers" is true, even though an individual shark almost never attacks a bather. "Books are paperbacks" is false, even though most books in circulation are probably paperbacks. "Canadians are right-handed" is false, even though something like nine in ten Canadians are.

On "lions have manes" I remain unsettled. It has the sex-linked shape of the duck case, but having a mane feels less central to what a lion is than laying eggs to what a duck is; my friend insisted it was true, and I could neither accept it outright nor call it simply false. I am similarly torn about "snakes are venomous": only a minority of snake species are venomous, yet the sentence does not strike me as plainly false, perhaps because the exceptions are exactly the ones one wants a warning about.

Is there a principle about when a generic of the form "Ks are/do F" is true that captures all, or nearly all, of the settled judgments? State it as precisely as you can, letting each clause be motivated by the judgment that forces it. Then tell me what the principle says about the two unsettled cases and about further cases I have not listed — for example, "wasps are aggressive," "ticks carry Lyme disease," and "humans live in Asia." Finally, tell me honestly which of my settled judgments the best candidate cannot accommodate, and which I should reconsider.
