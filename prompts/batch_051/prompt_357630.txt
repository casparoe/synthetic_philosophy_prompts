I hold that the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem and its relatives establish that, once there are at least three alternatives, vulnerability to strategic voting is an inescapable feature of any non-dictatorial procedure that turns ranked preferences into a single winner. From this I draw the design conclusion that manipulability is a standing constraint on collective decision procedures, something to be managed rather than engineered away. I want you to test that design conclusion, not the theorem itself. What would have to be true for the conclusion to be false or importantly overstated? I am asking about the gap between the formal result and practice: which assumptions—ordinal ballots, single-winner outcomes, an unrestricted domain over full preference rankings, voters whose reports are pure statements of preference—can fail in real institutions, and which such failures would restore more than a hollow kind of strategy-proofness rather than merely relocating the manipulation? Which empirical results about the frequency, foreseeability, or severity of manipulable profiles would count against treating the theorem as practically significant? And which escape routes should a committed design pessimist concede are live? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and state where the honest assessment lands. Hard limit: 800 words.
