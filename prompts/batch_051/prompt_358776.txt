I’m a journalist who covers behavioral science and ideas, and I’ve been chewing on a homemade distinction I want checked before I pitch a piece. I’ve been calling it “attachment as unexercised option value,” which is too finance-y but it’s what I’ve got. The claim is that the endowment effect isn’t a bias at all. When you own something, you hold a bundle of possible futures with it: you can use it, lend it, sell it later, modify it, or just keep the option of changing your mind. Selling it in the moment collapses all those futures into one cash amount. So the gap between what an owner demands and what a buyer will pay is just the option premium the owner is being asked to forfeit without compensation. Loss aversion, on this view, is a redescription of option value rather than an explanation of it. It predicts the effect should shrink for fungible items and grow for emotionally or practically entangled ones. It also predicts that people will hold bad stocks and bad relationships too long not because they hate loss but because the option to reverse course later still has value. Is this already in the literature under some other name? Is it just a dressed-up version of something obvious? Give me the three strongest objections and say whether any of them is fatal rather than just uncomfortable.
