I'm in a reading group on formal epistemology that's spent the last few months going in circles about Occam's razor, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict about who's right. The odd thing is that everyone in the room concedes the mathematics. One camp says Solomonoff induction basically settles it: if you assign prior probability by summing over programs, shorter programs get exponentially more prior mass, so preferring simpler hypotheses falls out of Bayesian updating on its own. A second camp says that's a non sequitur, because the Solomonoff prior is only defined up to a choice of universal Turing machine, the relevant quantities are uncomputable, and real scientific theories aren't programs anyway — so the theorem lives in a toy setting and can't underwrite the razor scientists actually use. A third camp says the first two are confusing a methodolgical heuristic with a claim about the world: simplicity pays because it controls overfitting and makes theories testable, not because simple theories are more likely to be true. And then there's the fourth person, who insists simple theories are more likely to be true because nature itself is simple, though he can never pin down what 'simple' means beyond 'compressible in whatever description language we happen to like.'

We've been at this for months and nobody has moved, and I'm starting to think there's no hidden factual question doing the work. My hunch is that the four positions are each demanding a different kind of justification for an epistemic norm — decision-theoretic, truth-conducive, pragmatic, metaphysical — and that they don't share a single fixed target called 'simplicity.' So what kind of disagreement is this, actually?
