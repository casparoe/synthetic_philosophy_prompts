I am a journalist who covers the social sciences and the circulation of ideas, and one dispute keeps crossing my desk in nearly identical form: whether generational categories such as the Silent Generation, Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, and Generation Z name real social kinds, or whether they are arbitrary bins that we have learned to treat as deep facts about human beings. I am not asking for a verdict on that question. What interests me is that the argument has persisted for decades — through the same surveys, the same counterexamples, and the same accusations of essentialism or naivety — without either side landing a fatal blow. I want you to diagnose the structure of the disagreement, not adjudicate it.

The two positions, as I encounter them, run roughly as follows. Defenders treat a generation as a birth cohort whose members are shaped by a common historical situation: formative economic conditions, wars, political moments, and technologies such as the smartphone. On this view the categories are real enough because they track a shared causal input, even if the boundaries are fuzzy and the members heterogeneous. Skeptics reply that the labels carve discontinuous boxes out of continuous variation, that the psychological differences attributed to cohorts are mostly age effects or period effects in disguise, and that the whole enterprise flattens differences of race, class, gender, and geography into a single invented identity. They also point out that many headline claims — that Generation Z is more anxious than its predecessors, for example — rest on cross-sectional data that cannot separate being young from being born late from living through a particular historical moment.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse has at least two layers. The empirical layer is underdetermined: standard data cannot, on their own, separate age effects, period effects, and cohort effects, because isolating any one of them requires assumptions about the other two, and the two camps refuse to share those assumptions. The conceptual layer concerns what a real kind must be: the defenders are, in effect, willing to count a category as real if it tracks a shared causal history, while the skeptics demand intrinsic, explanatory similarity and will not grant that being born in an approximate window qualifies. I offer this only as a hypothesis. The task is to diagnose the debate with reasons: distinguish the verbal disagreement from the methodological disagreement from the genuinely philosophical one, say whether the question is well posed, and explain what, if anything, would count as progress given that diagnosis.
