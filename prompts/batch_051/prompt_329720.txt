Exam question: State the problem of old evidence for subjective Bayesian confirmation theory, and illustrate it with a concrete example. Then explain the counterfactual strategy for handling the problem and say whether you think it succeeds.

Student's answer: Old evidence is evidence that was known before a theory was formulated. Glymour's problem is that such evidence can never register as confirming in the standard Bayesian machinery, even when scientists take it to. Mercury's anomalous perihelion precession is the usual illustration: the anomaly was measured decades before general relativity, and GR predicts it (given the usual auxilary assumptions). Since astronomers already knew the anomaly, P(E) = 1. Since GR entails the anomaly, P(E|H) = 1. Plugging into Bayes: P(H|E) = P(H)·P(E|H)/P(E) = P(H)·1/1 = P(H). So no confirmation, which clashes with how the case is usually described.

The standard fix is the counterfactual strategy. We think away E from our background knowledge and ask how likely E would be on the leftover background K*. For Mercury, K* plus Newtonian mechanics would make the anomaly very surprising, whereas K* plus GR would make it likely, so P(E|H & K*) is much greater than P(E|K*). Therefore the old anomaly confirms GR once the background is fixed properly. This is the consensus solution: the problem only arises because we carried E along as known, and subtracting it gives the right result. The one remaining worry is that if E was known with probability one, no counterfactual distribution can be constructed; but real scientists are never literally certain, so in practice P(E) is just below 1 and the problem is an unrealistic artifact.
