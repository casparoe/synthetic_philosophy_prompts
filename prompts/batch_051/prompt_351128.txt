I am in the final year of a law and philosophy degree and have spent the last two months on a work placement in the compliance team of a mobile app company with several million users, most of whom pay through in-app subscriptions. The team is small: two lawyers review a flow before the head of product signs off, but in practice the product managers are the ones who apply the checks. We put a new consent flow in front of users two or three times a month—an updated privacy notice when a data-sharing partner is added, a revised arbitration clause imposed across the whole user base, a change to automatic renewal, or an opt-in for location tracking. The stakes are real: an arbitration clause that a court refuses to enforce is useless precisely when we need it, a data-protection authority may fine us over an opt-in it regards as invalid, and users who feel tricked cancel their subscriptions. At present the only check is a thin legal one, namely whether a court is likely to treat the click, or a banner saying that continued use counts as acceptance, as sufficient, and my supervisor wants something better.

In the academic side of my degree we have been studying the difference between genuine agreement and mere acquiescence: whether a signature or a click has any independent normative force; whether consent must be intentional, adequately informed and sufficiently voluntary; and the ways in which standard-form terms fail to constitute agreement because they are hidden, bundled, or accepted in the absence of any real alternative. I have been asked to turn this material into a reusable procedure for the product managers, who have no legal or philosophical training, to apply in under forty-five minutes before a flow reaches legal review.

What I need is a decision procedure—a checklist, or a flow described in words, with tests run in a fixed order—that determines whether a proposed digital acceptance should be treated as actual agreement rather than formal acquiescence that is probably unenforceable. It should be explicit about which distinctions from the theory must survive the simplification and which can safely be dropped, and it should clearly mark the points where judgement cannot be eliminated and a human has to decide. Please include two or three worked examples of the procedure applied to realistic consent flows, at least one of which produces an answer the product team will not like, and close by saying what the procedure cannot guarantee even when it is applied carefully.
