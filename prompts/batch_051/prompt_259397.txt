I grew up in a Reformed church where the regulative principle was treated as obvious: whatever is not commanded in Scripture is forbidden in public worship, full stop. The pastor would soften it by saying the principle only governs the elements of worship, not the circumstances — the time of the service, the temperature of the room, whether the chairs are comfortable — but I could never get a straight answer about where that line falls. I've since drifted away from the position, and a friend from that church recently asked me whether I'd ever found anything that would actually count against it; I realized I couldn't say.

So I want to steelman the regulative principle as a living position and ask: what would count against it? I don't mean a list of reasons why it's false. I mean: what would the world, or Scripture, or the nature of worship, or the history of the church have to look like for the regulative principle to be false or badly incomplete? Would it count if the early church did things without any explicit command? Would it count if the commanded forms of worship turned out to be culturally arbitrary? Would it count if no non-arbitrary line between element and circumstance can be drawn? I want a map of the position's actual exposure: wich considerations would be decisive, which merely awkward, and which a committed defender would themselves admit count against it. And please say honestly whether the position, with all its standard qualifications, has been insulated into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that is a problem for a principle of this sort, or just what you'd expect of a theological rule.
