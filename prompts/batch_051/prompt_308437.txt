I teach a small upper-level seminar in philosophy of law, usually 12 to 16 undergrads, one 80-minute block. We're about to start the unit on ignorance of the law as an excuse: whether it's fair to hold people to rules they never had a realistic chance to learn. I've done the standard lecture before and it always lands flat---half the class says well the law's the law, the other half says it's just obviously unfair, and nobody actually feels the pressure of the question. I want an in-class game or simulation that makes them experience the problem first, before we name it or read anything.

I'm imagining the class split into rule-makers and rule-followers, or into people who know the rules and people who don't, with the people who don't know still being penalized for breaking them. It can't be so on the nose that they clock the point in five minutes, and I'd like at least one middle position---somebody who's heard the rule second-hand or in garbled form---because the interesting legal cases sit between knowing and not knowing. I need concrete roles, rules, materials, a point system that doesn't use real money, and a rough timeline for the 80 minutes. Penalties should be imposed by other students, not by me, so the fairness problem comes from inside the room. The phrase ignorance of the law should not appear until the debrief, which should map what happened to the distinction between a rule being unknown and being genuinely unknowable, the fair-notice idea, and the main arguments for and against strict liability for legal ignorance. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the real legal question the game can't touch and I'll have to supply myslef.
