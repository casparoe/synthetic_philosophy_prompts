As a philosophy professor working on action theory, I find myself caught between a compelling theory and a firm intuition. The theory is a public-conduct principle: the permissibility of a deliberately harmful act depends only on what the agent does and which harms she can foresee, not on what she privately aims at. Its virtues are real—it is public, it requires no mind-reading, and it evaluates observationally identical acts identically. The intuition comes from a matched pair. Two pilots fly identical bombing runs against the same munitions factory, foreseeing the same schoolhouse beside it and the same children in it. One aims solely at the factory and would spare the school if he could; the other would not have flown had the school been empty and welcomes the school's destruction for its effect on enemy morale. I find the first pilot's act permissible and the second's impermissible, even though the public-conduct principle must treat them alike. That gap is exactly what the doctrine of double effect is built to close. Give me the strongest case that my intuition about the second pilot confuses blame for his character with a judgment about the permissibility of his act, the strongest case that the public-conduct principle must be amended so that intention matters in itself, and your considered verdict about which should yield—or whether they can be reconciled. Keep the response under 800 words.
