I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I got roped into being parliamentarian for a small citizens' assembly on land use. Their standing rules, which date to 1995, includes this:

Article 7 (Conduct of Debate): "Every member shall have an equal opportunity to address the Assembly. Members shall speak in their own words. No member shall address the Assembly through another person, and no member shall use any device that gives an unfair advantage in debate."

We hit a case the drafters obviously never pictured. A member, Dana, has ALS and can't speak clearly enough to be understood in a meeting. She wants to use a brain-computer interface where she selects short phrase fragments, and a large language model expands each fragment into a fluent paragraph that she approves before a speech synthesizer reads it aloud. Half the members say the model is a ghostwriter, so she's speaking "through another" and not in her own words. The other half say that clause was aimed at people sending stand-ins to meetings, and the device clause was aimed at megaphones, not access tech; forcing her to use a slow letter-by-letter keyboard would shut her out, which contradicts the whole purpose of the rule.

The chair wants a one-page ruling from me. I have to say what Article 7 actually requires here, applying each of the usual interpretive approaches: the plain words, the purpose of the rule, what the 1995 drafters would have said if you could have shown them a modern large language model, and what a reasonable member today would take the rule to mean. Which reading controls? And here's the part I keep going back and forth on: the assembly was explicitly modeled on Habermas's ideal speech situation—equal opportunity to speak, only the strength of reasons is supposed to decide. If we read "own words" broadly enough to let Dana in, does that force us to let every member use an LLM to draft and polish their remarks? Is there a principled line between assistive tech that removes a disadvantage and tech that manufactures an advantage, or does the ideal speech situation actually not care about that difference?
