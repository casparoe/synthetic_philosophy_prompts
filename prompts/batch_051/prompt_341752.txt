so i just got back from arguing with my friend for most of the night about whether the evil demon is really the worst case scenario for knowledge, and i came away with a distinction i want checked before i use it on him again in the morning.

the distinction: skeptical scenarios come in two kinds, and i think we run them together when we teach descartes and hume as one continuous campaign. call the first kind forged-world doubts. that's the demon, the brain in a vat, the simulation someone is running on purpose: a deceiver is actively feeding you a whole coherent but false world. call the second kind broken-link doubts. there's no deceiver. the connection between your cognitive machinery and the world just isn't there—you're a well-made instrument tuned to nothing, like a thermometer with a beautifully regular internal scale that was never calibrated against any actual temperature.

my claim is that these two doubts aren't equally threatening, and that the traditional order gets them backwards. forged-world doubt is to a degree self-limiting: to counterfeit a world at all, the counterfeiter has to preserve a huge amount of structure. tables still behave like tables when you knock them, words keep enough of their meaning for you to follow the false story, and the demon has to keep the inside of your experience regular enough to be a world you can be wrong about. a demon who makes everything false, including arithmetic and the meanings of the words 'deceive' and 'false,' hasn't deceived you; he's made a noise that no longer even means the thing the skeptic wanted to say. so the only statable version of the demon is one that borrows a real world's grammar to fake the details. that's uncomfortable, but it's not paralysis.

broken-link doubt, i think, is the one that survives. hume's point about induction isn't that a demon is faking the regularities; it's that the link between past and future can't be justified from inside the very faculty you'd use to justify it. that's a broken-link problem. the problem of the criterion—you need a criterion for knowledge to evaluate your criterion for knowledge—is also a broken-link problem, not a forged-world one. and hume's so called skeptical solution (nature makes us believe it anyway, we can't help it) is what you'd expect for a broken link: the machine works, the question is only wether its working is connected to anything.

so what i want to know: is this a real distinction or am i just relabeling something obvious? does it already have a name i don't know? and where does it break—especially the bit where i said a total demon is unstatable, because that's the move i'm least sure of and i kept having to wave my hands at him at exactly that point. be honest, and tell me how confident you are in each judgment and which one of your own claims you're least sure about.
