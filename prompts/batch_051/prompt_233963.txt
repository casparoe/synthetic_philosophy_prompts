In product debates at work, a comparison keeps getting invoked to settle contested policy choices, and I suspect it is doing more work than it can bear. The comparison is that choosing a rule without knowing which user it will most affect is like designing the rules of a game before knowing which player you will be, so we should pick the option with the best worst-case outcome. A colleague who has read some philosophy pointed out that Rawls and Harsanyi part ways over exactly this setup—maximin versus equal-probability expected utility—and a third person argued for minimizing worst-case regret instead. I would like an audit of the game-design analogy itself. Please lay out what corresponds to what between the game and the decision problem, mark where the mapping breaks, separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that do not, and explain what the analogy quietly imports about how a rational chooser would reason before knowing their position. Does the maximin conclusion survive the disanalogies, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If the latter, what comparison would carry it better?
