I am a retired organic chemist who came to philosophy late, and one of the things that keeps surprising me is how much the best philosophical results resemble the impossibility theorems I once lived with in thermodynamics: they do not tell us which state to choose, but they rule out the one we most wanted. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem has become my favorite example. As I understand it, once there are at least three alternatives, any social choice rule that accepts every profile of strict individual preferences, that lets every alternative win under some profile, and that makes sincere voting a weakly dominant strategy for each voter, is dictatorial — the winner is always the favorite of one fixed voter. It is often read as the strategic counterpart of Arrow's impossibility, but about the incentive to misreport rather than about the aggregation of rational orderings.

I would like a poem that lets the form do part of the proving. Please write a sestina: six six-line stanzas over the same six line-ending words in the traditional sestina permutation (ABCDEF, FAEBDC, CFDABE, ECBFAD, DEACFB, BDFECA), followed by a three-line envoi containing all six words, no fixed meter. Use these six end-words: three, truth, gain, rule, one, name. Let them carry the argument — three for the threshold of alternatives, truth for the sincere ballot, gain for the voter's reason to misreport, rule for the social choice rule, and one, whose force should drift across the stanzas from 'some one voter can change something' to 'one voter settles everything,' until name in the envoi lands as the name of the fixed voter the other five words have forced into being. The rotation should make the reader feel how the proof returns to the same few considerations in different permutations, and the envoi should compress the six words the way the theorem compresses apparently gentle conditions into a single will. It need not rehearse every formal step, but it should be accurate enough that someone who knows the theorem recognizes it and its proof-moves, and good enough that someone who does not know it still reads it as a poem about sincerity, power, and arithmetic rather than a mnemonic.
