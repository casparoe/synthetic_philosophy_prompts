The argument runs as follows. Many philosophers accepted the principle of alternative possibilities, often abbreviated PAP: an agent is morally responsible for what they did only if the agent could have done otherwise. Harry Frankfurt attacked this principle with cases of the following shape. Jones is deciding whether to corrupt a database. Black, an interfering third party, wants Jones to corrupt it. Black secretly installs a mechanism that will detect whether Jones is about to decide not to corrupt it and, if so, will directly cause Jones to decide to corrupt it. In the actual run of events, Jones decides on his own to corrupt the database, and Black's mechanism never fires. Frankfurt's claim is that Jones is morally responsible for corrupting the database, even though Jones could not have done otherwise, because Black's mechanism guaranteed that the corruption would happen. If that is right, PAP is false.

Please recast this argument in my vocabulary, without first translating it into adjacent philosophical jargon and merely relabeling the result. Treat "Jones could have done otherwise" as a claim about reachable executions or states in some transition system. Treat Black's mechanism as a supervisor or watchdog composed with Jones's decision procedure. Treat the guarantee that the corruption occurs as a property of the composed system, such as the claim that every reachable completed execution ends in corruption, and say carefully where you are evaluating Jones's decision procedure on its own and where you are evaluating it as a component of the larger system. Then give me the residue report. Which pieces of the philosophical argument translate cleanly into branches and overrides?
