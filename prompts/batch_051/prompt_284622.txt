I've been reading Polybius, Histories book 6, because I keep seeing people cite him in arguments about whether modern democracies are on the same track as the Roman Republic, and I'm stuck on two passages that seem to say opposite things. The first is the mixed-constitution claim:

"As for the Roman constitution, it had three elements, each of them possessing sovereign powers: and their respective share of power in the whole state had been regulated with such a scrupulous regard to equality and equilibrium, that no one could say for certain, not even a native, whether the constitution as a whole were an aristocracy or democracy or despotism."

The second comes right after his account of the cycle of constitutions:

"This is the regular cycle of constitutional revolutions, and the natural order in which constitutions change, are transformed, and return again to their original stage. If a man have a clear grasp of these principles he may perhaps make a mistake as to the dates at which this or that will happen to a particular constitution; but he will rarely be entirely mistaken as to the stage of growth or decay at which it has arrived, or as to the point at which it will undergo some revolutionary change. However, it is in the case of the Roman constitution that this method of inquiry will most fully teach us its formation, its growth, and zenith, as well as the changes awaiting it in the future; for this, if any constitution ever did, owed, as I said just now, its original foundation and growth to natural causes, and to natural causes will owe its decay."

My friend says this is just Polybius admitting the Roman Republic would fall, and that people who use him to praise checks and balances in modern democracies are citing the first passage and ignoring the second. Is that fair? How can a constitution with a nearly perfect internal equilibrium also be subject to the natural cycle of decay? Does the mixed constitution escape the cycle, delay it, or just make the decline less destructive? And does his later prediction that Rome will slip into mob-rule once wealth and office-seeking get out of hand follow from the same model that produced the praise of equilibrium, or is it a different causal story? I'm looking for a reconciliation that doesn't just wave the second passage away as "he changed his mind" and that doesn't just say "texts contradict." Also, if the mixed checks only protect against certain kinds of decay, what exactly are the vulnerabilities left open, and would a modern liberal democracy with separation of powers and popular elections have any obvious analogue to those Roman vulnerabilities?
