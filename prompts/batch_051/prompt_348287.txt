Two friends of mine got into it the other night about whether there can be reasons without anyone doing the reasoning. Maya had been on an evolution kick and kept using the phrase "free-floating rationales." The argument went, near enough, like this:

Maya: Take a cheetah's legs. They're built to sprint. The reason they have that structure is that sprinting helped their ancestors catch prey. But no cheetah ever reasoned "let's build sprinting legs," and no designer did either. So the reason is doing real explanatory work — it's why the legs are there — but it was never in anyone's head.

Dev: That's a cause, not a reason. Sprinting helped survival, and survival caused that leg shape to persist. You, now, with a textbook in hand, are saying the legs are "for" sprinting. The legs don't have a reason; they have a causal history.

Maya: But the causal history is precisely one that sorted on sprinting. The variants that were worse at it left fewer descendants. Natural selection is a process that's sensitive to such reasons — not by representing them, but by sorting on their effects. That's what I'm calling a free-floating rationale: the rationale explains the trait, but there's no reasoner.

Dev: Selection isn't sensitive to reasons, it's sensitive to reproduction. It'll fix a trait that's bad for the organism if it happens to correlate with higher reproduction, and it can drive a lineage right off a cliff. A process that sometimes tracks "solutions" and sometimes tracks disasters doesn't earn the word "reasons." You only say "reason" when the outcome happens to look good from your point of view.

Maya: That's an objection to treating selection as wise, not to treating the rationale as free-floating. Even a bad outcome has a rationale: the harmful trait spread because it was tied to whatever reproduced better. "Because it was tied to higher reproduction" is the reason it's common. You're still giving a reason — an explanatory one.

Dev: Then you've made "reason" just a synonym for "cause," and you've lost the normative bite reasons are supposed to have. Reasons don't just explain, they justify — they say why something ought to be that way. A harmful trait's spread explains but justifies nothing. If every cause counts as a reason, the word has nothing left to do.

Maya: I can accept that explanatory reasons and justificatory reasons are different. I'm only claiming the explanatory ones float free. The normativity, insofar as there is any, comes from the problem–solution structure: the trait was a solution to a selective problem, even though nobody posed the problem.

Dev: But a problem is only a problem relative to a goal. Nature doesn't pose problems; it just kills things. Your "solution" story is you, after the fact, glossing differential survival in purposive language. And that's the very thing I won't grant — that the environment is somehow handing out tasks.

They went around like this for another hour and never resolved it.
