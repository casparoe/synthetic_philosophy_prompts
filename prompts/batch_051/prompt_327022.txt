I have spent the past year writing a novel whose hinge is an identity statement. The reader has known a figure only as "the night courier"; midway through the book, she is revealed to be the mayor. In trying to make that revelation work on the page, I have fallen into a philosophical problem about identity statements, and I would like a precise account of it rather than a literary hand-wave. Here are the judgments I have arrived at on a set of cases; each seems right to me on its own, but I cannot state the principle that binds them together. (i) "The night courier is the night courier" would be dead air; it tells a reader nothing. (ii) "The night courier is the mayor" tells the reader something real, and "The mayor is the night courier" does so as well; the order of the terms does not seem to matter. (iii) Yet if I print "The night courier is the mayor" again a chapter after the revelation, it tells an attentive reader nothing new; its informativeness seems relative to what the reader already knows. (iv) In an ordinary narrative sentence such as "The night courier burned the letters," substituting "the mayor" for "the night courier" preserves truth, since the two names pick out one person; but in "Lena suspects that the night courier burned the letters," the substitution fails, because Lena has not made the connection, and the resulting sentence would be false. (v) The point is not confined to proper names: "The masked person who burned the letters is the mayor" is informative in just the way (ii) is. (vi) However, if I stipulate the interchangeability from the outset—the narrator declares that "the alchemist" and "the Marquess" will name one and the same person—then "The alchemist is the Marquess" seems to tell the reader nothing about the story once the declaration has been made.

I keep half-forming a principle: the two expressions present the same person under different guises, and the revelation lies in learning that these guises coincide. But I cannot make that precise, and it seems to sit badly with (iv), where the two expressions are interchangeable in one sentence but not in another. Is there a coherent principle about meaning, reference, or what a sentence conveys that captures these verdicts? State it precisely, show how it handles each of the six cases, tell me which of my verdicts it would force me to abandon, and apply it to a case I have not yet settled: once Lena finally learns the identity, is it then acceptable to write "Lena suspects that the mayor burned the letters," and is the sentence "The person the narrator calls 'the night courier' is the mayor" informative in the same sense as (ii)?
