After a long argument with a friend about where persuasion ends and manipulation begins, I settled on four criteria for legitimate persuasion: it works through considerations the target can recognize and assess for themselves; it does not amplify emotion so as to displace or short-circuit deliberation; it does not exploit the target's vulnerabilities, dependencies, or the costs of refusal; and it is transparent about the fact that influence is being attempted and about any material information bearing on the decision.

Consider a case that does not fit cleanly. A friend wants me to visit an aunt from whom I am estranged and who is ill. They believe the visit would be good for me, but they are also tired of being the person to whom I confess guilt about the estrangement. They know that I become receptive after talking about family memories, that I dislike seeing myself as someone who lets old grievances harden, and that I respond strongly to stories of people who waited too long and regretted it. So they steer the conversation toward family recollections, tell me a true story about a colleague who delayed reconciling with a parent and now regrets it, and frame the visit as a chance to act on values I already profess. Everything they say is true and relevant, and they would answer honestly if I asked whether they were trying to influence me, but they selected the timing, the facts, and the framing because they know which of my deliberative habits they trigger.

Is this manipulation or legitimate persuasion? Work through the four criteria one by one, say where each yields a clear verdict and where it underdetermines one, and then say which, if any, of the criteria the case shows to be doing no independent work, misstated, or not necessary.
