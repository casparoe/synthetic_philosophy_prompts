I'm trying to settle on a working definition of preference — or, more precisely, of the relation someone bears to an option when we say they prefer it, and of the utility that economists take a utility function to represent — because the standard formulations I know lose their grip as soon as I press them against concrete choices. My desiderata are these. A definition should treat choice as strong but defeasible evidence for preference: choosing the bus over the train is a reason to think someone prefers the bus, but preferring the bus should be more than a synonym for having chosen it, since the preference is also used to explain the choice, to predict later choices, and to make sense of trade-offs. At the same time, the definition should not be so idealized that it applies only to fully informed, perfectly self-controlled agents, because most of the people I want the concept for are neither.

Here are seven cases I'd like the definition to be run against. (1) A shopper buys the cheaper of two jars of pasta sauce, though she would have chosen the pricier one if they cost the same. (2) An addict says he hates the drug, has asked for help stopping, and uses it anyway. (3) A dieter keeps a bar of chocolate in the house, looks at it every evening, and does not eat it. (4) A gambler takes a 50 percent chance of $120 over a sure $55. (5) A voter supports a candidate whose platform, by the voter's own stated priorities, would leave him materially worse off. (6) A patient accepts the first treatment a doctor describes without genuinely processing the second. (7) A person always ends up with the default retirement plan, but switches whenever the employer changes the default. I am fairly settled that (1) does reveal a preference — for the sauce rather than for the bundle — and that (2) should not be read as revealing a preference for using the drug, given what the addict says and does about it; the other five feel genuinely ambiguous to me.

Please propose a definition that gets the cases I regard as clear right, run it through all seven, and tell me explicitly which cases it handles poorly or gives no verdict on. Then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts as correct. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the definition and in each of the individual verdicts, and single out the one point you are least sure about.
