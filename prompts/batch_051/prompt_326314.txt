I've been going down a Popper rabbit hole after an argument with a friend about whether string theory has made any predictions, and I keep coming back to a passage in his work where he argues—if I'm compressing him fairly—that a good theory is fundamentally a prohibition: it says certain observable things won't happen, and the more such things it rules out, the more it actually says about the world. A theory that is compatible with every possible observation explains nothing. As an abstract epistemology point, this feels right to me.

But then I started wondering what would happen if you took the principle seriously as an actual allocation rule. Imagine a funding agency that introduces a falsifiability index. Every grant proposal gets scored by the number of distinct, reproducible observations it explicitly forbids, weighted by how surprising or specific those forbiddances are. Proposals below a threshold get no money. The panel is told to treat vague theories, theories with endlessly added epicycles, and theories protected by "just this once" auxiliary assumptions as having near-zero forbiddance. Scores are public.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral or epistemological position. What would it incentivize among individual researchers, among fields, and among funding bodies? How would people game the index without doing better science? What happens to areas like cosmology, epidemiology, or historical sciences where practitioners often can't forbid many observations but seem to be doing something more than astrology? Does the index fail in a way that's fixable by tweaking the weighting, or does it show that Popper's criterion stops being useful the moment you operationalize it this specifically?
