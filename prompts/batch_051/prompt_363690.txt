Pronatalist policy packages of the kind enacted in several below-replacement-fertility countries typically combine a large per-birth cash bonus, taxpayer-funded fertility treatment, housing and pension advantages that grow with the number of children, and public campaigns framing childbearing as a service to the nation's future. Yet the same governments often maintain, in environmental or migration contexts, that they take no position on whether the world would be better with more people, and they sometimes defend the policies as neutral measures to stabilize the age structure or to support parents' preferences. What theory of the value of population size does this pattern of policy presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit population axiology at work: does the package require that additional lives make an outcome better, or is it compatible with person-affecting views, average views, purely fiscal views about age structure, or several of these? Identify where the elements of the package presuppose different things, and explain what would have to change if the state consistently adopted a total view, an average view, or a strictly person-affecting view.
