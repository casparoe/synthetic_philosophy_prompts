After retiring from a career in the earth sciences, I have been reading philosophy in a more systematic way than I ever had time for, and I recently joined a discussion group on death and the meaning of life. In advance of one meeting, I read a popular survey article that summarizes Bernard Williams's treatment of the Makropulos case. As the article presents it, Williams shows that an unending life would eventually become unbearable. Any finite stock of goals and projects would sooner or later be completed, abandoned, or exhausted, and a human being can be engaged in life only through goals of that kind. An immortal person would therefore inevitably run out of sources of engagement and settle into a boredom so deep that no episode of pleasure could break it. The article concludes that immortality is not something a coherent person can rationally want, since the only routes out of the tedium would involve becoming so different from one's present self as to amount to a different person, which the author suggests is just another kind of death.

I suspect that this is too strong. I have since seen references to Williams's distinction between categorical desires and mere preferences or pastimes, and I have the impression that his actual argument is more carefully conditional than the survey implies: perhaps a thesis about what an endless life would be like for a person with a fixed character, rather than a proof that every conceivable immortal existence must go stale. But I do not know the text well enough to say where the distortions are. Which parts of the summary are faithful to Williams, where does it overstate or conflate ideas, and does its concluding claim that a rational desire for immortality is incoherent survive once the accurate parts are sorted out?
