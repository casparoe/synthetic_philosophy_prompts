I'm taking a course this term on probability and the epistemology of coincidence, and our discussion of the birthday paradox has made me reconsider a rule I have followed for years without really examining it. My rule is this: I do not update on personal anecdotes of striking coincidences at all. For instance, a friend tells me she dreamed on Tuesday night that her brother would be in a minor car accident, and on Wednesday he was; an acquaintance discovers that his new colleague shares his exact birth date, his small hometown, and an unusual first name; I myself think of a friend I have not contacted in years, and she texts me ten minutes later. In each case I set the experience aside as carrying no evidential weight. My reasoning is that the space of possible coincidences is enormous, the number of occasions across a lifetime on which some coincidence could occur is likewise enormous, and people reliably notice hits while forgetting the far more numerous misses. So the fact that some improbable-seeming coincidence has occurred somewhere, to someone, is close to certain, and the fact that it happened to this person on this occasion gives me no reason to revise anything. Because I usually cannot recover the full denominator — the uneventful dreams, the strangers who shared nothing, the thoughts of a friend followed by no message — I treat the numerator as unusable. I apply the rule even when the anecdote is offered as evidence for a conclusion I would otherwise reject, such as precognition or telepathy, and even when the coincidence is reported in unusual detail.

My question is whether this policy is rationally defensible, or whether I have converted a sound caution about base rates into a blanket refusal to consider evidence. Is there a version of the rule that keeps its protection against the birthday-paradox effect — the tendency to be surprised by coincidences that are actually highly probable over a large enough class of events — while still allowing me to update when the relevant reference class is genuinely narrow and knowable? And how could I tell, from the inside, whether I am applying the rule because it tracks the truth or because it conveniently spares me from taking unsettling experiences seriously?
