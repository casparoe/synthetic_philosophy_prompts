At a seminar on reasoning under uncertainty, the discussion kept circling a split over what the word "possible" contributes to a probability judgment. One camp held that to call an outcome possible is just to say that a model assigns it probability greater than zero—or at least that a responsible model should do so if we are to take the possibility seriously. The other camp held that possibility is a distinct and typically wider notion: an outcome can be genuinely possible even when a model assigns it probability zero, the standard cases being a fair countable lottery (each ticket loses with probability one, yet some ticket wins), a dart or spinner landing on an exact designated point (probability zero under the uniform measure, yet it lands somewhere), and a continuous variable taking a particular exact value. A few people added a third, more deliberative thought: even if a merely possible bad outcome receives probability zero, it does not follow that it deserves zero weight in decision, because the model's zero may be an artifact of idealization, convention, or an outcome the model never represented.

I am putting together a handout for advanced undergraduates and first-year graduate students in a philosophy-of-science course on idealization, and a colleague who teaches risk analysis wants to reuse it. I would like it to be a sorting instrument: a decision tree, or a short quiz with a scoring key, that classifies people's commitments about how modal and probabilistic notions relate without requiring them to know the labels in advance. Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of named positions. Begin with the diagnostic questions or claims, and let each branch be labeled by the specific claim or answer that splits it from its siblings. Use concrete cases—lotteries, darts, spinners, forecasts, reserve prices on risks, decisions under model uncertainty—phrased so that a respondent can answer without knowing formal semantics or measure theory. Each leaf should give the position's name if it has one (otherwise "unnamed"), a one-sentence statement of the view, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where a possible leaf has no actual defenders, keep it and say why it is empty (because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored), and where two positions cannot be separated by the diagnostic questions, flag that instead of forcing a distinction. Aim for at most nine leaves and at most eight diagnostic questions. Make sure the tree separates at least the following commitments: possibility just is positive probability; possibility is consistency with what one knows; possibility survives a probability-zero assignment and can still demand positive decision weight; and possibility is harmless once a sober model assigns it probability zero. Because my purpose is practical, do not end with a taxonomy that treats all of these as equivalent or leaves the decision-relevant disagreements unresolved.
