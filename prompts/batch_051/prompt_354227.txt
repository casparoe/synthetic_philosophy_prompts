I am in a seminar on the cognitive neuropsychiatry and philosophy of delusion, focused this term on the two-factor theory and the question of whether delusions are beliefs. The instructor has a habit of giving us a vignette and then announcing whether the subject counts as believing the content of the state in question. So far she has ruled as follows. A man who misreads a parking sign, is sure he will be towed, and jogs back to move his car: belief. A Capgras patient who says her husband has been replaced by an impostor, phones her adult children to demand they come identify him, and barricades the bedroom door at night: belief. A patient with persecutory delusions who says his neighbors are flooding his apartment with gas, seals the vents, and sleeps in his car: belief. A person with contamination obsessions who says, "My hands might be contaminated," but adds that she knows this is not really true and washes only to reduce dread: not a belief. A patient with schizophrenia who flatly recites that the neighbors are poisoning him, reports no particular worry, and takes no precautions: not a belief. A hypnotized volunteer who was told to experience her arm as paralyzed, says it feels limp, but catches a falling cup and later says she never really believed it: not a belief.

Next week's case is a patient with Capgras syndrome involving his wife. He says with complete conviction and evident sadness that the woman at home is not his wife, that she looks right but does not feel like her, and that he is sure of it. Yet he has not avoided or confronted her; he continues to share meals and a bed with her, speaks to her warmly, and when asked why he has not called the police or tried to check her identification, he says it would be pointless because a competent impostor would have the documents anyway. Predict what our instructor will say: does he believe it? I can see at least three principles that fit her verdicts so far: a functional or action-guidance account of belief, a conviction-or-avowal account, and a two-factor-friendly account on which a sincerely endorsed delusion formed in response to an anomalous experience counts as a belief. For each, say what it predicts about the new case, identify which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog, give your best guess about her ruling and the reasons for it, and describe a further vignette that would most cleanly settle which principle she is actually running.
