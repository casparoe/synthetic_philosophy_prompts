I have been trying to work out what I actually think about lying to children, and I have found it helpful to record my reactions to particular cases before trying to formulate any principle. Here are my verdicts.

1. Raising a child with the full Santa Claus story and the tooth fairy story, and letting her believe them until she notices the problems for herself: fine. I cannot yet say why this comes out differently from the cases I condemn below.

2. When our dog was put down, telling my four-year-old that he had gone to live on a farm: I keep going back and forth. This is the case I most want the principle to settle.

3. After a death in the family, telling a small child that Grandma is asleep rather than that she has died: not fine. It feels like an evasion of something the child has a right to know, and the substitute belief will only cause further trouble.

4. Telling a child that the ice cream truck plays music only when it has run out of ice cream, in order to avoid buying any: not fine, even though no one is really harmed.

5. Telling a child that the car will not start until her seatbelt is buckled, when in fact it would: fine.

6. Saying "this won't hurt" immediately before giving a child an injection that will hurt: not fine.

7. To a child who is afraid of monsters under the bed: "there are no such things as monsters, and nothing is going to get you": fine.

8. Calling a child's mediocre drawing the most beautiful drawing I have ever seen: fine.

9. A child asks whether she was adopted; her parents say no, intending to tell her when she is older: not fine.

I am aware that (5) and (6) look structurally similar — in both, a false statement is used to secure compliance with a safety measure — and I cannot yet say what distinguishes them. I want a principle that either explains the difference or forces me to revise one of my verdicts.

Is there a single principle, or a small set of principles, that captures all or nearly all of these judgments? State it precisely, and say which cases motivate each clause. Then apply it to case (2) and to one or two further cases of your own choosing that are not variants of the ones I listed. Be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate, and say whether you think the failure lies with the principle or with my verdict.
