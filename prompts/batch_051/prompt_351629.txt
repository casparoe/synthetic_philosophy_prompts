Question: Can a corporation be an appropriate target of moral blame, as opposed to merely being held legally liable? Present and assess at least one argument on each side of the debate.

Student's answer:
Corporations can be morally blamed, not merely fined or subjected to liability. The law already recognizes corporations as persons for purposes of contracts and criminal punishment, so the default view should be that they are apt targets of blame as well. French's classic argument supports this: on his view, a corporation qualifies as a moral person because it can be fined and penalized through the legal system, and legal personality shows that society already treats its acts as the corporation's own. More strongly, a corporation has an internal decision structure—explicit policies, procedures, and chains of command—that lets it form intentions not reducible to the intentions of any single employee. When a firm adopts a pricing policy that no individual employee chose, the policy is intentional at the corporate level. Since the corporation has intentions of its own, it is an appropriate object of moral judgment. The standard objection is that a corporation lacks consciousness and therefore cannot feel guilt or remorse. But this proves too much: blame does not require that the wrongdoer actually feel bad, since we continue to blame people who are entirely unrepentant. Therefore corporations can legitimately be morally blamed.
