In two weeks I am giving a paper at a joint workshop of a political theory seminar and a formal methods group where I will defend this thesis: in Rawls's original position, the parties should choose principles of justice by maximin — select the arrangement whose worst-off representative position does best — rather than by the rule Harsanyi derives, on which the parties maximize expected welfare over an equal probability of occupying each position. I accept that Harsanyi's theorem is valid as mathematics, and that it does establish that von Neumann–Morgenstern rational parties with those equal probabilities would choose to maximize average utility; my argument is that the theorem cannot do the normative work claimed for it here, because it applies the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms and a definite equal-probability assignment to a deliberative situation in which exactly those things are at stake — the veil of ignorance is a model of fairness, not a lottery the parties actually face. My argument sketch, so the objections have something precise to target, is: (1) maximin is normally an unreasonably timid decision rule, but the original position has the exceptional features that license it — catastrophic worst outcomes for the representative worst-off, no probabilities a party is entitled to treat as genuine, and a non-repeatable choice of basic structure; (2) Harsanyi's derivation of average utilitarianism is conditional on von Neumann–Morgenstern rationality plus equal probabilities, and in the original position those are substantive commitments rather than neutral assumptions; (3) fairness between representative positions is better tracked by the difference principle than by average utilitarianism, which permits gains to the better-off to outweigh losses to the worse-off. The audience is mixed and will not be gentle. About ten political philosophers, most Rawlsian or Rawls-adjacent, who care more about whether the original position models the right thing than about the formal details; two of them are skeptics who think the veil-of-ignorance device does no independent work. Alongside them sit four decision and social-choice theorists, at least two of them committed Harsanyians who will read my refusal to apply expected utility behind the veil as either a misunderstanding of what the theorem establishes or outright axiom-denial.
