For a two-page handout in an upper-level philosophy of science seminar, write a ten-question sorting quiz called "Which interpretation of probability do you actually hold?" The quiz sorts respondents into one of seven categories: the classical indifference/symmetry interpretation, the logical (objective-Bayesian) interpretation, frequentism, the propensity interpretation, subjective (personalist) Bayesianism, the best-system (Humean-chance) interpretation, and a deflationary/instrumentalist view that denies there is a single real quantity called "the probability" in many of the cases.

Each question should present a concrete case with two or three answer options expressing a substantive judgment about that case, never a preference for a label. No question may ask directly whether probabilities are frequencies, degrees of belief, or propensities. Use at least the following cases: a fair coin about to be tossed; a coin of unknown bias; a fixed but currently unknown value, such as the trillionth digit of pi or a sealed prediction; a single unrepeatable event such as the outcome of the next national election; a deterministic but chaotic system, such as a die thrown from fully specified initial conditions; and a radioactive atom's probability of decaying within the next hour.

The questions and the scoring key should be built around five separating fault lines:

(a) whether probabilities are features of the world that can exist and have determinate values unknown to any mind, or features of an agent's information and commitments;

(b) whether symmetry or total ignorance by itself forces a determinate numerical probability;

(c) whether a single unrepeatable event can have a probability at all;

(d) whether a deterministic system can carry genuine, non-epistemic probabilities;

(e) whether two agents with exactly the same evidence can rationally assign different probabilities to the same proposition.

Construct the scoring key by first laying out the combinations of answers that the five fault lines allow, then locating the seven categories within that space. Mark any combination that no listed category occupies and say briefly whether it looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored. Also flag any pair of categories the quiz cannot cleanly separate and state what additional question would separate them. End with a short note identifying the two or three questions most likely to misclassify respondents and explaining what the key should do about those cases.
