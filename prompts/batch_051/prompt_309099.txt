I keep noticing what looks like the same structure in two debates that rarely talk to each other, and I want to know whether the resemblance is substantive enough to do any work.

The first debate is about the state's monopoly on legitimate violence. The standard defense says that if no central authority successfully claims the exclusive right to use physical force within a territory, enforcement becomes a private, decentralized affair; each person or faction polices its own grievances; and the predictable result is a spiral of feuds, preemptive attacks, and security competition. The standard critique replies that the central enforcer is itself a source of arbitrary violence and a single point of failure, and that a monopoly is not necessary for public safety: decentralized, community-level practices of de-escalation, accountability, and dispute resolution could do the work without concentrating force in a police apparatus.

The second debate is in distributed computing. Early designs assumed that a set of processes could stay coordinated only if a designated leader serialized every decision, and that removing the leader would produce a split brain, in which different parts of the system make incompatible decisions. The field later produced consensus protocols that let a collection of processes, some of which may crash or even behave adversarially, converge on a shared decision without a central coordinator, along with impossibility results bounding what can be guaranteed under which failure assumptions. The results I have in mind include the following: in a purely asynchronous system—one with no bounds on how long messages may take or how fast processes may run—the presence of even one process that may crash is enough to prevent any deterministic protocol from guaranteeing both that the non-faulty processes always agree and that they eventually decide; and protocols that tolerate Byzantine (arbitrarily misbehaving) processes can do so only up to a bounded fraction of them. The computing debate also contains the same anti-centralization rhetoric: a single coordinator is a bottleneck and a trusted point whose corruption compromises the whole system, whereas decentralized designs are advertised as removing the need to trust anyone in particular. The guarantees that matter are usually divided into safety (no bad thing ever happens, such as two incompatible decisions being made) and liveness (a good thing eventually happens, such as a decision actually being reached).

The mapping I have in mind is this:

- The state's monopoly on legitimate violence corresponds to the single leader or central coordinator.
- Private actors with their own capacity for violence correspond to processes that may crash or behave adversarially.
- The predicted spiral of private feuds and preemptive attacks corresponds to split brain or non-termination.
- Reform, defund, and abolish positions correspond roughly to constrained leader election, reducing dependence on the leader, and fully leaderless consensus.
- The legitimacy of the central enforcer corresponds to the safety and liveness properties a consensus protocol is supposed to preserve.

Is this a genuine structural isomorphism, a loose analogy, or a confusion? Please first state where the mapping holds and where it breaks, including what would correspond on the political side to a protocol's specification, to a Byzantine fault, and to a quorum. Then say what would have to be true of both sides for the impossibility and feasibility results from distributed computing to transfer to the debate about policing: in particular, could a polity even be said to run a consensus protocol, and is there any political analogue of proving that a protocol terminates? Finally, name at least one concrete prediction the parallel seems to license that a defender of the parallel should regard as false, and say whether that falsity is local or undermines the analogy as a whole.
