On my campus philosophy podcast we always try to make guests defend the weakest joint in their view, not just explain it. Next episode I'm interviewing a philosopher who defends a broadly Schopenhauerian ethics of compassion: compassion is the one thing that can make an action morally good, and the barrier between my suffering and yours is ultimately an illusion, so the fitting reaction to any suffering is to relieve it. She's read all the Nietzschean objections and she has one response that keeps showing up: when someone gives an example where pity goes pathological, she says they're describing sentimental, soft-hearted pity, not the calm objective compassion Schopenhauer actually meant. She's smooth enough that this usually ends the exchange right there.

I need your help writing eight to ten interview questions that won't let her rest on that move — questions that force her to say what "calm objective compassion" actually amounts to in the bad cases, plus a follow-up for each that's aimed at the answer she's most likely to give.

Make sure the set covers, among other things:

- Nietzsche's point that pity may multiply suffering rather than reduce it, since the spectator now hurts too.
- How much of the ethics stands or falls with Schopenhauer's metaphysics (the will as thing in itself, tat tvam asi, the idea that individual separateness is a kind of error).
- The empirically familiar failures of compassion: empathic distress, burnout, the drop-off in concern as the number of victims rises, and the way it favors people who are near us or look like us.
- Where shared joy fits. If she says a well-formed compassion also affirms and shares others' happiness, push on whether that stretches the word past what Schopenhauer meant.

When you write the follow-ups, target the real sophisticated move, not a caricature: if she leans on the contrast between compassion and emotional contagion, ask what keeps that distinction from collapsing when the spectator is worn out or the sufferer is someone she hates; if she says impartial institutions exist to correct compassion's biases, ask whether compassion has at that point stopped being the only source of moral worth.
