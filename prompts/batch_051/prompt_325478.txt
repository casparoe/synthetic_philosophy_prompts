Two friends of mine, Alex and Sam, spent most of a dinner party arguing about a three-way distinction between honor cultures, dignity cultures, and victimhood cultures, and I could not tell who was getting the better of it. I'd like you to referee their exchange move by move.

(1) Alex opened with the case for treating the three as stages of moral progress. He claimed that dignity culture is plainly the most morally advanced: it teaches people that their worth does not depend on being treated with deference, so an insult no longer demands violence; it replaces private vengeance with impartial third parties; and it avoids both the pathologies of an honor culture—dueling, feuds, the obsessive policing of women's reputation—and the status competition he sees in victimhood culture, where people compete to present themselves as the most injured.

(2) Sam objected that Alex was comparing the ideals of one system with the pathologies of the others. Honor cultures, she said, predominate where the state is absent or predatory; when calling the police is not an option, the only reliable deterrent is a reputation for answering harm with harm. So honor norms often look less like a moral defect than like a rational adaptation to an environment without trustworthy third-party enforcement. And dignity culture's habit of shrugging off insults, she added, is possible only because someone else stands ready to use force on your behalf; outsourcing your enforcement to a sheriff is not morally nobler than doing it yourself. It is just safer.

(3) Alex replied that she was confusing an explanation with a justification. The fact that a norm is adaptive under state failure tells us why it exists, not whether the conduct it requires is morally good. What matters is the content of the norm: does it recognize that strangers have claims on you independent of whether you fear them? Dignity culture's norm of nonretaliation does, and honor culture's imperative to make yourself feared does not. And there is, he insisted, a moral difference between coercion centralized and constrained by law and coercion left to an individual's sense of injury, even if both use force.

(4) Sam answered that constraint by law matters only for those the law actually serves. For communities the legal system has historically ignored or attacked, dignity culture can collapse into an instruction to absorb abuse quietly, which is not obviously a moral improvement. The move toward victimhood culture that Alex dismisses as regression is, in large part, a response to exactly that gap: when formal institutions will not grant you a hearing, you appeal to a public audience and make a claim of harm. That is not thin-skinned status seeking; it is third-party enforcement by reputation rather than by courts. If Alex is willing to count access to third-party enforcement as a mark of moral development, he owes an account of why a courtroom's audience is more principled than a crowd's.

I could not work out which moves went through and which were beside the point. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the four moves, say which parts are sound, which are overstated or mistaken, and whether the move actually answers the one before it. Then say what Alex and Sam are really disagreeing about—whether it is the moral status of the practices themselves, the role of institutions, the standard for calling one cultural pattern better than another, or something else—and what a defensible answer to their underlying question would have to include.
