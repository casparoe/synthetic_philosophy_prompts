I sit on the grants committee of a small private foundation that funds cultural survival and documentation projects, and I have to write the decision memo for a proposal that has us split down the middle. The applicants are two folklorists, a linguistic anthropologist, and a small regional museum. They're asking for about $65,000 to reconstruct a particular style of polyphonic harvest singing that was once specific to a single valley. The last people who learned it as a living practice died in the late 1960s, and there is no community today that uses it; at most, a few older residents remember hearing grandparents hum bits of it. The plan is to piece together a performable reconstruction from old field recordings and notebooks, teach it to a volunteer choir, and make a short documentary plus a teaching site. Half the committee says this is exactly what we exist for: the practice is part of the human inheritance, and its value doesn't vanish just because no living community currently carries it. The other half says that's taxidermy: a tradition is something people hold and pass on, and once the culture-bearers are gone what's left is an archival object, not a living practice, so we'd be paying to stage a revival for an audience that never asked for one. The underlying question seems to be whether a tradition can have value beyond the people who hold it. Our next meeting is in two weeks and I need to circulate something actionable, but I dont want to pretend the losing side is stupid.

Could you act as a professional philosopher who works on value theory and the ethics of cultural preservation? Start by brainstorming the strongest reasons on each side before you settle on a recommendation. Then draft the short memo I'll circulate. It should recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions—with a clear bottom line the committee can actually vote on, any conditions or time frames spelled out, and an honest, serious treatment of the strongest argument on the side you're rejecting. Keep it formal enough to circulate but not dry. Also, please be careful to distinguish what is genuinely settled among people who study cultural value and heritage policy from what is contested; don't present a contested philosophical view as if it were the field's consensus. Please write in American English.
