I chair the monthly capital-projects review committee for a mid-sized city. We decide whether to approve infrastructure projects — roads, drainage, facility renovations, transit upgrades — and each approval carries a cost and schedule estimate that goes to the city council and into the budget. The committee meets once a month, proposals reach members two weeks ahead, and the members are engineers and finance officers who will never read philosophy. We have a recurring failure: our detailed bottom-up estimates, built from materials, crews, weather windows, and permitting timelines, run 30 to 50 percent low, while the few reference-class numbers we have pulled from comparable projects completed in other cities come much closer. Yet the committee keeps discounting the reference-class numbers because they ignore our specific plans, and keeps trusting the bottom-up plans because they are detailed.

I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the inside-view/outside-view distinction and in what epistemic modesty actually demands, for deciding how much weight each kind of evidence should get in a cost or schedule estimate. Write the procedure so a committee member can run it without knowing the theory: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure forces an outside-view anchor and under what conditions the detailed plan may override it; where it stops and either refuses to issue a single-point estimate or hands the decision to a person; and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, flagged as such. Then apply the procedure to two worked cases: a wastewater treatment plant upgrade, and a citywide bike-lane network. Make sure at least one application produces an answer the committee will not like, and say so explicitly. Also state what the procedure cannot do — for example, when the reference class is thin, heterogeneous, or from a different era — and the failure mode it is most likely to reintroduce if misused. End with a last line beginning "Summary:" that gives a one-sentence summary of the procedure.
