I am preparing a unit for an introductory ethics seminar in which I want to pair Plato's ring of Gyges with H.G. Wells's The Invisible Man. The Glaucon reading I am working from is roughly this: justice is practiced only because people fear the reputational and punitive consequences of being seen to act unjustly, so a person with the power of invisibility — guaranteed impunity — would have no remaining reason to be just, and the just person and the unjust person would become indistinguishable. I want you to read the novel through that lens and the thought experiment through the novel with equal seriousness. Does Griffin read as a confirming case for Glaucon, or does the novel quietly show that the thought experiment's premise is not so easy to secure — that invisibility in an embodied, causal world does not deliver impunity but only rearranges exposure? Work through the concrete ways in which Griffin remains legible and constrained while invisible: footprints and breathing, cold and hunger, locked doors and windows, sound, the need for food and shelter, his dependence on Thomas Marvel, the alertness of the villagers in Iping, and the way he is ultimately cornered, betrayed, and killed. Identify which scenes carry the most philosophical weight and which would be most productive to teach. Then say where the Gyges reading distorts the novel rather than illuminating it: Griffin is already arrogant, violent, and unscrupulous before the experiment; the invisibility does not so much corrupt a just man as strip the last barriers from a deeply unjust one; and the novel is at least as much about scientific ambition, class resentment, and the body's stubborn claims as it is about the morality of impunity. Finally, state what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that the bare thought experiment misses — whether that deepens the challenge to morality, begins an answer to it, or does something else. Finish with a single last line beginning 'Summary:' that states your overall reading in one sentence.
