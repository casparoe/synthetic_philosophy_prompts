I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so forgive me if I get the terms wrong. My next book has two characters who've been arguing for years about how to put ethics into an AI: one is a top-down person who thinks you should write the moral principles into the system explicitly — rights, duties, prohibitions, the sort of thing you can inspect and audit — and let a reasoning layer apply them, with machine learning only handling perception and the situation model. The other is a bottom-up person who thinks moral competence is to tacit and context-bound to write down, so you should train the system on human moral feedback and large corpora of cases and let the norms emerge, with principles as post-hoc descriptions at best. For the novel I need the document these two should have written after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Draft it for me: a numbered list of claims both would actually sign; then a disagreements table with each position phrased so its own side would say "yes, that's what I mean"; then the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling them would actually move at least one of them. I suspect one crux is whether a finite, inspectable set of principles can cover novel moral cases without an arbitrary priority ordering quietly deciding the hard dilemmas, and another is whether human-feedback data contains mostly moral signal or mostly majority prejudice and status-quo bias. For each crux, give a concrete test or case that both sides would accept as bearing on it — e.g., a held-out set of hard cases scored by a diverse panel, or systematically generated novel dilemmas neither system saw in training, or a transparency audit where outside reviewers try to predict the system's verdicts from its documentation — and write down, in advance, what each side predicts the test would show. Also, as a novelist I need to know which parts of all this are actually settled in the field and which parts are genuinely contested, so separate those clearly and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
