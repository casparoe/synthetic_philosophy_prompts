I have been reading about the ethics of autonomous weapons and keep running into a tension I cannot resolve. On one side, I am attracted to the idea of meaningful human control: because lethal force must answer to judgments about proportionality, discrimination, and responsibility, there ought always to be a human being who decides whether and how a particular attack occurs. This view has systematic virtues—it offers a clear standard for when lethal autonomy is illegitimate, it preserves the possibility of holding someone accountable, and it fits the traditional categories of the laws of armed conflict. On the other side, I have a firm intuition that if a system could be shown, on the best available evidence, to distinguish combatants from civilians more accurately than human operators and to make fewer lethal errors in cluttered urban fighting, it would be perverse to prefer the human-led process simply because a person is pressing the button. The underlying commitment seems to be that the point of the control requirement is to protect people from unjustified harm, not to preserve a role for human decision-making for its own sake.

I want to work out which should yield. Present the strongest version of both positions: the case for treating the intuition as a confusion between what is instrumentally good and what is permissible, or as resting on an unrealistic fantasy of a provably reliable weapon; and the case for amending the theory so that meaningful human control resides in the targeting parameters, constraints, and oversight that a human sets in advance rather than in per-strike authorization. Is there a reading on which the theory and the intuition are compatible? What evidence or further argument would justify each move—debunking the intuition, revising the theory, or showing that the apparent conflict is an artifact? Please write your answer in British English.
