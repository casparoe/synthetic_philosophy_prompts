I'm on the autonomous systems review board for my country's defense ministry, and we've got a case that's splitting us down the middle. A contractor wants clearance to field a loitering munition — call it the Kestrel-9 — that finds and engages targets on its own, with a single operator "on the loop" who can abort an engagement during the twenty seconds before impact. Our governing policy says we can only clear a system if it retains "meaningful human control" over the use of force, but the phrase isnt defined anywhere, and this is the first case that's actually forced us to fight about it. In live trials the operator's display showed mostly a bounding box, a confidence score, and a red stop button; operators hit it in under 2% of target runs, and afterward they often couldn't say why the system had flagged a track at all. The contractor's line is that a human who can stop the attack at any point up to impact is, by definition, in control. The skeptics on the board say that's a legal fiction: if the person doesn't know what they're looking at and almost never intervenes, the system is making the call and the human is just a spectator with a button. A third group says the real test is whether we'd actually hold the operator responsible for a bad strike they didn't veto — if we wouldn't, then the human isn't the one deciding, whatever the wiring diagram says.

Draft the board's decision memo. It should recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and it should put the bottom line up front. Please use these sections in this order, with these rough lengths: Summary and recommendation (100 words), What the system does (150 words), The case for clearance (250 words), The case against (250 words), Conditions and reasoning (250 words), Bottom line (75 words). And please engage the strongest version of the losing side, not a strawman.
