I'm a PhD student in environmental philosophy, and I keep going back and forth on something. The older argument for protecting wilderness — the one that says untouched nature has value in itself, not just as a resource or a place for recreation — leans pretty hard on the idea that there is such a thing as untouched nature. But the newer ecological and historical picture basically dissolves that baseline: there's no place left without human fingerprints, naturalness is a matter of degress, and the line between wild and managed is one we drew rather than found. My question is whether the intrinsic-value argument actually needs that baseline or not. If we accept the no-pristine-nature picture, does the argument still go through, does it need repair, or is it dead — and does the value claim quietly turn into a relational or instrumental one once the baseline is gone? I'm not after a policy position; I'm after which premise is doing the real work and what a rebuilt version would have to assert. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introductory or concluding sentences, walking through what the argument needed, what the new picture actually undermines, and what's left standing.
