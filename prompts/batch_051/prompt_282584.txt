My philosophy of physics seminar has spent the term on Maxwell's demon and its various exorcisms, and two participants have settled into opposed positions that now feel tractable rather than mutually unintelligible. One defends the information-theoretic resolution: the demon can in principle measure molecular positions or velocities reversibly, so measurement itself is not where the demon pays; the unavoidable entropy cost comes when the demon erases its accumulated records, and Landauer's principle—erasing a bit of information at temperature T dissipates at least kT ln 2 of heat into the environment—is what saves the Second Law, with Bennett's treatment and the one-particle Szilard engine as the canonical illustrations. The other regards erasure as a red herring: on his view, once the demon is modeled as a finite physical system, with a real door and detector in thermal contact with the gas, it fails for ordinary mechanical reasons, the Second Law is saved without any appeal to the thermodynamic cost of information, and the phrase "information entropy" obscures more than it explains. We want to produce the document both of them should have been able to sign months ago.

Write the adversarial-collaboration statement these two people could both sign. It should contain: (i) a numbered list of agreed claims about the demon scenario, the Second Law, what Landauer's principle asserts, and what a satisfactory resolution would have to show, with no vacuous or slanted formulations; (ii) a disagreements table whose rows state each contested issue, the information-theoretic camp's position in wording that camp would accept, and the demon-mechanics camp's position in wording that camp would accept; (iii) the cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side—I suspect one crux is whether a measurement can be performed with no entropy increase even in principle, and another is whether an erasure-free demon with unbounded memory is a real possibility or only an artifact of idealization; and (iv) for each crux, a concrete test, model, or case both camps agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance. If part of the disagreement is verbal—for instance, over the words "measurement" or "information"—say which part and why.
