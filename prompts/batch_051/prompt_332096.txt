Here are the criteria I use when I ask whether a historical period is real rather than a label later historians impose on the record. A real period, on my view, must satisfy four conditions. Boundedness: inside the interval the change is one of kind rather than merely degree, so that before and after differ in how inquiry, institutions, or thought actually operate. Coherence: the developments placed within the period share an explanatory commonality — a method, an aim, an institutional form — rather than sharing nothing but dates. Self-awareness: participants or near-contemporaries registered that something had broken with the past or that a new kind of undertaking had begun. Explanatory work: invoking the period explains later developments better than a continuous narrative that makes no cut there.

Now test the Scientific Revolution, conventionally taken to run from Copernicus to Newton, against these four criteria. Go criterion by criterion and do it honestly, including the awkward evidence on both sides: the continuities between the new astronomy and mechanics and their late-medieval and Renaissance predecessors; the possibility that there was no single new method but several rival programs later bundled together as "the scientific method"; the extent to which the language of revolution was itself a rhetorical and self-promoting move by seventeenth-century actors and later polemicists; and the degree to which the period's apparent unity depends on an unflattering contrast with a "Dark Ages" that much later scholarship has dissolved. Where the Scientific Revolution fails a criterion, say whether that is a problem with the period, with the criterion, or with expecting any historiographical construct to meet the criterion cleanly.

Then give your verdict: on these criteria, is the Scientific Revolution a real thing, a later invention, or a case that shows the real/invented distinction is the wrong instrument? Finally, tell me whether any of my four criteria is doing no real work and, if so, what should replace it.
