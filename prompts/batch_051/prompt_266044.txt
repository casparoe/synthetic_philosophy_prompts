I'm a PhD student working on Ramsey's later papers, mostly on probability and the pragmatist bits, but I've been rereading the end of his Epilogue—the piece tacked on to The Foundations of Mathematics—where he says his picture of the world is drawn in perspective and not like a model to scale, with human beings in the foreground and the stars as small as threepenny bits. I've seen multiple readings of that passage, but after a seminar last week two friends got into it and I couldn't settle it.

Maya said Ramsey is actually answering the 'cosmic significance' worry: the mistake is assuming that a scale model, where physical size and spatial extent dominate, is the neutral picture. A perspective drawing isn't a distortion; it's the only way values can appear at all, because importance shows up only relative to a valuer. If you demand that the universe care about us, you're asking a picture to be drawn from no point of view.

Dan replied that this just redefines the problem away. The objector doesn't say size is metaphysically value-laden; they say our local cares are cosmically arbitrary. Ramsey's perspective painting concedes exactly that—it foregrounds human beings precisely because it's anthropocentric. Saying 'there is no view from nowhere' doesn't make the anthropocentric view any less provincial.

Maya came back: every picture has a frame, so the objector's scale model isn't viewless either—it pretends to be. Choosing a scale model over a perspective drawing is still a choice of perspective, just a worse one for human life.

Dan answered: that's an epistemic point about possible representations, not a point about value. Sure, no view from nowhere. But some perspectives systematically shrink what is actually vast; calling Ramsey's picture a perspective drawing doesn't show it's warranted, it just labels the distortion. If the universe has no valuer at its scale, then 'cosmic significance' is either false or merely relational, and Ramsey has only argued for a flattering kind of relation.
