A colleague in the machine learning group where I work has been arguing that the no-free-lunch theorems in learning theory amount to a proof that induction has no rational basis at all. I find the conclusion absurd, but his argument is tight enough that I cannot locate the flaw, so I am hoping someone can walk through it step by step. He states it this way:

(1) Any rule for moving from a finite body of observations to expectations about unseen cases — whether "the future will resemble the past," "prefer the simpler hypothesis," or a trained neural network — is a learning algorithm; induction is just one such algorithm among many. (2) The no-free-lunch theorems show that if one averages over all possible sequences of outcomes, weighting every sequence equally, all learning algorithms have exactly the same expected performance; a rule that always predicts the same value is no worse than any other, and every advantage on some sequences is paid for by an exactly matching disadvantage elsewhere. (3) Therefore, whenever any algorithm does better than another on the actual world, that edge cannot come from the data themselves; it can only come from the world's happening to lie inside the restricted class of sequences the algorithm is biased toward. (4) That bias — say, that the world is simple, or stationary, or has the symmetries my architecture encodes — is itself adopted only because it has worked before, which is another induction and is caught by the same theorem. (5) So every inductive method finally rests on an assumption that is arbitrary as far as the data are concerned, since any competing assumption would do just as well over the space of all sequences. (6) But if the choice among inductive methods is arbitrary from the data's point of view, then no expectation about the future is better supported than any other — that the sun will rise tomorrow, that it will vanish at noon, or that my desk will turn into a flock of pigeons.

He insists that the conclusion is not a reductio of the argument but of induction itself: a person has no more reason to expect the sun to rise than not. I would much rather give up one of the steps than the thought that some expectations are better supported than others, but I do not want to reject a premise only because I dislike the conclusion. Which step is the weakest, and what exactly does rejecting it cost?
