I cover mathematics and adjacent science for a general-interest magazine, and I have a rule about results whose proofs are too large for any single reader to follow end to end. When a theorem rests on a computation—a search through millions of configurations, or a formal verification assembled by software—I will describe it in print as "computer-verified" or "accepted on computational evidence," but I refuse to call it "proved" unless a competent human has, or could in principle have, surveyed the whole argument. The rule puts me in an awkward position with things like the original computer-assisted proof of the four-colour theorem and the formal verification of the Kepler conjecture: I treat both as facts and would happily build a story on them, but I stop short of the unqualified word "proved."

A mathematician I respect tells me this is sentimental. If two independent implementations agree to the last line, he says, that is a stronger check than any referee's tired pass over a long human proof, and my insistence on surveyability makes the human brain out to be a better instrument than it is. I suspect he is only half right. When computational proofs fail, the error is often imported: a shared library, a shared misunderstanding of what a formal sentence means, the same gap between a model of the mathematics and the mathematics itself. "Independent" implementations, in other words, are rarely as independent as they look. Yet I also know referees miss errors, that surveyability is partly a feeling, and that my rule may just be an old-fashioned deference to arguments I could imagine holding in my head.

So my question is whether this habit is rationally defensible, or a bias dressed up as a principle. Under what assumptions about how errors in computational verifications correlate should I change my usage? And is there a version of the rule that keeps its caution—requiring independent formalizations, or independent checkers rather than mere re-runs—without treating "surveyable by one human" as the gold standard for the word "proved"?
