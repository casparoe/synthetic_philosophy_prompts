After 30 years in molecular biology I've been spending retirement very slowly reading philosophy, and the one who's really got his hooks in me is Zhuangzi, especially on death. I have a request, not an essay question. My friend Dan died last month at 72. He was a physical chemist, and in his last decade he kept coming back to one story in the Zhuangzi — the one in the chapter often called Perfect Happiness, where Zhuangzi's wife dies and Huizi finds him drumming on a tub and singing. Dan loved the part where Zhuangzi says, roughly, that before his wife had qi she had no body, and before having a body she was part of the great jumble of wonder and mystery; then qi changed to body, body to birth, birth to death, just like the procession of the four seasons. Dan left a short note for his memorial: no "he's in a better place," no "watching over us," and if anyone says he's been reunited with his folks he'd count it as a failure of nerve. I'm giving the eulogy. The room will be his kids and grandkids, some of them quietly religous, and a table of his old lab people who are aggressively secular and will catch any intellectual fudging. I need a five-minute eulogy that uses that Zhuangzi passage to do real work — comfort the family without saying anything Dan would have rolled his eyes at, and don't collapse the Zhuangzi into a generic "we're all stardust" science-y thing, because for Dan the seasonal picture wasn't supposed to be consoling in the usual way.
