For the past two years I have been part of a reading group on cultural evolution, and our recurring argument about whether culture is Darwinian in any literal sense has hardened into a standoff. One camp holds the standard memetic view: cultural items are copied units — beliefs, techniques, expressions, rituals — that vary and are selectively retained, so that cultural change runs on meme lineages much as organic change runs on gene lineages. A second camp replies that cultural transmission is not faithful copying at all but reconstruction: each learner infers a novel amalgam from many models and partial cues, so the particle-like units the first camp needs are not stable, countable lineages, and talk of selection among memes is either an empirical claim that has failed or a metaphor dressed up as mechanism. A third group in the room thinks both are half right: Darwinian change requires only variation, differential persistence, and some continuity of inheritance, not particulate replicators, so culture could be Darwinian without being memetic.

What unsettles me is that we agree on nearly all the empirical material — transmission-chain experiments, cumulative-culture studies, ethnographies of craft apprenticeship — and no one moves. The replicator camp points to laboratory chains in which a text or a design is copied with enough fidelity to track differential success; the transformation camp points to cases in which designs are systematically reworked at every step and insists these are the rule rather than the exception. The argument always circles back to the same boundary dispute: which cases are central, which are edge cases, and what the word "Darwinian" has to include before it says something substantive.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not primarily empirical. I suspect the two sides differ about what would make an evolutionary explanation of culture satisfying: one side wants a mechanistic story with countable, transmitted units, while the other wants a population-level statistical story and treats the demand for particle-like units as a gene-centric prejudice rather than a scientific necessity. But I can also imagine that it is a largely verbal dispute about the word "Darwinian," or a genuinely empirical disagreement about how faithful and how modular cultural transmission really is. I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict: what kind of disagreement is this, and, given that diagnosis, what would count as progress — a formal result, a new kind of data, a distinction that dissolves the question, or the honest conclusion that the debate is ill-posed? Finally, please keep separate what is settled among people who work on cultural evolution from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
