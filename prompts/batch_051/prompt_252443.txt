I am a high school student who has been reading behavioral economics on my own, and I keep running into a dispute that seems to have stalled despite decades of experiments. On one side, researchers treat the endowment effect and loss aversion as robust, well-documented biases: people who are given a mug demand much more to give it up than they would have paid to acquire it, and losses loom larger than equivalent gains. On the other side, critics argue that these effects are either fragile artifacts of particular experimental procedures or perfectly defensible preferences once one notices that owning an item can change what a person knows about it, that experimental subjects may be uncertain about an item's value, and that people often evaluate outcomes relative to what they expected to keep rather than against a neutral zero point. Both sides seem to work from the same data and the same rough definitions, yet neither moves. I am also unsure whether the endowment effect is best understood as a special case of loss aversion or as a distinct phenomenon, and whether that taxonomic question is part of the same impasse.

My working hypothesis is that this is not ultimately a factual disagreement about how people choose, but a disagreement about the normative baseline used to classify a choice as biased — in other words, about what counts as a legitimate preference and about which features of a decision problem we are entitled to hold fixed before calling someone's behavior a mistake. Is that diagnosis right, or is there instead a hidden empirical question, a disagreement about which experiments count as evidence, or two groups answering different questions under a single heading? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than a survey, and an honest account of what, if anything, could actually end it: a specific experiment, a conceptual distinction, or a reframing of the question.
