I worked for nearly forty years as a physical chemist, and I retired a few years ago and started reading philosophy, mostly epistemology. There's a habit I picked up in the lab that I used to think was plain good sense, and now I'm not so sure. When I make a prediction or have an opinion about how some particular thing will turn out, I start with the broad base rate for things of that kind and I force myself to treat it as a strong prior. The only way I let my own specific, story-flavored reasoning move me off that number is if I can say, in one sentence, what mechanistic feature of my case is genuinely not included in the base rate, and even then I only nudge the number, never more than maybe 20 percent. Everything else — the vivid details, the nice narrative about why this time is different, the feeling of comprehension — I actively distrust. I think of it as epistemic modesty: my narrative self has been wrong too many times. But last month it got tested in a way that's bothering me. My daughter is opening a restaurant (I know, the classic bad base rate), and she has a business plan, an experienced partner, and what looks like a genuinely underserved location. I refused to read the plan, and told her none of the details matter much because the first-year failure rate for restaraunts is what it is. She says I'm not being modest, I'm being lazy and using a statistic to avoid thinking about her actual situation. And the uncomfortable thing is, I can't tell whether she's right.

So my question is this. Is my rule — start from the base rate, distrust narrative detail almost to zero, and require a stated mechanism not already in the reference class before I'll move at all, and then only a little — a defensible epistemic policy? I'd especially like to know: under what conditions does the outside view deserve this kind of veto over particular evidence, and under what conditions is it itself just a heuristic that can be badly miscalibrated, for example when the reference class is too crudely chosen, or when the base rate almost becomes self-fulfilling because everyone who acts on it gives up early? Is there a better version of the rule that would have let me actually read my daughter's business plan without letting my narrative self run wild, and how would I tell from the inside, mid-argument, whether I'm being genuinely modest or just hiding from the work of judging the particular case?
