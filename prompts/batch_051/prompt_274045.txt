In my work on quantified modal logic I have found myself defending a view that is formally tidy and metaphysically stark, and I want to map its exposure. The view is this: the Barcan formula, ∀x□Fx → □∀xFx, and its converse, □∀xFx → ∀x□Fx, are logical truths, and the intended semantics of the modal quantifiers is constant-domain, so that the same objects are available to the quantifiers at every possible world. On this picture, whatever exists exists necessarily, and there are no merely possible objects; to say that there could have been an F is never to quantify over something not already in the actual domain. What would count against this view? Give exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Order them from the consideration that would be decisive if sustained to the one that would be merely awkward. Include at least one purely formal or model-theoretic consideration and at least one intuitive or metaphysical counterexample, and in the fifth item say whether the view as I have stated it has been insulated against all of this and whether that insulation is itself a liability.
