I've agreed to run a single seventy-five-minute session for a joint philosophy/computer science MA seminar on Fitch's paradox of knowability. The core of the session is the Church–Fitch argument: from the principle that every truth is knowable (in the sense that some agent could come to know it at some time), together with the factivity of knowledge and the claim that a proposition of the form 'p and no one ever knows that p' can be a truth, the argument derives that every truth is actually known — a conclusion that seems to refute the anti-realist or verificationist who accepted the knowability principle. The students have had one course in modal logic and can handle a derivation, but several of them will treat the whole thing as a formal curiosity unless the anti-realist motivation is put in place first. Do not rehearse the explanation itself; plan how I should teach it.

The plan should specify: (1) the order in which the ideas must be introduced so that the contradiction feels forced rather than manufactured, with the dependency structure made explicit — what must already be on the table before the derivation goes through, and where the anti-realist motivation has to be secured so that the conclusion actually bites; (2) one carrying example or concrete proposition to build the derivation around, and the criteria that make it apt — I would rather avoid a proposition that simply asserts of itself that it is an unknown truth, if that quietly imports what the argument is trying to establish; (3) the misconceptions I should expect and where in the sequence each should be headed off, including at least the slide from knowability to actual knowledge, the thought that the argument is only an empirical claim about human cognitive limits, and the mistake of taking the anti-realist to have been committed to p if and only if p is known all along; and (4) a final check of understanding that would separate students who can reproduce the derivation from students who see why the conclusion is a live problem for a theory of meaning rather than an obviously false empirical claim. Say as well what I should explicitly set aside at this level — for example, intuitionistic and paraconsistent responses, or the full bimodal semantics — so that the group leaves with the problem sharp rather than with a survey of repairs.
