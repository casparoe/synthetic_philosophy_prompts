This has been bugging me ever since I read Hobsbawm and Anderson seperately a while back. Both are usually lumped together as constructivists about nations, but they seem to be constructing quite different things. Hobsbawm’s point, roughly, is that many customs and rituals that feel immemorial were actually cobbled together quite recently, often to give modern nation-states a spurious air of continuity. Anderson’s point, by contrast, seems more about the subjective side: how people manage to feel solidarity with millions of strangers they will never meet, and how they can imagine themselves all moving through the same historical time together. So here’s the question. Suppose someone wanted to defend the idea that nations are older and more rooted than either of these accounts allows. Which of the two would be the tougher obstacle for that person: Hobsbawm on invented traditions or Anderson on imagined community? Or, to put it differently, is it easier to show that a national tradition is genuinely old, or easier to show that the felt community is not merely imagined? I want you to actually take a side and argue for it, not just summarise the two positions.
