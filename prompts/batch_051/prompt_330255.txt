Levinas held that the most fundamental ethical relation is my encounter with the face of the Other: not the Other's physical features, but a singular, vulnerable, commanding presence that interrupts my categories and places me under an asymmetrical and unlimited responsibility. This is why he called ethics first philosophy, prior to ontology, politics, or any system of rules. Victor Hugo's Les Misérables is usually read as a novel about justice, poverty, and redemption. Read it instead through Levinas's account of the face. Begin with the bishop's treatment of Jean Valjean: Valjean arrives carrying the passport that marks him as a former convict, is taken in, steals the silver, and is caught, but the bishop tells the police the silver was a gift and adds the candlesticks to it. In what sense does the bishop respond to a face rather than to a criminal record, and what does that response accomplish that punishment and social classification cannot? Then turn to Javert: when Valjean spares him at the barricade and Javert later lets Valjean go and drowns himself, is Javert's collapse intelligible as the breakdown of a world built on categories and law once a face breaks through it? Where do you think the novel resists or complicates the Levinasian framework—for instance, in its insistence that misery and injustice are also structural conditions that face-to-face mercy cannot by itself repair? The reading should be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the novel to an illustration of a theory, do not distort Levinas to make him fit Hugo, and close by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical account that argument alone might miss.
