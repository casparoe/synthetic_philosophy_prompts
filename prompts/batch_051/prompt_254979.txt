I am pulled between a theory I find almost compulsory and an intuition I cannot shake. The theory is the possible-worlds semantics for knowledge: S knows that P just in case P is true at every world compatible with S's information. Its virtues are the ones that make a formal semantics worth having. It makes knowledge factive; it turns 'knows' into a normal modal operator, so epistemic logic is elegant (S5, if introspection is fairly assumed); and it offers a single mechanism—restricting the set of open worlds—for information update, common knowledge, and group knowledge. But it has a notorious consequence: if P is true throughout S's open worlds and P entails Q, then Q is true throughout S's open worlds too, so S knows Q. Thus someone who knows the Peano axioms knows every theorem those axioms entail, and someone who knows that this is a hand knows every disjunction of that claim with an arbitrary proposition.

I should distinguish this from a weaker principle I have no quarrel with: if S knows P and knows that P entails Q, then S knows Q. That principle is plausible, perhaps trivial. The possible-worlds semantics delivers something much stronger—closure under entailment itself, whether or not S has ever considered the entailment. That is where my intuition digs in. I do know the Peano axioms, but I do not know the truth of every theorem that follows from them; I could follow a proof of some consequence for the first time and learn something I did not already know. If 'knows' means what I mean when I say I know the axioms, the semantics has made knowledge of the axioms unrecognizably strong.

People whose judgment I respect offer different resolutions. One says the semantics was never intended to describe finite reasoners: it idealizes a fully rational agent, and my discomfort is a confusion between knowledge and the psychological capacity to deploy it. A second says to bite the bullet—I do know the theorems implicitly, much as a database contains everything entailed by its entries. A third says to amend the semantics, adding impossible worlds, awareness functions, or a separate tier of explicit knowledge, so that omniscience no longer follows. A fourth says to keep the logic but stop calling it knowledge—call it information or acceptance.

I do not want to be told which resolution is standard. I want to know what each move costs, whether the right resolution is to debunk the intuition, to amend or abandon the semantics, or to reinterpret the terms so that both survive—and what, if anything, the distinction between implicit and explicit knowledge genuinely buys rather than merely relabeling.
