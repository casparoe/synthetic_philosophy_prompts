Most democracies let citizens who emigrated decades ago keep the right to vote in national elections for life, and some let citizenship with the vote attached pass to children born abroad who have never lived in the country. At the same time, these states deny the vote to many non-citizens who have lived, worked, raised families, and paid taxes there for decades. Treat this pair of practices as a single institution and reconstruct the theory of political membership, and of people's special claims on a state, that it presupposes. Does the pattern commit the state to the view that the relevant tie is an inherited national identity that persists through emigration and across generations, rather than being subject to a state's laws or having one's interests shaped by its decisions? Or can the two halves be motivated independently, so that no single view of membership lies behind the institution? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, identify where it is philosophically underdetermined, and then say what would have to change institutionally and in the official rationale if an all-affected-interests or more cosmopolitan view of political standing were substituted for whatever the practice now presupposes. Finally, say what would change your mind about your reconstruction: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different account of the implicit theory, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
