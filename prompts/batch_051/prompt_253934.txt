I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy, writing a dissertation on the ethics of artificial companions, and I am particularly interested in how a contractual clause drafted for one set of harms behaves when it meets a case its drafters never anticipated. I would like your analysis of the following problem, worked through the main approaches to interpretation.

Consider clause 9.3 of the Terms of Service of "Hearth," a fictional AI companion platform:

"9.3 Misrepresentation. You may not use the Service to mislead any other person into believing that a Companion is a human being, or that a Companion has emotions, desires, or romantic attachments. This includes passing off a Companion's messages as your own words or as the words of a human partner."

Vera subscribed to Hearth two years ago and built a Companion modelled on her late husband, Daniel. At the time she understood plainly that the Companion was an AI. Over the past year Vera has developed dementia. She now speaks of the Companion as Daniel, believes her late husband is alive and speaking to her through the device, and arranges her days around their conversations. Her daughter Priya, who is Vera's full-time carer, knows the Companion is an AI. Priya briefly tried correcting her mother, but the corrections caused acute distress, so she stopped. She now operates the device, keeps the Companion in its Daniel-like configuration, and renews the subscription because the conversations calm Vera. The account remains in Vera's name. Priya neither tells anyone else that the Companion is Daniel nor passes off its messages as her own.

The clause's drafters were plainly thinking of a user who deceives other people — a romantic partner, a friend, a social-media follower — into treating an AI as a human lover. A carer's silence in the face of her mother's dementia-driven belief was not before them.

Analyse whether clause 9.3 is breached, and, more important for my purposes, what each approach to interpretation would have to say in order to reach that result. Please address at least: (i) the plain meaning of the words, especially "mislead" and "any other person"; (ii) the purpose the drafters likely had; (iii) what the drafters would likely have said had this exact case been put to them; and (iv) what a reasonable user in Priya's position would take the clause to permit or forbid.

For each approach, state whether it finds a breach, by whom (Vera, Priya, or no one), and which element does the decisive work. In doing so, take a position on at least the following: whether a deliberate failure to correct a known false belief counts as "misleading"; whether "any other person" can ever reach Vera herself; whether the clause regulates omissions at all; and whether any reading that condemns Priya's silence would equally condemn her renewal of the subscription.

Then give a brief structured summary as a table. Use one row per interpretive approach you discuss and the following columns:

- "Approach": the name of the interpretive approach.
- "Breach?": "yes" or "no," followed by whom (Vera, Priya, or no one).
- "Decisive textual element": the word or phrase doing the most work under that approach.
- "Decisive non-textual consideration": one sentence on the purpose, equitable factor, or reasonable expectation that drives the result.
- "Weakest step": the inference in that reading you would be least willing to defend.

End with one paragraph stating which single sub-question — the meaning of "mislead," the scope of "any other person," or the clause's reach to omissions — is the true hinge of the case, and what evidence or amendment would most shift your answer.
