I'm reading a popular book about language whose grammar chapter summarizes a debate I have only half followed, and I want to know how much of the summary to trust. Here is the passage in question:

"Generative linguists have now settled the old dispute about whether parts of speech such as noun and verb are real features of the human language faculty or merely convenient labels for patterns in the data. Careful cross-linguistic fieldwork shows that every language distinguishes nouns from verbs, which is exactly what we should expect if these categories are innate and supported by dedicated neural machinery. Parts of speech are therefore natural kinds in a strong sense: the categories are out there in the structure of the mind, much as chemical elements are out there in the structure of matter, and the linguist's job is to discover them rather than to impose them. Reports that some languages—various Austronesian languages are the usual examples—lack a noun–verb distinction reflect incomplete fieldwork; when one looks carefully, even these languages turn out to have the categories and merely permit unusually free conversion between them."

Something about this passage strikes me as off. I know enough to suspect that the universality of the noun–verb distinction is more contested than the author admits, and that the phrase "natural kinds" is doing a great deal of argumentative work. But I cannot identify which specific claims are false as opposed to merely compressed. Could you go through the paragraph sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful linguist or philosopher of language would accept, qualify, or reject? I would especially like to know whether, and where, the paragraph slides from a defensible claim about widespread cross-linguistic tendencies to a much stronger claim about natural kinds and innate neural machinery, and whether the book's dismissal of the counterexamples in its final sentence is itself fair.
