I teach an upper-division philosophy of science seminar, about 16 students, 75 minutes. I want a structured in-class activity on the question of when a compelling theory should override a firm intuition, and when the reverse is right; by the end, I want students to see that neither source has automatic authority and that the right response depends on what each is tracking in the case at hand. Please design a runnable two-stage exercise built around a small invented system rather than a familiar textbook example, so students meet the problem fresh. In one stage a well-supported theory should pull against a strong intuitive judgment; in the other, an intuitive judgment should correctly track something the available theory misses. Attach real (small) stakes to their choices — points, a prediction score, or a penalty for changing one's mind — so that deferring to theory and refusing to defer both have felt costs, and arrange the stages so the conflict emerges from what students chose and scored before it is named. Include rules, materials, a timeline, and a debrief that maps moments in the activity onto distinctions such as predictive success, explanatory coherence, and intuitive plausibility, says what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it, and states honestly which feature of the philosophical question the miniature system cannot reproduce.
