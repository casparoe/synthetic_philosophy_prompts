I'm taking a seminar on play and leisure, and an example from the discussion has been nagging at me. The working definition we're using says that an activity counts as play to the extent that it is freely chosen, carried out in a time and place set apart from ordinary life, governed by rules internal to the activity, and pursued for its own sake rather than for any material product or necessity. The instructor added that these are not necessary and sufficient conditions but marks of the thing, more like a family resemblance than a checklist.

Now consider a close friend who is a professional chess player. She learned as a child and still says that when a game begins, the money, the rating points, and the travel all fall away; in that respect her experience has not changed since she was seven. But as an adult she plays on a contractual circuit: her schedule is set by sponsors, she studies openings the way one studies for a licensing exam, she works with a trainer on purely instrumental matters like time management and physical endurance, and she has told me that if the prize money and stipends disappeared she would retire from competition immediately, though she would go on playing casually in the park. The moves, the rules, and the absorption are identical in the two settings. Is what she does in tournaments play, or has it become something else — work that merely looks like play? Please go through the criteria one by one, mark where the case underdetermines the verdict, and then say what the hard judgment call is really about.

I would also like you to tell me which fact about her, or about the definition, changing would flip your conclusion, and where your own reasoning is least certain.
