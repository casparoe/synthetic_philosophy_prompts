I am running a Q&A for a philosophy discussion group whose next guest has spent a career defending Peter Abelard's thesis that sin consists wholly in consenting to what one believes one ought not to do, and not in the outward deed, the antecedent desire, or the consequences. She has a knack for treating the view's more startling implications as evidence that our ordinary categories of guilt and action are confused. Draft seven or eight questions for this Q&A, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The questions should press at least the following: where sin first enters among suggestion, delight, and consent, and whether enjoying a forbidden thought is already a sin; why the completed act adds nothing to guilt, and what then becomes of the moral difference between attempting and completing a wrong; how consent is to be distinguished both from a bare judgment that an action is allowed and from an act of willing; whether a sincere but erroneous conscience excuses, and whether the view is committed to saying that Christ's executioners did not sin insofar as they conscientiously believed they were acting rightly; whether punishment, deterrence, and the avoidance of scandal can be justified if exterior acts and their effects are morally inert; and whether negligence or self-induced ignorance can be sinful even though sin supposedly lies only in consent. Make the follow-up questions turn on the weakest point of each anticipated reply. No biographical or bibliographic questions.
