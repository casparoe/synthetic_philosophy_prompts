I'm not a lawyer or a philospher, but I've gotten kind of obsessed with what it means when a law just stops being enforced. Where I live there's an old ordinance against, I think, washing your car in the street on a Sunday, and it's been on the books for decades with nobody cited for it in living memory. Last month a new council member got elected partly on a promise to "clean up the books," and now the whole town is fighting about whether the law even still exists. Two friends of mine keep going in circles on this. One says a law that everyone has openly ignored for that long has quietly died; the community's settled habit is the real law now, and suddenly enforcing the old thing is an ambush. The other says that blurs what the law is with what people happen to do: the law is the law until the council actualy repeals it, and if it's a bad law you repeal it, you don't pretend it quietly vanished. I've talked them into doing a good-faith adversarial collaboration instead of another argument.

Could you write the document they should end up with? I want three parts. First, a numbered list of claims both can sign — not vague stuff like "the rule of law is good," but concrete claims about validity, enforcement, fair notice, and what the council would have to do. Second, a disagreements table where each real disagreement is spelled out twice, once in wording the still-a-law side would accept for its own view and once in wording the law-died side would accept for its own view, so nobody can yell "strawman." Third, the cruxes: the smaller questions that would actually move at least one of them, with a concrete case for each — the Sunday-car-washing law, a sudden jaywalking crackdown after forty years of non-enforcement, an emergency law where the emergency ended long ago but the law stayed on the books — and for each case write down in advance what each side predicts a court should do, what each predicts a court actually would do, and what each would count as evidence against their own view. Don't give me a survey of possible formulations. Commit: pick the exact wording that's neither empty nor slanted, say which disagreements are really just about words and which are real, and take a definite position on what each case would show and who would have to give ground. And if their deeper split — one thinks the rule of law is about predictability, the other thinks it's about law tracking the community — turns out to be a crux itself, treat it like one.
