I am a novelist who teaches an advanced fiction workshop of sixteen students, and I want a single ninety-minute session on writing non-human minds. My students readily write the interiority of a dog and balk at writing a bee, and I suspect the difference is not craft but an unexamined conviction about which animals have inner lives. I want an exercise that makes them feel the philosophical problem beneath that conviction—how consciousness is distributed across species, whether there is a principled line, and what it means to grant or withhold a first-person narrator.

Design the session around a runnable structured deliberation, not a lecture. Here is the kind of pressure I want: participants begin with gut judgments about which non-human narrators an anthology should permit, then must convert those judgments into a single written criterion that justifies every yes and every no, then must apply that criterion to unfamiliar cases and live with the odd results. They should discover for themselves that a dog and a honeybee cannot be cleanly separated by any rule they are willing to state—that every plausible criterion either reaches too far, stops too short, or was quietly gerrymandered to protect a prior verdict.

Give me the full plan: the framing and instructions; the exact rules and constraints; the materials, such as species profiles and worksheets; a realistic ninety-minute schedule; and a debrief. The species profiles should use only widely known facts about behavior and anatomy—nothing that would send a student to a reference. Include a ladder of cases running from a creature nearly everyone will grant, such as a dog, through contested cases such as an octopus, a honeybee, or a jumping spider, down to creatures nearly everyone will deny, such as a jellyfish or a sponge, and explain what work each rung is doing.

The debrief should map moments in the activity onto the underlying philosophical points: the gap between consciousness and flexible behavior, the problem of other minds, inference to the best explanation from behavior, the temptation to gerrymander a criterion to protect a prior conviction, and the possibility that the boundary is not sharp. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run the exercise—the arguments that will break out, the stalemates, the student who refuses the terms—and how to handle those moments without resolving the question for the group. Identify what the exercise cannot itself produce and what the debrief must supply. Do not name the philosophical issue until the activity has already generated it. Please write the answer in American English.
