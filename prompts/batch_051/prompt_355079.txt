You are a philosopher of mind who works at the border between consciousness science, animal ethics, and the study of how institutions absorb new measurement technology. I want a careful, mechanism-level prediction of a counterfactual, not an essay about what would be desirable. Think slowly: begin by sketching the main causal pathways and the actors whose behavior would change, then convert those pathways into predictions with explicit confidence levels.

It is 2030, and a device we can call the sentience index has appeared. It is cheap, handheld, non-invasive, and works on any living animal: point it at an organism for thirty seconds and it returns a calibrated probability that the organism is phenomenally conscious at that moment — that there is something it is like to be it. The device is not an oracle, and it makes no claim about self-consciousness, intelligence, or moral standing. It integrates a wide battery of physiological, neural, and behavioral markers into a single score. It was trained and validated on human cases where first-person report is available — ordinary wakefulness, REM sleep, deep sleep, several anesthetic depths, and locked-in patients who later regained the ability to communicate — and its nonhuman readings track scientific consensus where that consensus was already strong. Within a few years, most researchers and welfare regulators treat the device as strong evidence, though it has known blind spots: it can under-score brief flickers of experience, it performs poorly on animals undergoing metamorphosis or in very early development, and it prints probabilities to three decimal places whose final digit nobody should take seriously.

The nonhuman results are released in stages. The mammal, bird, and fish numbers mostly confirm the direction of recent welfare reforms, give or take a few shocks at the edges. The genuinely disruptive results are among invertebrates and other small-bodied animals, and they are not a tidy monotonic gradient: there are high scorers in unexpected phyla and near-zero scorers in groups where welfare activists had invested the most effort. Rather than asking you to guess the exact table of scores, I want a structured prediction: (a) what happens over the next fifteen years if the surprise is a *high floor* — hundreds of billions of farmed and wild invertebrates score well above the level that would make current insect farming, pest control, and incidental slaughter difficult to justify; (b) what happens if it is a *low ceiling* — almost all invertebrates cluster near zero, so the animal-welfare movement loses its broadening premise but gains confidence about a defensible line around fish and above; and (c) what happens if the scores are scattered and lineage-unpredictable — mollusks high, most insects low, one mite species absurdly high. In each branch, trace the consequences for industrial animal agriculture, insect farming, biomedical research, wild-animal welfare, vermin and invasive-species policy, and the philosophical debate over the distribution of consciousness. Then say which outcomes are robust across all three branches and which hinge on a single branch. For your highest-confidence predictions, state the earliest real-world evidence over the next five years that would make you retract them, and explain what would have to be true about human psychology or institutional inertia for your prediction to be wrong.
