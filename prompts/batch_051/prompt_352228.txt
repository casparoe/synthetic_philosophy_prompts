I'm the student rep on my university hospital's research ethics board and I'm taking a clinical ethics seminar, so the chair dumped next weeks decision memo on me. We have to decide whether our hospital should join a multicenter RCT for newly diagnosed rheumatoid arthritis: patients get randomized to either immediate treatment with an established injectable biologic or a step-up protocol where they start on a cheaper oral drug and only escalate if they miss their treatment target after six months. Both strategies sit inside current guidelines, and the oral-first route has decent observational data but no trial evidence for this head-to-head comparison. The wrinkle is that nearly every rheumatologist at our hospital goes biologic-first, and three local investigators have said privately they wouldn't want a family member randomized to the oral-first arm, even though they're willing to enroll patients. Half the board says that kind of personal preference destroys equipoise and makes the consent conversations dishonest, so we should either not join or bar those three from recruiting; the other half says what matters is genuine disagreement in the wider expert community, not any single doctor's hunch, and that barring them changes nothing because the rest of the department quietly agrees with them. Draft the memo: it needs a concrete recommendation (approve, reject, or approve with conditions), an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side, and it should be exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, with no intro and no conclusion. Also flag which parts are actually settled in research ethics and which are genuinely contested among people who know the field, and don't present a contested view as consensus.
