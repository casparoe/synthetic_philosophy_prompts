I keep going back and forth on a mock case I wrote for my bioethics unit, and I'd like someone to lay out the interpretive moves rather than just argue the policy. The case turns on a clause from a (fictional) Parental Licensing Act I made up, which the students are told to treat as law:

"No license may be granted to any applicant who, in the judgment of the examining board, is likely to rear a child in a manner that unnecessarily leaves the child with a substantially narrowed range of options in adulthood, as to education, vocation, association, or belief."

The facts: two Deaf parents apply. They plan to use a sperm donor with hereditary deafness so that the child will be deaf, and they say they'll raise the child in ASL, in Deaf culture, at a residential school for the Deaf. The licensing board's counsel says that's squarely within the clause: the parents are deliberately choosing a narrower range of options than the child would otherwise have. The parents' lawyers argues that every upbringing narrows options — you can't raise a child as both an orthodox Jew and an atheist, in both Mandarin and ASL, prepared for both medicine and farming — and that the clause was aimed at neglectful or abusive homes, not at a loving family that opens up a different set of options.

What I want is the interpretation, not your views on licensing parents as such. Apply the clause to this case using, one at a time: (a) the plain words; (b) the purpose of the clause; (c) what the drafters would have said if asked, at the time, about parents selecting for deafness; and (d) what a reasonable reader at passage would have understood it to prohibit. Then say which approach should control here and why. And I have a hunch the word "unnecessarily" is doing more work than it seems — tell me if that's right.
