I edit the science and ideas desk at a general-interest magazine, and the computing beat keeps producing the same editorial problem. About once a month, a press release, preprint, or pitch crosses my desk claiming that a system has gone beyond the Church-Turing boundary. The claims take several forms. A startup says its analog chip, whose behavior depends on a continuously variable physical quantity, computes a function no Turing machine can compute. A theory paper introduces a coherent formal model of computation strictly stronger than Turing machines — say, machines equipped with an oracle for the halting problem — and announces that the Church-Turing thesis has been refuted. A research group reports that a finite physical device has solved the halting problem for arbitrary programs, with reproducible measurements. The stakes cut both ways: if we write up a pseudo-refutation of a foundational idea, we mislead readers about the state of science; if we reflexively kill every such claim, we risk missing work that genuinely changes how the field thinks about computability. The editors running these calls have no training in computability theory and about an hour to make an initial judgment, with the final sign-off belonging to me.

I would like a reusable decision procedure for that first hour. Write it as an ordered set of questions an editor can ask, with an explicit statement for each of what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with something like do not report, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is philosophical rather than factual. The procedure should keep several distinctions available in plain language: between the mathematical thesis (the equivalence of the informal idea of an effective method with Turing computability and its formal equivalents), the physical thesis (the claim about what any physically realizable process can compute), and the epistemic reading that treats the thesis as a bound on what any finite, rule-governed procedure could settle; between extending a formal model of computation, which is a mathematical act and rarely challenges a serious reading of the thesis, and demonstrating that a physical or cognitive process actually computes beyond the Turing limit; and between computing a function in principle, simulating a process exactly, deciding a problem, and merely encoding an answer in a real-valued parameter whose exact value cannot be extracted by finite measurement. For each distinction, the procedure should also tell an editor how it should change the framing of the story, not just the verdict.

Then run the procedure on three representative cases: (a) the analog-chip startup whose real-valued parameter is claimed to compute a non-Turing-computable function; (b) the theory paper whose oracle-strengthened model is claimed to refute the thesis; and (c) the experimental claim that a finite physical device has solved the halting problem for arbitrary programs. Please make sure at least one worked application returns an answer my editors will not like, and say explicitly, for each case, what the procedure cannot resolve.
