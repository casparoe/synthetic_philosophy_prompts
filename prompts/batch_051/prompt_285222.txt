Suppose Tolstoy could be shown, with a full explanation of how they work, a contemporary text-to-image program and a text-to-music program: a user enters a brief description, and within seconds the system produces a technically polished image, musical passage, or poem in whatever style is requested. Judged by the theory of art he sets out in What Is Art?, how would he classify and evaluate such output? In that book he treats a work as art only if the artist has actually lived through a feeling, has embodied it in external signs, and thereby infects an audience with that same feeling; the degree of infectiveness depends, on his account, on the individuality of the feeling, the clarity with which it is transmitted, and above all the sincerity of the artist. He further holds that good art transmits feelings that unify people, while bad or counterfeit art flatters the separateness and pleasure of a cultivated minority, and he identifies borrowing, imitation, calculated effects, and interestingness as the standard devices by which what is not art mimics art. The obvious verdict is that a system with no feelings, no lived experience, and no aim beyond stylistic imitation is counterfeit by definition. But work through Tolstoy's criteria one by one and identify precisely where such a system first fails, whether any imaginable configuration of the technology could satisfy him, and how, on his own principles, he should rank its output against the sophisticated art of his own day that he condemned as counterfeit. Also attend to the awkward cases: for example, whether the systems might accidentally meet some of his conditions, and whether any of the verdicts his theory delivers here sit badly with his broader purposes.
