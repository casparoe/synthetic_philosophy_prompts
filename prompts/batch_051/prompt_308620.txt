I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, mostly health and behavioral economics, and I need a second opinion before I write up a panel I attended last week. The panel was about how a regional health system should spend a fixed pot of money on pain. One option was a broad "comfort and dignity" program—better food, nicer rooms, volunteers, music—that would make tens of thousands of patients mildly more comfortable. The other was a small program for the few patients whose pain is so severe it regularly overwhelms medication, giving them round-the-clock specialists. Everyone expected the usual cost-benefit tradeoff. Instead, one panelist, a philosopher, argued there is no tradeoff at all. His claim was that once pain passes a certain intensity, it stops being in the same currency as comfort: it's not that a severe pain equals a thousand nice meals, it's that no number of nice meals or pleasant afternoons can ever be added up to outweight one episode of that kind of pain. So the choice isn't close—it isn't even a real comparison.

I came away not sure if he'd said something deep or something silly, and my editor doesn't want a "one side says" piece. I cant find the flaw myself, but I also don't know how far the argument is supposed to go. If it works for hospital budgeting, does it also mean a society should spend enormous sums to eliminate one case of extreme agony before it spends a dime on making lots of people happier? Is this just a roundabout way of saying some things can't be given a price, or is there a real position hiding there? Should I present it to readers as a serious challenge to cost-benefit reasoning, or as a clever idea with an abserd conclusion?
