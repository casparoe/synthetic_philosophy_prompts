You are an analytically trained philosopher working on causation and explanation. I want you to help me design a classroom activity for a university epistemology-and-metaphysics seminar: about 18 students, 90 minutes, mixture of philosophy majors and scientists. I want the activity to make them feel, before they are told, the force of Russell's claim in *Mysticism and Logic* that causation is a relic of a bygone age and that mature science does not look for causes in the traditional sense.

Here is the passage I have in mind. I am quoting it from the essay On the Notion of Cause, which Russell published in the Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society and later collected in Mysticism and Logic. Please treat the quoted wording as the fixed text to work with:

The law of causation, I repeat, is a relic of a bygone age, surviving, like the monarchy, only because it is erroneously supposed to do no harm.

I want the activity built around the idea that causation-talk is not forced on us by the phenomena, but is a particular way of organizing experience into useful or misleading narratives. I want participants to experience how easily they slip into causal language, then see what happens to their explanations when causes are systematically removed.

Design the activity as a game or structured task in which participants are given a concrete physical system — something like a pool table simulation, a simple mechanical device, or the rule-governed changes in a grid-world — and are asked first to explain why a particular event occurred, then to rewrite their explanation under increasingly strict bans on causal vocabulary. The key moment should be one they reach themselves: either they notice that their allegedly indispensable causal explanation can be replaced by functional or differential-equation descriptions, or they notice that they cannot make sense of their ordinary explanatory practices without implicating some notion of cause. Let the result be genuinely open, not rigged to force the Russellian conclusion.

Please give me:

1. A runnable activity plan: role, sequence of tasks, materials, a specific payoff or victory condition that does not require money, and the estimated time for each phase.

2. The precise ban-list or replacement-list for causal vocabulary, with examples of moves that are allowed and not allowed. Distinguish, for example, between \'A caused B\' and \'B is a function of A\' or \'A is materially sufficient for B under these standing conditions.\'

3. A debrief that maps what happens in the activity to Russell\'s passage, including the difference between eliminating causal language in mathematical physics and eliminating it from everyday explanation, and the difference between saying that causal talk is unwarranted and saying it is merely heuristic.

4. Your honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time I run it, and how I should handle those failures. In particular, say how to handle participants who think this is just a game about word-taboos and not about metaphysics.

Start by brainstorming two or three possible physical systems, then pick one and justify it. Think carefully about the phase where the ban is introduced: I want the resistance to Russell\'s claim to show up in the room, not just be told to them. Where the activity cannot reproduce a part of Russell\'s view, say so plainly in the debrief notes.
