I run the science funding arm of a mid-sized private foundation. Twice a year we review a portfolio of multi-year research programmes in the natural and social sciences and decide which to renew, which to renew only with major conditions, and which to sunset. The stakes are high: each renewal commits roughly three years of support, and closing a programme is politically costly because our board and our grantees are invested in continuity. The decision has to be made in a single half-day meeting by three programme officers, none of whom has any formal training in philosophy of science, so we need a written procedure we can follow rather than a set of abstract principles.

Please convert the core of Imre Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes into such a decision procedure. For our purposes the key ideas are these: a research programme has a hard core of commitments that its practitioners refuse to give up, and a protective belt of auxiliary assumptions that may be adjusted; a progressive programme is one whose adjustments generate independently testable, novel predictions that are then confirmed, while a degenerating programme mostly adapts after the fact to save the core from refutation. Turn that into an operational checklist or decision tree, with the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the process stops with a decision not to renew, and which steps are unavoidably judgment calls. Be explicit about the limits of the procedure, including what to do when a programme is too young to have produced novel predictions, when a proposed belt modification is testable in principle but not in practice, and when a programme keeps making novel predictions but none of them pan out.

Then apply the procedure to two portfolio cases with enough specificity that I can see the scoring at work. First, a fifteen-year-old evolutionary biology programme whose hard core is a strong claim about the dominant driver of a widely studied trait; over the past decade it has absorbed repeated anomalies by adding auxiliary assumptions about measurement error, gene flow, and developmental plasticity, but three of those additions were later independently confirmed and each pointed to a new experiment whose surprising result matched the programme's prediction. Second, a six-year-old behavioural science programme built on a simple, very flexible core mechanism; its additions to the protective belt have mostly been introduced after unexpected results, are difficult to test independently, and have produced few confirmed novel predictions, though the programme is popular in the press and with our board. I want the procedure to yield a clear renewal decision for each, and I specifically want to know where it would force an uncomfortable call, such as sunsetting a popular programme or retaining an unfashionable one, and on what grounds.
