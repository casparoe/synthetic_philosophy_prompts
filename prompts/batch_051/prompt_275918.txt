I retired a few years ago from a career in experimental physics, and since then I have been convening a continuing-education discussion circle at our local library, this term on the paradox of tolerance. I have been disappointed by how quickly the conversation sorts itself into people announcing which slogan they already prefer, so I want to hand the group a classification instrument instead: an eight-question quiz, "What is your operative theory of tolerating the intolerant?", for educated adults with no formal philosophy background, with three answer options per question. The questions should be concrete vignettes phrased so that respondents cannot tell which option belongs to which theoretical label, and the instrument should sort people by what they say about cases, never by what they call themselves.

Please build the following eight cases into the quiz, though you may adjust their dress: a disciplined anti-democratic party, currently lawful and polling at roughly four percent, that applies for a permit to hold a rally in a public square; a university that has invited a speaker whose doctrine denies the equal standing of some minority but whose announced lecture is offensive rather than inciting; an employee dismissed by a private firm for organizing a lawful intolerant association in his free time; a provincial museum or theater that withdraws a provocatively intolerant exhibition or booking that breaks no law; a social media platform that removes an account advocating intolerant policies but nothing illegal; a public-school teacher who holds intolerant views strictly off duty; a speaker whose explicitly non-inciting rallies are predictably followed by harassment of minority residents near the venue; and an intolerant group whose leaders openly state that, once in power, they will suspend free elections and the independent press. The leaves of your scoring key must be these six positions: the expressive-liberty view, which restricts only direct and narrowly defined harm, never viewpoint as such; the Popper-style view, which condones restricting organized intolerance aimed at destroying the open society itself; the political-liberal view, which condones restriction only when the intolerant group credibly threatens the society's basic political liberties, not merely because the group is bigoted; the militant-democratic view, which favors the use of state power to suppress anti-democratic parties and movements before they become dangerous; the agonistic-pluralist view, which holds that every rule for deciding who counts as intolerant is itself politically contested, and therefore prefers counter-speech and institutional safeguards to prior restraint; and the prudential-statecraft view, which rejects any stable rule and restricts only when a particular group's conduct is genuinely destabilizing, judged case by case. I would like the instrument built from the logical space rather than from the list of names: first identify the three or four underlying joints on which these positions actually come apart (for example, viewpoint versus conduct; whether the threat must be credible and imminent; whether public institutions carry obligations that private bodies do not; whether a rule for identifying intolerance can itself be neutral), lay out the combinations of answers those joints permit, locate the six positions within that space, and explicitly diagnose the unoccupied combinations, saying whether each is empty because the view is incoherent, because nobody with those commitments would defend it, or merely because the named positions happen not to have staked it out. Flag any pair of leaves that the eight questions cannot separate, and propose the single extra question that would best separate them.

Please organize your answer into the following titled sections, in this order and at roughly the indicated lengths: "The instrument" (one short paragraph stating the purpose, audience, administration, and how scores are assigned); "The eight cases" (each vignette in three or four sentences, followed by three neutrally worded response options); "The scoring key" (how each option maps onto the six positions, including weights and any tie-break rule); "The logical map" (the underlying joints, the occupied and unoccupied combinations with a one-sentence diagnosis of each empty one, and the near-neighbor pairs the quiz cannot separate); and "Limits and corrective" (one paragraph on what the instrument overstates or overlooks, plus the additional question you would add if a ninth were permitted).
