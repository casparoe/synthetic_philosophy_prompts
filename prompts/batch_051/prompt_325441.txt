I'm a novelist. In the novel I'm drafting, a defrocked minister explains to his niece that she should never accept a report of a miracle: a miracle is a violation of a law of nature, laws of nature are grounded in uniform experience, so the evidence for the law will always be stronger than the testimony for the exception. I'd assumed this was basically Hume's argument about miracles, but a philosopher friend says I've turned it into a cruder, question-begging argument and that Hume's actual version is more careful. I dont quite see the daylight between them: if a miracle is defined as a violation of a law, and laws are just our uniform experience, hasn't the case already been settled before any testimony shows up? What could it even mean for the testimony to outweigh the law, on this picture? Or is the minister's line actually a real distortion of Hume, and if so, where exactly does it go wrong?
