I've been reading about the East India Company and the weird period when a for-profit corporation ran something close to a sovereign state—armies, taxation, diplomacy—and it made me think about a proposal my friend won't stop bringing up. His idea: every state should be rechartered as a public corporation. The constitution would become the corporate charter, with a written purpose clause. Any government action outside that purpose would be ultra vires and void. Citizens would be treated as shareholders: they could bring derivative suits against officials for wasting state assets or breaching fiduciary duty, and the state could not plead sovereign immunity in those cases. The president or prime minister and cabinet would be directors with ordinary corporate duties of care and loyalty, enforceable in regular courts. If the state lost, judgments would run directly against its assets, and in the extreme a court could revoke the charter, dissolve the state, and appoint a receiver. His pitch is that this gives citizens a private right of action instead of making them wait four years to vote the bums out, and that it imports centuries of corporate law about self-dealing, disclosure, and shareholder suits, with all it's precedents.

I keep feeling like this has to break somewhere, but I can't say exactly where. I want someone to take the proposal seriously as a mechanism and tell me how it would actually behave. What would it incentivize among officeholders, judges, and ordinary citizens? Who would game it, and how would they game it—through creative pleadings, through amending the purpose clause to bless whatever the government is already doing, through jurisdictions competing to be plaintiff-friendly? Does the analogy quietly collapse when you ask who the shareholders are, or who the residual claimant of a state is? And are the failure modes things you could fix by better drafting, or are they built into the attempt to bolt corporate law onto sovereignty? Give me a definite verdict: what equilibrium does this produce, who ends up with power, and is it salvageable—not a menu of possibilities.
