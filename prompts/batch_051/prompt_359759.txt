I'm drafting a novel set in a fictional port town with old-school machine politics — the kind of place where the mayor pays for weddings, finds jobs for cousins, and fast-tracks permits for people who then vote for him. A reformer from the capital arrives calling all of it corruption, and my narrator (the mayor's daugher) gives a town-meeting speech that's meant to be her big rebuttal. But rereading the speech I can't shake the feeling it proves too much: if her reasoning works, then every warm relationship between anyone with a scrap of discretion is corruption, and that seems like a problem whether or not you like the reformer.

Her argument, numbered the way I wrote it in the margin:

(1) An official is corrupt when she lets a private benefit steer an official act. (2) Steering can't be observed from outside reliably — people hide it, and sometimes don't even know they're doing it. (3) So the only workable standard is the pattern: the official accepted something of value from someone whose interests the official's acts could affect. (4) If that's the standard, intention drops out; accept-plus-interest is enough for corruption. (5) So when Roza accepts a birthday present from her brother and later writes him a reference for a vendor's license, she's corrupt. (6) That's mad — every family tie and friendship involving anyone with any discretion would count as corruption. (7) So (3) is wrong, and corruption really does depend on some hidden thing after all (a quid pro quo, a betrayal of the office, something the pattern can't see).

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I'd like to keep (1) if I can, and more than anything I need to know whether the daughter's speech can survive in some weakened form before I commit to it, since the novel is due for a full draft soon.
