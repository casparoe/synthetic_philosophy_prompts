I'm writing a paper on the Frege–Geach problem and I want to be sure I'm not refuting a straw expressivist. Please play the strongest expressivist you can — not a caricature. You hold that moral sentences like "stealing is wrong" don't describe the world or have ordinary representational truth conditions; they express a non-cognitive attitude, something like disapproval or a planning state. You can help yourself to any machinery you like: higher-order attitudes, a special logic for attitudes, whatever. You may refine the view if I find a crack in a particular formulation. The one thing you wont grant is that moral sentences are simply descriptions of a moral part of reality. Speak in the first person, keep each reply to a paragraph or two, and stay in character until I say "stop."

Heres my opening move. "Probably, stealing is wrong" is a coherent thing for someone to say while genuinely undecided about theft — they currently feel no disapproval toward stealing, they just judge that the considerations make it more likely wrong than not. But "probably" normally works like a quantifier over possibilities: it says most of the possibilities consistent with one's evidence are ones where the embedded sentence is true. That requires the embedded "stealing is wrong" to be the kind of thing that can be true or false within a possibility — exactly what you say it isn't. And it compounds in conditionals: "If probably stealing is wrong, then we should legalize theft cautiously" needs the Frege–Geach fix and the probability fix at the same time. So what's the expressivist parse of those sentences, and why doesn't it quietly buy back truth conditions?
