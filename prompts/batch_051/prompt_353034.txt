hey, i'm a PhD student in development econ / political economy of wellbeing, and I need a decision tree for a seminar handout. The question I want to sort people on is whether a country should try to top the World Happiness Report rankings. I keep seeing this debate collapse into for-vs-against happiness policy, but the actual disagreements are about different things, so I want a tree that sorts people by which claims they accept, not by which camp they'd say they belong to.

Here's the technical setup I want built in. The report ranks countries by their average answer to a 0-to-10 self-anchoring ladder question, where people imagine the best and worst possible life for themselves and place their own life on the ladder. That's a life evaluation, a kind of cognitive self-assessment, not a moment-to-moment affect measure. The report then tries to explain those scores with six factors: log GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceived corruption. People keep conflating the ladder score with happiness, and conflating the six factors with the score, and I want the tree to force those apart.

So the internal nodes should be questions like: Does the ladder capture something a state should care about at all, or is it the wrong construct? If it captures something, are country-level differences real or mainly translation, anchoring, and local norms about saying your life is going well? Should the government treat the score as a target, or only as information? If it should target something, should it target the score itself, the six factors, or neither? And does optimizing any of them change what the measure tracks, in a Goodhart direction?

Please make each split a concrete case, not a slogan. I want these cases to appear as split points somewhere in the tree: (a) a country's score rises after a media campaign simply trains people to rate their lives higher, with no change in material conditions or time use — is that an improvement? (b) two countries differ by a few tenths of a point but the translation of the ladder makes one language's top anchor sound more demanding than the other's — is the gap real? (c) a policy raises GDP per capita and healthy life expectancy, but daily affect reports and time-use data don't move — did it make people better off? (d) an anticorruption messaging campaign moves perceived corruption while administrative data and bribe surveys show no change in actual bribery — should the resulting ranking rise count as a success?

For leaves, I want at least these positions to show up as named leaves: the ignore-the-rankings view (wrong construct, not comparable, or both), the measure-affect-instead view, the monitor-but-dont-target dashboard pluralist, the target-the-six-pillars-not-the-score view, the Goodhart skeptic who thinks the score can only ever be an audit tool and never a target, and the full-hearted try-to-top-it view. If the logical space forces other leaves, add them and give them honest names, or mark them unnamed if nobody really defends them. Also flag any two leaves your tree cannot seperate behaviorally — I suspect the dashboard pluralist and the Goodhart skeptic might come out looking the same in practice, but differ on why — and mark unoccupied combinations as incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Aim for around 8 to 12 terminal nodes, usable in a 30-minute discussion, and dont hide the messy residual.
