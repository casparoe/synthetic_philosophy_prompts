A few months ago I sat in the tail end of an argument between two colleagues after a seminar on nonlinear dynamics, and I keep turning it over without settling it. I'll reconstruct the exchange as faithfully as I can. The seminar had covered strange attractors and the familiar butterfly-effect idea, and afterward one of them said that chaos makes "deterministic" do very little work, because a chaotic system is unpredictable in principle, not just in practice. The other said deterministic means only that the exact present state fixes all later states, and chaos leaves that untouched, so it can limit prediction only in practice. What followed went roughly like this.

Marta: The errors don't just grow; they grow exponentially. To keep a prediction within some tolerance at a later time, you need the initial condition specified to a precision that shrinks exponentially as the horizon recedes. Past a certain horizon, the required precision is finer than any precision attainable by a finite measurement in this world. At that point it isn't true that a better measurement would fix the problem; no physically available measurement could be good enough. That makes the unpredictability a matter of principle for any predictor who lives in the world.

Owen: That is still a limitation of the predictor, not of the system. The future is exactly entailed by the present. An intellect in possession of the exact state could predict arbitrarily far ahead. So the limitation is epistemic. You can say "unpredictable for us," but not "unpredictable in principle."

Marta: The intellect in possession of the exact state isn't a predictor at all; it's a stand-in for the laws themselves. Prediction is an inference from data, and data always have finite precision. In a chaotic system, finite-precision data do not remain approximately representative of the future state; the inferential route from what a predictor can have to what will happen simply gives out beyond the horizon. If "in principle" is meant to describe what a theory lets a predictor do, then chaos makes prediction fail in principle. What remains is a bare metaphysical entailment with no predictive content.

Owen: You're smuggling a stability requirement into predictability. Determinism has never meant that approximately similar causes produce approximately similar effects. It means the same cause produces the same effect. The fact that nearby starting points separate until they have nothing to do with one another is the butterfly-effect point, and it's exactly what makes chaos interesting, but it doesn't make the system indeterministic or unpredictable in the strict sense. If "predictable" in your sense just means "predictable from finite resources," then we've changed the subject.

Marta: I haven't said chaos makes the system indeterministic. I'm saying the sense of "in principle" that does real work in science concerns whether a procedure leads from an available state description to a future state. Strange attractors make this concrete: we can specify the attractor, and we can specify its invariant measure, but we cannot specify which individual trajectory will be realized from approximate data. The objects we can genuinely predict are statistical. If predictability in your strict sense were the only thing that mattered, the difference between chaotic and non-chaotic systems would be irrelevant to the theory of prediction, and it plainly isn't.

Owen: The difference matters for predictors with finite precision, which is exactly my point. The flow still exists, the attractor is still a set of trajectories, and each of those trajectories is still fixed by its initial condition. In fact, your appeal to invariant measures depends on that underlying trajectory structure being there. Statistical predictability is a fallback for beings who cannot resolve individual trajectories; it isn't evidence that individual trajectories are unpredictable in principle.

Marta: Then let me press on what the exact knowledge would have to be. The state is a point in a continuum. Specifying it exactly means specifying an infinite string of digits, and no finite procedure can do that for almost all points. So the imagined predictor's output is not the result of any possible procedure for typical states. A prediction, even an idealized one, should be the output of some possible procedure, not merely the value of an abstract function we gesture at.

Owen: The standard examples cut the other way. For the logistic map, or the Lorenz system, if the initial condition is a computable real, each later state is computable as well, even if the computation is expensive. So the idealized predictor need not be miraculous. And the non-computable cases are not a special problem for chaos; plenty of non-chaotic systems fail the same test when started from non-computable states. Computability is an orthogonal constraint, not something chaos introduces.

At that point the session resumed and neither got the last word.

Could you adjudicate this exchange point by point? For each move, say who is right about what, where each argument overstates or understates, and which uses of "predictable" and "in principle" are doing the work. Also say what both of them are missing, and what a defensible statement about chaos and unpredictability would look like once those distinctions are made explicit.
