A line of reasoning I keep going over seems to show that the standard Bayesian rule for updating on uncertain evidence is empty, and I cannot locate the step that lets the air out of it. Here is the argument as I understand it.

(1) Jeffrey conditionalization is supposed to be the correct rule for uncertain evidence: when an experience does not make any member of a partition {E1, ..., En} certain but merely moves your probabilities over the Ei to new values qi, your updated degree of belief in any proposition A should be Q(A) = Σi P(A | Ei) qi.

(2) Now take any probability Q that is absolutely continuous with respect to your prior P — that is, Q never assigns positive probability to a proposition P assigned zero. A standard representation theorem says that any such Q can be written in exactly this Jeffrey form, for some sufficiently fine partition and some weights qi.

(3) So every such Q is reachable by Jeffrey conditioning on some bit of uncertain evidence.

(4) But then the rule imposes almost no constraint on your new beliefs: the only thing it rules out is assigning positive probability to propositions the prior took to have zero probability, and every other conceivable shift of opinion can be represented as a Jeffrey update.

(5) That makes Jeffrey conditionalization vacuous as a norm of rational belief change. But that cannot be right, since the rule is advertised as a way of telling rational updating apart from arbitrary drift.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1), because the alternative — that uncertain evidence updates us by no rule at all — seems worse.
