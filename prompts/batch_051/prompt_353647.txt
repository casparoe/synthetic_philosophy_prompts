I've been arguing with a colleague about a scenario that neither of us can settle, and I'd value a committed answer. Suppose a common-law court in a jurisdiction with no AI-specific legislation has to decide how to situate a fairly autonomous system that can enter contracts, hold assets, and cause large, diffuse harms at speeds and scales its operators cannot realistically supervise. We agree that the system is not a moral person and that the question isn't about rights, consciousness, or punishment; it's about whether the familiar device of corporate personality is the right legal template. A corporation is a legal person in a thin, functional sense: it bundles contracts, property, and liability into a continuing entity, with entity shielding and limited liability for the human beings behind it. The attraction of the analogy is that it lets common-law reasoning extend an old device to a new problem without waiting for a statute. The worry is that a corporation is at bottom a way of organizing human interests — shareholders, managers, creditors, employees — whereas the autonomous system has no residual human beneficiaries, no members whose liability is being limited, and no natural person whose choices the system ultimately aggregates or represents. A sui generis status, or simply treating the system as a dangerous instrument the owner must answer for, might fit those facts more honestly. My colleague is around 0.7 that the corporate-person analogy is the right default; I am around 0.35, and we cannot locate the disagreement.

I'd like your actual credence in the following proposition: in a common-law jurisdiction with no AI-specific statute, a well-motivated court should adopt the corporate-person analogy as its default move for an autonomous system that holds property, makes contracts, and causes significant third-party harms — rather than a novel sui generis status or treating the system as a mere owner-controlled instrument. Please don't give a range, a refusal, or an it-depends; commit to a single probability and earn it. Decompose the question into the sub-issues your number rides on — for example, how likely it is that legal personhood really is a bundle of incidents rather than a status tracking moral agency; how likely those incidents remain intelligible once detached from human beneficiaries; how far the analogy has to be bent to cover an entity with no members; and how likely the alternative doctrines are to supply workable rules. Give a rough probability and a line of justification for each term, show how they combine into your final number, then tell me which sub-question your answer is most sensitive to and what evidence or argument would move it most.
