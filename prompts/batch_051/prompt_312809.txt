In my seminar on freedom and mechanism this week, I gave the class the standard, if somewhat stylized, example of the sphex wasp. The wasp, having stung and paralyzed a cricket, drags it to the threshold of her burrow, leaves it there, goes inside briefly, then comes back out and pulls the cricket in. If, while she is inside, an observer moves the cricket a few inches away, she emerges, hauls it back to the threshold, goes inside again, and repeats the whole sequence—apparently for as long as the observer keeps displacing it. The usual gloss is that the final act of pulling the cricket in is not released unless it has just been preceded by the leave-it-and-inspect step, so she never simply drags it straight in. The term sometimes used for this kind of rigidity is "sphexishness," and the example is widely taken to raise a pointed question: is our own flexibility of conduct something different in kind, or only a more elaborate version of the same?

Two students stayed behind to argue about the moral. Rachel maintained that the wasp is closer to us than we like to admit. When our plans are disrupted, she said, we too fall back on prepared routines: the writer who loses a paragraph and always re-reads the whole draft, the driver re-routed by roadworks who always selects the same substitute route. Adding a monitoring loop, she insisted, adds loops, not an author. Each higher-order routine must itself be triggered by some cue, and if we ask what triggers the triggering we must either land in an unmoved mover or admit that the regress bottoms out in something we simply find ourselves doing; either way, the difference is one of degree, and a difference of degree cannot supply the kind of self-determination free will is supposed to involve. Marcus answered that this gets the lesson backwards. The wasp's trouble, he said, is not that it is a mechanism but that it is representationally poor: it cannot update its model of the situation to register that the cricket is already where it needs to be, and it cannot treat its own routine as something to be revised. We can do both. We hold descriptions of what we are doing, notice mismatches between aim and state, and change the policy rather than merely rerunning it; that is a real causal capacity, not a ghostly addition, and it is the kind of thing an information-processing system can implement. So the example does not show that mechanism undermines freedom; it shows what a mechanism would need in order to be free. Rachel replied that this merely relocates the problem: the second-order capacity Marcus cites is itself triggered by cues, and unless he can say where the regress stops, he has described a more complicated wasp, not a self. Marcus rejoined that the regress objection confuses needing a first cause with needing a first self. A system counts as free, he said, to the extent that it can, across a relevant range of cases, make its own policy an object and change it in response to a recognized mismatch. We are not perfectly free, he conceded, but the wasp case invites a graded picture on which freedom is a set of control capacities rather than a metaphysical all-or-nothing. Rachel answered that a graded picture surrenders the very thing that made free will worth wanting—not mere flexibility, but the agent's being the source of what it does.

Each seemed to me to have put a real consideration into play and to have overreached at some point, and I could not cleanly referee on the spot. Please adjudicate the dispute as reported. Identify which specific claims by Rachel and by Marcus are correct, which are confused or overstated, and who is ultimately more right about the lesson of the sphex example for free will; then say what a more adequate treatment would add that neither student supplied. Throughout, separate what is settled from what remains genuinely contested among informed philosophers, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
