After a long career as an oceanographer, I have lately been reading moral philosophy in retirement, and a running dispute with my daughter — who lives abroad and is severe about expatriates who refuse to take up local practices — has left me with an argument I cannot dismantle. She persuaded me that tipping, queueing, and crossing the street as one's neighbours do are not merely matters of etiquette: the people around you have built their expectations, and sometimes their livelihoods, on the local practice. But when I tried to state the principle behind her view, it seemed to prove far more than either of us intended. The argument, as I now have it, runs as follows.

(1) A convention is a regularity of behaviour that persists because people conform to it, expect conformity from others, and prefer to conform when they expect enough others to conform. (2) Where such a convention is in force, a visitor who deviates from it frustrates expectations that locals have formed in reliance on the practice — the server who has treated the gratuity as part of the wage, the pedestrians who have stopped at the signal, the queue that has formed on the assumption that others will take their turn. (3) Frustrating another person's reasonable and settled expectations, when one could avoid doing so at modest cost to oneself, is morally wrong. (4) Therefore a visitor is morally required to follow local conventions for tipping, queueing, and crossing the street, at least when compliance is not costly. (5) The reasoning from (1)–(3) to (4) never mentions the content, fairness, or history of the convention; it turns only on the existence of the regularity and the fact of local reliance. So, if that reasoning is sound, it applies with equal force to any convention whatever, provided only that locals have built expectations on it and would be put out by noncompliance. (6) Now imagine a tourist district in which there is a stable convention of quoting every out-of-towner an inflated guest price, and the shopkeepers, hoteliers, and servers genuinely rely on visitors' paying it without protest and are genuinely put out by those who do not. By (5), a visitor there would be morally required to pay the inflated price without protest. (7) But that cannot be right: a practice that works by extracting a surcharge from outsiders precisely because they are outsiders cannot create a duty to cooperate in one's own extraction simply because the extractors expect it. (8) Something in this argument must therefore fail.

I would most like to keep something close to (3), because abandoning it seems to leave me unable to say why ordinary cases of queue-jumping or refusing to tip are wrong. But I cannot see what qualification would block the guest-price conclusion while leaving the original cases intact. Where exactly does the argument go wrong, and what does the least costly repair cost?
