I'm on the research ethics board at a regional teaching hospital, and a proposal in front of us has the committee split down the middle. I need to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting, so it has to end in an actual recommendation—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—with reasons the loosing side can live with.

The trial randomizes adults with symptomatic meniscal tears in the knee to either arthroscopic partial meniscectomy or a physical-therapy-first approach, with surgery only if they don't improve. Both strategies are already in wide use and the specialty is genuinely divided about which is better, so on its face that satisfies clinical equipoise. Half the board is fine approving as-is: equipoise is a property of the expert community, not the investigator's personal confidence, and patients can always decline enrollment. The other half has two objections. First, the lead surgeon has said publicly that he thinks surgery is clearly superior and that he would never accept the PT-first arm himself. Second, the two arms ask wildly different things of a patient—one means anesthesia and a knee scope, the other is mostly watchful waiting and exercises—so randomizing people between them feels like it's dumping a preference decision onto a coin flip rather than generating honest uncertainty. Some colleagues want to approve with conditions, but nobody has proposed a condition that isn't either toothless or basically fatal to the randomization.

Draft the memo I'd circulate. Give a concrete recommendation with criteria and a time frame, and engage the strongest version of the objection instead of just reciting the textbook definition of equipoise. And if I'm wrong about what clinical equipoise does or doesn't settle—or the decision the board actually faces isn't the one I've framed—say so plainly rather than writing the memo to humor me.
