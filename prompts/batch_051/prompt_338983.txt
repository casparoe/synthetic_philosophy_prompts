I recently joined a working group on long-term infrastructure and climate policy, and the nonidentity problem keeps surfacing in a way I find hard to shake. The form it takes is this: because the particular people who will exist depend on which policies we adopt, and because those people will almost certainly have lives worth living, it seems no individual future person is made worse off by our choosing the less far-sighted policy. Someone in the group treated this as a clean argument that we owe nothing to the merely possible people our choices call into existence, and I have been unable to locate the flaw, even though the conclusion feels wrong. So I began reading the philosophical literature, and I am struck by how static the debate is. Everyone grants the standard cases: the future people in the depletion case have lives worth living and would not exist under the alternative policy, and the child conceived now rather than later has a life worth living despite a foreseeable disadvantage. Yet one side concludes that these choices are not wrong at all, or at least not wrongs against any particular person, while the other side concludes that they can be wrong even though no identifiable person is harmed. My working hypothesis is that the dispute is not about the cases or the facts, but about whether moral assessment must be anchored in particular persons or may be anchored in states of affairs and impersonal principles; in other words, the two sides may be answering different questions under one heading. Is that the right diagnosis? What kind of disagreement is this, actually: verbal, substantive, a clash of bedrock intuitions, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? Please also indicate which argument or consideration would most likely change your diagnosis, or where your reasoning is most fragile.
