You, her former doctoral student and now a colleague in the same department, have been asked to give the toast at the retirement dinner of a historical linguist whose career turned on a deceptively simple question: what does it mean for a language to have a rule, and what becomes of the rule when it meets an exception? Her signature view has been that sound change is lawlike in its own domain, so that apparent violations must be explained by borrowing, analogy, or the overlap of independent changes; at the same time, she has argued that forms like go/went are not lapses in the past-tense rule but suppletion, in which a paradigm is pieced together from different lexical roots. Where other linguists saw mess, she saw two different kinds of order. Half the room will be linguists, many of them historical linguists who will notice every simplification or evasion; the other half will be her family and friends, who have no training in the field and will not forgive jargon. Write a five-minute toast in which her account of rules and exceptions does the real structural work of the tribute. The speech should honor her as a teacher and a person rather than as a list of publications, and it should land as affection, not as a lecture.
