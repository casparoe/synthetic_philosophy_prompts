I am trying to get clear on the authority I assign to an earlier self, and I want a principle that fits my reactions to particular cases. If a woman's advance directive says she is to receive no antibiotics for pneumonia should she develop advanced dementia, and she now has advanced dementia but appears content, I think the antibiotics should be withheld even though treatment would return her, briefly, to something like her old self. If the same woman, while still competent and only mildly impaired, says she has changed her mind and wants treatment, I think her present word wins and the directive should not be enforced. If a man's directive refuses a feeding tube, but after a stroke he is disoriented yet alert and asks for the tube because he is hungry, I think he should receive it. But if he left no directive and only years of settled statements that he would rather die than be spoon-fed with advanced dementia, and he now passively accepts being spoon-fed, I do not think those earlier statements should stop his daughter from feeding him. And if a directive contains a clause stating, "If I later refuse treatment while incompetent, disregard that refusal," I am unsure: I lean toward holding him to it, but not when the later refusal is calm and sustained over weeks. Is there one principle about the authority of the earlier self that captures these verdicts, or nearly does? Which verdicts would your principle force me to abandon, and what does it say about the case I keep returning to: a woman with a directive refusing all life-sustaining treatment who, during a lucid interval, pleads with her doctors to treat her?
