I am a PhD candidate in political theory, working on the evaluation of racialized political speech. For a chapter I am drafting, I have set down a number of commitments I seem to hold about what makes a statement racist, and before I circulate any of this I want a careful consistency audit of the set. Here is what I believe:

1. I reject private-intent accounts. A speaker's hidden racial attitudes are neither necessary nor sufficient for an utterance to be racist; what matters is the content the utterance expresses, not the speaker's inner life.
2. I also reject effect- and offense-based accounts. That an utterance causes racial hurt or offense is neither necessary nor sufficient: some true and important claims cause racial pain without being racist, and some racist utterances can occur without any contemporaneous harm to an audience.
3. I accept that dog whistles are real. An expression with innocuous surface content — phrases like "welfare dependency" or "inner-city crime" can carry this kind of coded message in the right context — may be racist in virtue of a secondary message that one audience reliably receives even though a broader audience hears only the surface.
4. The coded meaning of a dog whistle is social rather than private. What makes such a phrase loaded is the history of its use and its circulation in public discourse, not any explicit racial content in the speaker's head. A politician can activate the code without having articulated its racial content to themselves.
5. Nevertheless, when I turn to a particular utterance, I want to distinguish the politician who knows the code and trades on it from the politician who is genuinely ignorant of the phrase's racial history. I am drawn to saying that only the former has produced a racist dog whistle; the latter has, at most, said something regrettably coded but not racist.
6. I also accept an in-group reclamation carve-out. Certain terms can be non-racist when used by members of the targeted group in reclamatory contexts, even though the same surface words coming from an outsider would be racist. This suggests that the speaker's social position is at least sometimes determinative.

Please take these commitments in pairs and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to conflict. For the genuine tensions, rank them by severity, say what each would require me to give up, and identify the least costly revision that restores consistency in each case. I would rather have the audit flag apparent tensions and explain why they dissolve than miss a real one.
