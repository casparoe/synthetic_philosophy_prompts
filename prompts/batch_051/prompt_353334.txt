I am a novelist at work on a book set in a small Midwestern city between roughly 1910 and 1933, centered on a family that is split apart by the temperance movement and Prohibition. The characters include a Women's Christian Temperance Union organizer, a German-American brewery owner, a Protestant minister who preaches moderation but distrusts legal coercion, and a county official who enforces the Volstead Act selectively. I want each character to carry a real intellectual position rather than period costume. The standard story I keep hearing, that the Eighteenth Amendment was a triumph of organized virtue and that repeal was an admission that the remedy had become worse than the disease, feels too tidy to sustain a novel.

I would like you to treat the American temperance movement and Prohibition as a historical case study for examining the thought that temperance can be understood in three different ways: as a moral virtue, a cultivated disposition to moderate appetite; as prudence, a form of self-binding in which individuals and eventually the state manage weakness of will through commitment devices; and as repression, the coercive imposition of one class's or one culture's picture of self-control. Analyze the episode through each of these three lenses in turn. For each, map the lens onto the actual arguments, agents, and institutions involved, such as the Eighteenth Amendment, the Volstead Act, the organized dry campaigns, the saloon, and the movement for repeal, and say which features of Prohibition it explains well and which it misdescribes or leaves out. Where the three framings slide into one another, mark that explicitly. Conclude with your own assessment of what the episode shows about whether temperance is best understood as virtue, prudence, or repression, or whether the history reveals that a single word was doing several incompatible kinds of work. Please write the answer in American English.
