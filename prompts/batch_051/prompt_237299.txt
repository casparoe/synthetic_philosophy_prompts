OK, background: I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, and I've been going down a rabbit hole about something AI alignment people call indirect normativity. The move is: instead of writing down what "good" means and hardcoding it, you build a model of a person, crank up its intelligence, information, time, and consistency, and then treat whatever that idealized version would endorse as what the person "really" wants. I think I get the appeal, but as a programmer it looks to me like an unterminated loop: the idealization has to be specified by someone, and the spec is doing a lot of work.

So here's what I actually want. Take this proposition: for a given person, there is a determinate, stable deliverable from idealized deliberation — different reasonable ways of cashing out "more rational, more informed, more consistent" converge on roughly the same set of endorsed values, rather than diverging based on which idealization spec you happen to pick. What's your actual credence in that, as a number? Then decompose it: how much weight is on (a) whether a person's actual preferences are coherent enough to extrapolate in the first place, vs. an idealized version being a genuinely new agent rather than "me, improved"; (b) whether the idealization steps themselves are value-neutral or secretly smuggle in values; and (c) whether the convergence claim is even well-defined enough to be true or false. And tell me which sub-question would move your number most if you got a decisive answer. My credence is around 0.2; a friend who's deep in this stuff is at 0.7, and we cant figure out where the disagreement actually lives.
