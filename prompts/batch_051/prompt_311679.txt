I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but my current book has a philosophy professor as a main character, and I'm trying to get her research area right for a scene where she argues with a colleague. She works on conceptual engineering (she prefers "ameliorative analysis") — the idea that instead of just analyzing our concepts as they are, whether it's MARRIAGE, PERSON, RACE, or something else, we should ask whether those concepts are doing good work for us and revise or replace them when they aren't. I sat in on a talk by someone in that area who argued the whole project is confused, and I copied the argument down because it struck me as too slick, but I can't find where it goes wrong. It went like this:

(1) To revise a concept C because it doesn't serve our purposes, you first have to single out C. (2) The only way to single out C is to state the conditions under which the concept is currently applied — that is, what does and doesn't count as C. (3) But if you state the current conditions, you're relying on the very concept you set out to revise, so your target is fixed by the old, supposedly defective standard. (4) If instead you state different, improved conditions, then you're no longer picking out C; you've picked out a new concept C*, and you've changed the subject rather than revised anything. (5) So conceptual engineering is stuck between two failures: either it's circular (it presupposes the old concept as its target) or it's just stipulating a new concept and calling the change an improvement.

The speaker concluded that there's no third option, so a concept can be kept or replaced but never genuinely corrected. Is the argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails, and on what reading? I keep going back and forth on wether (2) is doing too much work or (4) is just asserting that any change of conditions is automatically a change of subject.
