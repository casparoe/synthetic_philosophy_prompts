In a reading group we got stuck on whether Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism can be stated without being undermined by its own critique of causation. A colleague set out the following parity argument. I found it clever because it turns the fire-and-cotton argument against itself, and suspicious because it seems to prove too much: the standard it applies to Al-Ghazālī would, if genuine, make every universal causal claim — the natural philosopher's as much as the occasionalist's — unstateable from observation. I cannot locate the misstep.

(1) In the fire-and-cotton argument, Al-Ghazālī reasons that all we ever observe is the constant succession of the burning upon the cotton's contact with fire; observation never shows that the fire brings the burning about or that the burning must follow. From mere observed conjunction, therefore, we are never entitled to assert that one created thing necessitates another.

(2) Now consider the universal claim at the heart of his occasionalism: no created thing ever brings anything about; God alone is the true cause. This claim says of every created sequence, observed and unobserved alike, that no real causal connection holds in it.

(3) By the standard stated in (1), particular observations of conjunction — and equally particular failures to observe a necessary tie — cannot license a universal claim about every case. To conclude from 'I have never observed a real causal tie' to 'there are no real causal ties anywhere' is the same style of ampliative inference the standard forbids.

(4) But the fire-and-cotton argument seems to supply only that experience-bound premise; on its own it reaches, at most, an agnosticism about this or that observed pair. Occasionalism needs the unrestricted conclusion, yet accepting the unrestricted conclusion requires the very inference step (1) disallows.

(5) So Al-Ghazālī's doctrine is either the local claim that some observed regularities do not prove an intrinsic necessary connection — which falls short of what he asserts — or the universal claim that God alone is a true cause, reached by an inference his own standard rules out.

(6) Therefore the fire-and-cotton argument cannot establish Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism as he states it; if it worked as advertised, it would establish at most a local, merely negative point.

If forced, I would rather reject (1) than (4), because I want observation to give us a defeasible but real grip on causal relations; but I worry that rejecting (1) turns Al-Ghazālī's argument into a bare induction-blocker and loses what made it significant. Which premise or step should be rejected, and what does each viable diagnosis require giving up?
