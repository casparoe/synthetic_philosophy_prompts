I'm repeatedly drawn to the precommitment account of constitutionalism: a constitution is best understood as a people binding itself, Ulysses-like, against its own predictable weaknesses—panic in emergencies, short time horizons, populist majorities—so that it can reliably pursue the values it reflects on and endorses in calmer moments. The view's virtues are real: it unifies entrenched rights, supermajority rules, judicial review, and bicameralism as different species of one device; it gives an elegant account of why democratic majorities might rationally choose to limit themselves; and it connects individual akrasia with collective self-government under a single model of agency. But I can't shake the intuition that something is wrong when a current majority is prevented from doing what it wants by provisions ratified generations ago. In Ulysses's case, the person bound and the person binding are the same; across generations, the 'we' that binds is not the 'we' that is bound, and the later majority hasn't consented to the earlier one's picture of its weaknesses. I find myself torn: sometimes the precommitment story reads as a profound account of democratic self-rule, other times as a flattering redescription of being governed by the dead. I want a negotiated verdict, not a side. Under what conditions should the intuition be debunked (say, as confusing 'not getting one's current way' with 'not being self-governing'), under what conditions should the theory be amended or abandoned (say, because it smuggles in a single agent where there are only successive generations), and is there a stable reading on which both survive—for instance, that constitutions may precommit procedures and institutional designs but not substantive values, or that the living always retain the constituent power to amend and are in that sense never bound?
