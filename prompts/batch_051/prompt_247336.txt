For a seminar I teach in the philosophy of social science, I would like a decision tree that classifies positions on the question, "Why are some nations rich and others poor?" The tree should not sort people primarily by which first-order factor they favor—institutions, geography, or culture—but by their underlying epistemic commitments about what comparative history can actually establish. Begin from the logical space of possible answers to a sequence of questions, then locate the familiar positions within that space, and explicitly mark and diagnose any combinations of answers that turn out to be empty.

The seminar's working hypothesis is that many of the familiar disputes between institutional, geographic, and cultural explanations are downstream of deeper disagreements about causal inference from history. Your branch questions should therefore bring out commitments such as the following, though you may revise, split, or add to them:

- Is the question well-posed, or does it presuppose a misleading unity in "nation," "wealth," and "the cause"?
- Are national trajectories independent enough that each country can count as a separate case, or are cases entangled by diffusion, empire, trade, and colonial borders?
- Can institutions be treated as an exogenous cause, or are they themselves produced by geography, culture, or prior wealth?
- Which kinds of evidence can discriminate among candidate causes: cross-country statistical associations, natural experiments, mechanism-tracing within individual cases, or only case-specific narrative?
- Does an adequate answer need to identify a cause, or can it settle for mechanisms and enabling conditions?
- How much contingency must a causal claim tolerate? If a small change at a critical juncture would have flipped the outcome, can "the cause" still be the cause?

Each branch node must be phrased as a question a seminar student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, so that the tree sorts readers by what they think about cases rather than by which label they prefer. Each leaf should carry the name of the position where one exists—for example, confident institutionalism, geographic determinism, culturalist accounts, pluralist interactionism, critical-juncture or path-dependence views, causal skepticism, and question-dissolution, understood as the view that the question is ill-posed—or be marked "unnamed" where no one defends that combination. Aim for between eight and twelve leaves and at most seven branch questions, and include a routing key so the tree can actually be administered in class.

Anchor at least three questions in concrete comparisons: North and South Korea since division, the two sides of Nogales on the U.S.–Mexico border, and the reversal of fortune among formerly colonized regions, in which places that were relatively prosperous before colonization are comparatively poor today and vice versa. Use these cases to force respondents to reveal what they think a particular comparison can and cannot show, not to signal which factor they find appealing.

Where the tree cannot separate two positions, say so plainly rather than inventing a question that pretends to. For each empty combination of answers, add one sentence saying whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, for each leaf add one sentence identifying the position's most awkward commitment, for use in seminar discussion.
