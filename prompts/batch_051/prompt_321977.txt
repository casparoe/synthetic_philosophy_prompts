I'm a philosophy undergrad and I've ended up with one foot in philosophy of science and one in philosophy of mind, and I cant shake what feels like a real parallel. Friedman's move—the F-twist, or whatever people call it—says you shouldn't judge a theory by whether its assumptions describe what agents actually do. Firms in models don't literally solve enormous optimisation problems; they just act as if they do, and that's fine so long as the predictions are good. That sounds to me almost identical to Dennett's intentional stance, where you predict a person or a chess computer by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires, while staying noncommittal about whether there's really anything in there that has those beliefs and desires in the way folk psychology pretends. Both look like licences to use deliberately false or at least unverifiable psychology because it buys predictive traction, and both tend to get attacked for the same two reasons: being empty, and sneaking in the very realism they disavowed.

So here's what I want to know. Is this one methodological move appearing in two fields, or is the shared as if just surface? If it's one move, does the condition Dennett puts on the intentional stance—that it pays off only for systems well designed by natural selection or engineers—transfer across? That would suggest Friedman-style unrealism in economics is only licensed when competition has actually shaped firms to be approximately rational, which is a substantive and maybe false claim about markets. Or is that exactly where the parallel breaks, because Dennett takes the rational pattern to be a real pattern in the world, while Friedman doesn't care whether firms maximise anything at all? I keep going back and forth, so a careful take on where the two structures coincide and where they come apart would help. And please write in British English.
