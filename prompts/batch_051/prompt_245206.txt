I've been reading the back-and-forth about whether LLM hallucinations are lying, bullshit, or just error, and this debate has completly stalled as far as I can tell. The Frankfurt contrast, as I understand it: a liar has to keep track of the truth, because he's trying to steer you away from it — he's guiding you to a falsehood on purpose. A bullshitter isn't steering you away from any particular truth; the truth just isn't on his dashboard. He's indifferent to whether what he says is true or false, and he picks his words because they do the job for the occasion, not because they're accurate. Then there's plain error: you're aiming at the truth and you miss.

Mapped onto LLMs, the error camp says hallucination is error: the system is supposed to produce true, useful answers, and when it produces false ones that's a failure of intended function, not an attitude. The bullshit camp says no: the training objective rewards plausible continuation, not truth, so the output is indifferent to truth by construction, and it only lands on true sentences by accident. Almost nobody wants to say "lying," since that seems to require an intention to deceive, but the bullshit-vs-error fight never moves.

My half-baked diagnosis is that the disagreement is secretly about where to locate the indifference: in the system's objective, in the designers, or in the output itself, and people slide between those without noticing. Is that right, or is the real issue something else — a verbal dispute about what "trying" or "caring" has to mean, two groups answering different questions (say, a technical one about training objectives versus an evaluative one about who or what to blame), or a question that's just ill-posed? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, an empirical claim worth checking, a change of question, or a reason to stop expecting the debate to resolve?
