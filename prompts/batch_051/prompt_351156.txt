Consider the following claim, which is increasingly pressing in aquaculture regulation: farmed decapod crustaceans — shrimp, prawns, crabs — have welfare in the full sense; their states feel like something from the inside, their lives can go better or worse for them, and at least some routine farming practices genuinely harm them.

A skeptic replies that the claim has two parts that resist being tested in the same way. The empirical part is whether decapods possess the functional machinery associated with pain-like states: nociceptors, central integration of noxious input, stress physiology, and behavior more flexible than a reflex. The conceptual part is whether any such machinery could ever settle the matter — whether one could in principle distinguish an animal that merely registers and avoids noxious stimuli from one that feels them. The skeptic holds that the gap is uncloseable by measurement; the advocate replies that the gap is no different in kind from the one we already accept for other high-level biological constructs such as stress, fear, or hunger, which are pinned down only by webs of behavioral, physiological, and neural indicators.

Suppose you are asked to arbitrate. Construct the strongest empirical program that could bear on whether a decapod's processing of noxious stimulation is valenced rather than merely discriminatory. Be concrete about the experimental paradigms you would deploy — for instance, motivational trade-offs in which an animal must give up a strongly valued resource to escape a noxious stimulus, conditioned place avoidance and its reversal by local anesthetics or analgesics, persistence and flexibility of responding after the stimulus ends, and physiological stress markers — and specify what pattern of results would favor the welfare reading and what pattern would favor the mere-nociception reading.

Finally, articulate the residue. What exactly would a positive result — behavior satisfying every operational criterion you propose — still fail to settle, and is that failure a temporary limit of method or a principled limit of the concept? What, if anything, would a negative result settle? In other words, separate the parts of the claim that evidence can reach from the parts it cannot, and say whether that unclosable gap should affect where the burden of proof falls when setting aquaculture policy.
