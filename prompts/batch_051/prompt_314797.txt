I keep going back and forth on this argument someone gave me, and I can't tell whether its valid or exactly where it breaks.

1. In science, the reason we prefer simpler theories is that they predict better: all else equal, they avoid overfitting and generalize further to unseen data.
2. In ethics, the data are our considered moral judgments, and the aim is to fit, explain, and systematize those judgments. We aren't trying to forecast future observations.
3. So the scientific rationale for parsimony does not transfer to ethical theory.
4. When ethicists penalize a theory with many independent duties as 'unparsimonious,' they are therfore applying a standard with no grounding in their own enterprise.
5. A many-duty theory can always be constructed to fit our considered judgments at least as well as a sparse one, because each extra duty can be tailored to catch some judgment the sparse theory misses.
6. By (3) and (4), parsimony has no grounding in ethics; by (5), parsimony is the only thing that would tell against the many-duty theory.
7. Therefore, when a sparse theory and a many-duty theory both fit our considered judgments, the sparse theory's parsimony gives us no reason to prefer it.

Is this argument valid? If so, which premise should we most resist, and why? If not, say exactly which step or steps fail, and whether the word 'fit' in step 2 and step 5 is doing two different jobs. I half suspect that equivocation but can't make it precise.
