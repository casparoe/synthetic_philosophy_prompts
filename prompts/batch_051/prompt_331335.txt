You are a metaphysician with a background in both ancient Greek philosophy and contemporary philosophy of time. Think through the following question carefully, premise by premise, and consider the strongest objections to your own answer.

The argument. Parmenides argued that change is impossible. His core thought was that for anything to come into being, pass away, or alter, it would have to involve what is not. A seedling would have to come from a seedling that is not yet; a cooling cup takes on a coldness it previously lacked; and in each case the earlier or later state is something that is not. But what is not is nothing, and nothing cannot cause anything, nor can it be thought or spoken of. So all coming-to-be, passing-away, and qualitative change must be unreal. He extended the same reasoning to plurality: if there were two things, each would have to not be the other, which again introduces non-being. Reality is therefore one, continuous, changeless, and undifferentiated, and the many changing things of ordinary experience are an illusion.

The development. Philosophers of time now distinguish two ways of ordering events: the A-series, ordered by past, present, and future, and the B-series, ordered only by earlier-than and later-than. On the B-theory of time, there is no objective temporal passage; the present is merely indexical, a matter of the speaker's location in time, not a moving spotlight. This view is often supported by special relativity, whose relativity of simultaneity undercuts an objective, universe-wide present, and it is associated with the block universe picture, according to which all times and events exist tenselessly. On this picture, change is not a thing's passing from being one way into being something it was not; it is tenseless variation. A single object has different properties at different times within a four-dimensional block, in roughly the way a road is straight here and curved there. The block itself does not come into being.

The question. Does Parmenides' argument survive this development? The B-theory appears to grant half of his conclusion, since there is no literal becoming, while rejecting the other half, since the block contains a plurality of things varying across time. Identify the specific premise of Parmenides' argument that the B-theory puts under pressure; say whether the argument is dead, intact, or in need of repair; and state the strongest repaired version of the Eleatic conclusion that the B-theory can still support, if any.
