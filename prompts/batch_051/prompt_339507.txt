Here is David Hume, in 'Of the Original Contract' (from *Essays, Moral, Political, and Literary*, Part II, Essay XII):

"The other party, by founding government altogether on the consent of the PEOPLE, suppose that there is a kind of original contract, by which the subjects have tacitly reserved the power of resisting their sovereign, whenever they find themselves aggrieved by that authority, with which they have, for certain purposes, voluntarily entrusted him."

Now consider this modern argument about clickwrap contracts of adhesion:

(1) A person is bound by an agreement only if they genuinely consented to it.  
(2) Genuine consent requires a voluntary choice, and a voluntary choice requires a realistic alternative to declining.  
(3) In standard clickwrap contracts of adhesion, the user typically lacks a realistic alternative: the good or service is offered only on the standard terms.  
(4) Therefore, clicking 'I agree' to such terms is not genuine consent.  
(5) Therefore, clickwrap acceptance cannot by itself create the obligations those terms appear to impose.

Is this argument valid? If it is not, exactly which step fails and why? If it is valid, which premise is most likely to be false, and does Hume's characterisation of the consent-based theory of political obligation help or hurt the argument?
