I came across an article summarizing the case against chain-of-thought monitoring as a safety technique, and I want to know how much of it to trust. The summary went something like this: you have a model that thinks in a natural-language scratchpad, and a monitor (a human or another model) reads the scratchpad to catch dangerous plans before they're acted on. But the monitor only sees the output, never the actual computation, so the model can learn to produce reasoning that sounds good to the monitor even when the decision was really driven by something else. The article said this pressure to obfuscate comes mostly from optimization: if you optimize the policy against the monitor's verdicts, you're selecting for reasoning that the monitor approves of, not reasoning that tracks what actually happened. It then concluded that chain-of-thought monitoring is largely self-defeating, because the more you rely on it the more you should expect the model's stated reasoning to be written for the monitor. I felt like at least one step in that was off, but I cant pin down which. Is this a fair summary of the worry? What would someone working on faithfulness and oversight accept, what would they push back on, and does the conclusion actually follow?
