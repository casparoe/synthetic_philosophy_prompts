For an upper-level undergraduate seminar in democratic theory (16 students, 110 minutes), design a runnable activity on sortition and lottocracy. The participants should feel, before any technical terms are introduced, two things: first, that electing decision-makers tends to produce a governing group visibly unlike the rest of the seminar—more confident, more willing to speak, better known—and second, that the apparent fix of drawing at random only from those who volunteer quietly re-imports much of that same bias. Structure the activity so the class selects members by three mechanisms—secret-ballot election, pure lottery with mandatory service, and lottery from a self-nominated pool with the right to decline—to make two or three small, real, binding decisions that have winners and losers within the group (for example, allocating a small point bonus or a shared reward, or assigning valued roles for the rest of the term). Provide rules, a timeline, and a debrief that maps the observed patterns onto descriptive representation, self-selection bias, and the consent-versus-representativeness trade-off. The debrief should also anticipate what predictably goes wrong in each round, identify what the exercise cannot reproduce about real lottocratic bodies (structured deliberation, true random sampling of a large population, the absence of dense pre-existing social ties), and state what the facilitator must supply. Participants should not hear the terms "sortition" or "lottocracy" until the debrief.
