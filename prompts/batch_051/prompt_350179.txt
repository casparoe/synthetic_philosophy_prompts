I have come to think that the St. Petersburg paradox does not force a bound on a utility function, a discount of small probabilities, or a revision of the decision rule. The paradox arises, on my view, from a mismatch between two kinds of average. The infinite expected payoff is an average over an unbounded ensemble of parallel plays; but an actual bettor does not play an ensemble—they occupy one history, and the quantity that governs whether the bet is good for them is indexed to that history. So I deny that there is a single finite fair price for the one-shot game, and I do so without appealing to risk aversion or to the practical limits of whoever offers the game. What would count against this? I want the strongest arguments, cases, and formal results that should make a defender of this view either modify it or give it up, ranked from decisive to merely troublesome, plus an honest judgment on whether the view has solved the paradox or simply renamed it.
