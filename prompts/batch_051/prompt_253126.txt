Analyze Captain Lawrence Oates's death during Robert Falcon Scott's Antarctic expedition as a case study for Hume's argument that suicide is not a crime against God, against society, or against oneself. I take the salient facts to be these: on the return journey from the South Pole in 1912, Oates was badly frostbitten and gangrenous, could no longer keep pace, and was slowing the party in conditions where speed was survival; during a blizzard he left the tent and did not return, and his companions recorded their understanding that he had acted deliberately to stop being a burden to them. I am not asking for the history. What I want is a disciplined two-directional analysis. First, take each of Hume's three main claims in turn and state precisely what it would say about Oates's situation: which features of the case the claim handles naturally, where the fit strains, and where Hume's framework is silent — for instance, about whether an act like Oates's is best classified as suicide at all, about the difference between an act's being permissible and its being praiseworthy, and about the epistemic position of an agent who acts on a grim but uncertain prognosis. Then reverse the direction: treat the episode as evidence. Does Oates's case strengthen Hume's permissive view of self-killing, expose a limitation in it, or show that the view was never designed to cover self-sacrificial cases of this kind? Be explicit about what the theory cannot say, rather than forcing it to speak.
