I chair a small conservation trust, after a career in forest ecology, and I have recently been reading the debates about whether the value of a place such as our reserve is intrinsic, relational, or merely instrumental. The reserve's 1987 covenant contains this clause: 'The Trust shall preserve the property in its natural condition and shall not deliberately introduce, or permit the introduction of, any organism not native to the property at the date of this deed.' The clause was drafted long before anyone concerned with the reserve took assisted colonisation seriously. Our principal canopy species are now failing under repeated summer drought, and the accepted projections indicate that the 1987 assemblage cannot persist here, while the nearest analogue woodland under the projected climate contains several species not native to the property in that sense that would not arrive by natural dispersal for many decades, if ever. The board proposes to underplant a small number of these species to keep a continuous, self-sustaining canopy. Does the clause permit this? Examine the wording, the purpose of the clause, and what its authors would most plausibly have said if asked; then say which approach should govern, and whether the disagreement tracks intrinsic, relational, or instrumental valuations of the reserve. Please answer in British English.
