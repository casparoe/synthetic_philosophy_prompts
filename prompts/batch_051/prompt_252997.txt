In a seminar on climate policy, a participant dismissed the idea that harms to future generations should count for nearly as much as harms today by saying that a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush, and then moved on as though the point were self-evident. I doubt the proverb settles anything, but I also cannot say exactly what claim it is supposed to be making. Separate out the distinct precise claims it could be used to assert. For example, it might be claiming that an earlier benefit is intrinsically more valuable than a later benefit of the same size; that a future benefit is uncertain while a present one is certain, so the comparison is between one sure bird and an expected catch of less than two; that extra goods matter less at the margin, so two birds later are not twice as good as one now; that the recipient may not be alive later; or something else entirely. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and flag any readings that turn out to be the same claim in different words. Then tell me which of these readings would actually justify discounting the welfare of future people in a climate policy calculation, as opposed to discounting future goods or sums of money, and which readings would fail to support that conclusion even if true. I am less interested in the history of the proverb than in a menu of its possible meanings and in which entries on that menu can carry the argumentative weight it was being asked to bear.
