I'm compiling a set of objections to the philosophical zombie argument against physicalism, and I want to check whether I've missed one that deserves a place on the list. The ones I have so far are these four.

(1) The phenomenal concept strategy: the conceivability of zombies only shows that our phenomenal concepts work differently from our physical and functional concepts, not that there is a real gap in the world itself, since two concepts can pick out the same property while remaining cognitively independent.

(2) The inconceivability objection: the zombie scenario is not genuinely thinkable in the first place; once you subtract experience from a being while holding fixed all its physical and functional facts, there is nothing left for the absence of consciousness to consist in, so the feeling that one has imagined a zombie is an illusion.

(3) The conceivability-to-possibility gap: even granting that zombies are conceivable in some sense, conceivability is not always a reliable guide to metaphysical possibility, because a posteriori necessities such as water's being H2O show that some conceivable scenarios are impossible, and the physicalist can place the consciousness-physical identity in that class.

(4) The self-stultification objection: in a zombie world, my physical duplicate would have exactly the same physical causes for every thought and report about consciousness that I have, including the conviction that it is not a zombie; so if zombies are possible, my own conviction that I am conscious gives me no distinctive reason to think I am not a zombie, and the first-person evidence driving the zombie intuition is thereby undermined.

I want an objection to the zombie argument that does not appear on this list and is not a restatement, special case, or combination of these four. It can be less standard than these, but it should be a real objection to the argument rather than a rhetorical jab or a generic dismissal. If you think no such further objection is worth having, say so plainly.

- "The new objection" (roughly 150–200 words): state the objection and explain the reasoning behind it.
- "How it differs" (roughly 80–120 words): explain how it is not merely a rewording, special case, or combination of the four objections I listed.
- "Is it any good" (roughly 120–180 words): assess honestly how strong the objection is, what it adds to the four on my list, and whether a physicalist should actually rely on it.
