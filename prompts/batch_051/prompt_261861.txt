I have been working through the socialist calculation debate — Mises's original claim that rational economic calculation is impossible in a socialist economy without genuine market prices for the means of production, Hayek's development of the argument into a point about dispersed and largely tacit knowledge, and the Lange-Lerner response, in which a Central Planning Board adjusts prices by trial and error until supply and demand clear, with enterprise managers instructed to follow rules such as setting price equal to marginal cost. What puzzles me is not so much who won as why the debate has never really converged. Each side has claimed victory, and the Austrian revival against market socialism in the 1980s and 1990s seems to have restated the old argument rather than resolved it. My working hypothesis is that the two sides were not answering the same question. Mises and Hayek were asking whether a board could replicate what an actual market process does — discovering opportunities, correcting errors, generating incentives under dispersed knowledge and real ownership — whereas Lange was asking the narrower question whether a socialist economy could satisfy the formal conditions of static general-equilibrium efficiency, once one grants the planning board the relevant data and a workable adjustment rule. If that is right, the impasse is partly a dispute about what a solution to the calculation problem would have to accomplish: a computational procedure for a problem whose data are given, or a discovery procedure for a problem whose data no one possesses in advance.

I would like a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a verdict on which side was right. Is the best account that this is an empirical disagreement about the feasibility of alternative institutional arrangements, a conceptual dispute about what should count as a price or genuine competition, a normative dispute about the standards to which socialism should be held, or a methodological dispute about the right style of economic explanation (equilibrium modeling versus process analysis)? And once the diagnosis is in place, what would actually count as progress — a formal result, an institutional experiment, a sharper distinction, or a change of question? I am less interested in a survey of the positions than in a precise account of why this debate resists resolution, and whether that resistance reveals something substantive or is largely an artifact of the terms in which it has been conducted.
