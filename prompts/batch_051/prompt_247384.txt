Ok so I've got an alphabet reform dispute and I want someone to work it through with me. The reform committee's charter says, in full: "The new alphabet shall be strictly phonemic. Every distinctive sound of the language shall be represented by one and only one letter, and every letter shall stand for one and only one sound. Spelling shall follow pronunciation, and nothing else." It's a clean rule and everyone signed off on it. But then they hit a case the drafters clearly didn't foresee.

The language, call it Vornian, has a rule that voiced stops surface as voiceless at the end of a word: /b d g/ come out as [p t k] word-finally. So the root for "wheel" ends in /d/: by itself it's pronounced [rat], and with the plural suffix it's [rader]. The root for "advice" ends in /t/: by itself it's also pronounced [rat], and with the plural suffix it's [rater]. The two singulars sound exactly alike, and the distinction between them only reappears once a suffix follows.

Now the fight is over the singular spelling. A stickler for "spelling shall follow pronunciation" says both words should be written R-A-T, because the pronounciation really is identical. But someone else says the rule was never about surface phones; it's about distinctive sounds, and the /d/ in "wheel" is still a distinctive sound even when it shows up as [t]. On that reading, spelling it R-A-D keeps the alphabet phonemic instead of merely phonetic, even though the letter d now maps to [t] in that position. The drafters wrote the rule without thinking about neutralization at all. What would they have said if someone had walked in and asked them about this exact case? And if we argue from the text, from the purpose of a phonemic alphabet, and from original intent, which reading should win? I care less about the practical spelling choice than about what each approach commits you to and which one actually controls here.
