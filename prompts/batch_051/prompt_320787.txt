A friend who took a course in the history of economic thought summarized the socialist calculation debate to me in the following terms:

"Mises and Hayek argued that a socialist economy cannot make rational decisions, because without markets there are no prices, and without prices planners have no way of knowing what resources are worth or what ought to be produced; any plan would therefore be guesswork. The socialist economist Oskar Lange answered them: a Central Planning Board could begin with arbitrary prices and then adjust them by simple trial and error—raise the price whenever a good is in shortage, lower it whenever it is in surplus—and in this way prices would come to reflect real scarcities, much as a market does; over repeated rounds, the planned economy would converge to the same equilibrium as a competitive market. Lange's defenders add that with modern computing power the Board could in principle skip the trial and error and solve the relevant equations directly. The usual conclusion drawn from this is that Lange won the debate outright: Mises and Hayek were wrong to think socialism faces a problem of information or calculation, and the only serious remaining objection to socialist planning is about incentives and political power."

I don't need a reading list, and I'm not asking you to referee footnotes. I want an accuracy audit of this summary. Which claims are accurate, which are legitimate simplifications, and which are misrepresentations? In particular, I'd like to know whether Mises's argument and Hayek's argument can really be collapsed into one claim in this way, whether a board adjusting prices in response to shortages and surpluses is genuinely equivalent to what prices do in a competitive market, and whether the computing-power point actually delivers the conclusion that the calculation problem is solved. I'm inclined to trust the summary because it matches the standard story I've seen elsewhere, but the phrase "much as a market does" feels like it might be smoothing over something. Does the "Lange won" conclusion survive the corrections, and what would a defender of Mises or Hayek say the summary still misses?
