I teach an undergraduate course in political philosophy with a unit on hunger and emergency. Every year, in student papers and in the public commentary we read together, I encounter the same argumentative move: famine is described as a natural disaster, on the model of an earthquake or a hurricane, and from that description a moral conclusion is drawn — that the catastrophe lies outside human agency, so that no one can properly be blamed and the appropriate response is one of charity rather than of justice. I would like to subject this analogy to a proper conceptual audit, and I want the result to be precise enough that my students can use it when they argue about concrete famines.

I teach the unit through Amartya Sen's entitlement approach, emphasizing the difference between a fall in total food availability and a collapse in the ability of particular groups to command food, and I use the Bengal famine, the nineteenth-century Irish famine, and the Great Leap Forward famine as the central cases. Please assess the natural-disaster analogy against that framework and those cases.

Specifically: produce a correspondence table showing what in a famine is supposed to map onto what in an earthquake or storm; identify which disanalogies are incidental and which are load-bearing; say what the analogy quietly imports — unavoidability, the absence of culpable agents, the moral irrelevance of prevention, or something else — and through which features of the target those imports enter; and then judge whether the moral conclusion usually drawn from the analogy survives once the mapping is corrected. Where it fails, say whether the dichotomy of natural disaster versus political crime is itself the right replacement, and whether a different analogy — a market crash, a siege, a fire in an enclosed space — would do better, or whether the question is best posed without analogy altogether.
