A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about whether mathematical definitions are ever correct in more than a conventional sense, and I came away unsure how to think about the question. Our argument kept returning to two examples. In the case of continuity, he maintained that the epsilon–delta definition is the genuine one because it directly encodes the intuition that small changes in input produce small changes in output; he regarded the open-set definition—a function is continuous when the preimage of every open set is open—as a gratuitous abstraction that buys generality at the price of meaning. I replied that the open-set definition does not discard that intuition but isolates its essential structure: it says a continuous map is one that pulls back open sets to open sets, which is exactly what it means to preserve the topology one has chosen, and that epsilon–delta continuity is simply the special case in which that topology comes from a metric. We had a parallel disagreement about the definition of a group. He argued that groups should be defined as collections of symmetries—bijective maps of a fixed object onto itself, closed under composition and inverses—and that the abstract axioms of associativity, identity, and inverses define something drained of the concrete content that gave group theory its point. I argued that stripping the definition down to those axioms was precisely what allowed the theory to apply far beyond literal symmetries, and that the axioms still encode the algebra of invertible transformations. I would like to understand the general issue underneath both disputes. When mathematicians generalize a concept and settle on one formulation among several candidates, what criteria make a definition the right notion rather than merely a more general one? Please explain the conceptual considerations involved, using open sets and groups as your running examples.
