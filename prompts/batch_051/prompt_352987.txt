A friend of mine has gotten really into the idea that sentience comes in degrees, and last week he defended it to me over dinner with an analogy I couldn't quite dismantle. He said sentience is like temperature: not the sort of thing that switches on at some threshold, but a scalar that everything has to some degree. We're running hot, a dog is cooler, a bee cooler still, and even a thermostat or a sunflower is just a hair above absolute zero. From there he slid straight to the moral claim: moral status should also be a smooth gradient, so squashing a moth is wrong in the same sense as kicking a dog, just much less wrong, and we should feel a tiny pang about unplugging a smart thermostat. I felt like the analogy was doing a lot of sneaky work, but I couldn't say exactly where.

Could you audit the temperature analogy for me? Lay out the correspondences first — what maps to what: the heat itself, the thermometer reading, absolute zero, whatever else ends up mattering — and then the places where the mapping breaks. Be ruthless about which disanalogies actually matter for his conclusion and which are just noise. In particular, I want to know what the analogy quietly imports about the step from 'is a bit sentient' to 'matters a bit morally,' because that step seems to be where the whole argument lives or dies. Does the graded moral status conclusion survive once its on the table, and is there a better analogy that does the work he wants without smuggling in the wrong physics?
