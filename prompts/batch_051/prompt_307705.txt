I am an undergraduate in an upper-level philosophy of cognitive science seminar, and this term we have been working through the stochastic parrots debate about whether large language models understand what they say. My study group is organizing a mock final-exam session, and I have been asked to produce one practice essay question. Please act as a philosopher of cognitive science with experience writing upper-level essay exams, think carefully about what would genuinely separate students who understand the issue from students who have memorized the stock phrases, and draft three things: the essay question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 marks.

Before you write the final version, briefly brainstorm the possible cruxes of the debate and choose the one that is hardest to fake. The question must not be answerable by reciting the familiar slogans — that large language models manipulate only form, not meaning; that grounding requires perception and action; that the octopus has never seen the world. The point where our seminar kept getting stuck is this. The anti-understanding argument needs the form-meaning gap to be one that no amount of statistical acumen could cross, even in principle; otherwise it collapses into the much weaker empirical claim that current models have not yet crossed it. But the pro-understanding side answers that this looks prematurely a priori: patterns of linguistic use are produced by creatures who live in a structured world, so the structure of the world may be recoverable from sufficiently rich patterns of form after all. What kept the discussion off-balance is that human readers acquire concepts they will never directly observe — quasar, mortgage, defamation — largely through text-like channels, and we could not agree on why a machine doing the same should be barred. Please build the question around that specific difficulty rather than a generic Does ChatGPT understand? prompt, and introduce a fresh concrete setup of your own, not the standard octopus example.

For the model answer, do not lean on named papers, quotations, or attributions; reward conceptual work, not recall. For the marking scheme, split the 20 marks across five moves a strong answer must make — for example, stating the anti-understanding argument in its strongest form; identifying exactly what the form-only premise is supposed to rule out; establishing whether that ruling-out is an empirical or a conceptual claim; saying what would count as evidence that a system has recovered semantic content; and taking a defended position on whether text-only acquisition of human concepts undermines the anti-understanding argument. Adjust the five moves as your chosen question requires. After each move, add one line on the typical way a weaker answer misses it. Then say explicitly what separates an A answer from a B answer. The question is for a timed exam and should be one a well-prepared student could answer in 75 minutes.
