I run a small community discussion circle for adults. I have no academic background in philosophy, but I have spent the last several months rereading a famous thought experiment about color and thinking through the arguments on my own. Next week I am interviewing a guest who defends a strong version of the knowledge argument. The guest holds that Mary, a scientist who has spent her whole life in a black-and-white room and has nevertheless learned the complete physical truth about human color vision, acquires a new fact on first seeing something red. He further holds that this fact is not a physical fact, and that its existence shows physicalism is incomplete.

I would like you to draft eight to ten interview questions. The conversation should be a careful cross-examination, not a chance for the guest to warmly restate his view. Please include at least one question on each of the following three replies, and after each main question include the follow-up you would ask once the guest gives the answer he is most likely to give; the follow-up should go after the weakest step in that answer.

1. The reply that Mary gains no new fact at all, only new abilities: knowing how to recognize, imagine, and remember red.
2. The reply that Mary gains acquaintance with redness rather than knowledge of a new fact, in roughly the way meeting a person gives you a direct familiarity that reading a complete biography cannot.
3. The reply that the thought experiment cannot get off the ground, because anyone who genuinely held all the physical facts about color vision and could reason perfectly would already be able to work out what red looks like; the alleged new fact is an artifact of not taking the premise seriously.

After those exchanges, put two further questions to the guest that would test how much work the argument actually does. One should ask him to run the same argument for a different raw feeling, such as pain, hunger, or the sensation of falling, and to say whether he accepts the same conclusion there and why color should not be treated as special. The other should present the reverse case of a person who has seen colors her whole life but has never learned the physical theory of sight: ask what, if anything, she learns when she first learns the theory, and whether the two cases should be symmetrical.

End with one or two questions that force the guest to say exactly what the argument, even if sound, would refute: physicalism as such, or only the narrower claim that every fact can be expressed from a detached, third-person point of view.
