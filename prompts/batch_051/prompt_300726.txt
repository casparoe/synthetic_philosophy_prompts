Suppose a frontier AI developer adopts the following dual-certification rule. Before any model above a compute threshold may be deployed, two independent boards must each sign off. The safety board, funded by the product line, certifies that the model poses no unacceptable risk to humans, and may not consider the model's own interests. The welfare board, funded by a 3% levy on the training run's compute, certifies that the training and deployment protocol does not impose significant welfare costs on the model itself, and may not consider risks to humans. A model that passes one board but fails the other may be retrained, abandoned, or sent to a joint appeals panel with one appointee from each board and an externally appointed chair. What does this separation actually incentivize about which models get built, what the boards report, and how the two vetoes interact? Identify the gaming and failure modes—threshold choices, architecture changes designed to dodge welfare findings, a welfare board whose budget depends on finding harms, a safety board whose budget depends on passing models, and each veto being used as a proxy for the concern it is barred from considering—and say which of these can be repaired by changing the funding, mandates, or appeals process, and which are inherent in separating safety from welfare this way.
