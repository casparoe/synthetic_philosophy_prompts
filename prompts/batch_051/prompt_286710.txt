I'm a novelist, and I've noticed something in the advice people hand out about thinking about your own thinking that looks like a double standard. When a writer is stuck, the cure everyone reaches for is to step back and inspect the thoughts themselves: what's the inner critic saying, what am I afraid of, what am I assuming about the reader. I've done this, I've taught it, and when I do it on a draft from yesterday it helps. But when a tennis player double-faults on match point, or a pianist flubs a passage they've played a thousand times, the diagnosis is the opposite: they "thought about it to much," they got in their own head, and monitoring the action is exactly what broke it. So stepping up a level to watch what you're doing is the remedy in one kind of case and the poison in another. Both are skilled people performing below their ability. What actually separates them — whether the thing being done is deliberative rather than sensorimotor, whether the meta-attention lands before the performance rather than during it, wether the monitoring is descriptive or evaluative, or whether the feedback loop can be paused? A novelist can stop mid-sentence and think; a tennis player can't pause the rally and rewind it.

I want you to actually decide, not just say "it depends and both have a point." Find the best candidate principle, test it against nearby cases — say, a stand-up comic who audits a joke while telling it, or a writer who edits each sentence as it comes out and freezes anyway — and then tell me whether the asymmetry in everyday advice is justified, or whether one side of it is just a folk story we'd be better off dropping. And at the end tell me how confident you are in what you claimed, and name the one point you're least sure of.
