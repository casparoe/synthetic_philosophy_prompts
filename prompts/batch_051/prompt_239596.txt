I keep coming back to the torture-versus-dust-specks puzzle after a long argument with friends about whether harm is really the sort of thing you can total across people. As I understand the original setup, you are offered a choice: one person is tortured for fifty years, or an astronomically large number of people — something like 3^^^3 — each receives a single, barely noticeable dust speck in the eye. The puzzle is that a straightforward aggregative calculation seems to make the specks the worse outcome once the number is large enough, while many people find that conclusion absurd and think the concentrated torture is worse no matter how many specks you pile up.

Here is the variation I would like you to work through. Keep two positions fixed for the discussion: the aggregative view, which scores each harm as intensity × duration and sums across persons, and the non-aggregative view, which denies that harms to different people can always be traded off on a single scale and holds that sufficiently severe harm creates a break that no amount of mild discomfort can cross. Now change the case. The tiny harm is a one-second episode of mild, fully reversible discomfort with no downstream effects, with each episode assigned 0.001 on a 0–100 intensity scale. The great harm is a single person's one year of pain at intensity 100 — still clearly torture-level, though far shorter than fifty years, and stipulated to cause no lasting physical damage. Compute the parity population N, where N people each receive exactly one such episode and the aggregate score of those episodes equals the total score of the one person's year-long pain. Then answer two questions. First, at exactly that parity number, does the original standoff survive? That is, does the aggregative view say the two options are strictly tied, so that any tiebreaker is decisive, while the non-aggregative view still says the concentrated year of torture-level pain is worse despite the arithmetic tie? Second, examine where each view lands once the numbers are explicit. What does the aggregative view's own continuity commitment force it to say about a finite number of mild headaches versus one broken leg — is that a reductio that should worry it, or a bullet it should accept? And what must a non-aggregativist say about where the line between harms that can be aggregated and harms that cannot falls, and whether that line can be drawn without facing a Sorites-style problem of its own? I am asking for an analysis, not just intuitions: run the calculations, say what each view says, and tell me what the variation does and does not change. Please write the answer in American English, indicate how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the point you are least sure about.
