Last night I spent a long dinner arguing with a friend about whether unending life would necessarily go stale. He kept reaching for the obvious fixes: God could install a fresh desire whenever we felt bored, or simply delete our boredom, or hand us an infinite menu of new experiences. I kept objecting that each fix either postpones the problem or quietly replaces the person whose life was supposed to be preserved. I now want to turn that argument into a one-hour seminar for first-year undergraduates — a mixed group, some religious and some not, with no prior work on personal identity — on whether endless life would be boring, anchored by Bernard Williams's Makropulos case of a woman who has lived for centuries on an elixir and eventually refuses another dose. Do not explain the position to me; I know the categorical/conditional desire distinction and the standard moves. Plan how I should explain it.

The plan should specify: the order in which the components must be introduced and why each depends on the one before it; the single carrying example the whole session should turn on, with the features it needs and the features it must not have; the misconceptions the students will bring or form, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; one closing question whose answer would show whether the idea has landed; and what I should deliberately leave out at this level and flag as omitted.

Format the answer as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

"session_goal": a one-sentence statement of the understanding students should leave with.
"dependency_chain": an array of objects, in teaching order, each with "step" (the component introduced), "why_this_order" (what earlier steps it presupposes and why it cannot come sooner), "depends_on" (an array of the step numbers it presupposes), and "head_off_here" (any misconception worth defusing at this exact point, or null).
"carrying_example": an object with "the_case" (the example that carries the whole session), "why_this_case" (what it makes visible that a generic example would not), "must_have" (the features it needs to do its work), and "must_lack" (the features that would confuse the point).
"misconception_map": an array of objects with "misconception" (the specific misunderstanding), "when_it_arises" (which step of the dependency_chain it tends to appear at), "how_to_defuse" (what to say or ask at that point), and "name_it_explicitly" (true if the misconception should be told to students as a known trap, false if it should be defused indirectly).
"closing_check": an object with "question" (the final question to ask), "good_answer_must_show" (the two or three things a correct answer must contain), and "what_a_miss_signals" (which dependency likely failed if students cannot answer).
"deliberate_omissions": an array of objects with "omit" (the topic or move left out), "why_omit_it" (why it is premature or distracting at this level), and "how_to_say_so" (one sentence flagging it as out of scope).
