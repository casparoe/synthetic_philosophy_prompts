Retired analytical chemist here; fell into philsophy a few years ago and now I help run a little reading group. One of the regulars, Carol, has been giving verdict after verdict on whether information about you is your property, and at this point I can't help treating her like a black-box instrument. I keep wanting to state the underlying rate law and then design the control experiment that would falsify it. That's what I'm asking you to do with me.

Here's her track record so far, as close as my notes get:

1. You own a chest-strap heart monitor, and it logs your heart rate for a year. The watch company wants to sell the anonymized data to insurers. Carol: yours — they can't sell it without your consent.
2. A supermarket tracks everything you buy through a loyalty card. Carol: the supermarket owns the purchase record. It's their ledger of their own transactions; you got the discount.
3. You mail a spit sample to a genealogy company. They sequence it and want to use your genome in a drug-development study. Carol: the raw sequence is at least as much yours as theirs. They need opt-in consent for research and have to delete it on request.
4. A street photographer takes a picture of you crossing a public plaza and sells it to a magazine. Carol: the photographer owns the photo; you have no veto just because you're recognizable in it.
5. A streaming service keeps a log of what you watch, when, and for how long. Carol: the service owns the log — a byproduct of running its own service, and you were a customer for those sessions.
6. A hospital does a functional MRI of your brain for diagnosis. A research group wants to train an algorithm on the scans. Carol: no. The informational content of the scan is yours; the hospital can own the physical images, but research use needs your consent.

This week's case: a company puts cameras on its storefront aimed at the public sidewalk and uses gait recognition to build a database of individual passersby, identified by how they walk. No transaction, no entry onto the property, no interaction with the company at all. Does Carol say they can build that database freely, or do they need permission from each passerby?

I care less about the yes/no than about the reasoning. Lay out the principle or principles that would reproduce her six past rulings, say which of those principles push the sidewalk case which way, and tell me your best guess about how she rules and why you favor that reading. If you can, name the one further case that would cleanly punch a hole in all but one candidate rule — the equivalent of a decisive control experiment.
