A city council is debating a limited futarchy trial: for certain spending programs, the council would first fix a quantitative welfare metric — say, pedestrian injuries per million trips or median commute time per dollar of operating cost — and then a prediction market on that metric would decide whether the program continues past a two-year trial; if the market price for the target outcome stayed above a preset threshold at the end of the trial, the program would renew automatically. When I raised the usual worries about manipulation and about the metric capturing only a thin slice of what people actually value, a friend who supports the trial kept answering with one line: the whole advantage of markets is that people have to "put their money where their mouth is."

I suspect that one line is doing several different jobs at once, and I don't want to grant it any of them until they are separated. Lay out the distinct precise claims that "put your money where your mouth is" could be expressing in this context. For each reading, specify the mechanism it alleges — for example, creating an incentive to reveal one's true beliefs, filtering out cheap talk, aggregating dispersed information, or punishing inaccurate overconfidence — and say whether it is close to trivial, plausible but contestable, or likely false, and what would have to be true about traders and markets for it to hold. Then say which of those readings, if any, actually supports the conclusion that a prediction market's verdict should decide whether a public program continues, and which readings would remain true while still being insufficient for that conclusion. I would like the answer in American English.
