I'm taking a course on how people learn science, and I've been noticing something in the interview transcripts we're coding that I can't quite line up with any of the labels we've been given. When a student gets a physics question wrong, the error isn't random, but it also isn't the same wrong model each time. The same person will say a heavier ball falls faster 'because it has more force inside it,' and then ten minutes later, on a question about a ball tossed upward, will say it keeps moving up because the throw left something behind in the ball that gradually runs out. If you ask her to explain, she gives a little rule that seems custom-built for that one diagram and never gets used again. But across a whole interview there's a consistency of a different sort: all her impromptu rules seem to be doing the same kind of work, namely keeping an object's motion the same unless a visible push changes it, but she only applies that idea to one surface feature of a problem at a time. And after the correct principle is taught, she produces these halfway mixes: she uses the right vocabulary for the examples from class, then re-inserts the old rule for a novel diagram, and when you point out that the two can't both be true, she doesn't see a conflict; she just says 'well, for this kind of problem...' Is there an established name for exactly that configuration — systematic at one level, patchy at another, and mixed rather than replaced after instruction? I've seen 'misconception' and 'naive theory' used for pretty much all of it, but I mean specifically the part where the rules are local and trigged by surface features, plus the coexistence of old and new after teaching. If there's no exact term, could you tell me what the nearest ones are, where each one misses part of what I'm describing, and what you would call the leftover?
