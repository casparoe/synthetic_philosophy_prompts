Last night a friend and I argued at length about whether a person who is in the middle of deciding what to do can coherently assign a probability to her own choice. He kept defending the innocence of saying, while you are still deciding, that there is an eighty percent chance you will send the angry email; I kept insisting that this mixes up the stance of a predictor with the stance of a decider, but I could not state the objection cleanly, and neither of us could say exactly where the real problem lay. I now have to lead a one-hour tutorial for first-year undergraduates on the claim that genuine deliberation about whether to act is in tension with holding an unconditional probability about the act itself, because prediction and decision are two ways of settling the same question and they impose incompatible demands on it. I want a plan for teaching that claim, not a restatement of the claim.

Plan the session. What has to go first before the core claim is even intelligible—perhaps the distinction in expected-utility reasoning between states of the world outside the agent's control, to which probabilities attach, and the act being chosen, to which they are not supposed to attach, together with an account of what it means for an option to be open to the agent? What depends on what after that, and at which step should the point be extended from certainties to partial credences so that 0.8 is shown to be problematic in the same way as 1? What single example should carry the whole tutorial, and what features must it have and what features must it lack—a private, low-stakes, two-option decision made now, with no external predictor, no precommitment, and no long-run base rates? Which misconceptions should I expect the students to bring or form, and at exactly which point in the sequence should each be headed off—for instance, that the whole issue is about free will or determinism, that only probability one and zero are a problem, that predicting yourself is just one more item of self-knowledge, or that the conflict is merely verbal? What should I explicitly decline to cover at this level, and how should I phrase that refusal so it does not imply that the omitted material would rescue the naïve view? Finally, what one question can I ask at the end to check that they have grasped the restricted claim—that one cannot take the predictive stance toward a choice one is currently making—rather than the overbroad slogan that you can never reason probabilistically about your own future conduct?
