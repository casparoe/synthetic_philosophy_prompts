There's something I've been stuck on with the old argument in Marshall Sahlins's essay The Original Affluent Society. The bit I mean is where he argues, roughly, that hunter-gatherers are the original affluent society because affluence is not about producing a lot but about having limited wants that are easily satisfied, so they work less and still have enough. I don't remember the exact wording, but that's the core of it. Then I started reading the newer work on the beginnings of farming, and it keeps saying that early agriculturalists were shorter, had more signs of nutritional stress in their teeth and bones, probably worked more hours, and in some places didn't take up farming because it was easier but because they were pushed by population pressure or climate change. So I want to know: does Sahlins's argument survive this evidence? I can see two ways it might not—the claim that hunter-gatherers had adequate means, or the claim that their wants were naturally limited. But maybe those weren't doing the real work, and the actual claim is just that we should judge scarcity relative to wants, not absolute production, and that part is untouched. Which premise is under the most pressure, and is there a repaired version of the argument that's still worth holding onto, or does the whole thing just become a romantic story once you grant that farming was a bad trade for health and leisure?
