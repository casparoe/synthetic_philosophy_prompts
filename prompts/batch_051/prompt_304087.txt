Someone in a policy seminar floated this and I want a real analysis of how it would behave, not whether it is a good idea. A small country writes into its budget law that the entire government's discretionary spending rises 0.2% for every place the country moves up in the annual ranking of countries by average self-reported life evaluation, and falls 0.2% for every place it drops; other countries change nothing. The number behind the rank comes from a reputable foreign pollster that each year asks a nationally representative sample the usual ladder question, where 0 is the worst possible life you can imagine and 10 is the best possible life you can imagine. The country's own statistics office is legally barred from collecting any of this, and the survey field dates are kept secret until the report comes out. Internally, each minister's bonus scales with the national score change, and each provincial governor's discretionary budget scales with their province's change on a separate poll. The stated target is to crack the top ten within ten years. Treat it as a mechanism, not a moral position: who games it first and how? What does the rule make the pollsters sampling frame, question wording, and interview schedule worth? Does the country end up happier, better measured, or just rearranged? What second-order effects appear in who gets surveyed, who gets elected, and how officials spend? And can any of the failure modes be patched without turning the scheme into something pointless? Please answer in American English.
