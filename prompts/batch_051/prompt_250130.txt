I am a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist, a prodigy in mathematical logic, becomes convinced that the semantic paradoxes are not technical curiosities but something like a grammar of family secrecy. I have read enough about the liar, Curry's paradox, and Yablo's paradox to follow the standard constructions, but I need a rigorous statement of a controversial position both to shape the character's thinking and to keep the novel's intellectual content from embarrassing itself before an informed reader.

Please write a sustained argument of about 1,200 words defending this thesis: Yablo's paradox does not establish that semantic paradox can arise without self-reference or circularity; it establishes only the weaker point that no individual sentence in the infinite sequence need mention itself. Address the strongest version of the objection directly: each sentence S_n says that every later sentence is false, so each sentence is about other sentences rather than about itself, and yet the sequence cannot be assigned consistent truth-values. Then answer it — for example, by locating the circularity in the sequence as a whole, or in the way the sequence is specified, or in the predicate "true" as it ranges across the sequence. You may bring in Curry's paradox as a comparison, since Curry shows that the ordinary liar's use of negation is not essential; the question at stake is whether reference to oneself is.

Conclude by separating a weak claim (no sentence in the sequence is individually self-referential) from a strong claim (the paradox involves no self-reference or circularity at all), and explain why Yablo's construction undermines at most the weak claim. No citations or bibliography, please; the argument should stand on its own. The writing may be as formal as the material requires, but end with a short plain-language paragraph that the protagonist could plausibly speak in narrative, one that preserves the technical point without sounding like a seminar.
