On Dennett's account of real patterns and the three stances, the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance are successive layers of description for predicting a system's behaviour, and one is warranted in adopting the intentional stance when treating the system as a rational agent with beliefs and desires gives real predictive purchase. The force of the position is that this is not a mere manner of speaking: the regularities the intentional stance tracks are real patterns, in the same sense in which the regularities tracked by the design stance or the physical stance are real, and there is no further fact about whether a system really has beliefs over and above the predictive utility of the stance.

Now consider a large language model. It is trained to minimise next-token prediction error over an enormous corpus of human writing; it has no explicit representations of beliefs or desires, no persistent goals of its own, and no planning faculty in the classical sense. From the design stance it appears to be simply a very large learned function from a prompt context to a distribution over continuations. Yet attributing to it something like wanting to be helpful, knowing the topic, or trying to complete the task often predicts its outputs strikingly well.

Does Dennett's argument for the legitimacy and the reality of the intentional stance survive this development? Identify the premise under the most direct pressure, and then either show that the argument still goes through unchanged, show how it should be repaired and what the repaired version says about such systems, or explain why it fails. If you judge the apparent pressure to be illusory, say exactly what feature of the language-model case fails to distinguish it from the systems Dennett originally defended.
