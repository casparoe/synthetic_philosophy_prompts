I am a high-school senior and serve on the steering committee of a service and ethics club at my school that has existed for over fifty years. Lately several of us have noticed that the projects we run now—mostly credential-building volunteer hours and college-focused events—have drifted far from the plain meaning of our founding charter, which emphasizes helping people who are not in a position to help us back and spending sustained time with the communities we serve. Our faculty sponsor calls this a local instance of value drift, and I have been reading about the general worry that institutions, and even whole civilizations, can slowly move toward values their founders would have rejected, not through any deliberate betrayal but because each generation applies the old words a little differently.

Rather than simply arguing about our club's current state, I want to understand a specific proposal one of our alumni has put forward for the next charter revision. Under the proposal, the statement of principles would become entrenched. Any amendment would require (1) a three-fifths vote of current student members and (2) certification from a five-person continuity council of alumni that the amendment is a natural development of the founding principles rather than a repudiation of them. The continuity council would be self-perpetuating: each outgoing member would choose their successor from among alumni the council judges faithful to the founding charter, with no input from current students or the school. The council's certification would be final and could not be overridden by a larger student vote, and the council would also be able to block the club from spending money on projects it considers contrary to the founding purposes.

Please treat this as a mechanism rather than as a moral argument. What would this amendment procedure actually incentivize among current students, among the wider alumni network, and among the members of the continuity council themselves? How would a clever student body, or the alumni network, game it over the next ten or twenty years? Where is it likely to fail over a longer horizon, and are those failure modes the kind that can be fixed by adjusting the design, or do they point to something deeper about trying to bind future members of a voluntary high-school organization?
