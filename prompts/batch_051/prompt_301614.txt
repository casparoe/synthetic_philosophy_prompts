I have been thinking about the following problem: the martial virtues — courage, valor, steadiness under threat, and the willingness to place one's body at risk for others — are among the oldest objects of moral praise, yet they are bound up with danger, and danger is the very thing that a successful civilization works to abolish. Suppose a large, wealthy society, otherwise continuous with the present, achieves by around 2075 an almost total elimination of involuntary physical risk. Interstate and civil wars have ended; policing, firefighting, and rescue are performed by autonomous machines; medicine and safety regulation have driven accidental death and serious injury to negligible levels; and the state aggressively suppresses recreational risk. As a result, no ordinary person, and almost no professional, is ever called upon to risk life or limb. My question is a predictive one: what happens to the martial virtues over the three generations that follow? Do they wither into ceremonial language? Do they persist in rarified institutions that deliberately manufacture danger, such as extreme sports, full-contact martial training, or volunteer emergency corps? Or do they migrate into non-physical domains such as moral courage and intellectual risk, and, if so, is that migration a genuine continuation of the virtue or a change of subject?

I want the answer to proceed as a chain of reasoning, not a story. Say exactly what mechanism you think transmits or extinguishes a virtue from one generation to the next, and what would have to be true for the causal story to go the other way. Identify which parts of your prediction you regard as robust, which are speculative, and what observable facts in the intermediate generation would indicate that the martial virtues are being successfully preserved rather than merely commemorated.
