I cover the science-and-ideas beat, and lately I've been reading about inverse reinforcement learning — the approach in AI where you watch an agent's behavior and try to back out the reward function it seems to be maximizing. The method quietly assumes the agent is, at some level, acting optimally for whatever it wants, so every action counts as evidence about its preferences. And I've realized my own everyday judgments about when behavior reveals what a person really wants follow something like that rule — except when they don't. I'm trying to figure out weather these gut calls add up to any principle at all.

Here are my verdicts on cases:

1. A friend has renewed a gym membership every January for three years and has gone maybe twice in that stretch. I do not read this as evidence that she values exercise. I read it as evidence that she values thinking of herself as someone who exercises — she's buying an identity, not revealing a preference.

2. A coworker has talked for years about quitting journalism to become a carpenter. He has never bought a tool, taken a class, or opened a job listing. I read his inaction as revealing: he doesn't actually want to be a carpenter; he wants to want to leave journalism. Where as in the gym case I let the stated goal dominate the behavior, here I let the behavior dominate the stated goal. That inconsistency is exactly what bothers me.

3. A smoker says she wants to quit, has tried patches, gum, and an app, and still buys a pack a day. Here I do think the behavior reveals a real preference-like pull — she does, in some robust sense, want the cigarettes — but she doesn't endorse it, and I wouldn't call it her preference all things considered.

4. A man gives twenty dollars to whatever canvasser stops him on the sidewalk, but has never once donated on his own initiative. I read the behavior as revealing something — maybe a preference to avoid feeling cheap in front of a stranger — but not a stable preference for any particular cause.

5. A woman says climate change is the most important issue in the world and then takes three long-haul flights a year for vacation. I do not think the flights show she doesn't care about climate. I think they show her climate concern hasn't been integrated into her leisure decisions, which is a different claim.

6. A man buys a Prius, then moves to a house twenty-five miles farther from work, so his total weekly driving goes up. I read the Prius as self-image again — he values the symbol — and the move as the more honest reveal about what he actually trades off.

So I treat some behaviors as privileged evidence about a person's real preferences and others as noise, performance, or compulsion, but I can't yet say what separates the two groups. The inverse-reinforcement-learning frame would treat all of it as equally valid evidence — every action optimal for some reward function — and that seems flatly wrong to me, but I can't put my finger on the principle I'm leaning on.

Is there a principle that gets all six verdicts, or nearly all of them, and what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a parent who says he values time with his kids but checks his phone through most of dinner? Please also tell me which of my verdicts the principle can't accommodate, if any. And be self-critical at the end: what consideration or argument would change your mind about the hard cases, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
