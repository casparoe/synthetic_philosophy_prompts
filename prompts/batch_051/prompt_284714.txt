In a recent panel on historical fiction and cultural appropriation, a fellow audience member challenged a white novelist who had set her book in a Black community with the one-liner “stay in your lane,” and a significant part of the room treated it as though that settled the matter. I have heard the phrase used in similar ways before: to dismiss a museum’s decision to display art made by people outside a tradition, to condemn a singer’s use of another culture’s musical forms, and to shut down even the question of whether someone has a right to represent experiences not their own. But I am not sure what precise claim the slogan is supposed to be making, and I suspect different people mean quite different things by it.

Lay out the distinct theses that “stay in your lane” could be expressing in debates about cultural appropriation in art. For each candidate reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something stronger that is probably false. I am especially interested in readings that distinguish between an epistemic claim (outsiders cannot know enough to represent a group’s experience faithfully), a moral claim (outsiders ought not to speak for or profit from a group they do not belong to), and a political claim (the authority to represent a group’s experience belongs to its members, not just as a matter of politeness but as a matter of standing or legitimacy). Which of these readings, if any, actually supports using the slogan to end the debate with the novelist, as opposed to merely cautioning her? And are there readings on which the slogan would overreach, for example by implying that artistic representation is always a kind of claim to speak for a group, or by making group membership an indivisible and exclusive thing?
