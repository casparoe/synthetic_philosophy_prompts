The Anthropocene Working Group spent the better part of the 2010s arguing that the Anthropocene should be ratified as a formal geological epoch beginning in the mid-twentieth century, on the grounds that the Great Acceleration left a globally synchronous, stratigraphically legible signature: plutonium fallout from hydrogen-bomb tests, the rapid spread of plastics, and other technofossils. In 2024 the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy voted the proposal down by a wide margin, a rejection later confirmed by the International Commission on Stratigraphy and the International Union of Geological Sciences, so the Anthropocene has no standing as a formal unit of the geological time scale. Does the argument for dating the Anthropocene to the mid-twentieth century survive that rejection once the claim is reframed as a thesis about a historical period rather than a geological epoch? Specifically, which premise of the original case did the geologists' decision actually put pressure on—the reality of the signature, its global synchroneity, its usefulness for correlation, or something else—and should historians or earth-system scientists regard the stratigraphers' verdict as settling the question of when the period begins and who decides? If the historical-period version of the claim needs repair after 2024, say exactly what should be revised and what should be kept.
