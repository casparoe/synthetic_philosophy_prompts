After a recent and somewhat humbling attempt to translate a short rhyming poem into rhymed English verse, I have been unable to stop thinking about what a translation under formal constraint is actually trying to preserve. I am sketching an essay in the spirit of the questions raised in Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot, and I would welcome comments on the notes below. Please attend especially to conceptual clarity, to whether the central distinction earns its keep, and to any place where the argument overreaches or quietly changes its terms.

Working title: Form as Cognitive Score, Not Vessel.

Argument sketch.

Translators often treat rhyme and meter as a container: something to be preserved when convenient and discarded when it interferes with meaning. I want to resist that picture. A poem's form is not a neutral vessel that carries content from one language to another; it is closer to a score that prescribes a sequence of cognitive and affective operations — expectation, delay, surprise, closure, the felt grouping of units, the way sound anticipates sense. Being faithful to a poem means preserving something of this score, because the score is part of what readers of the original experience as the poem's content.

This suggests a distinction between score fidelity and surface fidelity. Score fidelity asks whether the translation makes its reader perform recognizably analogous operations: the same kind of metrical expectation, the same pressure at the line end, the same weight given to sound as a prompt for sense. Surface fidelity asks only whether the same device — a given rhyme scheme, a given syllable count — has been reproduced, regardless of how it now functions in the target language. A translation can possess surface fidelity while losing score fidelity: a rhyme that falls naturally in the source language may read in the translation as forced, comic, or merely decorative, so the device survives but the experience does not.

If that is right, then the familiar opposition between fidelity to sense and fidelity to form is unstable. Much of what we call sense — irony, pace, emphasis, intimacy — is itself carried by elements of the score. A plain prose rendering has not preserved the sense and lost only the ornament; it has preserved a paraphraseable residue while letting part of the sense escape.

Finally, I want to consider the limit case in which preserving the score requires sacrificing so much lexical content that the original subject matter becomes unrecognizable. My current suspicion is that this is not a failure of form preservation as such, but a sign that the target language offers no material in which an analogous score can be realized. It would show that score fidelity cannot be an absolute requirement. I am unsure whether that concession is consistent with the earlier claim that form is internal to sense, and I would be particularly grateful for comments on that point.
