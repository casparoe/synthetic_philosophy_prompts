I teach philosophy and political theory, and I have been refining a distinction that I find useful for diagnosing why debates about American exceptionalism so reliably misfire. I call the view “indexical exceptionalism.” The core claim is that the sentence “America is exceptional” is not a proposition with a fixed truth-value but an indexical sentence whose content is partly supplied by context. Specifically, I take an exceptionalist claim to be a relation among three relata: a subject (the United States), a comparison class (which is usually left implicit), and a parameter (the dimension along which difference is measured, such as revolutionary origins, economic mobility, religiosity, imperial behavior, or absence of a mass socialist party). The comparison class and the parameter are the indexical elements: they shift between contexts and even within a single exchange. Thus, a historian may say the United States was exceptional in the weak sense that its political development lacked a feudal lineage, while her audience hears the strong sense that the United States is somehow outside the general laws by which other modern states operate. On my view, the ideological force of “American exceptionalism” comes from this very underdetermination: it allows a speaker to trade on a strong normative uniqueness claim while retaining the option of retreating to a truistic descriptive claim about some parameter or comparison class if challenged. I have been calling this “exceptionalism with an unbound comparison class,” or indexical exceptionalism.

Specifically: (1) Is this reconstruction any better than the familiar descriptive-versus-normative distinction, or does it just re-describe that distinction with extra machinery? (2) Are there uses of “American exceptionalism” that do appear to carry fixed semantic content, such that my claim of systematic underdetermination is too strong? (3) Does my account of the ideological function—that underdetermination is doing the persuasive work—collapse into a general theory of political abstraction, or does it capture something particular to the American case?
