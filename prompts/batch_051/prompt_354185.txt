I've been trying to pin down whether Mill's defense of free speech in *On Liberty* rests on an assumption about who is doing the speaking. In chapter two he argues that the chief part of intellectual cultivation is learning the reasons for one's own opinions, and that this is impossible unless one hears opposing arguments stated by people who actually hold them. He also suggests that when a doctrine is not contested, it becomes a kind of lifeless dogma rather than a living belief. That picture seems to depend on dissenting views being carried by sincere human minds who are themselves inquirers, with something at stake in their views and with the capacity to change their minds.

Now suppose a large share of the "opposing arguments" in public discourse are produced not by human inquirers but by language models or adaptively generated synthetic agents. These systems can produce unlimited dialectical opposition on demand, tailored to whatever preconceptions a given audience already has. They can simulate disagreement, but there is no human insight behind the dissenting view, and the outputs are optimized more for engagement or persuasion persistence than for advancing understanding. Would Mill's cultivation-of-the-understanding argument still support free speech in this setting? Or does the argument now support something else—perhaps that synthetic dissenting speech should be weighted differently, distrusted, or regulated, because the epistemic benefit Mill describes depends on the speaker's genuine belief and fallibility? I'm interested in which premise fails and whether the argument can be repaired, not in a general policy answer.
