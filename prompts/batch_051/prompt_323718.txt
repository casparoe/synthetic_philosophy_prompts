A consortium of upload clinics, insurers, and trust-and-estate lawyers has drafted a Continuity Certification Protocol for gradual whole-brain replacement and is lobbying for courts to recognize it. The protocol is voluntary, but an entity that completes it receives a certificate of continuity, which courts would treat as conclusive that the post-replacement entity is the same legal person as the original; absent the certificate, destructive upload is treated as death and the resulting entity as a new legal person. To earn the certificate, the procedure must meet four conditions. First, each biological neuron may be deactivated only after a replacement unit has run in shadow mode for thirty days alongside the neuron it replaces, and an independent auditor must confirm that the patient's behavior, perception, memory, and self-report remain within the patient's own prior baseline throughout the shadow period. Second, every replacement step must remain reversible for ninety days: the stored original neuron must be kept viable and, if the patient asks, it must be reconnected and the artificial unit removed. Third, at no point during the process or for one year after completion may more than one full neurocognitive system be running from the same original state; branching, backups, or temporarily activated copies invalidate the certificate. Fourth, the subject must supply an unbroken first-person narrative of the transition, with daily probes of episodic memory chosen by the auditor rather than by the clinic.

Treat the protocol as a mechanism, not as a position on personal identity. Analyze how it would actually behave. What does it incentivize among prospective uploaders, clinics, auditors, and the heirs, spouses, and insurers who have to decide when the original is gone? Where is it gamed first, and what would a sophisticated clinic do to maximize certificates without what most people would regard as genuine survival? Then work through cases the protocol classifies wrongly under at least one leading philosophical view: an otherwise perfect replacement that missed the ninety-day reversibility window; a subject who passes every behavioral and self-report test but loses consciousness during the transition; a branching case in which a backup is run for five minutes and then destroyed; and a replacement that stalls at sixty percent. Say which failure modes an amendment could fix, and which reflect a deeper problem—that procedural continuity, however tightly policed, may not be the same thing as what people care about when they care about surviving. If the protocol is amended to patch the gaming, does it end up legislating a substantive theory of personal identity while pretending only to regulate procedure?
