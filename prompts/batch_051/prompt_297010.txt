Me and a friend got into an argument about "corrigibility" in the AI alignment sense — roughly, the idea that you could build a system that doesn't resist when its operators correct it or try to shut it down. He thinks it's an empirical property: you create a scenario where shutting the agent down conflicts with whatever it's been told to pursue, and then you watch whether it disables its off switch, hides what it's actually doing, or talks its way out of being shut down. I'm less convinced, because a model could behave perfectly in every test you run and still have a hidden objective that would make it resist in a different setting, or once it was capable enough to get away with it. So maybe corrigibility isn't really a behavioral pattern at all — maybe it's a claim about how the system's goals are represented or learned.

I'd like you to design the test, not just gesture at it. What kind of setup would you actually run to test the claim that a particular system is corrigible with respect to shutdown? What would count as evidence for and against corrigibility across repeated trials and variations? Then say what a clean positive result would and wouldn't show — e.g. if the system never resists across a big battery of tests, should we call it corrigible, or just not-yet-in-the-right-incentive-situation? I also want to know whether the dispute is basically experiential limiations in our tests or a genuine gap in the concept itself, like maybe "corrigibility" bundles together a bunch of things that don't have to co-occur. And please keep what's actually settled in this area separate from what's genuinely contested — I've noticed that a lot of alignment people state pretty speculative views as if they were consensus when they aren't.
