I am currently taking a course on the history of mathematical standards, and we have been working through the part of Descartes’s La Géométrie where he draws a boundary between geometrical curves and mechanical curves. I would like help understanding a specific passage in Book II. I will paraphrase the passage rather than quote it directly, because I am reading it in an older translation that I find hard to reproduce exactly. Descartes argues there that the spiral, the quadratrix, and similar lines should not be received into geometry, since they are described by two separate motions between which there is no relation that can be measured exactly. Curves that are properly geometrical, by contrast, are traced either by a single continuous motion or by several motions whose relation to one another is precisely known.

I am struggling with the role that the tracing of a curve plays in this argument. Why should the manner of description determine whether a curve is a legitimate mathematical object? It seems to make the ontology of curves depend on kinematics, and I am not sure how literally Descartes means that. I am also puzzled by the quadratrix as an example: the two motions that generate it are each perfectly exact when considered alone, so the problem must lie in how they are coordinated. Is Descartes’s real objection that the relation between the two motions cannot be expressed exactly, or is he implicitly relying on a further assumption about algebraic representability? The two worries seem connected, but I cannot tell which is doing the argumentative work.

I would also like help understanding what work the phrase about “single continuous motion” is doing. If someone simply stipulated a new motion whose rule is taken as primitive, would Descartes’s criterion then admit the spiral? Or is his point that such a stipulation would smuggle in the very curve it is supposed to construct, so the curve would not have been genuinely generated? I am not asking for a modern verdict on Cartesian geometry, but for help reconstructing the internal logic of his distinction so that I can see why this passage is not simply an arbitrary restriction.
