A foundation that describes itself as effective-altruist in orientation has announced a grantmaking protocol intended to answer a standing criticism: that the movement systematically privileges interventions whose effects are easy to quantify. Under the rules, at least 75 percent of annual disbursements must go to projects with a pre-registered outcome variable, an 18-month measurement horizon, and an independent audit of the effect estimate. The remaining 25 percent may fund projects without such metrics, but each such grant must carry a public "shadow ledger" in which the grantee records, every quarter, the consequences it believes the work is producing that the official metrics cannot capture. Program officers' bonuses are tied to how closely their ex ante estimates of measured effect per dollar match the audited results for the projects they approved. Treat this as a mechanism rather than a policy to endorse or condemn: work through the incentives it creates for grantees, for program officers, and for the populations the funded programs are meant to serve. Where is it likely to be gamed first, what second-order effects emerge, and after five years would you expect it to have reduced measurability bias or simply shifted it into new forms?
