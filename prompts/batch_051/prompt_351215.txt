I am a high school student in an epistemology course, and I have become convinced by the conclusion usually drawn from the dogmatism paradox. The argument, as I understand it, is this: if I know a proposition, then any evidence against it must be misleading; and if I already know that the evidence would be misleading, it seems rational to set that evidence aside before examining it. The upshot — that genuine knowledge can license disregarding counterevidence in advance — strikes me as both arrogant and dangerous, and yet I cannot locate the flaw. I would like a structured account of what would count against this position, not a survey of standard objections. Which observations, arguments, cases, or results in nearby fields should actually move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? Please separate what a defender of the paradox's conclusion would themselves concede as counting against it from what they would dismiss as trading on a weaker sense of "knowledge" or "rational." Include at least one concrete scenario in which following the dogmatic policy seems plainly wrong even when the premises appear to hold, and at least one in which it seems vindicated. Then assess honestly whether the position has been insulated by its own definitions, and whether such insulation is a defect in a view of this kind. I suspect the thing that would most move me is a realistic case in which someone who knew something was nonetheless made better off by examining evidence against it; if you use that to weight the defeaters, flag it.
