I keep going back and forth on an old Rawls-vs-Harsanyi thing, and I'd like someone to actually adjudicate it instead of hand-waving at the usual SEP summaries. In A Theory of Justice, in the stretch where Rawls explains why the parties in the original position would rely on maximin, he argues that behind the veil you don't know your place in society, your conception of the good, or your appetite for risk, and with all of that hidden there is no rationally defensible way to assign probabilities to which life you'll end up living; so the parties should evaluate principles by how well the worst-off person fares under each candidate, which delivers the difference principle. In his 1955 Journal of Political Economy paper on individualistic ethics and interpersonal comparisons, Harsanyi sets up a similar-seeming device: a rational observer who is equally likely to turn out to be any member of society, and who then satisfies the usual Von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, must by expected utility reasoning choose whichever society has the higher average utility.

Its the same picture but two very different answers: maximin and the difference principle versus average utilitarianism. The obvious reconciliations are that Rawls's veil is deliberately thicker (it hides the risk attitudes that Harsanyi's observer is assumed to have, and it refuses the equal-probability step that Harsanyi treats as a moral axiom rather than as ordinary uncertainty), and that Harsanyi is doing ex ante evaluation of a risky prospect while Rawls is doing ex post evaluation of a realized distribution. I don't trust myself to tell whether the ex ante/ex post thing is doing real work or just relabeling the disagreement. So: can these two arguments be reconciled in a way that leaves neither party irrational by his own lights, and what exactly has to be given up to do it? Lay out the best two or three reconciliations, then say which one is honest and which is special pleading.
