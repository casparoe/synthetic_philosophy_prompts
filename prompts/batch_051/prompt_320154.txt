Question: Imagine a three-person panel that decides each proposition by majority vote. The conclusion R holds just in case both premises P and Q hold. Explain the discursive dilemma, showing how the panel can accept P, accept Q, and reject R even though every member is individually consistent. Then evaluate the usual escape route, conclusion-based voting, where the panel votes only on R. What does it fix, what does it give up, and is it enough?

Student answer: The discursive dilemma, also called Condorcet's paradox, is when a group of individually consistent judges produces a collectively inconsistent set of judgments. Take three judges deciding liability R, which requires both negligence P and a valid contract Q. Judge 1 accepts P and rejects Q, so rejects R. Judge 2 rejects P and accepts Q, so rejects R. Judge 3 accepts both, so accepts R. Under majority rule the panel accepts P (judges 1 and 3), accepts Q (judges 2 and 3), but rejects R (judges 1 and 2). Since each judge is consistent with R iff P and Q, the inconsistency has to come from the aggregation procedure itself, which shows the group cannot be treated as a single rational agent.

The standard fix is conclusion-based voting: have the panel vote only on R and ignore the premises. This removes the problem because only one proposition is put to a vote, so the collective view is automatically consistent. The cost is that the panel no longer tracks its members' reasons, and in the example the majority accepted both P and Q, which entail R, yet conclusion-based voting would reject R. So conclusion-based voting just reproduces the same inconsistency it was meant to solve. The better response is premise-based voting: aggregate the premises by majority vote and then derive R by logic. That way the group respects its own reasoning and the outcome is always consistent.
