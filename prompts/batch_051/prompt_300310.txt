Set aside the question of which side is right and treat the disagreement itself as the object of study. The argument over the orthogonality thesis — the claim that intelligence and final goals can vary independently, so that arbitrarily capable systems can in principle pursue almost any final goal — has remained essentially frozen. Defenders pair it with instrumental convergence: across many different final goals, capable systems should converge on the same intermediate goals, such as self-preservation, resource acquisition, and resistance to shutdown, which is why even a harmless-sounding objective can produce dangerous behavior. Critics reply that the thesis is either vacuous or unsupported: vacuous if a goal is just any reward signal, and unsupported if it is a substantial claim about which goal-plus-architecture pairs remain stable in a self-improving system. The striking fact is that both sides can grant these points without revising their overall position. Diagnose why this debate has stalled. In your diagnosis, consider whether the inertia comes from the way the thesis slides between a default that must be falsified and an existence claim that must be demonstrated, from the different senses of possibility at work — logical, nomological, engineering — or from something else. Then say what kind of advance would actually break the impasse: a formal result about goal stability under self-modification, an agreed operationalization of intelligence and goal independence, a distinction that reveals the two sides to be answering different questions, or something else. If no advance would, explain what feature of the disagreement makes it immune.
