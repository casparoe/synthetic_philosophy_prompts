Aumann's agreement theorem is often stated as: if two agents have a common prior, update by Bayesian conditionalization on private information, and their posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge, then those posterior probabilities must be equal. I want to test how much weight the common-knowledge condition is doing. Consider this case. Amina and Bo are honest, careful epidemiologists who share a common prior over the same family of transmission models and then update on different private datasets. Each computes an exact posterior probability that a newly observed variant is more transmissible. Before sharing, they both post their exact numbers to a shared dashboard that rounds every probability to the nearest ten percentage points for display; both know the rounding rule, and each sees what the other's display says, knows the other sees it, and so on. Amina's display reads 30%, Bo's reads 70%. Does the theorem apply to this pair, so that at least one of them must be failing some condition? Go through the conditions one at a time, say which are satisfied and which are only satisfied up to rounding, and then judge whether the case qualifies. Conclude by saying whether the example reveals that exact common knowledge of posteriors is a fragile idealization, or merely that the two have not met the stated condition—and whether repeated rounds of posting their rounded displays and updating on what they see would change your verdict.
