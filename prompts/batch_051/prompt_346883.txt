A friend of mine keeps using one analogy to settle an argument in my family, and I want someone to stress-test it before I concede. The argument is about what you owe someone when you tell them something. My brother's line is simple: if it turns out true, you were fine to say it; if it turns out false, you were wrong to say it. My friend says that misses the moment of telling. He says asserting is like promising: when you state something, you're giving your word about how the world is, the way a promise is giving your word about what you'll do. So just as it's wrong to promise when you can't or won't deliver, it's wrong to assert something you're not in a position to stand behind, even if it happens to be true.

It sounds right to me, but I keep fealing like the analogy is doing more work than it lets on. Could you audit it properly? Give me the correspondence table (what counts as the promise, the performance, the breach, being excused or released from the commitment), then the places the parallel breaks, and then tell me whether my brother's truth-only view or my friend's commitment view survives once the disanalogies are on the table. And whether the surviving rule is best stated in terms of truth, belief, or knowledge. If the promising analogy is carrying too much of the argument, I'd also like a better analogy for what you commit to when you assert something. Please answer in American English.
