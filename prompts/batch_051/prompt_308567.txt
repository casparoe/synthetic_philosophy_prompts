I keep noticing an asymmetry in how people talk about friendship, and I'm not sure it survives contact with Aristotle. If a person learns that their closest friend never actually cared about them—the warmth was all performance, kept up for years because the person was useful or entertaining—we'd say the relationship was fake and that they'd been wronged. But the same people will call a companion chatbot a "friend" (or a boyfriend/girlfriend) while knowing that the system has no feelings to reciprocate, no wish for their good, and no awareness of them between messages.

Aristotle, in the Nicomachean Ethics (W. D. Ross translation), writes: "to a friend we say we ought to wish what is good for his sake. But to those who thus wish good we ascribe only goodwill, if the wish is not reciprocated; goodwill when it is reciprocal being friendship. Or must we add 'when it is recognized'? For many people have goodwill to those whom they have not seen but judge to be good or useful; and one of these might return this feeling. These people seem to bear goodwill to each other; but how could one call them friends when they do not know their mutual feelings?"

That sounds as if it would put both cases in the same box—good will maybe, but not friendship. So what's going on? I want the strongest principle you can find that would let someone keep both ordinary judgments, tested against hard neighboring cases: a dog, a human friend who genuinely cares but is terrible at showing it, an AI that is actually conscious (if that changes anything), and a human pen pal who is only maintaining the correspondence for a visa. If no principle survives, say which judgment has to go and why. Treat this as a question about what "friendship" is doing in each judgment, not a policy question about whether chatbots should be regulated.
