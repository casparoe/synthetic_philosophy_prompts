A municipal government runs a workfare program: to receive income support, residents must show up and do tasks that serve no one on any ordinary accounting — repainting benches that are not worn, re-entering records that are already digitized, unpacking and repacking deliveries. The program could be replaced, at the same cost, by unconditional checks, but the government keeps it, saying that participants are doing real work for the money rather than receiving something for nothing and that the work preserves their dignity and self-respect. The same government is simultaneously automating its records office and eliminating the very kind of clerical tasks the program recreates by hand. What theory of the value of work — and of the relation between work, desert, and dignity — does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say whether a quite different theory, such as a purely incentive-based one, would license the same practice. Then explain what the program would have to look like if it were built instead on the view that work matters only through what it produces, and whether that alternative could justify any make-work at all.
