I'm a software engineer, and most of my philosophy has come from reading on my own rather than from formal study, so I want to check whether a parallel I've noticed is real or just pattern-matching.

The parallel is between transitivity in sorting APIs and transitivity in practical reasoning. In programming, a comparator for a sort is expected to impose a total order: for any x, y, z in the domain, if x compares less than y and y compares less than z, then x must compare less than z. When a comparator violates this — which is easy to do accidentally with fuzzy matches, partial orders, or subjective quality scores — the sort can return wrong results or throw an exception reporting that the comparison method violated its contract. Engineers treat this as a consistency requirement you fix, not one you negotiate with case by case.

In decision theory and ethics, the preference or betterness relation is also generally required to be transitive, and a standard rationale is that intransitive preferences can be money-pumped: if you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, someone can move you around the cycle while extracting payment from you at each step. Yet people's pairwise case judgments sometimes do cycle, and I gather philosophers disagree about whether that means the transitivity principle should be revised or the troublesome case intuitions should be discounted. That looks to me like the same shape as the programming situation: a global ordering constraint confronts local pairwise judgments that refuse to obey it.

The difference I can see is that a comparator is a contract the programmer chooses, whereas an agent's betterness relation is something we are presumably trying to get right about the agent or about value. My question: is this a genuine identity of structure, or only a loose metaphor? In particular, would the money-pump rationale transfer to sorting, and would the sort-failure rationale transfer to preference? What would have to be true of both sides for lessons to transfer, and what is one thing the parallel seems to predict that turns out false? Finally, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested position as the consensus.
