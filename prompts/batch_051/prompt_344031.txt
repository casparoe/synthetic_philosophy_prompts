I spent my career in atmospheric physics and only started reading philosophy seriously after I retired. Last year I got roped into helping a small town's flood-safety committee build a risk model for low-probability, high-stakes failures—levee breach, bridge failure, that sort of thing—where there's basically no historical data to go on. The argument we keep having is about the starting probabilities. One member says the only rational default is to spread probability as evenly as possible given the constraints we actually have. Another says a default is just a subjective choice and there's no principled answer. A third keeps pointing out that "as evenly as possible" isn't even well defined: spreading evenly over the annual failure rate gives a different answer than spreading evenly over the mean return period, which is just one over that same rate just described differently. I keep wanting to side with the first member—surely there's something rational about a maximally uninformative default—but I dont know how to answer the third. Is there a defensable way to pick such starting probabilities, or is the whole question misconceived?
