I've been going back and forth with a friend about something and I can't tell wich of us is being inconsistent. We both agree that if a city passes a zoning rule that keeps public buses — and therefore the mostly poor and Black people who ride them — from reaching a beach or a park, that rule is a political act and fair game for moral criticism; you'd call it discriminatory without hesitating. But when I brought up the (apparently real, or at least widely told) story about the planner Robert Moses having the overpasses on Long Island parkways built deliberatly too low for buses, so that the same people couldn't get to Jones Beach, my friend shrugged and said that's just infrastructure, a bridge has no politics, you can't blame a piece of concrete for who happens to fit under it. The two cases do the same thing — same effect, same intent — but one gets treated as a political decision and the other as a neutral artifact. I want to know whether anything actually justifies that asymmetry, or whether it's just the fact that one is written on paper and the other is poured in concrete. If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the version where both are chosen by the same official, in the same meeting, for the same stated reason, and the only difference is the material.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning. No preamble, no restating the question.
