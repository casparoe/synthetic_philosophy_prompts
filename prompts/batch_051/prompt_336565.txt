I keep finding myself caught between a compelling theory and a firm conviction, and I would like a careful adjudication rather than a simple choice of sides. The theory, associated with Wolfram's A New Kind of Science, is that nature's complexity does not require elaborate special-purpose mechanisms, because very simple iterative rules — such as those of cellular automata — can generate wildly intricate, apparently irreducible patterns, and that all systems whose behavior is not obviously simple are, in effect, computationally equivalent. I find this attractive because it is unifying and ontologically economical. But I also hold a firm conviction that a scientific explanation must do more than exhibit a simple rule that produces a pattern reminiscent of, say, turbulence or a mollusk shell: it must identify the mechanism at work in the particular system, be constrained by that system's measurable parameters, and make quantitative predictions that could have failed. Judged by that standard, the cellular-automaton program seems to substitute resemblance and classification for explanation. Which should yield — my attraction to the principle of computational equivalence, or my conviction about what explanation requires? Is there a reading on which they are compatible, and what precisely would justify debunking the intuition as a bias, amending or abandoning the theory, or reconciling the two?
