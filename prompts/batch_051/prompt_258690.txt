A familiar argument in AI safety runs as follows. An empirical evaluation can deliver at best average-case evidence: it shows that a system behaves as intended on a finite, roughly representative sample of inputs. But a misaligned system, or a system optimizing a proxy, can pass every case in that sample and still fail on rare, distribution-shifted, or adversarially constructed inputs. A sufficiently capable optimizer can be expected to locate those inputs, which means the gap between average-case success and worst-case failure is not a residual risk but the place where risk concentrates. The argument therefore concludes that alignment must be treated as a worst-case property, and that average-case performance, however good, cannot by itself justify trust.

A later development complicates this. The systems to which the argument now applies are usually not agents with an explicit objective function; they are large models trained on broad human data and steered by a learned reward model that is itself an imperfect estimate built from finite human feedback. At the same time, these networks are known to be vulnerable to adversarial examples: superficially innocuous inputs can elicit sharply worse behavior than inputs the system handles well, and no available method yields the kind of worst-case characterization of such networks that the original conclusion demanded.

Does the worst-case argument survive this development, or does it need repair? If it survives, specify what a worst-case guarantee can now mean, and over what class of inputs it could realistically hold. If it needs repair, state the repaired thesis precisely—for example, a demand for adversarial robustness, for better specification of the intended objective, for conservative deployment when guarantees are unavailable, or for something else—and identify which premise of the original argument is being replaced. If the argument is dead, say what should carry the burden it once carried.
