I have been turning over an argument I keep having about old software, and I want to submit the analogy at its center to a careful audit rather than just asking who is right. There is a small utility program I have used since the late 2000s. The company that published it dissolved years ago: the website is gone, the support address bounces, and nobody sells, updates, or enforces anything about it. A friend recently argued that using and sharing this program is morally like moving into an abandoned house. The owner cannot be found, nobody is present to suffer a loss, and the only remaining objection would be to the breaking of an abstract rule rather than to any harm done to a real person. I find the analogy persuasive for the house case, but something begins to feel wrong when I apply it to software, because copying a file does not exclude anyone else from it in the way that occupying a house would.

What I would like is for you to work out what actually maps onto what. Is the house the installed program, the source code, the license, or the publisher's right to control distribution? Where does the mapping break down? I can already see some candidate trouble spots, though I am not sure how much weight to give each: the difference between taking a scarce physical thing and reproducing a non-rival pattern; the difference between a dead person whose heirs could in principle be found through probate and a dissolved company whose successors are murky; and the possibility that distributing an abandoned program forecloses a future that a still-existing rightsholder might have wanted. Does the conclusion my friend wants to draw, namely that there is little or nothing seriously wrong with using and sharing such software, survive once these disanalogies are made explicit? If the abandoned-house analogy cannot carry that weight, is there a better analogy for what is actually going on with orphaned software?
