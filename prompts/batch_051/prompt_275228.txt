Consider the following three-part account of lying:

A speaker S lies to an audience A just in case: (1) S asserts to A some proposition p; (2) S believes that p is false; and (3) S intends A to believe that p.

Now consider this case. A used-car salesperson knows that the car she is selling has a failing transmission. The prospective buyer, suspecting evasiveness, asks: "Is there anything mechanically wrong with this car that you haven't told me about?" The salesperson replies: "The engine was rebuilt two years ago, the tires are new, and the car passed its emissions inspection last month." Each statement in the reply is true, and she knows each is true. She gives this reply precisely because she expects the buyer to treat it as a complete answer, to infer that there are no further mechanical problems, and she wants him to form that false belief.

Under the stated account, does the salesperson lie to the buyer? Much hinges on whether her reply, in the context of the buyer's yes/no question, counts as asserting the proposition that there are no further mechanical problems, as opposed to merely conversationally implying it. Work through the three conditions under each interpretation and say which interpretation you take to be correct, and why. Then address the further question: does this case show that the account needs to be revised, for example by adding a condition about the false belief the speaker intends the audience to reach by inference, or by replacing the assertion condition with a condition about the total communicative act performed? If a revision is needed, state the revised account and show that it still classifies a plain, knowingly false assertion intended to be believed as a lie; if no revision is needed, explain what distinguishes this case from a lie in a way that preserves the account's adequacy.
