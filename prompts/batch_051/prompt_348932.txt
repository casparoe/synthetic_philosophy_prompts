I teach a graduate methods workshop for quantitatively trained researchers in psychology and biomedicine, and I want a first-day sorting instrument called “Which philosophy of statistical evidence do you actually hold?” The instrument should have seven or eight questions, each built around a concrete post-replication-crisis methodological dilemma: collecting data until p < .05; two analysts reaching opposite conclusions from the same trial because they used different priors; a preregistered analysis that still yields an implausible effect; a failed replication with a smaller and noisier effect; a table of forty subgroup tests with two p < .05. Each question should give two or three answer options phrased as what the respondent would do or conclude, never as which school they like, and the questions must not name the schools. The scoring key should sort respondents into these leaves: Fisherian significance testing, Neyman–Pearson error control, subjective Bayesianism, default/objective Bayesianism, likelihoodism (only likelihood ratios carry evidential weight), an error-statistical severity view (evidence is how severely a claim passed a stringent test), and a preregistration-first reformer who is deliberately agnostic among formal schools. Build the tree from distinctions that actually separate these positions: whether stopping rules and researcher intentions change what the data say, whether probabilities attach to hypotheses or only to procedures, whether a small p-value is a post-data measure of evidence or a property of the procedure’s error rates, and how a failed replication should be handled. Include a map of the logical space marking which answer combinations no real position occupies, with a short note for each empty cell saying whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored, and flag any two positions your questions cannot cleanly separate. Deliver the instrument itself, not an essay about the positions.
