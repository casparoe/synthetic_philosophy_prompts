I'm a PhD student in theology and I keep going back and forth on whether my own tradition is coherent here. The criteria I was taught for sola scriptura are roughly: (1) Scripture is the only infallible rule of faith and practice; (2) Scripture is perspicuous, meaning clear enough on what's necessary for salvation that ordinary Christians don't need an infallible interpreter; (3) Scripture interprets Scripture, so unclear passages get settled by clearer ones; (4) no human authority (councils, popes, confessions) may bind conscience except insofar as it's derived from Scripture.

Now take a confessional Lutheran church body that requires unconditional (quia) subscription to the Book of Concord. Its pastors have to teach things like the real presence or baptismal regeneration in the confessional sense, and in practice a pastor who says the confessions get some exegesis wrong faces discipline. The official line is that the confessions add no authority to Scripture: they bind because they faithfully restate Scripture, and if the confessions ever contradict Scripture, Scripture wins.

Is that still sola scriptura, or has the confession become a de facto second authority that gets to resolve the regress of interpretation? Go criterion by criterion and give me a committed verdict: does it pass, does it fail, and if it fails, what's the smallest change that would make it genuinely sola scriptura? I especially want your take on whether the insofar-as-it-agrees-with-Scripture clause is a real safeguard or just a way to keep saying sola while the confession does the actual deciding. American English, please.
