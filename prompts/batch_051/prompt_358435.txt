I am a graduate teaching assistant preparing to lead a fifty-minute discussion section for first-year undergraduates who have had no previous course in philosophy of mind. The assigned reading for the week is Andy Clark and David Chalmers, "The Extended Mind" (Analysis 58, 1998). I want the entire session to turn on one paragraph of the paper, which the students will have in front of them:

In these cases, the human organism is linked with an external entity in a two-way interaction, creating a coupled system that can be seen as a cognitive system in its own right. All the components in the system play an active causal role, and they jointly govern behavior in the same sort of way that cognition usually does. If we remove the external component the system's behavioral competence will drop, just as it would if we removed part of its brain. Our thesis is that this sort of coupled process counts equally well as a cognitive process, whether or not it is wholly in the head.

I am not after a summary or an exposition of the view. I need a teaching plan for making this paragraph land as a conclusion rather than as a slogan. Please specify: the order in which the background ideas must be introduced and what depends on what; the single example or case that should carry the hour, and what it must have and lack (I keep wavering between the Tetris rotation case and Otto's notebook); the misconceptions these students will bring in or form—I already anticipate "the mind is just consciousness," "the thesis says the notebook is literally thinking," and "this is just Putnam-style semantic externalism"—and the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off; one question I could ask at the end to check whether they have grasped the coupled-system claim rather than merely learned to repeat it; and what I should openly set aside as beyond the scope of this session. Please write your answer in British English.
