I'm a PhD candidate in political science working on formal models of collective judgment, and I also serve as the methods advisor to a foundation that funds policy research. Three or four times a year, the board must decide which proposals clear a scientific-merit bar, and it currently treats a simple majority of external reviewers as decisive; each round commits several million dollars, so the error costs are real. I would like to replace this default with a reusable, written decision procedure built from the Condorcet jury theorem. Design a procedure my staff could run in order: before the vote, after the vote, and when interpreting what a margin between "yes" and "no" actually means. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with "do not aggregate" or "escalate to the board," and where it returns a vote-based recommendation rather than an irreducible judgment call. The procedure should directly address the theorem's conditions — two alternatives, individual competence better than chance, independence of votes, and sincere voting — and should be explicit about which of those conditions can be checked cheaply and which cannot. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) seven reviewers with individually calibrated track records around 0.6 accuracy, using fully separate reading lists; (2) nine reviewers who all rely on the same two unpublished preprints and one shared referee report; and (3) eleven reviewers with a documented conflict-of-interest bias that places their individual accuracy near 0.45 on comparable past decisions, who vote 9–2 in favor. Ensure that at least one case produces an answer the board will not like, and end by stating what the procedure cannot do.
