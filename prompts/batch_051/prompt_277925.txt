A bereavement-support clinic has adopted a protocol for its counselors working with newly bereaved clients. The first step is labeled "the grief-face." The counselor describes what Charles Darwin called the oblique eyebrows of grief—the inner ends of the eyebrows drawn upward by contraction of the muscles he discusses in chapter seven of *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals*. The counselor's paraphrase of Darwin's claim is this: the adult face of grief is a subdued, partly inhibited version of the infant's crying face, involving the same muscles, but with their action partly checked. The protocol then has the counselor invite the client to notice, and for two minutes deliberately maintain, that facial posture. The clinic's internal guidance says the aim is "not to intensify pain, but to treat the grief-face as the body's own statement of a loss that must be acknowledged rather than bypassed."

What view of the value of negative emotions does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as fully as you can, separating what the protocol strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Is the practice coherent if grief is judged to have no overall value—that is, if sadness is a cost with no benefit? And what would have to change in the protocol if the clinic instead held that expressions of grief are neurophysiological noise, meaningful only as signs of damage to be repaired?
