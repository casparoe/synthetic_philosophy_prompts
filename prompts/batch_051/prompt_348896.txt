I have been working out a distinction I call greenhouse morality and trellis morality, and I want to know whether it is a good one. The idea is that Mencius thinks human goodness is like a seedling: it already contains the pattern of mature virtue inside itself, but it needs the right soil, water, and protection from axes and livestock. On this view the environment causes growth or prevents it, but it does not redesign the plant. Xunzi, by contrast, thinks human nature is more like a vine that will not grow into a useful shape on its own: it needs an external trellis, such as ritual, law, and teachers, that gives it a form its raw tendencies would never produce by themselves. So a greenhouse account says moral education is mostly about removing obstacles and supplying conditions, while a trellis account says moral education is mostly about imposing a structure that nature does not already contain. I know there are standard ways of contrasting Mencius and Xunzi in terms of innate sprouts versus acquired artifice, but I think my distinction is slightly different because it is about the causal relation between natural endowment and moral character. For Mencius the environment is a precondition of a tendency that is already directed toward virtue; for Xunzi the environment is a cause in a stronger sense, since it supplies the very direction. Is this a real distinction or just a metaphor dressed up from the familiar contrast? Has someone already worked it out under another name? And where does it break: does it make Mencius sound too passive about reflection and learning, or make Xunzi sound as though raw human material is too formless, when he grants that people have the capacity to understand and practice ritual? Give me the strongest objection and tell me whether the distinction survives it.
