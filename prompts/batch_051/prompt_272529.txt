I am a novelist, and I am developing a scene in which two characters — a logician and a philosophically adept skeptic — argue about whether deductive inference can be justified. I need the argument itself worked out cleanly before I clothe it in prose, so the dialogue you write should be the argument, not a description of it.

The scene should turn on Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles," without reproducing Carroll's text. The logician's view is that a rule of inference such as modus ponens is not another premise: anyone who accepts "If P, then Q" and "P" is already rationally committed to "Q," and the demand to justify the rule either simply uses that very rule in the act of justifying it or mistakes the rule for a factual claim in need of evidence. The skeptic's view is that Carroll's Tortoise exposes the gap that opens as soon as the step from premises to conclusion is made explicit as something one accepts; every proposed rule then demands a further rule, so deductive transition cannot be given a non-circular justification but is at most a practice we are trained into or a commitment we simply take on.

Write the dialogue in exactly five numbered exchanges. Let each numbered exchange consist of one speech from each character, each speech a sentence or two, with each character responding to the point just made rather than restating a position. Neither character should be a strawman; each should be forced to concede something under pressure from the other. The fifth exchange should leave the disagreement sharper, not resolve it. Include no introduction or conclusion, and keep stage directions to a word or two at most.
