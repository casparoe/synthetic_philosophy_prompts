A friend and I got into it last night over whether you can still love an artist's work once you know the artist did something genuinly awful — the usual "separate the art from the artist" fight, which I always think I've settled until I'm actually in it. At one point he was defending a painter he likes who by all accounts was cruel to everyone around him. His line was that the cruelty just isn't in the paintings: they're quiet domestic scenes, they never name anyone, the warmth on the canvas does what it does, so you judge the canvases and not the man. Fine. But twenty minutes later he's on a filmmaker he loves who did something comparably bad, and suddenly the biography is essential to the experience — knowing what the director did changes the films, makes the running theme of performance and lying read as a dark self-portrait, and that makes them better, richer, braver. So the same kind of biographical fact gets let in when it raises the work, and locked out as irrelevant when it would lower it.

I've started noticing this everywhere in arguments about bad artists — critics do it too, not just my friend. Is there an established name for that specific asymmetry, where an artist's biography is treated as admissible evidence about the work when it flatters the work and as inadmissible when it doesn't? I don't just want "confirmation bias" or "motivated reasoning"; that's the generic gas in the engine, and I'm after the particular maneuver as it shows up in art-and-morality debates. If there's no settled name, say so and give it one, with a definition and boundary cases. What I actually want is the carving: when is importing biography legitimate (a confessional novel? a work that explicitly depicts events from the life?) and when is it this move? And where exactly is the line between "context" and smuggling? Also tell me where your own analysis would most easily break — what kind of case or argument would make you change your mind, or at least admit the distinction is doing less work than it looks like.
