I have been trying to pin down a pattern I keep noticing in discussions of self-knowledge, and I would like to know whether it has an established name, and if not, what the nearest named concepts are and how the thing I am describing differs from them. The pattern is this. Sometimes self-examination seems to damage the very thing it examines. If someone about to choose between two options they know well is first asked to list reasons for and against each option, they often become more fluent and more confident in their stated reasons, and yet the choice they reach is one they later regret and reverse, while people who chose without first listing reasons remain more satisfied with what they picked. Similarly, when someone in a low mood repeatedly asks themselves why they feel as they do, the explanations they generate feel clarifying, but the mood tends to last longer, whereas asking themselves to describe the situation concretely, in sequence, does not have this effect. I can see that this is somewhere near rumination, but rumination, as I usually see it used, names a style of repetitive negative thought rather than the more general failure mode in which self-examination operates at the wrong level and worsens what it sets out to understand. I am not asking for a bare label; I want the boundaries drawn. Does this family of cases have a name of its own, and how exactly does it relate to rumination and to the kind of self-examination people usually count as healthy reflection? If nothing established fits, what should it be called, and what are its clearest boundary cases? Please answer in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as you would in an email.
