I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I recently read a widely recommended book on the moral legacy of World War II. Its chapter on Karl Jaspers included a summary of The Question of German Guilt, which I reconstruct below from my notes:

After 1945, Jaspers distinguished four kinds of guilt. Criminal guilt attaches to individual persons who have broken clear laws, and only a court may determine it; in that sense the Nuremberg trials tried individuals, not a people. Political guilt falls collectively on the citizens of a state for the acts of that state; it is a shared liability for what one's government does, with consequences such as reparations or loss of sovereignty imposed in practice by the victors. Moral guilt is what an individual incurs before his or her own conscience for what he or she did or failed to do, and it cannot be judged from the outside. Metaphysical guilt rests on the solidarity of all human beings: a person shares responsibility for wrongs done in his or her presence or with his or her knowledge when he or she did not do what could be done to prevent them, and this guilt is a matter before God alone. The author added that Jaspers called political guilt the nation's moral guilt, and that Jaspers's overall conclusion was that this collective guilt rightly attaches to later generations, not only to those who lived through the war.

I suspect this is unfair to Jaspers — the last two sentences in particular seem to collapse distinctions the whole fourfold scheme exists to keep apart — but I don't have the background to say exactly where or why. Please give me a committed accuracy audit: take a definite position on whether the summary is fair overall, identify which parts are accurate and which quietly distort Jaspers, and say whether the author's closing claim about later generations follows from the accurate parts. Where the summary goes wrong, explain what Jaspers's distinctions actually license and what they explicitly rule out.
