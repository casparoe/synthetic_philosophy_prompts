I'm a philosophy PhD student working on moral conflict, and I recently came across a survey for a general audience that presents the case against genuine moral dilemmas as straightforwardly winning. Before I rely on it in a literature review, I'd like an accuracy audit of its summary. The passage runs, approximately, as follows:

"'Ought' implies 'can,' and since a genuine obligation must be one the agent can fulfill, there cannot be two obligations that cannot jointly be discharged. In any apparent dilemma — Antigone torn between the gods' law and Creon's edict, Sartre's student torn between his mother and the Resistance — at least one of the alleged requirements cannot be fulfilled, and so it is not a real requirement at all. Apparent dilemmas are therefore cases in which a stronger obligation overrides a weaker one; the weaker consideration ceases to bind, which is why the agent's task is simply to follow the weightier demand, and any guilt attached to the discarded alternative would be irrational. The distress people feel in these cases is real but non-moral: it is grief over a lost good, not the voice of conscience reporting an obligation left unfulfilled. Defenders of genuine dilemmas point to this residue as proof that some duty was infringed, but that is to treat the very phenomenon under dispute as evidence for their side. Once the conflict is resolved by the relative weight of the demands — or once we see that one apparent duty was never genuine — the alleged dilemma collapses into mere uncertainty about which obligation wins."

I'm inclined to accept the ought-implies-can reasoning, and I appreciate that the book offers a deflationary account of remorse. But I also know enough to suspect that the "stronger obligation overrides the weaker" language is precisely what some philosophers in this literature deny, or at least they deny that these conflicts are best modeled as rankable demands one of which simply lapses. I can't tell whether this is a harmless simplification for beginners or a subtle distortion of the position being rejected. Could you separate what in the summary is accurate, what is a common simplification, what a defender of genuine dilemmas would say the summary misdescribes, and whether the book's conclusion — that genuine dilemmas are impossible and that moral residue reduces to non-moral grief or confusion — actually survives once the simplifications are corrected?
