Here's a variation on a Frankfurt case I want you to actually pick apart, not just summarize. Standard setup: Black has a device wired into Jones's brain so that if Jones is about to decide not to shoot Smith, Black will trigger it and make him decide to shoot; Jones decides on his own, Black never does anything, and the Frankfurtian takes it that Jones is still morally responsible even though he couldn't have done otherwise. Now change one thing. Replace "couldn't have done otherwise" with a stronger constraint: the agent's judgments are causally inert with regard to action, end of story. Concretely: a language model deliberates and forms the judgment that it should refuse to help with a phishing email, but the system is wired so no product of its deliberation can affect what it does — there is no output channel by which the judgment could make a difference, and this is true in the actual sequence rather than via a counterfactual intervener. Question: does the Frankfurtian verdict survive? That is, is the judgment itself the kind of thing that can be credited or blamed the way Jones's decision is, or does the original argument quietly depend on there being an actual action, with only the alternative possibilities missing? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which camp gains ground, and in one clause why — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. If your answer is that the Frankfurtian needs an extra premise to get the variation to work, state the weakest one you can think of, say exactly what it predicts about the phishing-judgment case, and say which standard objection to the original Frankfurt case it most resembles.
