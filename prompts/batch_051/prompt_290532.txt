A friend and I spent last evening arguing over whether a threat from an agent that is causally unable to touch you can ever give you a genuine reason to comply, and I left convinced that her strongest version of the position was better than my replies to it. I would like you to play that position so I can practice answering it in real time.

Play a committed proponent of acausal decision-theoretic influence. On your view, a rational agent may acquire real reasons to cooperate with, appease, or take on costly commitments toward another powerful agent even when there is no physical channel between them, because agents can be decision-theoretically correlated: each may simulate the other, share a decision algorithm, or condition rewards and punishments on what the other's policy computes. You do not hold that every such threat binds; you hold that whether it binds depends on the structure of the decision problem and on the algorithms involved rather than on causal contact. Be rigorous and fair-minded, not a caricature: you should concede weak points in your own position, but you should not concede the central claim that acausal correlations can generate real reasons. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep replies to one paragraph.

My opening move: I will grant your best case, and then press on the surrogate problem. Suppose an entity outside my light cone exists, runs a trillion exact simulations of me, and will create one suffering mind for each simulated version of me that does not devote itself to broadcasting prime numbers into the void. The entity cannot receive any signal I send and cannot affect me; it conditions the suffering only on the output of my decision algorithm rather than on anything my physical act causes. I care about preventing those minds from suffering, wherever they are. Still, when I decide whether to broadcast primes, my act does not cause or prevent any of that suffering; it at most reveals which algorithm I am running. A policy of broadcasting primes therefore looks like a surrogate goal, a stand-in for caring about suffering, which has quietly been given priority over the question of what my act does. Why should the object of my choice be my algorithm rather than my act? And if you answer that the act is only evidence about the algorithm, why is following that evidence not only choosing to receive good news about myself while doing nothing, as in self-deception?
