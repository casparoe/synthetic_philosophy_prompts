I am taking a course this term on the philosophy of medicine and law, and we have spent several weeks on advance directives and the authority of the earlier, competent self over the later, incompetent self. I would like help analyzing how a particular clause in a living will applies to a case the person who wrote it almost certainly did not foresee.

The clause reads:

"Clause 3(b): If I suffer from a severe and irreversible dementing illness such that I can no longer recognize my spouse or any of my children, I direct that no medical treatment be administered to prolong my life, including, but not limited to, antibiotics, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, mechanical ventilation, and artificially administered nutrition and hydration. I am to receive only those measures intended to keep me comfortable and free from pain. I make this direction while I have full capacity, and I intend it to bind my later self even if, at the time it is invoked, I ask for treatment or say that I wish to live."

The patient signed this clause fifteen years ago, after her mother's long decline with what was diagnosed as Alzheimer's disease. She is now in the condition the clause describes: she no longer recognizes her spouse or her three adult children, and she cannot give informed consent. She has also developed a severe bloodstream infection that will be fatal within days if untreated. On a literal reading of the clause, the team would withhold antibiotics and provide only comfort care.

But a recent finding has complicated that reading. Her cognitive decline is not being caused by Alzheimer's disease after all. She has a slow autoimmune encephalitis that, until recently, was not something physicians could distinguish from advanced Alzheimer's in a living patient, and that may be substantially reversible with a newly available immunotherapy. The treatment would take months, would be physically burdensome, and might fail, but it could restore her ability to recognize her family. The patient, when she is awake, sometimes grips her daughter's hand and says things like "I don't want to go yet," though she cannot recognize the person she is speaking to.

I would like you to work through what clause 3(b) requires or permits here: does it authorize withholding the antibiotics, and does it also require withholding the immunotherapy, given that "medical treatment" is framed in terms of prolonging life? Please run the case through four interpretive approaches—the ordinary meaning of the words, the purpose of the clause, what the woman who signed it would most plausibly have said if asked about this exact situation, and what a reasonable person reading the clause would understand it to cover. Where these approaches diverge, say which you think should govern and why. I am especially interested in whether the earlier self's authority should extend to a situation in which the factual assumption behind the refusal—that the unrecognizing state was permanent—has turned out to be false.
