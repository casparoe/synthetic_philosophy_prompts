Take the classic geometric picture of meaning that emerged from static word embeddings: a word's meaning is its position in a high-dimensional vector space; semantic similarity is proximity in that space; and semantic relations correspond to linear operations, the standard illustration being king − man + woman ≈ queen. The picture was advanced as a substantive claim about semantic structure, not merely a representational convenience: co-occurrence and use patterns were thought to encode meaning, and the resulting geometry was treated as interpretable. Now consider the shift to contextual embeddings such as those produced by transformer language models. There, a word like bank receives a different vector depending on whether it occurs in a sentence about a river or about a financial institution, and the representation is trained to predict text in context rather than to summarize a word's standing meaning. Does the geometric argument survive this development? Be precise about which premise bears the pressure: the premise that lexical meanings are stable and context-independent, the premise that co-occurrence statistics capture meaning, or the premise that semantic relations are linear and additive. Then say whether the claim can be repaired — for example by identifying a word's meaning with a region, a trajectory, or a function over contexts rather than a single point. Or does contextuality show that the earlier geometry was an artifact of the static embedding method rather than a discovery about meaning itself? I want an analysis of the argument's fate, not a survey of embedding architectures.
