I have been trying to convert a set of gut judgments about when it is rational to avoid information into a single principle, and I want the principle tested and repaired rather than just named. The backdrop is Good's theorem, which as I understand it says the expected value of cost-free information is never negative for a consistent expected-utility maximizer: at worst you ignore the signal, and sometimes it lets you choose a better act. On that result, a rational agent should never pay to avoid information that would otherwise arrive for free. But my verdicts on cases keep coming apart from the theorem, and I would like to know which side has to give.

Here are the verdicts.

1. If someone could tell me, with certainty, the date and manner of my death, and I knew the knowledge could not change any decision I would ever make, I would pay a substantial amount not to be told.

2. If a test revealed a genetic risk that would clearly change which screenings I should receive, I would not pay to avoid the result, and I would consider paying to get it sooner.

3. I would pay a small amount not to overhear the ending of a film I will watch tonight, even though I will watch it either way.

4. If I could learn with certainty that a long-term partner had been unfaithful years before we met, and I knew the fact could not change anything I would do going forward, I would still want to know, even though I also know it would make me substantially less happy for a long time.

5. I would rather not be told that a former colleague secretly found me contemptible, when I will never interact with them again and the information cannot affect anything I will do.

6. I would pay a small amount to learn a completely nonactionable fact that I am merely curious about, so my principle has to allow that some knowledge states are worth money simply because knowing them feels good.

The obvious first-pass principle is that the value of information has two components: an instrumental component, which Good's theorem captures, and a direct hedonic component carried by the mental state of knowing. The direct component can be positive, as in case 6, or negative, as in cases 1, 3, and 5; information is worth paying to avoid exactly when the negative direct component exceeds the instrumental component. But that principle seems to force me to reject case 4, because both components look negative there. It leaves no room for wanting to know a painful truth as such. Maybe case 4 should go; maybe there is a third kind of value that the principle is missing. Notice the awkward pair: case 1 and case 4 are alike in that no decision hinges on either, and knowing would hurt in both, yet I want to avoid the first and seek the second; I cannot tell whether that distinction is defensible or a rationalization.

What I would like is the best one or two precise principles that can account for these verdicts, with each clause motivated by a case, plus an explicit statement of which of my six verdicts each principle cannot accommodate. Please also apply the candidate principle or principles to two further cases: a free offer of a complete transcript of everything my friends currently say about me when I am not in the room, guaranteed never to affect any decision; and a free genetic test for an untreatable condition whose result would make no medical difference to me but would predict years of misery with moderate probability.
