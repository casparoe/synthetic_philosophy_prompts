Last night two people in my book club got into it about grammar and I still dont know who was right.

Rosa was venting about an email from her manager, full of "between you and I" and "please forward it to myself." She said you'd hope a VP would write better than that, it's just bad grammar.

Todd pushed back. As far as he's concerned "bad grammar" doesn't exist. English has no academy and no official rulebook; grammar just is whatever native speakers systematically do. The things she listed are used by millions of native speakers in certain registers, they're rule-governed, and calling them bad is a status judgment dressed up as fact.

Rosa said hang on, "between you and I" isn't even a dialect pattern like "might could" or "fixin to" — people mostly say it when they're trying to sound formal, so it's a hypercorrection, someone applying a half-learned rule. And the southern constructions she's honestly fine with in speech. The point is that in a company-wide email there are actual conventions, and violating them has real costs. If grammar is just whatever native speakers do, then "ain't" is fine in a cover letter, which is obviously bad advice.

Todd said she was smuggling in register. Avoiding "ain't" in a cover letter isn't grammar, it's pragmatics, or prestige enforcement: readers will judge you. The linguist's job is to describe the system a speaker actually acquired, and by that standard no native speaker is ungrammatical in their own variety. "Ain't" is grammatical in the variety where "ain't" is grammatical, and "between you and I" is grammatical for speakers who've internalized it in formal contexts, even if it started as hypercorrection. Once it's systematic, it's grammar.

Rosa said he'd just defined grammar so her claim comes out trivially false, and left no word for the actual thing she was invoking: the explicit norms of a certain register. Call it etiquette if he wants, but the content of that etiquette is still pronoun case and verb inflection, not fork placement. When the VP writes "between you and I" there's a norm he's violating, and it's a norm about grammar. If descriptivism can't say that sentence is worse in that context than "between you and me," then it's given up on a distinction everyone else needs.

Todd came back that he can say it's worse for that context; he just wouldn't call it ungrammatical. And the reason that distinction matters is that the prestige register is stuffed with fake rules — don't split an infinitive, don't end a sentence with a preposition — that got there because people wanted English to work like Latin. So a lot of what gets called "bad grammar" is a stylistic in-joke that got encoded as moralizing.

Then Rosa conceded the Latin point about split infinitives and stranded prepositions, but said "between you and I" isn't one of those: the written register has genuinely used "between you and me" for centuries, and it takes a grammar of that register to tell the real conventions from the Latin imports. Call it "standard written English" if we must, but that's still grammar.
