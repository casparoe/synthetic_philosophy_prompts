I am settling my late aunt's estate, and I need to know how to handle one item under her will. My aunt collected paintings by the early twentieth-century painter Helena March, and her will contains the following clause: "I give to the Lindenmarsh Municipal Gallery every original oil painting by Helena March in my collection at the date of my death, to be kept together and displayed as the Helena March Bequest." For as long as anyone in the family can remember, the collection included a small canvas known as Low Tide at Lindenmarsh, insured and displayed as an early March and bearing what appears to be her signature. Shortly after my aunt's death, an examination of the pigments and of old dealers' records concluded that the canvas is almost certainly a skillful forgery in March's manner, painted by another hand in the 1930s. The will was written many years before this possibility came to light, and my aunt always believed the canvas to be a genuine March. The canvas is nonetheless a unique, hand-painted oil painting; it is not a print or a photographic reproduction. I had always taken the word "original" in the clause to mean simply "not a copy or print," but this discovery has made me wonder whether it also carries a requirement of authenticity. Must I deliver Low Tide at Lindenmarsh to the gallery under the quoted clause? I would like you to work through what the words of the clause, the purpose of the bequest, what my aunt would have said if asked after learning of the new attribution, and what a reasonable reader of the will would take the clause to require each suggest, and then say which of these approaches should control here, and why.
