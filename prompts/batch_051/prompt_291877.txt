I’m working on a novel where a character—a retired psychologist, sort of a warm cynic—keeps telling his daughter that the more she asks whether she’s happy, the less she’ll be. A friend in my writing group read the chapter and said, Oh, so you’re doing the paradox of hedonism. She pulled out her phone and showed me a paragraph from some essay she’d saved. It said something like: “Mill held that happiness is the only thing ultimately worth wanting, but he realized that you can’t get it by wanting it; happiness only comes as a by-product of pursuing other things for their own sake. Therefore a sensible hedonist should never ask whether he’s happy, but should simply lose himself in work, love, or study, and let the feeling take care of itself.” Is this a fair account? I don’t want my character to be the mouthpiece for a misreading. Where does this gloss get the paradox right, where does it shade into nonsense, and does the conclusion actually follow? I’m happy to keep it loose for fiction, but if the logic collapses under pressure I need to know.
