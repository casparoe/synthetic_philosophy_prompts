I am a high school student in an after-school political theory club. We have been working through Isaiah Berlin's distinction between negative and positive liberty, and then a guest speaker introduced the idea that liberty might instead mean non-domination: not being subject to someone else's arbitrary power even if they do not actually interfere with you. Next month each member has to present something creative that captures what we have learned, and I want to write a villanelle. The form appeals to me because the refrains keep returning the way these two older ideas keep returning in my head, but I want the poem to do more than repeat slogans.

Could you write a villanelle in American English, nineteen lines, in which the two refrains are competing voices: one voice insisting that liberty is freedom from interference, the other insisting that liberty is self-mastery or acting from one's true, higher self. The first five tercets should let those two voices circle each other through everyday examples where they disagree, such as whether requiring teenagers to stay in school until a certain age is a restriction or a way of helping them become self-governing. Then in the final quatrain the volta should bring in a third idea: that freedom is not only about how often you are interfered with, but about whether you live subject to another's arbitrary power, and that republican self-government is a shared institutional achievement rather than an inner battle. The two refrains should finally meet in the closing couplet in a way that shows why this third idea refuses to treat the first two as exhaustive. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who has read Berlin and the republican critics would nod, not wince.
