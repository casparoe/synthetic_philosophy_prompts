I'm a PhD student working on scalable oversight, and I keep coming back to the following reconstruction of an argument that process-based supervision is informationally redundant. It seems too quick to me, but I can't pin down why. Consider tasks with a single, verifiable final answer.

(1) For such tasks, the only property of a solution we ultimately care about is that the final answer is correct.
(2) Accordingly, an intermediate step has exactly one job: it must not close off the possibility of reaching a correct final answer; a step that is compatible with a correct completion has done nothing wrong, and a step that rules out every correct completion has done everything wrong.
(3) But "this step is compatible with a correct completion" and "this step rules out every correct completion" are properties defined entirely in terms of the final answer.
(4) Properties defined entirely in terms of the final answer are the very thing outcome-based supervision gives feedback on, and with enough data an outcome-based critic could learn to score steps by those properties.
(5) Therefore process-based supervision tracks no property of intermediate steps that outcome-based supervision could not in principle reconstruct.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I keep suspecting a slide in step (2): it seems to treat "the step is locally correct" as interchangeable with "the step does not prevent eventual success," but a step can be locally wrong in a chain that later recovers, and locally right in a chain that ultimately fails. Yet maybe (2) is simply stipulating what matters. Is the argument valid? If not, give the number of the first step that fails and restate that step in the corrected form it would need.
