Im not a biologist, just someone who went down a rabbit hole about whether "species" is a real thing in nature or just a box we put organisms in, and I want someone to nail down what a good definition would even have to do. Every definition I find seems to break somewhere: some animals can interbreed and produce fertile offspring (wolves and coyotes?) but no one calls them one species, so "can interbreed" cant be the whole story; some things that look almost identical don't interbreed; bacteria don't do interbreeding at all; and fossils can't be tested either way. So write me the full list of demands a species concept has to meet before I'd accept it — the cases it has to say something sensible about (asexual organisms, ring species, hybrids, extinct forms, whatever else comes up), the distinctions it has to respect (like the difference between being a real natural kind and being a useful filing system), and which demands are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of particular camps (like that species have to have sharp boundaries or a shared essence). Also tell me which single requirement does the most work excluding otherwise tempting definitions. If some of the demands can't all be met at once, say where the list itself forces a tradeoff — that's the part I actually want to see, not a scorecard of existing definitions, I'll do that myself. And please keep straight what's basically settled from what's genuinely contested among people who actually study this, and don't dress a contested view up as the consensus.
