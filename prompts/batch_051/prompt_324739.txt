I have been trying to figure out what kind of panpsychism, if any, my own moral intuitions actually support, and I keep returning to a passage from W. K. Clifford, "On the Nature of Things-in-Themselves" (1878):

"The universe, then, consists entirely of mind-stuff. A moving molecule of inorganic matter does not possess mind or consciousness; but it possesses a small piece of mind-stuff."

Here are my unvarnished verdicts on cases. A single electron: no consciousness, and no direct moral considerability. A rock: none. A thermostat with a feedback loop: none. A single neuron: not conscious, though I hesitate because it is living; but I do not think it has any inner pain. A human being in deep dreamless sleep: not currently conscious, but fully morally considerable. A dog in pain: straightforwardly morally considerable. A physical system with the same causal organization as a human brain but built out of silicon chips: I would treat it as conscious and morally considerable if it behaves and reports as I do, but I am unsure. A vast network of people passing marks around by hand to implement the same pattern as a brain, with no single person aware of the whole pattern: I do not think it is conscious. A gas cloud with as many particles as a brain and similarly complex interactions but no stable boundary or memory: probably not conscious. A single water molecule: no. A single integrated microchip: no.

I am not asking for a survey of panpsychism. I want to know what principle about the relationship between physical organization and consciousness best captures these verdicts, and where my verdicts conflict. In particular, does the principle that captures my verdicts allow me to keep Clifford's claim that a molecule of inorganic matter has mind-stuff but no consciousness, or does that sentence commit me to something I would reject? What does your preferred principle say about a human brain organoid, an uploaded human emulation running on a computer, and a system that has high information integration but no output behavior? I especially want to know whether the principle puts suffering at the base level or only at high integration: if an electron has a tiny piece of mind-stuff, does it have a tiny piece of suffering, or is suffering a high-level phenomenon that requires integration? Please state the principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot preserve, and explain why you would overturn those rather than complicate the principle. I would like your answer in continuous prose, as in an email or letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
