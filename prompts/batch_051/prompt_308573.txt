I write software for a living, mostly systems that have to make decisions under uncertainty, and I keep finding that a philosophical problem I read about in my spare time resembles my day job but refuses to fit the same machinery. The problem is not which moral theory is true, but what an individual should do when genuinely unsure which moral theory is true. In my work, uncertainty is a distribution over states of the world: you evaluate each option under each state, weight by probability, and take an expectation. When I try to carry that template over to uncertainty across entire moral theories, it breaks in ways I lack the vocabulary to describe. The theories may not share a common unit of value, so the expectation seems to average over incommensurable quantities. A theory may contain an absolute prohibition rather than a degree of wrongness, and it is unclear how a small probability assigned to that theory should interact with a large probability assigned to a permissive one. And the problem is self-referential: I can be uncertain not only about which moral theory is right, but about what one should do in light of that very kind of uncertainty.

Before I read another account of acting under moral uncertainty, I want the specification written down. Give me the desiderata that any adequate theory in this area must meet: the cases it has to handle correctly; the distinctions it cannot collapse, such as the difference between uncertainty about empirical facts and uncertainty about the evaluating principles themselves, or between a large probability in a view and a vanishingly small one; and the formal constraints from decision theory that it must respect or explicitly reject, together with the consideration behind each item. Then flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps, identify any pair or larger subset that cannot be jointly satisfied (for instance, does ruling out fanaticism about tiny probabilities in extreme theories force giving up maximizing expected choice-worthiness, the moral analogue of expected utility?), and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise plausible proposals. I am less interested in a scorecard for named positions than in the criteria themselves and the cases that motivate them.
