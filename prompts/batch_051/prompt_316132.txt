I've been going back and forth on the old "why did Rome fall" argument after rereading the closing pages of Gibbon's Chapter 38, and I keep feeling like the modern "it didn't fall, it transformed" school and Gibbon actually share a weird meta-move while disagreeing about almost everything else. Here's the passage that's been bugging me, from Edward Gibbon, The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, Chapter 38:

"The rise of a city, which swelled into an Empire, may deserve, as a singular prodigy, the reflection of a philosophic mind. But the decline of Rome was the natural and inevitable effect of immoderate greatness. Prosperity ripened the principle of decay; the causes of destruction multiplied with the extent of conquest; and, as soon as time or accident had removed the artificial supports, the stupendous fabric yielded to the pressure of its own weight. The story of its ruin is simple and obvious; and, instead of inquiring why the Roman empire was destroyed, we should rather be surprised that it had subsisted so long. The victorious legions, who, in distant wars, acquired the vices of strangers and mercenaries, first oppressed the freedom of the republic, and afterwards violated the majesty of the purple. The emperors, anxious for their personal safety and the public peace, were reduced to the base expedient of corrupting the discipline which rendered them alike formidable to their sovereign and to the enemy; the vigour of the military government was relaxed, and finally dissolved, by the partial institutions of Constantine; and the Roman world was overwhelmed by a deluge of Barbarians."

So here's the thing I want to get at. Gibbon seems to be giving a pretty clean internal-decay story: the empire got too big, the army became expensive and unreliable, the constitution got corrupted, and barbarians were more a downstream symptom or final push than a cause. But at the very same time, he does this suspiciously modern-looking inversion, saying the real puzzle isn't why Rome was destroyed but why it lasted so long. The late-antiquity continuity people do something similar when they say the western empire didn't really collapse, it just reorganized into successor kingdoms, with tax systems and bishops and landownership carrying over more or less intact. Compare Gibbon's account in this passage with that transformationist view. Which specific claims in the passage does a continuity-minded historian of late antiquity have to reject outright, and which ones do they quietly accept but reframe? Is there a non-trivial sense in which Gibbon himself is already doing the "there is less to explain here than you think" move, even though he clearly does believe in decline and ruin? I'm not looking for two separate summaries of the positions; I want the actual points of pressure where they are forced to say different things about the same facts.
