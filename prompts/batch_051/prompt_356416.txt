Question (closed book; 100 points total: (a) 15, (b) 25, (c) 30, (d) 30).

A result sometimes called Good's theorem says, roughly, that the expected value of cost-free information is never negative: an agent who may observe a signal before choosing an act can expect to do at least as well as an agent who must choose without observing the signal. (a) State the result precisely, making clear what it assumes about the agent and what exactly it concludes. (b) Explain the proof idea. (c) Identify two realistic ways in which one of the theorem's assumptions might fail, and say what each failure does to the conclusion. (d) Does the result show that a rational agent should always seek out free information? Justify your answer.

Student answer:

Good's theorem states that the expected value of costless information is nonnegative, and positive whenever the signal is informative about the payoff-relevant state. To prove it, let a* be the act that maximizes expected utility relative to the agent's prior beliefs, and let a_s be the act that maximizes expected utility relative to the agent's posterior beliefs after observing signal s. Since a* is an act the agent could still choose after seeing s, the posterior expected utility of a_s must be at least that of a*. So for every signal s, EU(a_s | s) ≥ EU(a* | s). Taking expectations over signals, weighted by the prior probability of each signal, gives E_s[EU(a_s | s)] ≥ E_s[EU(a* | s)] = EU(a*), where the last equality follows from the law of iterated expectations. Hence the expected value of the information, E_s[EU(a_s | s)] − EU(a*), is nonnegative.

The result assumes that the agent updates by conditionalization, that the information is free, and that the agent chooses the act that maximizes expected utility relative to her updated beliefs. If any of these assumptions fails, the conclusion can fail. For example, if the agent updates in a biased way, the information may push her posterior away from the truth and the value of information can be negative. Also, in realistic settings information is rarely free in the relevant sense, because acquiring and processing it takes time and attention; these are real costs, so it can be rational not to acquire information even when the theorem's conditions otherwise hold.

For part (d), I think the theorem does show that a rational agent should always seek out free information. Since the theorem shows that costless information cannot make an agent worse off, and an expected-utility maximizer should never pass up an option that can only improve things in expectation, declining free information would be irrational. The only exceptions are cases where the information itself changes the decision problem, but those are outside the theorem rather than counterexamples to it.
