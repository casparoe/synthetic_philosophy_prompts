In my philosophy of science course we are reading Woodward's interventionist account of causation, and the course reader opens with a short introductory chapter that summarizes the view. I have paraphrased the chapter's summary below. I have a feeling it is broadly right but that it may turn one or two of Woodward's concessions into refutations, and I would like to know how seriously to take it.

According to the chapter, Woodward's interventionism says that causation is fundamentally a matter of what would happen if we manipulated things: X causes Y just in case wiggling X would systematically change Y while other variables are held fixed—that is, when X and Y are correlated under an experimental manipulation and the relationship between them is invariant. Because mere correlation can be spurious, Woodward requires that the manipulation be an intervention: an idealized change in X produced by an external switch that does not affect Y through any route other than X and is not tangled up with the other causes of Y. But, the chapter says, specifying what an intervention is inevitably uses exactly the causal facts the account was supposed to explain—an intervention must not cause Y except through X, and so on. Woodward, according to the chapter, effectively concedes this circularity by saying that the point of the account is not to reduce causation to non-causal terms but only to illuminate how causal structure is revealed by manipulation. The chapter then concludes that this is a serious retreat: without a non-circular characterization of intervention, the account cannot tell us which changes count as interventions without already assuming causal knowledge, and it makes causation unacceptably anthropocentric, since the causal relation would depend on what we are physically able to manipulate. So, the chapter says, interventionism either rests on a vicious circle or gives up on being an account of causation at all.

I would like an accuracy audit of this summary. Please go roughly sentence by sentence and distinguish what a careful defender of interventionism would accept, what is a simplification or compression, and what is inaccurate or overreached. In particular, I want to know whether the circularity worry lands as decisively as the chapter suggests, whether the slide from manipulation to anthropocentrism is legitimate, and whether anything in the accurate parts licenses the chapter's either-or conclusion. If the chapter is fair to Woodward's reply but still thinks the reply fails, tell me where exactly the disagreement lies.
