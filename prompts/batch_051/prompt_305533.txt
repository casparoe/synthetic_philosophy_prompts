I have a friend who has thought her way to an unusually firm line between medical therapy and enhancement, and over the years I have collected her verdicts on particular cases because I find them strikingly consistent. For her, insulin for type 1 diabetes is clearly therapy, as are an SSRI for major depression, Botox for chronic migraine, LASIK for a nearsighted adult, growth hormone for a child with a diagnosed growth hormone deficiency, a cochlear implant for a child born deaf, a prosthetic leg for someone who lost a leg below the knee, and a flu shot for a healthy adult. She is equally firm that it is enhancement when a college student without ADHD takes a stimulant to pull all-nighters, when a healthy marathoner blood dopes, when a woman of ordinary height gets leg-lengthening surgery in order to be taller, when a healthy person gets Botox to smooth wrinkles, and when a violinist with no anxiety disorder takes a beta-blocker before a performance to steady her hands.

The case I cannot predict is this. An eight-year-old boy is healthy, with no growth hormone deficiency and no underlying disease, but his doctors predict an adult height of four feet ten inches, well below the ordinary range for men. He is not suffering now, though his parents worry about how he will be treated as he gets older. If I asked my friend whether giving him growth hormone now is therapy or enhancement, what would she likely say?

I am less interested in the bare prediction than in the rule that produces it, and I suspect that more than one rule fits everything she has said so far. Lay out the plausible rules that fit all of her past verdicts, say which of those rules give different answers for this boy and what each predicts, give your best guess about what she would say and why you prefer that reading, and name a further case that would most cleanly separate the leading candidates.
