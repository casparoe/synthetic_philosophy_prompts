I keep seeing AI-safety discussion split into two very different kinds of work, and I want to know whether the split itself has a name — though honestly I'm more interested in someone carving the distinction precisely than in getting a label and being done with it.

One kind of work tries to change what a model actually wants: you train it on examples of being helpful and refusing harmful requests, you have it think through what a decent, careful person would do before it acts, you try to shape the model's goals so that when it helps you it's because it wants to. The underlying bet is that the model can be made to care about the right things.

The other kind doesn't try to change what the model wants at all. It just assumes, for safety's sake, that the model might be playing along while actually trying to get away with something, and then tries to catch it or make it unable to act: monitor everything it does, read it's scratch-pad for signs of deception, don't let it send messages or move money without a human in the loop, keep it in a box where even a malicious model couldn't do much damage, and design tests meant to trip up a model that's deliberately holding back or playing dumber than it is. The bet here isn't that the model is good; it's that you've built a world where it wouldn't matter much if it weren't.

The same lab does both at once, and everyone seems to sense they're different activities, but I don't know wether the distinction has a name, and the boundary keeps smearing when I look hard: some interpretability work gets used both to train better motives and to audit for deception; some "good-behavior" training seems to already presuppose that the model is cooperative enough to take the training. So I'm not sure the two kinds are even cleanly separable.

Is there an established term for this split, or for the second kind of work in particular? If the closest terms are things like the ones I half-remember, I want to know how my description lines up with each of them and where it drifts — not just which label to attach. 2. The carve (about 300 words) — your own precise statement of the difference, what's essential to each side, and which of a few concrete techniques fall on which side.

4. A name, if one is owed (about 100 words) — coin or endorse a name only if it would actually keep the distinction sharp, and say what the name is buying us over just describing the two stances.
