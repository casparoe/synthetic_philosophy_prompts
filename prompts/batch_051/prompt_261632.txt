Consider Alan Turing's discussion, in the 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence, of the objection he attributes to Lady Lovelace: an engine can do whatever we know how to order it to perform, but has no pretensions to originate anything. Turing does not deny that the machine's behavior is caused by its initial instructions; his reply turns on the fact that a program can produce results that surprise the person who wrote it, and that the ability to trace the result back to instructions does not make the result something the programmer already had in mind.

Draft the adversarial-collaboration document that the following two researchers should have produced after arguing for a year about whether machine learning systems can make intuitive leaps beyond their training distribution.

Camp A holds that no meaningful novelty beyond the training distribution occurs: apparent leaps are interpolation in high-dimensional space, recombination of memorized patterns, or artifacts of search that operate only over objectives fixed by the training setup. Camp B holds that composition, search, and learned heuristics can yield solutions or conjectures that are not contained in the training distribution in any explanatory sense, and that some of these are properly called intuitive leaps. Both camps accept that the model is a trained computational system and that its behavior is caused by its weights and inputs; the dispute is about whether causal dependence on training eliminates novelty.

The document should contain:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims that both camps can sign. These should be substantive, not vacuous: they should cover what a training distribution is, why out-of-distribution generalization is expected in some cases, how "novelty" will be kept distinct from "usefulness" or "surprise to a human observer," and what minimal condition a behavior must meet for both sides to call it a candidate intuitive leap.

2. A disagreements table. For each row, give a neutral label for the question; a precise statement of Camp A's position, phrased so Camp A would sign it; a precise statement of Camp B's position, phrased so Camp B would sign it; and a one-sentence judgment, if appropriate, that the disagreement is partly verbal.

3. A crux section. Identify two or three sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. For each crux, state a concrete test from ordinary machine-learning practice, not a fanciful scenario, that both sides agree bears on it; then record in advance what each camp predicts the test would show. Explain what would lead each side to revise its view and what would not.

4. End with a one-paragraph joint statement that both camps can issue, setting out what the dispute is not about and what remains genuinely open.

Do not adjudicate the dispute, and do not appeal to papers, experts, or further reading. The work is in the drafting: the formulations should be exact enough that both sides can see their own position represented without caricature, and that residual disagreements are separated from ones that were only about words. Use Turing's discussion as the framing question, not as an authority that settles it.
