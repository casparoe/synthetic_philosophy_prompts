I co-run a fortnightly philosophy discussion group for adults with no formal background in the subject; there are about fourteen of us, we meet for two hours, and we take turns planning the sessions. For our next meeting I want an activity on obedience and authority: specifically, on how ordinary, reasonable deference to someone in charge can gradually turn into something a person would not defend if they looked at each step by itself. I do not want a lecture, and I would prefer that names such as Milgram or Arendt not appear until the debrief. The aim is for people to feel the force of the question — when is deference a virtue and when is it an abdication of judgement? — before they have a label for it.

The activity must be runnable by one of us, with no special equipment beyond paper, pens, a table, and perhaps a score sheet. Everyone should be involved throughout; no spectators. I imagine a two-part exercise. In the first part, a rotating 'operator' gives instructions that are plainly sensible, and the group is rewarded for following them quickly; this is meant to establish that deference to a co-ordinating role can be good and satisfying. In the second part, the same format continues but the instructions shift gradually — small extra steps, slight restrictions, requests that are individually minor but cumulative — and participants have to decide, in real time, when they will no longer co-operate. There should be small but genuine stakes, such as points or losing a break privilege, so the choices are not merely hypothetical. Somewhere near the middle, perhaps before the final round, each participant should privately write down the most recent instruction they now think they should have refused; the slips are pooled and read aloud without names, so that the gap between private judgement and public behaviour becomes visible before anyone names it.

Please give exact roles and rules, a script or sentence frames for the operator at each stage, a timeline for the full two hours, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the real phenomenon: where reasonable deference ended, which features of the situation had changed by that point, which justifications for continuing were sound and which were post hoc. I suspect the relevant boundary is between someone who outranks another person only in co-ordination and someone to whom one has handed over one's judgement, but I want the activity to let the group find that line rather than have me assert it. The debrief should also distinguish the legitimacy of a role from the legitimacy of a particular instruction, and quick obedience from considered consent.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time we run it — for example, someone refusing too early to make a point, the operator feeling they have to improvise crueller instructions than the rules allow, or the group treating it as a joke — and how to handle each failure in the moment. End with an honest note on which feature of real-world obedience the activity cannot reproduce, such as the weight of genuine consequences, the presence of an institutional hierarchy extending beyond the room, or the slowness of the real-life slide, and how I should supply that in the debrief without turning it back into a lecture.

After the prose, please include a structured summary in table form. The first table should have one row per phase of the activity, with columns: phase, time allowed, what the facilitator says and does, what participants do, and the likely failure and its correction. The second table should cover the mapping to reality, with columns: feature of the real phenomenon, how the activity recreates it, how it falls short, and what the debrief must add. Use British English throughout.
