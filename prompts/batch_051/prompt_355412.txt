Two friends of mine, Maya (who works in ML) and Dan (who works in evo bio), spent most of dinner last week arguing about whether it makes sense to call evolution an optimizer at all, and I ended up as the supposed referee. I honestly couldn't do it, so I'm passing it to you.

Dan opened by saying the whole evolution-as-gradient-descent-on-a-fitness-landscape thing is a just-so story. A genotype is a discrete string, mutations are jumps between strings, and fitness isn't a smooth function of the genotype, so there is no gradient there to follow. He said people who talk about selection climbing local optima are just projecting the math of neural nets onto something with no such structure.

Maya said that overstates the difference. Actual gradient descent is already discrete steps on a non-convex surface, and what matters is a local signal that correlates with improvement; a selection coefficient is exactly that kind of signal, just noisy. At the right level of abstraction, backprop and selection are both loops of variation plus selection plus evaluation, so the two aren't different kinds of process, they're two settings of the same kind.

Dan's counter was that the information backprop runs on is exactly what selection lacks. Backprop computes, against one fixed objective, which direction would improve it and roughly by how much. Selection only tells you which variant persisted relative to others; it's blind to why it did better, so it can't separate a trait that helped from one that hitchhiked, and it can't use local smoothness because a single mutation can change the phenotype wholesale. He said that's not a hardware limit, it's the absence of the quantity that makes gradient descent optimization.

Maya answered that the why-blindness is just a coarse credit-assignment problem, which stochastic gradient descent has in a weaker form too, since you attribute error to whatever a batch happened to contain. And moving fitness landscapes from coevolution are just the online multi-agent case, like GANs or self-play; nobody denies those are optimization just because the target moves every step.

Dan then brought up explainability. He said gradient descent on overparameterized nets works because the loss surface is smoothish and full of acceptable minima. Organisms look designed because selection crawls through the space of discrete developmental programs with nothing like that smoothness; if you force the GD analogy, you either assume the smooth gradient-rich world that would make the design problem trivial to explain, or you quietly switch what optimizer means until the claim is empty. So the analogy isn't false, it's useless exactly where it's supposed to help.

Maya said that's backwards. The payoff of the comparison is separating the necessary ingredients of optimization—variation, selection or retention, and an evaluation that correlates with improvement—from implementation details. Natural selection is one configuration of those ingredients, backprop is another, so calling evolution a stupid optimizer is a comment on how badly one configuration does on smooth landscapes, not a denial that selection optimizes. She said Dan's real objection is teleological: he doesn't want to say selection optimizes toward anything because there's no target. But an optimizer doesn't need an intended target, just a mechanism whose outputs systematically improve on some measure.

Dan replied that "systematically improve on some measure" is doing the heavy lifting. Outside a fixed fitness landscape, the measure is just current prevalence under current conditions, which changes while you're optimizing it. Maya said that's true of any learner trained on a shifting data distribution, so it can't be a knock on the analogy.

At that point they both looked at me, and I coudln't pick a side. Please referee the actual argument point by point: who is right about which specific claims, where does each of them overreach, what are they both missing, and who is more right overall? I don't need a survey of the evolution-versus-gradient-descent debate; I need a verdict on what they actually said.
