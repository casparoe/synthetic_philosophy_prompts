Consider the standard Spence-style model of education as a signal. There are two worker types, high-ability (H) and low-ability (L), with fixed productivities q_H > q_L. Education level y is observable; ability is not. Workers bear education costs c_H(y) and c_L(y), and in the canonical version high-ability workers find education less costly: for all y, c_H(y) < c_L(y) and c_H′(y) < c_L′(y). Education has no effect on productivity. Employers set the wage for each observed education level equal to expected productivity conditional on that level. Under these assumptions a separating equilibrium exists: H chooses some y* that L finds too costly to imitate, and the differential cost of the signal is what keeps it honest.

Now make one change. Leave the timing, information, and wage-setting rule unchanged, but suppose the two types have exactly the same cost function, c_H(y) = c_L(y) = c(y), while education stops being unproductive: each year of school raises an H worker's output by v and raises an L worker's output by zero. Both v and the type-dependence of the return are common knowledge, but employers still observe only y and not output before paying.

Does a separating equilibrium still exist in this revised model, or does the separation argument simply stop applying once the difference between types is moved from the cost side to the benefit side? If separation fails, identify precisely which feature of the original model was load-bearing, and say what the generic equilibrium looks like instead. More generally, what does this variation show about how the handicap principle should be formulated: does honest signaling require that the signal be costly, that its cost be lower for higher-quality signallers, or merely that the signal's net payoff schedule differ across types in the right way? Under the revised assumptions, would education be better classified as a handicap, as an index (a signal whose reliability comes from being causally or physically tied to the trait rather than from strategic cost), or as something else, and why?
