I'm writing a term paper on concepts of health and disease and I've gotten genuinely stuck, not in the usual 'everything is complicated' way but on one specific case. The naturalist view attracts me because it promises a value-free test: a condition is a disease just in case some part or process is functioning below the statistically typical level for the appropriate reference class (age, sex, species), relative to what the thing is biologically designed to do. That would make disease something doctors discover, not something a society votes on. I also don't love the normativist alternative—if disease is just 'a harmful condition that medicine ought to treat,' then the history of psychiatry (homosexuality in the DSM, the fights over diagnoses being added or removed) shows how quickly that becomes a political cudgel.

But the case I can't dissolve is pathological gambling. I have a very firm intuition that someone whose life has collapsed into chasing losses is sick, and sick in a way medicine ought to take responsibility for. The trouble is that their reward-learning system doesn't look like it's malfunctioning; it looks like it's doing exactly what it was selected to do—tracking rewards and shaping behavior—just in an environment packed with supernormal stimuli no ancestral reward system ever faced. So the naturalist seems to force me to either say gambling disorder isn't a disease (which I can't bring myself to say) or loosen 'dysfunction' until it includes a normal mechanism in a mismatched environment, at which point the value-free test has quietly swallowed a value judgment about what environments a human life ought to contain. Is my intuition a reason to abandon naturalism, amend it, or treat it as a folk hangover to be debunked? Or is there a reading on which the theory and the intuition don't actually conflict? I'd want to know what would justify each of those moves, not just a verdict.
