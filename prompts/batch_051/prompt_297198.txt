You are a specialist in orthography and the philosophy of language, retained by a small national language academy to give a carefully reasoned opinion on a spelling dispute. Work through the question step by step: begin by distinguishing the plausible readings of the rule quoted below, then apply each reading to the case, and only then give a verdict. At the end, state how confident you are in your verdict overall, and single out the specific point you are least sure of.

In 1921 the academy's orthographic commission issued the following rule, here translated: "A word shall be written as it is spoken by educated persons, with no sound omitted and no sound added. Each distinct sound of the language shall have its own letter, and one letter shall have only one value. Where educated usage is divided, the speech of the capital shall settle the spelling."

The language's native p is plain and unaspirated: it is produced without a following puff of breath, in all positions, and no pair of native words is distinguished by the presence or absence of such a puff. The alphabet therefore has one letter, <p>. Through recent contact with a neighbouring language, the community has borrowed a unit of dry measure whose name, in the source language, begins with a strongly aspirated p — a p followed by an audible breath. In the source language, aspirated p and plain p are two different sounds: a near-identical word beginning with plain p names a different measure. In the borrowing language, however, aspirated p has never occurred in native words, and educated speakers divide: some pronounce the borrowed name with the foreign aspirated p, others with the native plain p. Since the two measures would be confused in writing if the names were spelled identically, the academy cannot postpone the question.

The academy must decide the official spelling of the borrowed name. Does the 1921 rule require a new letter for the aspirated p, or does it require the existing <p>? Interpret the rule in four ways: (a) the plain words of the text, (b) the purpose of a uniform and learnable script, (c) what the 1921 drafters would most plausibly have answered if asked whether plain p and aspirated p are one sound or two, and (d) what a reasonable present-day reader, alert to modern linguistics, should take the rule to mean. Then say which approach ought to govern and why. In answering, take up the deeper point the dispute exposes: does a spelling rule framed in terms of "sounds" implicitly commit its authors to a theory of what counts as a distinct sound of the language — a phonemic division of speech — and was the commission's rule phonemic or phonetic in character, whether or not its authors had those terms?
