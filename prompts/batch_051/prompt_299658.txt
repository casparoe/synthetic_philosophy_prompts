I read Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences a while back, and one thing in it has been bugging me. Early on, the books whole pitch is that analogy-making isn't one respectable kind of thinking among others—it is all of thinking. Every concept you have is just a stock of remembered analogies, and theres no separate logical engine underneath that occasionally reaches for a comparison; the comparison is the engine. But then a lot of the later discussion is about analogies that mislead people: shallow similarities, categories extended too far, catchy comparisons that send a whole line of thinking down the wrong path. And I can't figure out what makes those cases bad. If analogy is all there is, what is an analogy failing to do—whats the standard that exists outside the analogy? Are the two claims (analogy is the core of all thought, and many analogies are wrong) actually reconcilable, or does the second one smuggle in exactly the kind of external, non-analogical standard the first one denies? Walk me through the best reconciliation, and be honest about the places where it starts feeling like special pleading. Also say how confident you are in your answer and single out the point you're least sure of.
