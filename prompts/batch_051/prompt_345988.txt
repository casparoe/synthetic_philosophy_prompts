My roommate and I have been going back and forth on this for two days and I can't find the exit. He's got an argument he says shows plain Bayesianism can't handle the most ordinary kind of scientific evidence — the kind you already knew before you formulated the theory. He writes it out like this:

(1) On a Bayesian view, evidence E confirms a theory H just in case conditioning on E raises your credence in H: P(H|E) > P(H).

(2) If E is old evidence, meaning you already knew E when you proposed H, then your credence in E is already 1. It's fully soaked into your background knowledge.

(3) If P(E) = 1, then P(H|E) = P(H&E)/P(E) = P(H&E), and since P(E) = 1 we get P(H&E) = P(H).

(4) So for old evidence, P(H|E) = P(H), and by (1) E doesn't confirm H at all.

(5) Therefore a Bayesian is committed to saying old evidence never confirms a new theory.

(6) But that seems flatly false. The anomalous part of Mercury's perihelion had been known for decades before Einstein proposed general relativity, and it's accounting for that pre-existing data was one of the main early reasons people took the theory seriously. That's a textbook case of old evidence confirming a new theory.

(7) So simple Bayesianism is false.

I'd like to keep both the broad Bayesian picture and (6), but he says I can't have both. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? If one of the premises has to go, which one should a Bayesian reject, and what does the repair actually cost?
