I am a doctoral student in the history of science, and after a campus screening of a new prestige biopic about Charles Darwin, two of my fellow students got into an argument that I have not been able to resolve. In the film's final scene, Darwin gives a public lecture, holds up a Bible, denounces it, and throws it into a fire. The filmmakers have acknowledged that this episode never happened; they say it was included to dramatize Darwin's estrangement from Christianity. The argument went roughly like this.

(1) Sofia, a historian, said the scene wrongs Darwin. It attributes to him a public act he never performed and that is not in character: Darwin was, by all accounts, a private man and anguished rather than polemical about religion. A viewer will carry away the false belief that he did this, and the dead cannot correct it.

(2) Adam, a film-studies student, replied that no competent viewer treats a biopic as a documentary, and a scene this plainly symbolic is read as symbol, not record. The dramatist's obligation is to render the meaning of a life. An invented act that condenses a real inner development — Darwin's loss of faith — is a legitimate device, and forbidding it would forbid serious drama about the dead.

(3) Sofia answered that this collapses interpretation into fabrication. Interpretation selects and emphasizes documented events, or puts into a character's mouth thoughts his letters show he actually held. Inventing a public scene that reverses what is known of the man's manner creates a new event rather than re-presenting a life, and it borrows the name's authority while disowning its discipline.

(4) Adam said there is no event-neutral telling of a life. Every biographer selects, compresses, and emplots; the idea that the documented record by itself yields a depiction is a fantasy. Filmmakers make the same choices in a dramatic idiom, and since the work announces itself as dramatization, no one who has not already confused genres is misled.

(5) Sofia replied that "no one is misled" is false in practice: audiences regularly absorb biopics as history. But the deeper point is that a continuum between selection and invention does not erase the distinction at its endpoints. There is a line between emphasizing the facts and manufacturing episodes, and historians draw it every day.

(6) Adam came back that the line is a convention of Sofia's discipline, not a moral boundary: dramatists have different ends. And her opening charge of "wronging Darwin" hides the real issue. The dead have no interests left to set back, so talk of a duty owed to Darwin is sentimentality; what is really being defended is a professional norm, not a person.

I could not settle the exchange point by point, and the two of them have asked me to referee. Please go through the argument move by move, say for each move whether it is clearly mistaken, largely mistaken, largely right, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say which side is right about which specific claims, what both parties are missing, and who is more right on balance.
