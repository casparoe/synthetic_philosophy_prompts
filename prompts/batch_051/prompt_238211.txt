I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm writing a long feature about a reform proposal aimed at the oldest objection to judicial review: that a handful of unelected judges can override laws passed by an elected legislature. The proposal's architects don't want to abolish judicial review; they want to scale its strength to the democratic mandate behind each statute.

The mechanism, which they call margin-indexed review, works like this. The jurisdiction has a 100-member unicameral legislature and a nine-justice constitutional court. A statute passes with a fraction L of the full legislature voting in favour, so L runs from just over one half to one. Under the proposal, the court may declare the statute unconstitutional and unenforceable only if the number of justices voting to strike, out of the full nine, is at least nine times L, rounded up to the next whole justice. Concretely: a law passed 51-to-49 can be struck by five justices; a law passed 60-to-40 requires six; a law passed 67-to-33 requires seven; a law passed unanimously requires all nine. There is no override provision, and a court that falls short of the required number simply leaves the statute standing, though it may issue a non-binding warning.

I want a dispassionate mechanism analysis for the piece, not a legal brief and not a survey of the literature. Walk me through how this rule would actually behave. What does it do to bill-drafting, coalition-building, and recorded votes in the legislature? Who games it first, and how? How does it change the court's strategic behaviour — which cases it hears, how justices vote, how opinions are written? What different effects does it have on laws that protect unpopular minorities and on laws that burden them? What second-order effects should I expect after two or three election cycles? And tell me which failure mode seems hardest to repair within the design, and which intuitive-sounding objection actually misses the real weakness.
