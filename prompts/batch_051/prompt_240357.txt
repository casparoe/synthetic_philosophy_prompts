I'm a CPA, not a philosopher — I do financial accounting and some audit work, and my patience for loose talk about "what people owe each other" is limited. Lately I've been arguing with a friend about gratitude: specifically whether the fact that someone helped me at real cost, or that I got a break I didn't earn, or that I'm the product of a past I never chose puts me in any kind of position that's remotely like a debtor. My friend keeps using words like obligation and indebtedness, and I keep wanting to ask: what's the liability, when's it due, who's the counterparty, and can it be forgiven or written off? He says I'm flattening the whole thing, and maybe he's right, but I can't see what's left over.

So here's what I'd like. Act as an expert in both moral philosophy and accounting, and first brainstorm, briefly, which accounting concepts could possibly carry the philosophical content. Then take the main philosophical positions about what we owe to benefactors, to luck, and to the past — the view that gratitude is a debt or obligation, the view that it's about recognizing the benefactor's goodwill, the view that it's an emotion or virtue rather than a promise, and any other central positions you think belong — and restate each one as faithfully as you can in accounting vocabulary: liabilities, payables, receivables, contingent liabilities, impairment, bad debt, forgiveness of debt, deferred credits, going concern, audit trail, whoever. Be honest about which parts translate cleanly and which you had to fudge. Then give me the residue report: what does not survive the translation, and is the residue there because accounting is missing something, or because the philosophical claims were less crisp than they look? In particular, tell me whether my "it's all just bookkeeping for feelings and luck" take is right, or whether the translation is the thing doing the flattening.
