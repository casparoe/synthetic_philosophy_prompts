I run a first-year undergraduate seminar, and I have a single fifty-minute session to introduce the idea that some truths are necessary but knowable only a posteriori — the necessary a posteriori that rigid designation opens up. The students' only relevant background is the analytic–synthetic distinction and a rough grip on empirically discovered fact. Give me a teaching plan, not the explanation itself. I want: the order in which the pieces must be introduced and the dependency relation between each — the separation of the epistemic axis (a priori/a posteriori) from the modal axis (necessary/contingent), the four-cell table with one cell left conspicuously empty, the distinction between rigid and non-rigid designation, and only then the identity-of-rigid-designators move that fills the empty cell; the single carrying example you would use, such as Hesperus–Phosphorus or water–H2O, and what that example must have and must lack for the point to land; the misconceptions to expect and where in the sequence to head each off — for example, reading "discovered by looking" as "could have turned out otherwise," treating possible worlds as distant places, collapsing H2O into a definition, mistaking chemical necessity for metaphysical necessity, and overextending the pattern to ordinary empirical predications; a check for understanding that would separate students who grasp the two-axis structure from students who have merely memorized a slogan; and what you would explicitly refuse to explain at this level — the contingent a priori, two-dimensional semantics, and the full case against the descriptive theory of names — with a sentence on why each is deferred. The conceptual work should go into the dependency structure and the misconception map, not into a polished lecture.
