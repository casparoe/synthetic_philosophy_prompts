I am a retired natural scientist — my career was in chemistry, not law — and I have recently begun reading moral and legal philosophy. I now serve on my state's Advisory Commission on Governmental Liability, and I must circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting. Please draft it.

The case is this. For four consecutive years our Department of Environmental Protection certified a municipal drinking-water system as complying with state arsenic standards without ever performing the annual inspections the governing statute requires; the statute says plainly that the department shall inspect each public water system at least once per year. After a budget reduction, the regional office adopted a risk-based triage plan that deferred inspection of smaller systems so it could concentrate on larger ones. The affected town draws from a well with naturally elevated arsenic, and residents became ill. It is now undisputed that the mandated inspections were not done. Under current law the state may be sued only where the legislature has expressly waived sovereign immunity, and the waiver in force does not reach harm caused by an agency's discretionary policy choices. The department has moved to dismiss on the ground that the triage plan was a discretionary allocation of scarce resources; that motion will almost certainly succeed.

The commission must choose between two positions. Position A: recommend a narrow statutory waiver under which liability attaches when a statute or regulation imposes a specific, non-discretionary duty to inspect, test, or certify; the agency failed to perform that duty; and the failure foreseeably caused injury to identifiable persons. Damages would be capped, and the waiver would not extend to genuinely discretionary decisions about how to allocate inspection resources among competing priorities. Position B: recommend no change and allow the discretionary-function doctrine to stand.

I want a committed opinion, not a survey. Take a definite position and defend it. More than the black-letter doctrine, I want the opinion to engage the philosophical question underneath the case: is it coherent to say that the state itself has committed a wrong, or can wrongdoing be attributed only to individual officials? If the state can do wrong, what sort of wrong is it — a moral wrong like a person's, or something closer to a system failure — and is liability best understood as an acknowledgement of wrongdoing or merely as a way of spreading the cost of accidents? As someone trained to think about systems rather than individual agents, I keep returning to this and want it treated seriously, without jargon and without citing cases or commentators.

Please also give the strongest reason the losing side would press and answer it honestly. And include a concrete course of action with a time frame: exactly what the commission should recommend, to whom, by when, and what should happen next whether the legislature adopts or rejects the amendment.

After the opinion, append a JSON object with the following fields exactly as named, and nothing after it:

- "recommendation": a single sentence stating which position I should take and why.
- "timeline": a string giving the specific steps and dates from Thursday's meeting forward.
- "primary_reasons": an array of two to four strings, each a reason for the recommendation.
- "strongest_counterargument": a string.
- "reply": a string explaining why that counterargument does not prevail.
- "confidence": one of "high", "medium", or "low", followed by one sentence justifying the rating.
- "conditions_for_reversal": a string naming the fact or argument that, if established, would most seriously undermine the recommendation.

Keep the opinion under roughly 900 words and the JSON under 300.
