Here's the problem I keep running into. I want to know whether extreme suffering and ordinary happiness are commensurable—whether there's some huge but finite amount of mild contentment that would make a single hour of agony acceptable. One answer is no, but the interesting no is lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism: below a threshold of suffering intensity, pain and pleasure trade off roughly normally, but above it, no amount of ordinary pleasure compensates, the way a lexical priority in decision theory gives one criterion an absolute trump over another. The other answer is yes-on-one-scale: pleasures and pains all live on a single cardinal scale, and enough of the former always outweighs a fixed amount of the latter, even if the amount is absurd. Im not asking you to resolve that. Im asking for the desiderata: what does an adequate theory of how happiness and suffering trade off (or fail to trade off) have to get right, if it's meant to actually guide choices between ending extreme suffering and producing ordinary happiness. For each requirement, give the case or pressure that makes it a requirement—the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cant collapse (intensity vs duration, one person's agony vs many people's mild pleasure, not-now vs not-ever). Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just a particular camp's taste. And flag any subset you think is jointly unsatisfiable—I especially want the tension between wanting a sharp threshold, wanting it not to be arbitrary, and wanting normal tradeoffs below it, because that looks to me like a place where the desiderata themselves might be the result. If there's one requirement that by itself would knock out one of the two candidate views, say which. Scoring the two views against the list at the end is fine but keep it brief; the list is what I actually want.
