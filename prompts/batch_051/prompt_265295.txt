Defenders of chain-of-thought reasoning, and much of the public discussion around language models, lean on an analogy with a student who shows their work on a math exam. The thought is that just as a teacher asks to see the intermediate steps and can then check how the student actually reached the answer, a model that emits step-by-step prose is making its own computation inspectable, so that we can supervise it, catch its mistakes, and treat its stated reasons as a window into what it really did. I want a careful audit of how far this analogy holds. Please spell out the intended mapping in detail: what is supposed to correspond to the student, the exam problem, the final answer, the written working, the student's underlying cognitive process, and the teacher's act of checking. Then identify the disanalogies, and separate the ones that threaten the conclusion the analogy is used to support from the ones that are merely incidental. Among the things I would like you to examine are whether a language model has anything analogous to a student's introspective access to the computation that produced its answer, whether training a model to predict likely continuations rather than to report its own processing matters, whether a model could emit a plausible but unfaithful chain, and whether anyone on the outside could tell the difference. Also consider whether the student in the analogy is more transparent than he or she actually is. Finally, say what does and does not survive of the original reassurance: can a chain-of-thought output be treated as a report of the model's own reasoning in anything like the way shown work is treated, and if not, is there a better analogy that captures what interpretation of a model can realistically amount to? Please write the answer in continuous prose, as you would a letter or an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold type, or tables.
