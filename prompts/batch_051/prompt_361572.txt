So I've been turning over how the law handles memory and personal identity, and it looks like a patchwork that can't decide what counts as the same person over time.

Take this. A 90-year-old can be tried today and sent to prison for a killing he committed at 22, even if he has no memory of it at all and stopped recognizing his 22-year-old self decades ago. Amnesia isn't a defense, and "is this the same person as the killer?" gets treated as a question about bodily continuity and the legal record, not about whether the man in the dock can now appropriate the deed. But the same system will refuse to try him at all if he's too demented right now to follow the proceedings — so present capacity matters, memory of the crime doesn't. And that same system will treat a person with advanced dementia as bound by the advance directive she signed while lucid, even if she'd now read it and insist she has no idea who wrote it.

What theory of personal identity is this practice presupposing, if any? It can't be Locke's memory criterion outright, since the amnesic 90-year-old fails it and still gets punished as the offender. But it also can't be just the continuing biological animal, since the law does let present mental continuity (competence, capacity) override that in places. This feels like the exact tension in Reid's brave officer: the old general remembers being the officer but not the boy who got flogged, and transitivity snaps. I want three things. (a) Does the legal patchwork strictly need any single metaphysical criterion of identity, or is it underdetermined between several? (b) What's the cheapest view that makes it hang together, and which parts are doing real philosophical work versus just it's own institutional pragmatics? (c) What would actually have to change in criminal law if a jurisdiction took either pure memory-connectedness or pure bodily continuity seriously?
