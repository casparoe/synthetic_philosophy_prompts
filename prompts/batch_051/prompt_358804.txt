I'm applying for a two-year research fellowship and need help turning a half-formed idea into a properly framed proposal. The topic is the philosophical significance of Bayesian convergence theorems: the informal idea that when rational agents with different priors conditionalize on the same evidence, their posterior probabilities eventually come into agreement, so the initial priors wash out. I think the existing discussion is pitched at the wrong level. One side defends the washing-out story by appealing to ideal convergence results; the other side deflates it by pointing out that the guarantees fail under misspecified models, when the true hypothesis is absent from the agents' hypothesis spaces, or when agents are computationally bounded. But both sides largely assume that the thing which matters is convergence of credences over hypotheses. I suspect the more fruitful question is partly downstream: which features of a prior wash out for posterior predictions and for choices made under a loss function, even when hypothesis-level credences do not converge, and at what rates? My loose conjecture is that hypothesis-level convergence is too fragile to be the canonical case and too coarse to be what the epistemological picture needs: predictive and practical agreement can emerge earlier and under weaker conditions, while some prior differences get amplified rather than washed out.

I would like it to include: a precise statement of the question, including what counts as washing out for predictive and decision-theoretic purposes; why the existing philosophical positions leave this question open rather than settling it; the method, month by month, including what formal results I would aim to prove or adapt and what small simulations or toy models I would use to develop intuitions; what a successful outcome would look like and what would count as the project failing; and an honest assessment of whether this is achievable in two years.
