I lead a four-person model-governance team at a property-casualty insurer. Every quarter we certify the prior probability distributions used in our pricing and capital models: storm frequency in each basin we write, the sensitivity of losses to wind speed, the fraction of a regional portfolio inside a catastrophe zone, claims-cost inflation after large events, and similar parameters. We work under an inherited rule: when an analyst has no information that favors one value of a parameter over another, they are to assign equal probability to the available alternatives. This rule is now producing inconsistent certified distributions. Two analysts starting from the same evidence often finish with different distributions because they described the same quantity in mathematically equivalent ways: one worked with a damage multiplier and another with its logarithm; one used a storm arrival rate and another the expected number of storms per decade. When I brought this to the chief risk officer, she told me, in effect, to fix the rule so that a change of variables cannot change the answer, and to show her exactly where the rule stops being mechanical.

Write the operating procedure my analysts should follow from next quarter onward. It should be a reusable document stating: the questions to ask in order before the no-information rule can be invoked; the explicit conditions under which the rule may be applied to a parameter at all; the checks for detecting when a choice of parameterization is doing the real work; and the points at which the procedure must stop and hand the matter to a human reviewer or to the chief risk officer. Then run the procedure on three decisions in our upcoming certification: (a) a prior for storm frequency in a basin for which we have only six years of internal data and no reliable industry benchmark; (b) a prior for the fraction of a client's coastal book that will fall inside a newly drawn flood zone, which every credible estimate places somewhere between 20% and 80%; and (c) a prior for the wind-damage exponent in our loss model, where our two external model vendors use different functional forms. At least one application should yield an answer my team will dislike. State plainly what the procedure cannot decide. Keep it short enough to be read aloud in thirty minutes; my analysts can handle one page of notation at most.
