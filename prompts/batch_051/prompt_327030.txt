I teach an upper-level early modern philosophy seminar, and I am drafting the final exam. I want one essay question on Berkeley's Master Argument — the argument that you cannot genuinely conceive of a tree, a book, or any other sensible thing as existing wholly unperceived, because the moment you try to conceive it, it is before your mind as something conceived. The question should be usable as a 50-minute essay, and it should not be answerable by restating the standard narrative about immaterialism. It should make students do three things: (1) reconstruct the argument precisely enough to say what conclusion it is actually in a position to reach — whether it can establish esse est percipi, or only something weaker about the limits of what can be represented in thought; (2) confront, in its strongest form, the familiar objection that the argument trades on a confusion between the act of conceiving and the object conceived — for example, by distinguishing conceiving of a tree as unperceived from conceiving it as perceived, or by applying a de dicto/de re distinction to show that an inability to represent an unconceived object does not by itself settle whether such objects are possible; and (3) consider whether Berkeley has a non-question-begging reply to that objection, and what remains of the argument if he does not.

Please write the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain. The marking scheme should identify where in the argument each objection is supposed to bite rather than allowing a student to get by with merely asserting that Berkeley commits a confusion, and it should list characteristic errors to penalize — for instance, reciting the argument without identifying its intended target, treating the de dicto point as if it proved that no unperceived object could exist, or conflating being conceived by me with being perceived in the idealist sense. The difficulty should come from the crux rather than from extra reading: a strong student should be able to answer it from the primary material and careful thought alone.
