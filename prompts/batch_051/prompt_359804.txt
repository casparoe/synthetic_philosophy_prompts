I've been using a distinction I call adjudicative versus elaborative progress in philosophy, and I want an honest assessment. Adjudicative progress is the kind the sciences exhibit: a debate converges on an answer, and the losing positions stop being live options. Elaborative progress is what I claim philosophy mostly exhibits: the field keeps generating better-articulated positions, sharper distinctions, and stronger objections, so the set of live options becomes richer and more defensible even though almost nothing is ever crossed off. For example, the free will debate has not converged on one position, but its main positions are far more precise and better defended than they were a century ago. I make two claims: that this distinction is not just the familiar point that philosophy progresses by discovering problems rather than solving them, and that philosophy makes almost no adjudicative progress. Is the distinction already drawn under another name? Is it coherent? Is the second claim true, and where does the distinction itself break down? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Also say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
