You are a formal epistemologist with expertise in Bayesian epistemology and the foundations of rational belief change. Think carefully, and before committing to a verdict, brainstorm the premises of the argument and the different ways imprecise credences might alter the situation.

Jeffrey conditionalization (probability kinematics) is the standard Bayesian proposal for how to revise beliefs on uncertain evidence: evidence that shifts one's probabilities over a partition {E_1, ..., E_n} without making any individual cell certain. Given such an input, the rule says one should update any proposition H by

P_new(H) = Σ_i P_old(H | E_i) P_new(E_i),

while holding conditional probabilities on the partition fixed, so that P_old(H | E_i) = P_new(H | E_i) for every H and every i. The standard arguments for this rule—appealing to diachronic coherence, Dutch-book considerations, or minimal change—presuppose that the agent's doxastic state is a single precise probability function and that the evidential input can be represented as a new precise distribution over a single chosen partition.

A substantial later development in formal epistemology, however, is the imprecise-credence framework: the view that rational partial belief should sometimes be represented by a set of probability functions rather than a single one, motivated by Ellsberg-style ambiguity and by the fact that evidence often underdetermines exact degrees of belief. In that framework, uncertain evidence may arrive as a constraint on a set of probabilities—an interval, a set of acceptable distributions, or a restriction on some but not all events—rather than as a point-valued redistribution over a neatly specified partition.

Does the standard argument for Jeffrey conditionalization survive this change? Identify exactly which premise of the argument is under pressure, and say whether the conclusion should be abandoned, restricted, or repaired. If a repaired, set-valued form of probability kinematics is possible, spell out what it would have to look like and what normative promises of the original rule it would have to give up.
