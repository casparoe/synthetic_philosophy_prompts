You are an expert on collective memory and on the interpretation of legal and quasi-legal texts. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the possible readings of the clause and the factual hinges of the case before committing to an interpretation.

Suppose a city's heritage-preservation ordinance contains the following clause: "Where the documentary record and the living memory of the community conflict, the living memory of the community shall govern the presentation and interpretation of local history." The clause was enacted in the 1990s to keep an old settlers' account on official plaques after amateur historians challenged it with courthouse records. Now the city archive has been digitized. The documents show that the city's founding involved the forcible displacement of an earlier settlement: the "founding family" celebrated each Founders' Day acquired its land through a fraudulent probate arrangement, and one founder was tried for burning the earlier settlers' meeting house. The established residents' associations say nothing should change: the community's memory, however inaccurate, still governs under the clause. But descendants of the displaced settlement—long excluded from the official story—argue that the clause actually supports them, because their memory, suppressed and transmitted privately for generations, is also the community's living memory, and it agrees with the record. The Landmarks and Heritage Commission must decide whether the clause requires, permits, or forbids revising the city's official historical interpretation.

Work through what the plain wording, the purpose of the clause, what its drafters would have said if asked, and what a reasonable reader would understand each dictate; say which approach should win here and why; and then answer the underlying question: when a community's memory and the record conflict, and the community's memory turns out to be internally contested, which should give way?
