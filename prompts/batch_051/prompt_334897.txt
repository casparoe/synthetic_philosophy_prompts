I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and several times a week I face the same decision under deadline: whether a study or an institutional press release actually supports a causal claim of the form X causes Y, X raises the risk of Y, or X improves Y, or whether I should hedge, reframe, or drop the story. I know enough to distrust a bare correlation, but in practice I improvise — I react to phrases like controlling for, longitudinal, and statistically significant without running the same checks every time, and my editor, who has to sign off on anything that runs, has begun to push back when my causal language is stronger than the design warrants. I would like you to give me a reusable procedure, grounded in the interventionist approach to causation associated with James Woodward: roughly, that a causal claim says something about what would happen to the target variable if we intervened on the candidate cause, and that what matters is whether the relationship is invariant across the right range of changes rather than a fragile accident of one dataset. Translate that into a checklist or decision procedure I can actually execute in about half an hour before filing, without running a study of my own. It should specify the questions to ask in order, what counts as a minimally adequate answer at each step, where the procedure stops and says the paper does not license a causal claim, and where it must hand the decision back to me because a step is a judgment call dressed up as a mechanical test.

The procedure also needs to be honest about two kinds of failure. First, I rarely have a number for many of the quantities a careful causal inference would want; tell me what the procedure should do when a required quantity cannot be extracted from the paper. Second, the circularity worry: an intervention is itself described in causal terms — you have to know what affects the candidate cause and whether the intervention reaches the target only through that cause — so I want you to say whether the checklist can earn its keep once that is admitted, or whether it just restates my priors in fancier language.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a randomized trial finding that a four-week mindfulness course reduces physician burnout; an observational study finding that teenagers who sleep at least eight hours get better grades than those who sleep six, after adjusting for household income; and a city-level policy evaluation claiming that a new universal school-meal program caused a drop in suspensions. I want at least one of the three to end in a verdict I will resist, and I want you to flag, for each case, the exact step at which the procedure is doing real work rather than laundering a prior.
