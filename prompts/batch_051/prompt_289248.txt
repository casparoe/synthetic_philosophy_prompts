Here's a thing I've never been able to square in biology. Standard textbooks will tell you viruses aren't alive: they dont metabolize, don't maintain themselves, and can't reproduce without hijacking a host cell's machinery. But those same textbooks will also say viruses evolve, adapt, have genomes, and call them obligate intracellular parasites — and nobody seems to find it weird that parasitism sounds like something only living things do. Meanwhile, astrobiology has a popular working definition of life along the lines of a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution. Viruses obviously flunk the self-sustaining part, but they pass the Darwinian-evolution part as well as a lot of bacteria do, since their copying involves variation and selection. So I get the feeling the virus-excluding practice is presupposing something more specific than just "reproduces and evolves." What theory of life is it actually presupposing? A metabolism-first view? A cell-theory view where being alive means being a self-maintaining cell or multicellular organism? Some mix? Reconstruct the implicit definition well enough to explain why viruses are out and bacteria are in, then say whether the "viruses aren't alive" line is genuinely entailed by that definition or is mostly a convention or legacy. And tell me what else would have to get reclassified if the field instead defined life around Darwinian evolution, dropping or weakening the self-maintenance requirement.
