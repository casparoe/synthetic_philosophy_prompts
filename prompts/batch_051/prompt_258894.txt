In popular accounts of Schopenhauer, the relation between will and intellect is frequently compressed into a single image: a blind giant of enormous strength carries on his shoulders a sighted but lame man, the blind giant supplying all motive force and all ends while the sighted rider supplies only foresight and the selection of means. The image is standardly made to license a pessimistic conclusion, namely that reason is at bottom an instrument of a blind and insatiable will, and that conscious insight and rational self-government are largely surface phenomena. Audit this analogy against Schopenhauer's actual metaphysics of the will. What maps onto the giant, the rider, the blindness, the lameness, and the direction of travel? Where does the mapping break, for instance in the relation between will and intellect as thing-in-itself versus representation, or in the claim that the intellect merely chooses means? Which features of the image quietly import a more independent or cooperative picture of the intellect than Schopenhauer's doctrine allows? And once the disanalogies are on the table, does the pessimistic conclusion the image was carrying survive, or was it riding on the picture?
