I'm currently taking a university course on the ethics of influence, and we have been wrestling with the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion. I have been keeping a list of my own gut verdicts on small cases, and I would like help turning them into an explicit principle. My verdicts are as follows.

A used-car salesperson opens the negotiation by telling me I clearly know my research, then says, "Since you're the kind of buyer who reads the fine print, let me show you the honest cost breakdown." I find this fine, though it makes me a little wary: it is flattery, but it does not feel like manipulation.

A close friend, when I turn down a request to lend them money for rent, says, "I never thought you were the kind of person who would leave a friend in this position." I find that not fine. It feels like an attempt to recruit my self-image against my own judgment.

A doctor tells a patient who is reluctant to change his diet, "If you take this seriously now, you will be at your grandchildren's weddings; if you do not, I am honestly worried you won't be." I find this fine, even though it works through fear and hope rather than through a list of probabilities.

A charity canvasser on the street stops me with the question "Do you think every child deserves clean water?" and only after I answer asks me to sign up for monthly donations. I find that not fine, but only mildly; it feels designed to trap me into a position I cannot then abandon without looking inconsistent.

A professor spends twenty minutes asking me about my career plans and then says, "Given what you have described, you should seriously consider joining my lab." I find this fine, even though the request was preceded by flattering attention.

My supervisor at work, when asking me to cover the weekend, says, "You are the only person on this team I can trust with this." I find that not fine, and I am bothered that I cannot say why it is different from the professor's case: in both, someone who knows my self-conception appeals to it while making a request.

A travel booking site displays a red banner saying "Only 1 room left at this price," which I later learn is generated by an algorithm and is often false. I find that not fine.

A political advertiser runs a spot criticizing an incumbent: there are no policy figures, just slow-motion footage of closed factories over quiet music. I find this fine, or at least not manipulative, though I am less sure than I would like to be.

My half-formed rule was that manipulation is influence that bypasses a person's capacity to assess reasons, but that does not seem to work: the doctor and the political ad both stir feelings rather than laying out arguments, and I still want to call them fine. So either the rule is wrong, or one of those verdicts is.

Could you give me the best candidate principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely, and show how each clause is motivated by one of these cases? Then say which of my verdicts each principle cannot accommodate, and whether you would overturn those verdicts. Please also apply the resulting account to a case I keep going back and forth on: a friend asks me to help them move by saying, "I'd do it for you, no question," and I cannot decide whether this is a warm expression of goodwill that makes the request clearer or a subtle way of making me feel I would be disloyal to refuse.

Finally, please tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict on the crucial cases, and where in your reasoning you think a mistake is most likely to be hiding.
