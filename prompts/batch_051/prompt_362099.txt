I'm a lit PhD student putting together a guest lecture that reads Moby-Dick through the dollar auction and escalation-of-commitment stuff—sunk costs, self-justification, throwing good money after bad. I want the reading to treat the Pequod's voyage as a trap where each new cost (the crew's labor, the smashed boats, the diverted profit, the men he loses) raises the price of admitting the whole thing was a mistake, so Ahab keeps doubling down because quitting would mean facing the waste. But I also don't want to force the novel into a game-theory template: in the dollar auction there's a rival bidder and a fixed payoff, whereas the whale never bids back and Ahab's payoff is revenge or making the lost leg mean something. So I need a reading that uses escalation-of-commitment to diagnose the pursuit, then says honestly where the model strains, and ends with what Moby-Dick adds that the decision theory alone misses—especially the identity side, the way not backing down becomes who he is. Aim for about 600 words, pitched at undergrads who've read the novel but not the decision theory. Start with the reading itself, no preamble, and don't quote the novel; just describe the scenes you'd lean on (the quarterdeck, the doubloon, Starbuck's pushback, the final three-day chase).
