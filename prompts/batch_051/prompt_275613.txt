Since I retired and started reading moral philosophy, I keep being struck by a parallel between a problem in infinite ethics and two things I remember from my time in the natural sciences. The ethical problem is that once a world contains infinitely many people, or infinite stretches of time, the total value of that world seems to depend on the order in which one adds up individual utilities: the same world can be made to look better or worse by regrouping or rearranging the terms. This looks formally similar to the behavior of a conditionally convergent series, whose sum changes under rearrangement, and also to the situation in quantum field theory where naive sums give divergent answers and one introduces a cutoff, then demands that the physical answer not depend on that cutoff. My question is whether the parallel goes beneath the surface: does infinite ethics need something analogous to absolute convergence or to cutoff-independence, that is, a principled restriction that would make the ranking of infinite worlds unique, or does the analogy break because ethical evaluation lacks anything like a physical observable against which to check the sum? I would like to know whether this is a deep structural similarity or a superficial metaphor.
