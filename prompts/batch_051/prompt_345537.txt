I am the editor of a quarterly journal that publishes work at the interface of philosophy and theoretical computer science. Roughly a third of our submissions claim that some traditional philosophical problem—induction, logical omniscience, strong AI, or practical rationality—is best understood through computational complexity theory. Too often the phrase “computationally complex” is doing decorative work: the author never specifies a complexity class, a resource bound, a reduction, or a conditional result, and the philosophical argument would be unchanged if the word “complex” were deleted. I want a desk-review procedure my associate editors can follow before sending a paper to referees, to mark submissions where complexity theory genuinely does philosophical work and those where it is used as shorthand or prestige.

Take Scott Aaronson’s essay “Why Philosophers Should Care About Computational Complexity” as the underlying standard. In that essay he writes: “I find it surprising that complexity theory has not influenced philosophy to anything like the extent computability theory has.” Treat that sentence as a diagnostic challenge: the procedure should help the journal encourage submissions that actually close that gap, by making complexity theory shape the philosophical question rather than decorating an argument that would be unchanged without it.

The deliverable should be a reusable procedure, not a one-off review: an ordered checklist, a set of tests an editor without a complexity-theory background can apply, two or three worked examples including at least one where the procedure recommends rejecting a paper the editor liked, and an explicit list of the points where editorial judgment remains irreducible. Specify the stakes and constraints: associate editors have limited time, authors expect a desk decision within four weeks, and the journal’s reputation depends on not publishing work that merely borrows technical vocabulary to sound rigorous.
