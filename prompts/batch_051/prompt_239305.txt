You are an ancient historian whose work sits on the boundary between archaeological evidence and oral or epic tradition. My colleague takes the following passage from Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 1.10 (translated by Richard Crawley), as her methodological charter:

"Now Mycenae may have been a small place, and many of the towns of that age may appear comparatively insignificant, but no exact observer would therefore feel justified in rejecting the estimate given by the poets and by tradition of the magnitude of the armament. For I suppose if Lacedaemon were to become desolate, and the temples and the foundations of the public buildings were left, that as time went on there would be a strong disposition with posterity to refuse to accept her fame as a true exponent of her power. And yet they occupy two-fifths of Peloponnese and lead the whole, not to speak of their numerous allies without. Still, as the city is neither built in a compact form nor adorned with magnificent temples and public edifices, but composed of villages after the old fashion of Hellas, there would be an impression of inadequacy. Whereas, if Athens were to suffer the same misfortune, I suppose that any inference from the appearance presented to the eye would make her power to have been twice as great as it is. […] We have therefore no right to be skeptical, nor to content ourselves with an inspection of a town to the exclusion of a consideration of its power; but we may safely conclude that the armament in question surpassed all before it, as it fell short of modern efforts; if we can here also accept the testimony of Homer's poems, in which, without allowing for the exaggeration which a poet would feel himself licensed to employ, we can see that it was far from equalling ours."

On her reading, the passage issues two requirements that pull in opposite directions: do not dismiss a tradition merely because the visible remains look meagre, and do not let poetic exaggeration inflate what the evidence can actually bear. I am revising a chapter with her, and I need to anticipate how she will rule on a fourth case before our next meeting. Her published verdicts are these. On the Trojan War: the late-Bronze-Age destruction of the citadel at Hisarlik (Troy VIIa) gives the epic a genuine historical anchor, so the war in outline is historical, while the named heroes, speeches, and divine interventions are not. On King Arthur: there is no good reason to assert a historical Arthur, since the usable sources post-date the supposed life by centuries and the figure is best explained as a literary and dynastic construction assembled over time. On the Exodus: the exodus as the Bible narrates it, a mass flight of hundreds of thousands with plagues and a generation in the wilderness, is not historical, since neither the Egyptian nor the Levantine material record supports such a migration; at most the tradition preserves dim memories of small-scale movements of West Semitic people out of Egypt.

The fourth case concerns Solomon. The tradition presents him as a tenth-century BCE king who ruled a large and wealthy Israelite empire, built the Jerusalem temple, and dominated trade and diplomacy. The state of the evidence is disputed: no contemporary inscription names Solomon or attests his empire; tenth-century Jerusalem appears on most readings to have been small; some monumental architecture once dated to his reign, notably at Hazor, Megiddo, and Gezer, is now often redated a century later; and the earliest extrabiblical reference to his dynasty, the ninth-century Tel Dan inscription, names the House of David rather than Solomon himself. Mainstream archaeology generally treats the biblical golden age as a later idealisation, while the existence of a historical king of that name is neither confirmed nor excluded by the material record.

Please think about this carefully and, before settling on a verdict, canvass the various rules that could jointly rationalise her three earlier rulings, each rule stated so that it becomes visible which reading of the Thucydidean passage it embodies. Item 5: a further case, specified enough to be usable, that would most cleanly discriminate between any rules still live, and what a ruling there would show.
