I'm retired now — spent my career as a bench scientist — and I only started reading philosophy a few years ago, so forgive me if my terminology is off. Lately I've been fixated on the Ellsberg urn, and I tried to turn the paradox into a clean deductive argument. I think it shows the standard "single probability" picture of belief has to go, but a philosopher friend says I'm smuggling something in. I want to know whether the argument is valid, and if it isn't, exactly which step fails.

Setup: an urn has 90 balls. 30 are red; the other 60 are black or yellow, in a proportion nobody has told you. One ball will be drawn. You value money monotonically.

(1) Assume a rational agent's uncertain beliefs can be represented by one subjective probability function P over the possible colors, and that for any two bets with the same payoff, she strictly prefers the bet on E to the bet on F exactly when P(E) > P(F).

(2) First choice: bet A pays $100 if the drawn ball is red; bet B pays $100 if it is black. The agent strictly prefers A to B. (This matches how most people choose: red is a known 1/3, black could be anything from 0 to 2/3.)

(3) From (1) and the preference for A over B: P(red) > P(black).

(4) Second choice: bet C pays $100 if the ball is red or yellow; bet D pays $100 if it is black or yellow. The same agent strictly preferes D to C. (Again, most people do: black-or-yellow is a known 2/3, red-or-yellow could be anywhere from 1/3 to 1.)

(5) From (1) and the preference for D over C: P(black-or-yellow) > P(red-or-yellow).

(6) Since the colors are mutually exclusive, additivity gives P(black-or-yellow) = P(black) + P(yellow) and P(red-or-yellow) = P(red) + P(yellow). Plugging into (5), P(black) + P(yellow) > P(red) + P(yellow), so P(black) > P(red).

(7) But (3) says P(red) > P(black). Contradiction.

(8) So no single probability assignment can rationalize both of the agent's own preferences, even though each one seems sensible on its own. That seems to mean the subjective-expected-utility package — one probability function plus maximizing expected utility — either has to call this ordinary-looking agent irrational, or has to admit it is not a complete account of decision under uncertainty.

My question: is the deduction from (1) to (7) airtight? If it is, is (8) the right lesson to draw, or is there a way to keep the single-probability picture and just say the agent made a mistake — and if so, where exactly is the mistake? If the argument fails earlier, name the precise step, give a corrected version of it, and tell me whether the repaired argument still gets anywhere near (8).
