I'm trying to work out my actual view in population axiology — about when one possible world is better than another, purely in terms of the lives it contains. I have the following gut verdicts, and I want to know whether any principle can capture them.

1. World A has one million people at welfare level 10; world B has the same one million people at welfare level 20. I take B to be clearly better.

2. World C has one million people at welfare level 20; world D has those same people at level 20 plus one extra person at level 10. I take D to be better, not merely not worse.

3. World E has one million people at welfare level 100; world F has those same million at level 100 plus an additional trillion people at level 1. I do not think F is better than E; I lean toward thinking F is worse.

4. World G has one million people at welfare level 100; world H has a trillion people, all at welfare level 1, with no overlap in populations. I take H to be much worse than G.

5. World J has one million people at welfare level 100; world K is identical except that it also contains one extra person at welfare level -50, a life not worth living. I take K to be clearly worse.

6. Finally, I cannot decide about a world with no people at all versus a world with one hundred people, each at welfare level 1. I keep wanting to say the second is better, but I cannot give a stable reason.

The tension I am stuck on is between verdicts 2 and 3: adding one happy-but-below-average life seems good, yet I resist saying that adding a trillion barely-positive lives is good, let alone better. Is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts or comes closest? State the principle precisely, show how each clause is motivated by the cases, say what it implies about the empty-world case and about further cases I have not considered, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it overturns or merely leaves as brute. If no single principle fits, tell me which verdicts I should be least attached to and why.
