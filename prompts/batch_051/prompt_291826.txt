I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I am in the early stages of a long essay on the way the phrase American exceptionalism does actual work in foreign policy argument. My working view is that the term is not one thesis but at least three, and I would like to pressure-test that view against a single case rather than proceed from dictionary definitions. The first register is a historical-sociological one: the United States developed along a distinctive path, without a feudal past, with a comparatively weak socialist and labor-party tradition, with unusually high religiosity, and with a national identity organized around creed rather than ethnicity. The second is a normative one: the country is uniquely virtuous, uniquely exemplary, or uniquely indispensable, and is therefore licensed to act where others may not. The third is ideological in the critical sense: a justificatory frame that recodes strategic interests, domestic pressures, and the ordinary pursuit of power as disinterested mission, innocence, and historical necessity.

I want to run this three-register scheme through the 2003 invasion of Iraq. To keep the salient facts in view: the public case for war rested heavily on claims that Iraq retained weapons of mass destruction and could pass them to terrorists; the administration had embraced a doctrine of preventive war; the invasion went ahead without a second Security Council resolution and was waged by an ad hoc U.S.-led coalition; in May 2003 a banner announced the end of major combat operations; no WMD stockpiles were subsequently found; and the occupation that followed faced a prolonged insurgency and sectarian violence. My question is not whether the war was justified. It is how far the three registers of American exceptionalism can explain what happened. What does each register say the relevant actors faced and what they ought to have done? Which features of the run-up, the rhetoric, the planning assumptions, and the domestic coalition does each register illuminate, and which features does it flatten or leave unexplained—ordinary geopolitics, oil, bureaucratic and electoral incentives, the intelligence process, groupthink? And what does the case show in return about the concept itself: is American exceptionalism best treated as a coherent doctrine, a loose family of rhetorical moves that coexist despite tension, or a term that does its work precisely because it remains underspecified?

Finally, fold in one wrinkle I have not been able to resolve. The war was also publicly framed around democratization and the spread of freedom across the Middle East. I cannot tell whether that is a fourth register—a Wilsonian, liberal-internationalist one—or merely an unstable blend of the normative and ideological registers, and I would like you to commit to one reading rather than leave the question dangling.
