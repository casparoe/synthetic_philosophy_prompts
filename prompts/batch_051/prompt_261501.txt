Consider two claims that figure prominently in debates about the risks of advanced artificial intelligence. The orthogonality thesis holds that an agent's terminal goals are not fixed by its level of general capability: in principle, any degree of intelligence could be paired with any final aim whatever. The instrumental convergence thesis holds that certain subgoals—self-preservation, resource acquisition, protecting the content of one's current goals, and improving one's own capacities—are useful means to an extremely wide range of terminal goals, so that a sufficiently capable agent would tend to adopt them almost regardless of what it ultimately wants.

Please argue for the following position: the conjunction of these two theses, taken alone, does not license any concrete prediction about the behavior of a highly capable artificial agent—in particular, it does not license the prediction that such an agent would seek power or resist being shut down. The argument should do three things. First, identify the suppressed premise that any such prediction must add: roughly, a claim about which terminal goals are typical or probable among the goals an advanced agent could have. Second, explain why the orthogonality thesis, construed as a claim about in-principle compatibility, cannot supply that premise, and why supplying it means going beyond what the two theses state. Third, answer the strongest objection available: that seeking power and preventing one's own shutdown are useful for nearly any terminal goal one can name, and therefore remain reasonable things to expect. Be precise about the difference between a subgoal's being useful for nearly all possible aims and its being useful for the aim that is in fact held. Reformulate either thesis if doing so is needed to avoid ambiguity, and frame the question as one about what the two theses establish rather than about what will in fact happen. Write the answer in American English.
