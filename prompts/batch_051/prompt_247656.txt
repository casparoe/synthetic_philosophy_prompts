I recently joined the board of a small cooperative gallery, and in my second month we had to vote on whether to remove a painter from a group show after members of the public threatened to disrupt the opening over the painter's past. I voted to keep her work, and found I could not defend that vote in terms that also made sense of my reactions to a whole family of cases I have since collected. I would like you to find the principle behind my verdicts, if one exists.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts.

A university rescinded an invitation to a speaker whose public career has consisted in insisting that a well-documented historical atrocity was invented and in coining names that mock its survivors; the invitation had been issued by a small student society without wider consultation. I think the withdrawal was defensible. The speech is the wrong, and the event would mainly have laundered it.

A supermarket in my town fired a cashier after a customer uploaded a video of him, in uniform, telling a customer that she did not belong here and should go back to her own country; the exchange began over a disputed coupon. I think the firing was fine. The counter is not his forum, and the employer is entitled to insist that staff not abuse customers on the clock, whatever he privately believes.

A streaming platform closed the account of a mid-sized political commentator who had named a county election clerk, a private person with no public profile, accused her of fraud without evidence, and told his audience to make sure she never knew another peaceful day; strangers later came to her house. He had been warned repeatedly. I think the closure was fine. The account's business was directing a crowd at a private person.

A neighborhood bar went out of business after a leaked donor database showed that its owner had given two hundred dollars to a committee supporting a ballot measure restricting same-sex parenting rights. The bar itself took no political positions, the owner did not raise the subject at work, and the donation reflected his church's teaching. A boycott and a campaign directed at the landlord followed. I think this was not fine. Whatever one thinks of the measure, this destroyed a man's livelihood and his employees' jobs over a private act of conscience.

The painter whose canvases my gallery voted to keep had, sixteen years ago, served a short sentence for assaulting a then-partner; since then she has maintained sobriety, apologized publicly, and volunteers teaching restorative justice. The removal motion described her canvases as a distraction that would invite disruption. I think removal would have been not fine. It would have been a second punishment for an already-served offense, and it would have converted her professional standing into something any stranger could take away with noise.

An actress with a line of children's books and a toy partnership was dropped by both after a home video surfaced of her at fifteen using a slur against a classmate; she apologized the same day without qualification. I think this was fine. A brand aimed at children is allowed to be skittish about who is attached to it, and she is not thereby prevented from speaking or working anywhere else.

The pair that most bothers me is the last two: in one, an old offense and a long record of repair strike me as a reason the person should not be dropped; in the other, an old offense and a same-day apology strike me as not enough to shield her from losing child-facing contracts. I cannot tell whether this is a real distinction or my own cowardice about defending an unpopular painter, and I suspect one of the two verdicts has to go. I am also less sure than I would like about the line between the university speaker and the bar owner, since both involve punishing a person for political expression, yet I treat them as opposites.

There is one further case I cannot settle. A hospital has suspended a nurse who, off duty, spoke at a rally against vaccination mandates and said on video that unvaccinated people were having an untested product forced on them; patients have recognized her and two have asked for a different nurse, and the hospital says the suspension is temporary while it reviews whether she can be trusted to follow infection-control protocols. I have no stable verdict. Her speech is off duty, but trust in a nurse is not a matter of formal qualifications alone.

What I want from you is this. Is there a single principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures my verdicts? If so, state it precisely, and say which clause of the principle each case motivates. Then apply it to cases I have not listed, including the nurse. Then say exactly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether the collision between the painter and the actress forces me to surrender one of those two verdicts, and if so which one. If no single principle will do, give me the smallest set that will, and show me where the seams are. Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
