For the last few months I have been helping build audience models for a public-interest campaign, and I keep noticing a strange double standard in how my colleagues treat generational categories. They will move without strain from a survey line like born in the late 1990s to broad claims about what Gen Z wants from institutions, what it finds credible, and how it should be addressed. Yet when I proposed, as a thought experiment, that we segment the public into even-year and odd-year births, or into surname initials A–M and N–Z, and then ask whether even-year people are more group-loyal or A–M people prefer security, everyone treated it as obviously unserious. The odd thing is that those joke groupings have cleaner boundaries than generations do: the standard birth-year cut points for the cohorts drift by several years depending on the source, and the people using the categories freely concede this. So the asymmetry is not simply that one set of categories is fuzzier than another; if anything, the fuzzier categories are the ones being treated as more real.

I can think of candidate justifications for the asymmetry. A generation might pick out a shared formative environment—a recession, a war, a technological shift—rather than a numerological accident, and it might matter because people who pass through the same events at roughly the same life stage develop recognizably different defaults. The labels may also partly constitute the phenomenon, since people come to identify with a cohort name and behave accordingly. And generation in the kinship sense names a real relationship of transmission, where odd-year does not. But each of these seems to wobble under pressure: the same historical event lands differently on an eight-year-old and a twenty-five-year-old; self-identification is itself partly a downstream effect of the marketing and journalism that use the categories; and biological generations cut across, rather than along, the usual cohort boundaries. So here is what I want to settle: is there a principled, non-gerrymandered basis for treating generational cohorts as a better kind of social category than even/odd-year or surname-initial cohorts—one that licenses the confident generalizations made about Gen Z while ruling out the identical move for odd-year people? Or is the asymmetry just the familiar situation of categories whose boundaries have been drawn to make the resulting averages look interesting, after which we forgot that we drew them? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the closest case you can construct: a grouping by birth-year parity or by decade, chosen without reference to historical events, but used to make exactly the same style of inference.
