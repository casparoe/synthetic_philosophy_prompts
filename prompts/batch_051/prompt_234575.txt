I teach an undergrad philosophy and film class, and next week we're doing 12 Angry Men (the 1957 Lumet film). I've taught it before as a study in reasonable doubt and juror bias, but this year I want to read it through the epistemology of testimony — specifically the old fight between reductionists and anti-reductionists. Quick version: the reductionist (Hume, roughly) says testimony only justifies belief if you have independent reasons to think the testifier is reliable, while the anti-reductionist (Reid, roughly) says testimony is a basic source of justification, on a par with perception and memory, so you're entitled to trust what people say unless you have specific reasons not to.

What I'd like from you is a reading of the film that tracks this debate through actual scenes. The obvious material is the eyewitness testimony the jurors spend the movie pulling apart: the old man who says he heard the boy threaten his father and saw him running down the stairs, and the woman who says she saw the murder through the windows of a passing elevated train. Where does the film look Humean, with jurors demanding independent corroboration for each bit of testimony, and where does it look Reidian, with some jurors starting from the assumption that the witnesses are telling the truth unless the evidence gives them reason not to? But I also want you to be honest about where this frame stops fitting. The standard of proof, beyond a reasonable doubt, shifts the question from whether the testimony is justified to whether it has been undermined enough, and some of the jurors' resistance to the witnesses is class prejudice or personal resentment rather than anything epistemically respectable. One more thing I keep noticing: most of what the jurors actually evaluate is not the witnesses' testimony firsthand but other jurors recounting and reenacting what the witnesses said. Does that second-order structure — testimony about testimony, argued over by twelve people — matter for the reductionist/anti-reductionist fight, or do the theories mostly ignore the social part of what is going on?

Please think like a philosopher of testimony and a careful film reader at once. Start by brainstorming the moments where a juror accepts, rejects, or revises his view of a witness's word, and then build a reading that tries to be fair to both the film and the philosophy. End with what you think the film adds to the debate that standard philosophical examples can't capture. And don't worry about citations or a bibliography — I know the film and the positions; I want your own close reading.
