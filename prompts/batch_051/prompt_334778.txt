In a seminar on the governance of emerging technologies, a classmate defended the precautionary principle by way of an analogy to criminal law. Her claim was that the principle simply relocates the presumption of innocence from the courtroom to the regulatory agency. A novel substance or process, she argued, should not be convicted of harmfulness before the evidence proves it harmful; but for interventions whose effects are plausibly irreversible, diffuse, or slow to appear, the proponent should bear the burden of establishing safety to a demanding standard, just as the prosecution must prove guilt rather than the defendant being required to prove innocence. From this she concluded that anyone who accepts the criminal-law presumption of innocence is committed, on pain of inconsistency, to some version of the precautionary principle.

I found the analogy rhetorically powerful but suspicious, and I want to know whether it can actually carry that conclusion. Please audit it in detail. First produce an explicit correspondence table: what is supposed to play the role of the defendant, the victim, the crime, the jury, the burden of proof, the standard of proof, the presumption of innocence, the punishment, and the acquittal? Then identify the places where the mapping strains or breaks. I would like you to give particular attention to these candidate disanalogies: the asymmetry between convicting an identifiable person and permitting a harm that is diffuse, cumulative, or intergenerational; the difference between a one-shot trial and ongoing regulation under uncertainty in which evidence keeps arriving; the absence of any natural baseline of non-intervention, since declining to license a technology also has consequences and casualties; the difference between establishing that a past act occurred and establishing a negative, namely safety across unspecified future conditions; the availability or unavailability of compensation and reversal; and the question of whether the relevant error is falsely convicting a technology or falsely acquitting a risk, and whose error costs should count. For each candidate disanalogy, say whether it is fatal to the intended use of the analogy or merely a difference that does not matter for the conclusion.

End by rendering a verdict. Does the legal analogy actually support the precautionary principle, or was it doing the persuasive work by quietly importing the criminal law's asymmetry between false convictions and false acquittals into a setting where the asymmetry may run differently? If some version of the precautionary principle survives the audit, state precisely what the analogy can still legitimately license. If the analogy collapses under the weight of the disanalogies, say what a better analogy for precaution would be, if there is one.
