Two people in my building got into it last night about whether a translation app ever really means anything by its outputs, and I ended up scribbling their moves on a napkin. I'll reconstruct it so you can referee.

Jess went first with a Chinese Room style point: the app maps strings to strings according to probabilities, so whatever meaning its output has is supplied by designers and users. She said there's a real difference between her thought that it's raining—which is about rain all by itself—and a sentence the app produces saying it's raining, which is only about rain because we read it that way. She called her thought's intentionality original and the app's derived.

Marcus said that's just Searle's line, and it's the line Dennett tried to dissolve with the two-bitser. A quarter is a slab of metal; its being worth 25 cents is entirely derived from a surrounding practice, and yet the quarter really is worth 25 cents—that's all there is to being a quarter. He said our thoughts are the same kind of thing: chunks of brain whose content is fixed by a surrounding practice (evolution, learning, other interpreters), and there's no extra ingredient that Searle gets to call original.

Jess came back that the quarter example proves too much. Quarters don't have their status independently of users, but users do have their content independently of any further interpreter; that's the bottom. If every meaningful item is meaningful only relative to another interpreter, you get an infinite regress, and the original/derived distinction is exactly what stops it.

Marcus said the regress bottoms out in a stance, not a property. At some point we treat a system as having beliefs and desires because that's the only way to predict it, and the so-called original intentionality is just the case where we're inside the system and can't see ourselves taking the stance. The Chinese Room's real lesson, he said, isn't that the guy in the room doesn't understand Chinese; it's that no symbol inside any system has intrinsic semantics—not even your neurons.

Jess said there's still a causal difference. Her belief that it's raining can make her grab an umbrella; the app's output token doesn't make the app do anything, because what the app does is already fixed by code and input. In her case content is doing real work in the causal story; in the app's case it isn't.

Marcus said she'd moved to a functional criterion, which is fine, but then a thermostat's bimetallic strip is also "about" room temperature in its causal role, and the app's hidden state is "about" the sentence it's halfway through translating. You can grade these things, he said, but you can't draw a line above the app and below a brain without smuggling in something Searle hasn't earned.

They never resolved it and asked me to send this to someone who could go through it move by move. For each move, say which of the specific claims hold up and which don't, where the argument actually turns, and what both of them are missing. Then give your overall verdict: is there a principled original/derived distinction here, or does the two-bitser reframe the whole question?
