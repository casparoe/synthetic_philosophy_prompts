A familiar philosophical argument against strict liability in the criminal law — call it the culpability objection — runs as follows. A just system of punishment may condemn and punish a person only for conduct for which that person is at fault, and fault in the criminal law requires a guilty mind — mens rea — in the form of intent, knowledge, or recklessness. Strict liability offences, by contrast, permit conviction and punishment on proof of the forbidden act alone, without proof of any guilty mind. Strict liability therefore licenses punishment without fault and, on this argument, is unjust.

A later development puts pressure on the argument's unstated assumptions. An increasing portion of serious harm is now produced by data-trained systems — autonomous vehicles, clinical triage and diagnostic algorithms, bail and lending models, industrial control systems — whose particular decisions no individual human fully intended, foresaw, or consciously risked. When such a system causes a death or a serious injury, the natural locus of fault is dispersed across designers, trainers, deployers, and regulators, and it can happen that no identifiable person satisfies the mens rea standard for the specific harm even though the harm was statistically foreseeable at the level of the system as a whole. Legal systems increasingly respond with strict or enterprise liability precisely because individual fault is unavailable.
