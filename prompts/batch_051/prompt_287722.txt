In her short treatise *Discours sur le bonheur* (usually translated *Discourse on Happiness*), Émilie du Châtelet argues that happiness consists less in tranquility or the absence of pain than in the exercise of strong passions—she names erotic love and the love of study among them—and that some of the illusions that sustain those passions should be cultivated rather than dispelled. Treat that pair of claims as fixed points that a theory of happiness must accommodate. What would a theory of happiness actually have to get right? Give the desiderata list: the phenomena and cases it must handle, the distinctions it cannot collapse (for example, between feeling happy and judging one's life good, or between an illusion that narrows agency and an illusion that energizes it), and the results from moral psychology it cannot contradict. For each requirement, give the consideration or case behind it, and then say which single requirement knocks out the most popular theories of happiness. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating the question. Write in American English.
