I am a doctoral student in history and philosophy of science, and I have been asked to lead a one-hour methods session for first-year graduate students on why anachronism matters when reading past mathematics and science. The group is mixed humanities and STEM, and I expect several to arrive thinking there are only two options: translate old results into modern notation and concepts, which they regard as harmless, or treat historical figures as confused forerunners of the present. I want the session to teach a sharper distinction—between using our concepts as an analytical frame and attributing them to the historical actors. Plan the session for me rather than writing the lecture. What components have to come first, and what depends on what? Which single example or short primary source should carry the hour, and what must it have and what must it avoid—I would rather not build it around a vague period narrative like the Scientific Revolution or a story of slow groping toward a modern result. What misconceptions should I expect, and at which point in the sequence should each be headed off? I am anticipating at least three: that anachronism is just a vocabulary problem; that the only alternative to presentism is antiquarian fidelity; and that narrating the past as a struggle toward the modern result is harmless. Finally, include one closing question or miniature exercise that would tell me whether the students can actually distinguish translating into modern terms from imputing modern concepts, and say explicitly what I should leave out at this level.
