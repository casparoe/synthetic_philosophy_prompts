I am a PhD student in philosophy working on rationality and strategic interaction, and I am preparing a handout for a mixed graduate seminar in philosophy and economics on the backward-induction paradox raised by the centipede game. I would like you to write a decision tree that takes a respondent from concrete reactions to centipede-game scenarios, plus answers to a few commitment questions, to a descriptively labeled position in the debate. The point is diagnostic sorting: the instrument should sort people by what they say about cases and claims, not by which label they recognize and prefer.

Before laying out the tree, work through the logical space. Identify the commitments on which serious positions about the paradox genuinely come apart, turn those commitments into branch nodes whose questions a graduate student can answer without already knowing the position labels, and then locate the actual positions as leaves in the resulting space. Flag any cells of the logical space that are empty, and say briefly whether each is empty because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored. Also flag any pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate and explain why.

Use as the baseline case a standard six-move centipede game with a pot that grows substantially at each pass, played once, anonymously, with no communication, no future interaction, and with both players known to care only about their own monetary payoff. Present the payoff table explicitly so the numerical questions can be answered. Ground at least four branch nodes in concrete variants or in moments within this game; at minimum, use these two diagnostic scenarios, and feel free to add one or two more if the logical space requires them:

(i) In the baseline game, play reaches the fourth node after both players have passed so far. What should Player 2 conclude, and what should she do?

(ii) In a variant, Player 1 publicly and non-bindingly announces before play a complete plan to pass at her first two moves. Player 1 then passes at the first node. What should Player 2 do at the second node, and does the announcement change anything?

Let the tree be four to six questions deep and yield six to eight leaves. The angle I care about is not merely what backward induction prescribes but which premise or presupposition gives way first once someone resists the standard conclusion, and what that implies about whether the backward-induction play is uniquely rational. Make sure the tree can separate at least the following positions, renaming them descriptively wherever the usual labels are theory-laden:

- an orthodox subgame-perfect-equilibrium view, on which taking at the first node is the unique rational play and observed passing reflects error or bounded rationality;
- a social-preferences reconstruction, on which passing can be rational because the players' true utilities include other-regarding concerns, so the numerical payoffs misdescribe the game;
- a common-knowledge-failure diagnosis, on which the backward-induction conclusion needs common knowledge of rationality that a pass immediately destroys;
- an off-path-belief view, on which backward induction at unreached nodes requires counterfactual beliefs the standard solution concept does not determine, so continuing can be rationalized without abandoning individual rationality;
- a team-reasoning or joint-maximization view, on which players may rationally take the pair's aggregate payoff as their objective.

End with a one-sentence note for each leaf on its most awkward commitment.
