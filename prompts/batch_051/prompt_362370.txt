I am defending my dissertation chapter next month before a committee weighted toward formal epistemology, with an outside member who is a Bayesian statistician and a computer-science reader who works on model selection. The chapter argues that the underdetermination of theory by evidence is not dissolved by modern model-selection practice: two models can be predictively equivalent over the range of data we can actually gather while disagreeing about unobservable structure, and the choice between them is settled by extrascientific commitments rather than by the data alone. My compressed argument: any finite data set consistent with one model can be matched by indefinitely many models that agree on in-sample likelihood and on out-of-sample performance under a prediction protocol fixed in advance; cross-validation, information criteria, and Bayes factors each presuppose a comparison class, a loss function, and in the Bayesian case a prior over models, none of which is read off the data; and the appeal to simplicity or naturalness merely relocates the extra-evidential commitment, since complexity is a feature of a parameterization or a descriptive language rather than of the evidence.
