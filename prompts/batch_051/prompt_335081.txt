I'm a high school student, and I've been trying to make sense of a contrast in how the United States and much of Europe handle surrogacy and egg donation. As I understand it, many European countries decline to give commercial surrogacy contracts legal force — either paying someone to carry a pregnancy to term is criminalized or the contract is left unenforceable — and women who provide eggs may be compensated only for their out-of-pocket expenses rather than paid for the eggs themselves. In the United States, by contrast, there is something closer to a market: agencies advertise payment ranges for egg donors, intended parents pay surrogates under enforceable contracts, and those contracts determine who the legal parents will be at birth. I am not asking which policy is more just or humane. I want to know what each policy is implicitly committed to.

Please reconstruct the implicit picture each practice relies on: what a parent is, what things may legitimately be bought and sold, and what roles birth, intention, contract, and money are taken to have in making a family. For instance, the American framework seems to suppose that parenthood can be fixed by prior intention and agreement, and that reproductive services can be bought like other services; the European framework seems to suppose that the person who gestates a child has a parental status that adults cannot simply sign away, and that paying for eggs or gestation treats something about women's reproductive lives as though it should not be for sale. Develop both pictures in detail, separating what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and flag any points where more than one philosophical theory could justify the practice.

Then test each picture for internal consistency. Does the European position rely on claims about the body, parental status, or the child that would sit awkwardly alongside how those same places treat abortion, adoption, or sperm donation? Does the American position rely on an ideal of voluntary contracting that would sit awkwardly alongside how the same legal system treats organ sales or payments in adoption? Finally, pick one side and explain what would have to change in that practice if its central presupposition were replaced — for example, if parenthood were held to be fixed by gestation no matter what any contract said, or if reproductive labor were treated as a fully transferable commodity.

End by saying how confident you are in your reconstruction overall, and identify the specific point you are least sure about.
