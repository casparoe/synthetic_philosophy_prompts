Write a villanelle about the units of selection debate. One refrain should be the gene's-eye view — genes are the replicators that persist across generations, and bodies are just vehicles they build — and the other refrain should be the modern multilevel selectionist's answer — selection can act on groups too, when groups differ heritably in ways that make some groups persist or reproduce better than others. Make sure the group-selection refrain isn't the old naive for-the-good-of-the-species line, but the careful version where within-group selection usually beats between-group selection and altruism only wins when between-group differences are strong enough. The form should do the actual argumentative work: the two refrains keep returning and passing each other the way the two levels of description keep talking past each other, and in the final couplet they should finally sit side by side to make the point that gene-level bookkeeping and group-level selection arent really rival claims — they're the same evolutionary process partitioned at different levels. A philosopher of biology should be able to tell which line is which and shouldn't wince; someone who has never heard of the debate should just think it's a decent poem.
