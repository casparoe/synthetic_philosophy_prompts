I have recently begun volunteering at a rescue group that keeps horses and dogs, and I keep catching myself describing the animals sometimes as if they were reasoning about what I am doing and sometimes as if they were just associating cues with food. The topic has become more than academic for me, and I want a careful piece of analysis rather than a survey. Specifically, I want you to analyze the Clever Hans affair through the lens of C. Lloyd Morgan's canon and the passage from his work that I describe below.

In that passage, from An Introduction to Comparative Psychology (1894), Morgan considers a dog that has learned to open a gate by lifting a latch. His point, as I read it, is that this performance does not by itself justify attributing to the dog an understanding of the latch mechanism, because the same result can be explained by the gradual association of a particular bodily movement with the disappearance of the obstacle. He presents this as an application of his general principle that an action should not be attributed to a higher psychical faculty when a lower one would account for it.

Now take the Clever Hans episode. The salient facts, as I understand them: around the turn of the century, Wilhelm von Osten's horse Hans appeared to answer arithmetic problems and other questions by tapping a hoof. Oskar Pfungst investigated and concluded that Hans was not doing arithmetic but was responding to involuntary cues, especially slight movements of the questioner's head or posture; when the horse could not see the questioner, or the questioner did not know the answer, the performance collapsed.

Analyze this episode through the passage and the canon. I want the mapping explicit: which candidate explanations of Hans's behavior count as higher psychical faculties by Morgan's standard, which count as lower, and what the Pfungst explanation does to the distinction. Then turn the lens back on the theory. Does Clever Hans show the canon working well, or does it reveal a problem in the canon's instruction to prefer the lower explanation whenever one can be found? In particular, consider whether the canon would have led researchers to underestimate Hans's sensitivity to fine human movements because such sensitivity looks like a non-cognitive, associative capacity, and whether that itself is a form of under-describing animal minds. Reach a definite conclusion about whether Morgan's canon, as expressed in the passage, is still a sound rule for deciding when to attribute cognition to animals, or whether the Hans case exposes a flaw serious enough that the principle should be qualified or abandoned.
