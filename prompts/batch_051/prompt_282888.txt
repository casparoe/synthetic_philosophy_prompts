I serve on my town's school naming committee, and the leading proposal before Thursday's meeting is to name the new middle school after Harry S. Truman. The case for Truman seems strong on the surface: he made hard wartime decisions, ended the war, and later took real political risks to integrate the armed forces. But one committee member, a retired nurse with no formal training in philosophy, has put forward an objection I cannot dismiss. Her view is that Truman ordered the bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki knowing the bombs would kill enormous numbers of civilians, and that no future benefit can turn the deliberate killing of noncombatants into something a community should honor on a school building. She says a school name functions as a standing moral recommendation to children, and that recommending Truman means teaching them that innocent people may be used as a means so that others might live. She also rejects the usual defense: she says if we would refuse to name a school after a general who ordered a city of civilians burned with ordinary bombs, the fact that Truman used a new and more terrible weapon should not change the analysis, and the claim that the bombings saved more lives than they took treats a prohibited act as if it could be made clean by good results. Another member counters that the committee is not a war-crimes tribunal, that Truman faced a choice between the bomb and an invasion that might have cost far more lives on both sides, and that a person's whole record, not his worst day, is what we honor. A third member wants to table the matter for a public hearing.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend one concrete course of action: name the school after Truman; remove Truman from consideration and choose from the district's backup list, which includes a local conservationist and a former teacher; or hold a public hearing before deciding. The opinion should justify that recommendation by engaging seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side. I am not asking the committee to relitigate the military decision as a historian would; I am asking what the act of naming commits us to. The question I most want the opinion to address, in plain language, is what an act of public honor actually does: whether there is conduct serious enough that a community should withhold a name regardless of a person's overall record, and whether the distinction between aiming at civilian deaths and accepting them as a foreseen side effect should carry the weight the objector places on it.
