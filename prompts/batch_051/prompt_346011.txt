I'm writing on a dispute about what Dutch book arguments actually establish. The classic result is this: if an agent's degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, then a static Dutch book can be made against her — a set of bets, each fair or favorable by the lights of the single set of prices she states at one time, whose combined payoff is negative no matter how the world turns out. The standard reading takes this to show that the agent's belief state is defective: she is probabilistically incoherent. A colleague resists the inference. On his view, being Dutch-bookable may not reflect a defect in the agent's credences at all. It may instead reflect the way we elicited them: forcing her to state a single fair price for every gamble can manufacture apparent incoherence out of imprecise credences, out of state-dependent utility (she values money differently depending on which state of the world obtains), or out of preferences that are not representable by a single real-valued utility function in the first place.

The claim under dispute is this: susceptibility to a Dutch book is evidence that an agent's credences are genuinely incoherent, rather than evidence that a particular betting-based elicitation procedure has extracted a misleading artifact.

I would like you to tell me how one could bring evidence to bear on that claim. In particular: (1) set out the most informative test you can design, concretely enough that a researcher could run it, specifying what you would measure, under what conditions, and with what controls; (2) state in advance what pattern of results would count for the standard view and what pattern would count for my colleague's view, such that the two outcomes can genuinely be told apart; and (3) identify what no test could ever settle about the claim, even in principle, and why that residue is not merely a practical limitation of current methods.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. Do not give a preamble or restate the question.
