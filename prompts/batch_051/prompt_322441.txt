I've been going down a rabbit hole on self-sufficiency lately, partly because a bunch of my friends are either starting homesteads, learning to can vegetables, or deep into "buy nothing" communities, and partly because I'm teaching a class where we touched on national autarky. Here's what I can't shake. If a person tries to become self-sufficient — grows their own food, keeps a few animals, learns to fix things, stops depending on shops and employers — we tend to call it admirable: self-reliant, resilient, grounded. Some people roll their eyes if it goes too far, but the baseline reaction is respect. If a nation-state tries the same thing, though — food and energy autarky, import substitution, closed borders, making sure it doesnt depend on other countries for anything essential — most of us, including my students, call it economically ruinous, paranoid, even a warning sign. The word "autarky" basically functions as an insult. Same aspiration, two very different judgments. The obvious move is that persons and states are just different kinds of things, so of course the standard is different. But when I push on it, I'm not sure what's doing the work: we also think a person who can't accept help is emotionally stunted, and we think a nation embedded in alliances and trade networks is normal and healthy. So the asymmetry cuts both directions, and I keep finding autarkeia in the old texts being run together — the self-sufficient person and the self-sufficient polis — before someone split them apart.

I want the strongest principle you can find that would generate both of our reactions: the respect for the self-sufficient homesteader and the suspicion of the autarkic state. If you think no single principle can do it and it's mostly a psychological or tribal thing we've papered over, say that straight. If you do find a principle, test it against some nearby cases: a person who refuses care when they're sick because they want to stay self-sufficient, a family that homeschools and opts out of local institutions, a city trying to make itself self-sufficient in food, and a country that makes its own weapons but imports its food. Tell me where the principle holds, where it breaks, and whether you'd keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up.

Also, please answer in American English.
