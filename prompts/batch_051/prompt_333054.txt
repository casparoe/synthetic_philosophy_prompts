I'm putting together notes on imaginative resistance—the puzzle that people will happily go along with a story full of backwards causation, talking donkeys, or whatever wild descriptive claim you like, but suddenly refuse to accept it when the story says something morally loaded, like a character did the right thing in doing something hideous. The stock examples usually involve a woman killing her baby for being a girl, which I find too grim to use in a talk, so I've been switching in a milder case: a story says a man did the right thing when he cheated his own brother out of an inheritance, and we're asked to imagine that as fictionally true.

On my read, the explanations I've run across for why the resistence happens split into four families. (1) The 'can't' view: there's something about the concepts right/ought that makes it impossible for us to imagine the scenario under that description, the way we can't imagine a square circle. (2) The 'won't' or export view: we could imagine it, but we refuse, because the fiction is trying to change our actual moral outlook and we don't want to let a story have that kind of grip on us. (3) The proximity view: resistance tracks how close the fictional evaluation is to one we actually hold—we can go along with an alien honor code or a distant society's inverted values, but not with an inversion of something we really endorse. (4) The authority/genre view: in descriptive passages we let the narrator stipulate what's true in the fiction, but in evaluative passages we slip into reading the sentence as just the narrator's view, so the moral claim never becomes fictionally true. I need one more proposed explanation that is genuinely different from those four—not a restatement, special case, or combination—and a sentence or two on which of the five (including yours) handles the milder inheritance case best, and whether switching from the baby case to the inheritance case changes anything the standard accounts rely on.
