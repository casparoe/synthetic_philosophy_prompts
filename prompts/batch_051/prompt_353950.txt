Suppose we adopt the following working definition of an antimeme. An antimeme is an item of information that (i) is intelligible and, once grasped, is treated by its holders as practically significant; (ii) is self-suppressing in transmission — there is a systematic mechanism by which holding or passing on the item tends to reduce its future circulation; and (iii) is self-suppressing in virtue of its content, rather than merely in virtue of external incentives such as legal penalty. Consider, as a borderline case, a password. A password is an arbitrary string whose entire function is to be known only to an authorized party and to be useless if widely known. Anyone who holds a password has a standing practical reason not to repeat it; each act of transmission either destroys the item's function or triggers its own invalidation, as when a breached password is changed. And yet the string itself is semantically inert: its resistance to circulation appears to come entirely from the role we assign it, not from anything intrinsic to the sequence of characters. Does a password qualify as an antimeme under the stipulated definition? Work through the criteria one by one. Where the answer is no or indeterminate, say whether this is because of the criteria, because of the case, or because the third criterion presupposes a clean distinction between content and functional role that the case dissolves. Finally, say whether the case shows that the definition needs revision, and if so, how you would revise it.
