So this is a bit embarrassing, but I've ended up commited to giving a paper at my department's ethics workshop next month, and the only reason I have a paper at all is that my best friend stopped speaking to me for a week after I kept her waiting — again — for lunch. I'm one of those people who is always ten or fifteen minutes late, never on purpose, always with a reason, and I'd always filed it under minor character flaw, like chewing too loud. She said something that stuck: that when I show up late she has to decide whether to wait or start without me, and either way I've made her spend her afternoon on my schedule without asking. So I started reading about promising, and now the talk is roughly this: being late isn't just etiquette or a coordination failure; it's the breach of a small promise. When you say "I'll be there at three," you're not making a prediction about your arrival, you're giving an assurance that lets the other person stop considering their other options and make plans that depend on you. The wrong of lateness is only partly the minutes lost; its mainly that the other person has already made choices on the strength of your word, and your lateness retroactively turns those choices into worse ones. Chronic lateness, then, is a standing disposition to spend other people's planning for them, one small breach at a time, and that's weightier than philosophers usually treat it.

The audience is what's scaring me. The workshop is mostly people working on promising and relational normativity — the sort who think wronging someone is about failing to be able to justify your conduct to them, not just about causing a bad outcome. They'll be friendly to the assurance framing but probably hostile to the idea that a bare appointment rises to the level of a promise. There's also one act-consequentialist who I know is going to ask what the actual harm of fifteen minutes is, and a Kantian who's going to push on whether punctuality is a perfect or imperfect duty and whether any particular lateness can wrong a particular person. So: prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way this crowd would phrase them, each with a short reply and a note on where that reply is weakest, plus what I should concede up front and the one objection I'm probably not seeing that I should be most scared of.
