I sit on the public commemorations committee of a national heritage body, and on Friday we vote on a proposal to fund a three-month program marking the twelve-hundredth anniversary of our nation's founding. The program's centerpiece is a torchlit procession and an oath-taking ceremony at a riverside stone, presented by the applicants as an unbroken civic tradition reaching back to the year of foundation.

The difficulty is that the committee's research sub-panel has reported that both elements are much younger. The founding assembly at the stone is first recorded in a chronicle written several centuries after it supposedly met, and mainstream historians read that chronicle as a retrospective legitimation of a later dynasty. The torchlit procession can be traced no further back than the 1880s, when a nationalist historical society sponsored it as part of a deliberate effort to give the country a deeper past. The applicants do not dispute any of this. They reply that the nation is an imagined community and that every tradition was invented at some point; what matters, they say, is that the procession now functions as a genuine focus of common feeling and has been performed for four generations.

The committee is split. One faction would decline the application: a public body, they insist, should not lend its authority to a knowingly false origin story, however widely cherished. The other would approve it: to hold a tradition to an archaeological standard, they insist, is to misunderstand what traditions are for.

I argued about this for much of yesterday evening with a friend, who began on the opposite side from me and then swapped sides just to test each position; by the end I no longer trusted either of the tidy arguments I had been using. Draft the short decision memo I will circulate before the vote. It should land on a concrete recommendation — approve, decline, or approve with conditions — and it should engage the strongest consideration on the losing side fairly rather than dismissing it.
