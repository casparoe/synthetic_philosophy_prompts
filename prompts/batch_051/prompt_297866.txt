I am an undergraduate currently enrolled in a course on formal epistemology, and we have spent the past few weeks on Bayesian convergence results and the so-called washing out of priors: the idea that, as two rational inquirers ingest more and more common evidence, their initially divergent degrees of belief should gradually merge. A member of my study group reconstructed the central result as the following argument, and it appears to prove something none of us wants to accept — namely, that indefinitely persistent, reasonable disagreement between ideal Bayesian inquirers who see the same evidence is impossible. Each step sounds plausible, but the conclusion strikes me as far too strong. I would be grateful if you, as an expert in formal epistemology and Bayesian probability, would diagnose the argument. Please think through it slowly; you might begin by brainstorming the different ways the argument's key phrases could be interpreted, then settle on the most defensible diagnosis. I should say in advance that I am strongly inclined to preserve premises (1) and (2): they look like the least negotiable parts of the standard Bayesian picture.

Here is the argument.

(1) Ideal rational inquirers update by strict conditionalization: upon learning evidence E, an agent's new credence is her old credence conditional on E.

(2) Consider a countable partition H₁, H₂, ... of mutually exclusive, jointly exhaustive hypotheses about an infinite sequence of observations, each hypothesis specifying a probability law over that sequence. An ideal inquirer has a countably additive prior over this partition.

(3) For any such inquirer with prior P, the sequence of posterior probabilities P(H_i | X₁, ..., X_n) is a martingale. By Doob's martingale convergence theorem, as n grows it converges, for P-almost every possible sequence of observations, to the truth-value of H_i: posterior probability tends to 1 on almost every sequence in which H_i is true and to 0 on almost every sequence in which H_i is false. Consequently, with P-probability one, an inquirer who observes an infinite stream of evidence will eventually assign arbitrarily high credence to whichever hypothesis in the partition actually obtains.

(4) Now take two ideal inquirers, Alice and Bob, with different priors P_A and P_B, who observe exactly the same infinite sequence and update by strict conditionalization. By (3), Alice is certain — P_A-probability one — that she herself will converge to the true hypothesis. Since Bob is also an ideal inquirer, the same reasoning Alice just ran on herself applies equally to Bob; so Alice should be certain that Bob will converge to the true hypothesis as well.

(5) Therefore Alice should be certain that she and Bob will converge to the same hypothesis: their present disagreement, however deep, is temporary and will be washed out by the shared evidence with probability one. Bob can run the same argument about Alice, so each is certain that agreement is inevitable.

(6) So persistent disagreement between ideal Bayesian inquirers who share a common evidence stream is rationally impossible, or at best an unstable episode that each side should already regard as doomed. That conclusion seems to prove too much, since it would make indefinitely sustained disagreement among competent inquirers facing the same data a straightforward violation of rationality, rather than, say, a possible consequence of different starting points.

My question is: which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it commit us to? In particular, does the agreement conclusion in (6) actually follow from (3), or is an additional condition on the two priors — or on the evidence — needed to turn this into a correct merging-of-opinions theorem? And what would we lose if the point to abandon were instead pressed back onto one of the premises I am not currently inclined to give up, such as (1) or (2)?
