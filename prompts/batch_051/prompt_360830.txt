Last night I spent most of an evening arguing with a close friend about whether his habitual lateness to casual social plans is a moral failing or merely a difference in how we value time. The argument kept stalling on a more basic question, and I would like it settled before we take the matter up again: what exactly counts as a small promise, and what does it mean to break one? Give me a working definition of 'a small promise' — or, if you prefer, of the wrong involved in breaking a small promise — that correctly classifies the following cases.

1. A friend says 'see you at six' for dinner, knows the route well, but begins a small household task twenty minutes before leaving and arrives at six-forty. (I count this as a broken small promise.)
2. At the end of a phone call, a friend says 'I'll send you that article later.' Nothing depended on it, and he forgets; you only remember three days later. (No settled view.)
3. A neighbor agrees to water your three houseplants while you are away for a weekend; he waters two and the third dies. (Clearly a broken promise, though a small one.)
4. A colleague says 'I'll have the file to you by five.' At four-thirty her child is injured at school and she leaves immediately; the file does not arrive. (No settled view.)
5. After a long talk, you tell a new acquaintance 'we should have lunch sometime' and mean it vaguely; neither of you ever follows up. (I do not think this is a promise at all.)
6. A friend RSVPs 'yes' to a small backyard barbecue with twelve people, then simply does not show up and sends no word. (Clearly a broken small promise.)
7. A person is dependably ten or fifteen minutes late to every social plan; her friends have started telling her an earlier time to compensate. She was late again to dinner on Thursday. (I cannot decide whether each lateness breaks a promise, or whether this is a different kind of failure.)
8. A friend says 'I'll try to stop by' the open house; he does not come. (No settled view; possibly not a promise.)

I have marked the cases where I have a firm view; I have no settled view about several of the others. My suspicion is that ordinary talk about small promises is doing two jobs at once: one about voluntarily binding oneself to another person, and one about being a predictable, considerate presence in other people's lives — and that our argument kept sliding between these two. If you agree, give me both definitions and say which cases move between them. If you think a single definition does all the work, defend it.

Run your proposed definition through every case, say plainly where it gives what I would consider the wrong answer or no answer at all, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdict. Finally, tell me which of your classifications is least secure.
