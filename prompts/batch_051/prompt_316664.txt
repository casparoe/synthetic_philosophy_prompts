I have a distinction I keep invoking in debates about higher-order evidence, and I want it stress-tested rather than just praised as interesting. I call it the two-ledger rule. One ledger records evidence about the subject matter itself; the other records evidence about my own competence, calibration, and reliability as an inquirer. My view is that the second ledger may change my epistemic practices — it may tell me to recheck the work, collect more direct evidence, or hold my future unaided judgments at a discount — but it may not directly move my present credence about the proposition. To lower the credence itself on the strength of a fact about myself is to enter the same item in both ledgers, as if one had learned something about the world by reading one's own error bar. On this view, the drunken mathematician who has carefully proved a theorem should not lower his confidence merely because he is drunk; he should re-prove the theorem in the morning, or stop relying on the proof in the meantime. I realize this runs against much of what is said about peer disagreement and about being drunk or sleep-deprived. I want a candid assessment: is this distinction already in the literature under a name I should know, does the rule collapse once the permitted practices are spelled out, and what is the strongest case in which I am forced to move the credence itself rather than merely change my practices?
