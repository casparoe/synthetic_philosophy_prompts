I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am working on a piece about the ethics of family obligations in an aging society. In the course of my reporting I have been drawn repeatedly to Jane English's friendship model of filial duty: the view that adult children owe their aging parents no special debt merely because they were raised, and that whatever children do owe tracks the actual, ongoing relationship between parent and child — roughly, what two friends would owe each other, with no extra layer of obligation added by biology or history. The model's plausibility seems to depend on a certain picture of old age: the parent remains an independent adult, dependency is a comparatively brief or limited phase, and the relationship can continue to be reciprocal in the way that friendships require. It is not clear that this picture still holds. In many countries, the years after sixty-five have become a long phase of life, a substantial portion of which is lived with dementia, frailty, or other conditions that produce years of one-sided, physically intimate, and financially crushing care. Adult children are often hundreds of miles away, employed, or themselves approaching retirement; the parent may no longer recognize them, cannot reciprocate, and may not have been a warm or reliable parent in the first place.

I am not asking about policy — not about who should pay for care or how institutions should be arranged — but about the fate of the argument itself. Does the friendship model survive this shift in the structure of old age, or did the argument quietly rest on a premise that the demographic transition has removed? If the model can be repaired, I want to know which premise gets restated: is the notion of friendship being stretched to include a one-sided final chapter that ordinary friendships would not require, or is a separate, non-friendship duty being smuggled in? If the argument is dead rather than repairable, I want to know what killed it — the sheer size of the burdens, the collapse of reciprocity, the erosion of the person to whom the obligation was supposedly owed, or something else. And I want a principled way to decide whether we should revise our moral theory with the demographics or hold the theory fixed and let our view of the cases change with it.
