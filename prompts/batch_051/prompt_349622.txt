Here is an argument I have reconstructed from Robert Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain example and the entitlement theory of justice behind it. I have paraphrased rather than quoted Nozick. Each premise seems plausible to me in isolation, but the conclusion seems unacceptable, and I cannot locate the precise step at which the argument goes wrong.

(1) A distribution of holdings is just if and only if it arose by a chain of just original acquisitions and just voluntary transfers, together with whatever rectification is owed for past violations of those two kinds of justice.
(2) Suppose the existing distribution, D1, is just in this sense.
(3) Suppose further that a million spectators each voluntarily pay Wilt Chamberlain twenty-five cents to watch him play, that the payments are made without fraud or coercion, and that no third party's entitlements are affected.
(4) Since D2 arises from a just distribution by a sequence of just transfers, D2 — in which Chamberlain is two hundred and fifty thousand dollars richer and each spectator twenty-five cents poorer — is also just, regardless of its overall shape.
(5) Any patterned principle of distributive justice — equal shares, a guaranteed minimum, distribution according to need, or any other end-state principle — would judge that D2 ought to be altered so that some of Chamberlain's earnings are transferred to people who never paid for a ticket.
(6) Bringing about any such alteration would require taking some of Chamberlain's earnings without his consent.
(7) Taking holdings from a person without consent is unjust when those holdings were justly acquired.
(8) Therefore, enforcing any patterned principle of distributive justice requires continuous injustice; and a society whose holdings are just by the entitlement theory may leave people destitute without any injustice having occurred.

I find (8) very difficult to accept. The difficulty is not merely humanitarian. If (7) is read strictly, the same reasoning seems to condemn the taxation that funds courts, police, and record-keeping, without which the transfers in step (3) could not reliably be voluntary at all. So the argument appears to prove too much: it either makes the practice of voluntary exchange depend on an institutional background that its own principle forbids financing, or it forces me to draw a line I cannot yet justify. Which premise or step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it commit one to? I would most like to keep the description of the Chamberlain transfer in (3) and (4), and I am least attached to (7), but I want to know whether retaining the former and abandoning the latter is principled or merely a way to keep my preferred conclusion. A good answer will name the step, explain why it looked unassailable, and price the alternatives, rather than survey the secondary literature.
