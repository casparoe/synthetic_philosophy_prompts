I have been trying to settle how much authority a person's earlier self should have once that person can no longer make their own medical decisions. I have firm reactions to particular cases, but no general statement of the principle behind them, and I would like one. My rough rule has been "prefer the most recent competent expression of the person's wishes," but I can see that it fails me in some of these cases, so I need something better.

Here are the cases and how I judge them.

1. A man with a history of severe manic episodes writes, while stable, that if he becomes manic again and demands to stop treatment or leave the hospital, he is to be held and treated anyway. During his next episode he declares with full conviction that this is his real wish and that the written statement was a mistake. I judge that the hospital should follow the written statement.

2. A woman who has long feared dementia signs a directive saying that, once she can no longer recognize her family, she refuses antibiotics for any life-threatening infection. Years later she has moderate dementia and no longer recognizes her family, but she is calm, sociable, and clearly takes pleasure in her days. She develops pneumonia, and antibiotics would return her to just that same state. I judge that she should be given antibiotics, the directive notwithstanding.

3. A different woman has signed the same kind of directive. She now has late-stage dementia and is frequently frightened, crying out, and resisting care, with little that seems to comfort her. She develops pneumonia. I judge that the directive should be followed and life-prolonging treatment withheld.

4. A man signs a written order refusing resuscitation while seriously ill. A year later, while fully competent, he tells his doctor and his family that he has changed his mind, though he has not yet gotten around to replacing the paperwork. He then has a cardiac arrest. I judge that the later change of mind should be respected over the stale document.

5. A man with no psychiatric history is brought to an emergency room confused after a head injury. He says he wants no treatment and wants to be left to die, even though his family, who know him well, report that he had always said he would want everything done in such a situation, and they are certain this is the injury speaking rather than a real change of mind. I judge that he should be treated.

Across these, I can feel a pattern but I cannot state it. In cases 1 and 5 the earlier self wins; in cases 2 and 4 the later self wins; and in cases 2 and 3, involving the same kind of directive, I come out on opposite sides. What principle, if any, captures all five judgments? If no single principle can capture them without becoming contrived, which of my judgments should I abandon first? And what does the best available principle say about this further case: a woman in early dementia writes a directive refusing a feeding tube if she becomes so impaired that she can no longer enjoy anything; years later, in just that condition, she has brief lucid moments in which she says she wants to keep living, though she is not competent enough to make the decision. Should the feeding tube be withheld?
