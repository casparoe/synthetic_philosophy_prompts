I have been working on a concept I call "valor displacement," and I want an honest assessment of whether it is coherent or just an overextended metaphor. It grew out of this passage from Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book III:

"Now the noblest form of death is death in battle, for it is encountered in the midst of the greatest and most noble of dangers. And this conclusion is borne out by the principle on which public honors are bestowed in republics and under monarchies."

My theory is that Aristotle here is doing more than defining a virtue; he is identifying a social point at which courage becomes publicly legible. In his account, courage in the proper sense has its natural home in facing a noble death in battle, and public honor tracks that. A peaceful society, then, does not stop needing that kind of valor; it loses the standard occasion for it. The need gets displaced onto substitute arenas — firefighting, search-and-rescue, high-stakes whistleblowing, dangerous political protest, maybe some forms of sport or financial risk — but these substitutes look structurally unable to meet the original conditions. They are either too safe to be genuine near-death courage, or their deaths are more like the drowning or disease deaths Aristotle explicitly excludes, or they lack the civic frame that makes the death noble rather than merely accidental.

The result is what I call a civic valor deficit: a peacetime polity still needs a publicly honored, non-accidental, death-adjacent venue for courage, but it cannot supply one without either militarizing its culture or manufacturing risk. I think this explains the recurring drift of peaceful societies toward military nostalgia, paramilitary aesthetics, moralized risk-taking, and adventure as substitute combat.

Tell me: is this concept already worked out under another name? Where does it break? In particular, does Aristotle's own taxonomy actually imply that peacetime courage is always analogical rather than proper courage, or is there a reading of the passage on which civilian substitutes can satisfy the criterion? Give me the three strongest objections, and say whether any is fatal rather than just uncomfortable.
