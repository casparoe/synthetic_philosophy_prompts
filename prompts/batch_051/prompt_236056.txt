I'm a junior in high school, and I run this small philosophy club where we've been arguing for a month about whether the felt quality of being conscious — like the redness of seeing red, or the ache of a headache — is a real thing over and above brain processes, or more like a trick the brain plays on itself. One side keeps defending what I've seen called illusionism: the view that phenomenal consciousness, the what-it's-like stuff, is an illusion. The other side I'll call phenomenal realists: they think that felt character is real and can't be explained away or reduced to function. We mostly just talk past each other, so we've decided to try an adversarial collaboration, the kind where two opposing camps write one document they can both sign.

Could you draft the document they'd produce? I want it in three parts. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can agree on, and please keep them substantive — not just "humans have brains" — things that actually constrain the debate. Second, a disagreement table where each row states the exact point of disagreement in neutral terms, then gives each side's own wording in a way that side would actually accept. Third, a section on cruxes: the sub-questions that, if answered, would genuinely move at least one side, and for each crux a concrete experiment or case both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. A friend who reads more philosophy than me says the real cruxes might be the meta-problem of consciousness (why we judge and report that we have phenomenal states in the first place), whether there's phenomenal overflow — conscious stuff you can't report or attend to — and whether illusionism is automatically self-refuting because even having the false judgement requires something it's like. I don't fully understand those, so use them only if they actually fit. And if part of the dispute seems seperate from the real disagreement and is just about words, say which part and why.
