My friend Chris, who does data work at a health nonprofit, got on a soapbox the other night about the replication crisis, and I jotted down his account when I got home. As best I could reconstruct it, he said this: the real problem is that scientists read p < 0.05 as meaning there's only a 5% chance the result is a fluke. But a p-value is the probability of getting data at least that extreme assuming the null is true, and what we actually want is the probability that the hypothesis is true given the data. Frequentists can't give you that; Bayesians can, so the fix is to stop obsessing over significance and just report posterior probabilities. The reason so many findings don't replicate, he said, is mostly the file drawer: journals only publish p < 0.05, so published effect sizes are systematically inflated, and when later studies are bigger and cleaner the effects shrink or vanish. His kicker was that if the whole field went Bayesian, the replication crisis would basically be over, because a Bayesian analysis already tells you how much to believe the hypothesis, and a failed replication wouldn't be embarrassing — you'd just update your posterior and move on.

I followed the p-value part fine, but something about the kicker feels too neat, and I came away more confused then when we started. If the prior is bad or the model is misspecified, isn't a Bayesian posterior just as capable of misleading you as a misread p-value? And how does switching to Bayes stop journals from only publishing flashy results? Which parts of his story are fair, which are subtly wrong or overstated, and does his conclusion actually follow from the accurate parts?
