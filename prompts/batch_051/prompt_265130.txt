I am a novelist, and there is a dispute I keep entering — in friendships, in essays, in rooms full of writers — that never seems to move. It concerns whether we owe one another legibility. One side treats sincerity and transparency as duties of respect: to remain opaque to another person is to force them to guess at one's motives, to offload the interpretive labor onto them, and thereby to treat them as less than an equal. The other side treats opacity as a right, perhaps the deepest one, because to be legible is to be available to prediction and management, and the demand that a person make themselves legible is a form of coercion that gets mistaken for intimacy. What unsettles me is how rarely the two sides disagree about any particular case. Both will allow that withholding one's reasons can be self-protection or manipulation. Both will allow that self-disclosure can be generosity or control. The argument nevertheless goes in a circle.

The dispute shows up in my work in a different costume. The novel has long argued about how much of a character's inner life should be rendered on the page. The confessional mode assumes a person becomes more themselves the more fully they can be narrated; the reticent mode assumes a person's reality lies in what is held back, and that a narrator who explains every motive has already committed a small tyranny over the character. I have come to suspect these are the same stalled argument, and that underneath both is a disagreement about what makes an action or a life one's own: either it is mine insofar as I can account for it and make myself intelligible, or it is mine insofar as my reasons remain mine, not fully surrendered to anyone's understanding.

What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on it. Is the conflict substantive, or is it verbal, with 'legibility' meaning different things — predictability, accountability, self-narratability — in different hands? Is a hidden empirical claim doing the work, about what people actually do with knowledge about one another? Or is the question itself ill-posed? And, given whatever diagnosis is right, say what would count as progress: a distinction, a case, a change of question — or whether I should expect the stalemate to persist.
