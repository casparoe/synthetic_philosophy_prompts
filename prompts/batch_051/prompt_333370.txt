I'm preparing for a graduate seminar on the gender-equality paradox: the reported cross-national finding that countries scoring highest on measures of gender equality — the Nordic countries being the standard example — often show larger, not smaller, sex differences in fields of study and occupational preferences. In last week's session, a classmate invoked this finding to dismiss a proposed quota for engineering admissions. As he put it, the Scandinavian data show that where women and men face the fewest external obstacles to choosing freely, their career choices diverge more, not less; that must mean the divergence reflects underlying biological dispositions rather than social pressure, and so 'biology is destiny' — policies aimed at equal representation are trying to legislate against nature.

I don't trust the move from the data to that conclusion, but I'm not sure exactly where it missteps, partly because 'biology is destiny' seems to blur several different claims at once. I'd like you to lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that slogan could be used to express — about what is innate, what is unchangeable, what is biologically caused, and what is thereby justified or inevitable — and for each say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. Then tell me which of those readings my classmate's argument actually needs in order to go through, and whether the cross-national pattern he cites supplies evidence for that reading or only for something weaker. One thing worth folding in: the paradox has a standard rival explanation, namely that where material security and genuine freedom of choice are higher, pre-existing differences in interests simply get to express themselves more. I cannot tell whether that story is genuinely distinct from the 'biology is destiny' line or is just a more polite way of asserting the same thing, and I'd like that sorted out as well.
