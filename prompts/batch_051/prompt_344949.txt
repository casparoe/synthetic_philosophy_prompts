Karl Popper's argument against the possibility of scientific laws of history can be reconstructed as follows. Any would-be law of historical development would have to predict large-scale features of the human future. Those features are decisively shaped by the future growth of knowledge. But the growth of knowledge cannot itself be scientifically predicted: a prediction made today of what we will discover tomorrow would amount to already possessing that knowledge today. Therefore the course of history is not the kind of thing that can be predicted, and historicism—the project of discovering laws of historical development as a basis for long-range prediction—rests on a mistake.

In the decades since Popper advanced this argument, a different style of program has emerged: treating the growth of knowledge as an aggregate phenomenon that can be modeled and forecast without specifying the content of any particular future discovery. Researchers and institutions now attempt to extrapolate trends in publication volume, scientific workforce, computational capacity, success rates on standardized benchmarks, and technological capability curves, and some of these trajectories are treated as robust regularities. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that such aggregate forecasting became genuinely reliable over multi-decade horizons—reliable enough to anticipate the rate and rough distribution of future knowledge growth, even though it cannot name the discoveries themselves.

Does Popper's argument survive this development? Identify which premise is actually under pressure: (a) the premise that the growth of knowledge cannot be predicted, (b) the premise that history is shaped by the growth of knowledge, or (c) only the inference from the unpredictability of the content of knowledge to the unpredictability of the course of history. Then give a verdict: is the anti-historicist argument dead in the face of aggregate forecasting, does it go through unchanged, or is a repaired and weaker version the right result? If a repaired version survives, state exactly what it still rules out and why that matters.
