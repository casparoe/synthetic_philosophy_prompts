I am a novelist. I am drafting a novel centred on a small, theologically serious Christian congregation that treats Scripture as authoritative. Three of the main characters quarrel, at first over whether the women of the congregation should resume covering their heads in worship, and then over the deeper question of which biblical commands bind today and which were intended only for their original setting. I want the three characters to hold distinct and internally rigorous positions rather than simplified or two-dimensional ones, and I want to see the whole field of live options so that I can write their arguments without caricature.

Please compose for my use a classification instrument — a decision tree, or a taxonomy with an explicit tree-like structure — mapping the chief positions on the question of which commands in Scripture are culturally limited to their original situation and which are transcultural, binding across situations. Build the instrument around the three recurring test cases that set my characters off: head coverings in worship, the commands addressed to slaves and slaveholders, and dietary law, together with the later biblical passages that appear to set dietary law aside.

Construct the instrument from the logical space rather than from a list of established labels. Begin with the questions or commitments that actually separate positions, locate positions as combinations of answers to those questions, and only then attach names. Each branch should be labelled with a concrete question or claim on which the positions genuinely diverge, phrased so that a person could answer it without already knowing the names of the schools. The kinds of question that may earn their place include whether a command outlives the disappearance of its original social institution, whether a later scriptural statement can revise an earlier command and how one tells, whether an explicit appeal to creation order makes a command permanent, and whether a present moral conviction against slavery should count as a datum or as a distortion. Use whichever splitting questions do the most work, and ensure that the three test cases actually exercise the distinctions rather than collapsing into a single question about change.

Each leaf should carry the position's name where one exists, or a descriptive label or "unnamed" where it does not, together with a single sentence on the commitment a defender of that leaf would find most awkward. Please note cells of the logical space that are empty, and say briefly whether each is empty because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. If two positions cannot be separated by the questions you have chosen, say so plainly rather than inventing a distinction.

Assume I am not a theologian but can handle precision and do not mind conceptual work; keep the instrument usable as a writer's reference while I draft dialogue. Aim for no more than ten leaves, and fewer if the logic requires fewer. Do not provide citations or a reading list, and please avoid quotations — what I need is the internal logic of the positions, not scholarly attribution. Please write in British English.
