I was playing with a model checker at work the other day and something has been nagging me about it. In Kripke semantics, the formalism underneath, there is no special actual world baked into the logic. You have a set of states W and an accessibility (or transition) relation R, and a formula is always evaluated at a state; whichever state you're at plays the role of the here-and-now for that evaluation. Validity is truth at every state in every model, so no state gets treated as the one real world. That instantly reminded me of David Lewis's modal realism: all possible worlds are equally real, and actual is an indexical — our world is just the one we happen to be in. A model checker even reports things the same way: it tells you the set of states where some property holds, and it doesn't care that we inhabit one of them.

My question is whether this parallel is deep or superficial. I can argue it both ways. For: both are saying actuality is a matter of where you evaluate from, not a metaphysical privilege. Against: Lewis's worlds are supposed to be concrete, maximal, spatiotemporally isolated universes, while Kripke states are abstract points in a mathematical structure; and Kripke frames have the R relation doing work that Lewis doesn't obviously need (or maybe he replaces it with counterpart theory or similarity orderings?). Also, in model checking we usually do privilege a set of initial states, which sounds un-Lewisian.

So which is it? If it's just a loose analogy, what exactly does Kripke semantics have — or lack — that makes the no-privileged-world point cheap there and radical in Lewis's metaphysics? And if it's deep, does that mean model checking is quietly committed to modal realism, or at least to an indexical view of actuality?

When you answer, try to seperate what's really settled here (e.g. how the formalism works) from what's genuinely contested among people who know the material (e.g. what Lewis's view commits you to), rather than presenting a contested reading as the consensus.
