I've been teaching Dennett's careenium thought experiment in my phil mind course for a few years now, and I've ended up with a small theory of my own that I'd like you to take apart honestly. I call it semantic traction. Here's the idea: in the careenium — a canister full of gyroscopes that spin up when they sense light, so the whole contraption lurches toward the nearest light source — we want to say the reason it moved is that it was trying to get more light, or that the content of the gyroscope states as light-detectors explains the motion. But no new physical force is involved: just gyroscopes, friction, momentum. Still, I think the semantic facts do something. They don't add forces, but they have a weak causal grip: they select, from the space of physically possible trajectories, the ones that are robust under certain counterfactual interventions. The symbol doesn't push the gyroscope the way a gear pushes another gear; rather, the symbol's being about light makes some physical paths stable and others not, the way a chess move's being a blunder makes it causally relevant to losing even though the blunder isn't a physical object.

My question is wether this is a real, non-epiphenemonal kind of causation, or whether I've just dressed up a description in causal clothing. I want a candid assessment: where does semantic traction collapse into standard positions like interventionist theories of causation or Kim-style causal exclusion worries, and where, if anywhere, does it actually earn its keep? Also, does the view survive the harder version of the careenium, where the parts get simpler and stupider until it's basically a wind sock? I worry I've built a theory that only works for medium-sized intentional systems, and that seems like a dodge.
