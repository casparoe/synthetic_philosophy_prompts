As a philosophy professor preparing a graduate seminar on aesthetic psychology, I have been struck by how confidently contemporary entertainment institutions treat the consumption of distressing fiction as an ordinary consumer preference. Streaming services market “emotional” films, review aggregators sort by “sad” mood, and audiences describe being devastated by a novel or film as a recommendation. At the same time, the same people who seek out tragedy will generally avoid real suffering whenever they can. I want to ask what this practice presupposes about the relation between pleasure and the negative emotions—not merely what it says about individual psychology, but what view of emotional experience the practice would have to be true for it to be coherent.

In his essay Of Tragedy, David Hume offers a diagnosis of this very practice. As I read him, Hume argues that spectators of a well-written tragedy receive pleasure from sorrow, terror, anxiety, and other passions that are in themselves disagreeable and uneasy, and he explains this by appealing to the eloquence, art, and imitation with which the passions are communicated, which transforms the original distress into something agreeable. I am not asking for an exegesis of Hume. Rather, I want to know which philosophical commitments the contemporary practice would presuppose if Hume is right, and which it would presuppose if rival explanations—purgation, conversion, meta-emotion, or something else—are right. What exactly does the practice of seeking out tragic fictions require us to believe about negative emotions? Does it require that the emotions felt in fictional engagement are not the same emotions as real sorrow and terror, or only that they are differently evaluated? And is the practice internally consistent with our institutions’ treatment of real grief as something to be managed and mitigated rather than curated?
