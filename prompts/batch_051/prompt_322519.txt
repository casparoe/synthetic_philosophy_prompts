Ok here's a thing I've never fully got. Van Fraassen's Reflection principle says, roughly, that my current credence in A, conditioned on the event that my future self assigns credence r to A, should itself be r (when the conditional is defined). Is that quite the right statement of it, or is there a subtly different standard version? And what exactly is the argument for it supposed to be—people wave at Dutch books, but I want to see where the guaranteed loss is supposed to come from and what premises it leans on. Now the bit I can't make cohere. Suppose I've just swallowed a pill that I have excellent reason to think makes people wildly overconfident about whatever proposition they're asked about ten minutes later. Doesn't Reflection then tell present-me, on learning that drugged-me is 90% sure it'll rain, to adopt 90%? That seems obviously wrong. So either the principle needs an implicit restriction (to future selves whose credences are formed by good reasoning), or it isn't a rational requirement in the unconditional way it's often stated. If you add the restriction, what counts as 'good reasoning' here, and does the principle then just become a dressed-up rewrite of 'trust good updaters'—in which case it looses its bite? I want the conceptual structure rather than references.
