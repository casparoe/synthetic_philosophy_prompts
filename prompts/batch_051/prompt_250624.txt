I'm on my university's academic integrity board, and we've got a case next week that's split the committee down the middle — it's the first one in two years that's made me lose sleep, partly because the student involved, Sam, took my intro course last year and I would of guessed he knew better. The chair keeps saying the question is whether Sam was complicit even though he didn't cause anything, and I think that's the whole fight.

Sam's a junior. All last semester he kept giving his housemate access to his completed problem sets and, near the end, to a detailed final-paper outline, even after he knew the housemate was copying from him. Sam never submitted anything copied himself and he never wrote a word of the housemate's paper — the housemate used the outline and two paragraphs lifted from old drafts Sam had left in a shared folder. The wrinkle is that the housemate has admitted that if Sam had cut him off, he would have just bought a paper online, so Sam's help probably made no causal difference to whether a plagiarized paper got handed in.

Half the board says: no causal difference, no finding — Sam didn't cause the misconduct and the wrong would have happened anyway. The other half says that's the wrong test entirely; knowingly facilitating wrongdoing is itself a violation of academic integrity even when the facilitator is replaceable. Our honor code has a facilitation clause, but it's vague about whether the facilitated misconduct has to actually depend on the facilitator's contribution.

I need to circulate a short decision memo before Thursdays meeting. It should recommend either a finding of responsibility or not, and if responsible, a sanction ranging from a formal warning to a one-semester suspension. It has to give the losing side's strongest argument real weight rather than strawmanning it, and end with a concrete motion we can actually vote on. Write it in a voice I could send to the committee.
