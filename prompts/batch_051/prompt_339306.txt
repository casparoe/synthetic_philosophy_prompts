Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether moral thought requires general principles. He maintained that there are no true moral principles: he produced cases in which the same feature counted in favor of an action in one context and against it in another, and every rule I proposed could be pushed into a counterexample by adding enough detail. I could not refute the examples, but I also could not accept that moral judgment is just a matter of seeing what each case requires, one case at a time. The position I ended up defending is one I will call procedural generalism.

Procedural generalism concedes that first-order principles, generalizations about lying, promise-keeping, harm, and the like, are never true without exception, and that adding an other-things-being-equal clause leaves them empty. But it denies that morality is unstructured all the way down. The real generalities, I proposed, are second-order: they do not state which features count as reasons, but how reasons behave, how they combine, cancel, and reverse valence across contexts. For example, whether the pain of a medical procedure counts for or against performing it is not fixed by the particular facts alone; I claimed it is governed by a second-order regularity about authority and consent. Such regularities would explain how moral judgment can be learned, taught, and criticized. Without them, the particularist seems stuck saying that competent judges simply see what each case requires, without being able to say what they see. My friend objected that this supposed second-order regularity is just a first-order principle about authority wearing a disguise, and I did not have a sharp reply.

I want an honest assessment of procedural generalism. Has something close to it already been defended under a standard label? What are the hardest objections to it, do any of them show that it collapses into plain particularism or plain generalism, and are its problems cracks that can be repaired or a failure at the foundation?
