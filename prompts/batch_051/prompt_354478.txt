I keep coming back to a weird tic in William James's essay The Dilemma of Determinism, and I want to know if there's a recognized category for it, because it's messing with my sense of how the whole free will dispute is even set up. The essay is from 1884, and here's the passage that bugs me. James writes:

"It is a well-known fact that the enthusiasm for pessimism is strongest where the circumstances are most favorable, and that the most robust optimism is often found in the persons who have experienced the greatest hardships."

Wait, that's not right, sorry. Let me try to remember the actual lines. The ones I mean are where he talks about the "antipathy to the idea of chance" and says something like: "The first thing which determinism does is to make us feel that the truth of the world is something which must be accepted, not something which can be altered." And then he goes on to say that this feeling is "the result of a confusion," but he also spends a huge portion of the essay treating that same feeling as a live, almost metaphysical datum — not just a mistake to be cleared away, but something that reveals what the debate is actually about.

So here's the phenomenon I'm after. A philosopher introduces a gut-level reaction or pre-philosophical feeling into an argument, declares that it is confused, and then continues to let that same feeling do real argumentative work, as if the refutation didn't stick to the feeling itself but only to a certain narrow formulation of it. It's not exactly a fallacy, or at least I don't have the name for it. It's like the thinker wants the emotion to be simultaneously inside and outside the argument: disowned enough to avoid the burden of defending it, present enough to move the reader.

Is there an established term for that move — where a dismissed intuition is kept on as a kind of unacknowledged engine? I don't just want a label; I want to know whether the nearest concepts (like "sneaking it back in" or "having your cake and eating it too") actually capture the structure here, or whether James is doing something more specific to late-nineteenth-century philosophy, maybe something about how "temperament" functions in his pragmatism. If no established term fits, what would you call it, and what would count as a clear case versus a merely rhetorical case?
