B. Quitting alcohol altogether after the episode. This felt like temperance in the proper sense, and I admire it in myself and in others.

C. Running through shin pain because I am anxious about gaining weight. I judge this as compulsion rather than self-control, even though it involves overriding a strong desire to stop.

D. Leaving my phone in another room during dinner with my daughter so that I will not look at it. This feels like a small virtue, partly because it is done for someone else's sake.

E. Installing a site blocker during my working hours. This feels like prudence, maybe even a substitute for virtue: I arranged the world so that I would not have to keep exercising self-control. But I cannot see anything wrong with it.

F. Counting calories every day. This began as disciplined clarity, and after six months it began to feel like an incipient obsession. The act is the same; my verdict has reversed; and I do not know which verdict is mature and which is denial.

G. Not losing my temper with a colleague who had publicly misrepresented my work, because I was afraid of the confrontation. At the time I called it self-control. In hindsight I suspect it was cowardice wearing self-control's clothing.

H. White-knuckling through nicotine cravings, as against avoiding the places and routines that trigger them. I admire the first more as an exercise of will, but the second works far better. I suspect my admiration is a moralistic mistake, yet it persists, and I want a principled way to say whether it should.
