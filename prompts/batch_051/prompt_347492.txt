My father died a couple of weeks ago, and the family asked me to give the eulogy at the funeral this month. He was a genuinely uneven person: he did some real good and some real damage, and the room will end up split between people who remember him as a saint and people he hurt who remember more of a bully. I dont want to paper over either half, and I don't want the speech to become a lecture. I've been reading Plutarch lately, and what's stuck with me is the way he treats biography as a kind of moral learning—not by holding up flawless heroes to copy, but by asking us to weigh whole lives, where a small revealing gesture can matter more than the big public deeds. I'd like the eulogy to work the way Plutarch does: it should neither collapse into hagiography nor reduce him to a cautionary tale. Write me a five-minute eulogy that lets both halves of the room recognize the man they knew without either side feeling lied to. Make the Plutarchan idea do the structural work of the speech, not just sit on top of it as decoration.
