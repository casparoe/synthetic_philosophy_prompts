Last spring I was appointed as the community (non-scientist) member of the institutional review board at our regional teaching hospital, and I have quickly discovered that the discussion I am least prepared for is the one that decides whether a randomized trial may proceed. The board reviews two or three clinical protocols a month, mostly in oncology and surgery, and each protocol gets about half an hour of deliberation before a vote. Members use the phrase clinical equipoise as if it settled the matter, but they plainly mean different things by it: sometimes that the investigators say they genuinely do not know which arm is better, sometimes that the data are not yet conclusive, and once, in a single meeting, both at once. We deadlock when one member says a trial comparing two accepted treatments is not ours to block, while another insists it is unethical to randomize a patient to an arm that most specialists would not choose for themselves. The stakes are real patients, institutional trust, and votes that are hard to reverse, and I want to stop being swayed by whoever argues last.

Please construct a reusable procedure, a checklist or decision tree expressed in words, that a non-specialist IRB member with limited time can apply to any proposed randomized comparison to judge whether it satisfies the standard research-ethics idea of clinical equipoise. The core idea I want preserved is this: the relevant question is not whether proof of a difference exists, and not whether any individual investigator is uncertain, but whether there is honest, professional disagreement within the relevant community of clinical experts about which arm is preferable. The procedure should translate that idea into concrete steps a lay member can run during protocol review, while being explicit at each step about what the member must supply by judgment rather than by following the checklist. It must handle at least these recurring cases: a trial against placebo or a sham procedure where no proven treatment exists; a trial of two widely used treatments where the evidence is thin and practice varies by region or specialty; a non-inferiority trial in which the new treatment may be slightly less effective but cheaper or less toxic; and a trial whose comparator is usual care at a single institution, where usual care may simply be that institution's habit. It should also distinguish the equipoise question from adjacent questions that members will be tempted to drag into it, such as whether the study is well designed, whether enrollment is fair, or whether the consent form is clear.

Because the board needs the same instrument every month, write this as a standing procedure rather than advice about one protocol. As part of the answer, work through two or three realistic protocols of the kinds listed above, and include at least one case in which the procedure yields an answer I will not personally like, for example obliging me to vote to approve a randomization I would otherwise have opposed, or to vote against one that looks harmless. At each step where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment, say what that judgment is, how a non-specialist can recognize that they are at such a step, and what the likely failure mode is if the step is skipped. Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle, including cases where the relevant expert community is itself disputed or too small to yield a meaningful consensus.
