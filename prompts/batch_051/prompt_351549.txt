I've been refining a concept I'd like an honest assessment of. I call it the "predation tax." In an ecosystem where predation holds prey numbers roughly stable, predators impose a standing flow of experiential costs on prey: the stress of the chase, the drawn-out death, the enormous fraction of juveniles killed before they do much of anything. Ecologists describe such systems as healthy because biomass, abundance, and growth rates are all in order, but those metrics are valence-blind; they register that life continues, not how much of that continuation is routed through fear and pain. I therefore want to say that an apparently flourishing ecosystem can be running a hidden welfare deficit, in the way an economy with solid GDP growth can be running down its soil or its trust. The tax is invisible because it is levied on experience, not on carbon. I need two things. First, an account of whether this is a real distinction or just a dressed-up version of the older point that wild nature is harsh — and whether the existing literature on wild animal suffering has already captured it under a better name. Second, a candid sketch of where the framing breaks. I can already count a few suspects: the idea seems to presuppose cross-species hedonic aggregation, it treats the predator's own welfare gain as somehow not part of the same transfer, and its counterfactual to predation may be starvation and boom-bust cycles rather than something pastoral. But I would like a systematic judgment about which failures are fatal and which are fixable.
