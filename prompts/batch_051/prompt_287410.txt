A course on political philosophy has made me suspicious that the textbook contrast between being left alone and being able to act is missing something, so I have been collecting my own untutored reactions to a set of concrete cases and trying to work out what freedom actually consists in for me. My state now fines motorcyclists who ride without helmets. I do not ride, but riders I know are indignant about it; even so, I cannot bring myself to regard the helmet fine as a serious loss of freedom, though I grant that the state is dictating what they may wear. A friend of mine works in a restaurant where the manager may legally dismiss anyone at any moment without stating a reason. The manager happens to be kind and has never done it, yet I think my friend's position is less free than it looks. A different friend was fired from a warehouse after missing a posted productivity quota for three consecutive months; the quota was clear, she had been warned, and a review procedure existed. Losing the job was a real blow, but I do not feel that it violated her freedom the way the restaurant arrangement does. On the political side, I voted against a zoning measure that passed by a wide margin and now I must live under it. I am annoyed, but I do not think my freedom was compromised, so long as I keep my vote and the process stays open. And I have a relative whose gambling is compulsive: no one forces him, and he says he wants to stop but cannot. There I do think a dimension of his freedom has been lost, even though no one is interfering with him.

What unsettles me is that these verdicts seem to invert the familiar idea that freedom is simply non-interference. I judge the restaurant worker and the gambler unfree without any actual interference, and I judge the warehouse worker and the losing voter free despite real coercion or constraint. Is there one principle of liberty, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? Please state the principle precisely, identify which case or clause motivates each part of it, and tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and whether I should overturn that verdict rather than complicate the principle. Then apply the resulting account to a case I still go back and forth on: a live-in domestic worker whose employer holds her passport and could make leaving very difficult, though she is paid well, treated kindly, and insists she has no wish to leave. Is she free, and does she have the right kind of grievance to call her condition a loss of freedom?
