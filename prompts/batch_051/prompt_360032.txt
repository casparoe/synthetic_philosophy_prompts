Our social app's content policy has this clause, and it predates all the recommendation machinery:

"Users may not manipulate others into believing or doing something by exploiting their emotions, cognitive biases, or personal vulnerabilities. Persuasion is allowed when it presents accurate information and explicit reasoned argument."

We've built a political-advocacy feature that does what I'll call sniper sequencing. Every message in a sequence is true, every inference is valid, and every factual claim links to a credible public source. The only machine-learned part is the packaging: the system decides whether a given argument appears first or fifth, whether the copy opens with a question or a flat statement, whether the image is a chart or a face, and whether the tone is warm or stern. It learns these choices from a large dataset of user behavior, including inferred mood and a proxy for how much the user enjoys effortful thinking. The result is that the same set of true, valid arguments can be arranged into a few hundred packages, and users who receive the package matched to their profile are substantially more likely to change their mind than users who see a randomly ordered package—even though no individual message is false, threatening, deceptive, or high-pressure.

Our team is split. The clause was written before per-user sequence modeling of this kind existed, and the drafters were mostly picturing fake scarcity, guilt-tripping, astroturfing, and dark-pattern checkout flows. I half suspect the answer turns on whether we count ordering and tone as "content" or "channel," so address that if you think it matters.
