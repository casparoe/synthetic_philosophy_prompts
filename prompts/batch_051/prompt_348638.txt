I’m a doctoral student in political theory working on nineteenth-century liberal imperialism. In a seminar I reconstructed an argument that is now making me uneasy because it seems to force a choice I did not intend. It runs as follows. (1) According to Mill’s harm principle, the only legitimate ground for exercising power over someone against his will is to prevent harm to others. (2) The principle is intended to apply generally whenever one agent proposes to restrict another’s liberty—that is, it is not merely a claim about the internal governance of already liberal European states. (3) British rule in India was imposed and maintained without Indian consent, and its ordinary functions included taxation, administration, education, and the pursuit of what its defenders called improvement, not simply the prevention of harm to others. (4) So by (1)–(3), British rule in India was illegitimate. (5) Yet Mill’s writings on India and his defense of East India Company government do not treat that rule as illegitimate; he seems to accept a form of despotic government for India, at least as a temporary means of improvement. (6) Therefore either the harm principle contains a restriction to peoples deemed capable of being improved through free discussion—a restriction that is not obvious in the principle itself—or Mill’s position is straightforwardly inconsistent. I find (6) hard to accept because it suggests that one of the most influential statements of the harm principle either is not a universal principle of liberty or is compatible with colonial despotism. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? If I must choose, I would rather qualify premise (2) than convict Mill of mere inconsistency, but I want to know what it would cost to insert such a qualification.
