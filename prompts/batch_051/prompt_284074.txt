Locke writes in An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter XXVII: “This being premised, to find wherein personal identity consists, we must consider what person stands for;- which, I think, is a thinking intelligent being, that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, in different times and places; which it does only by that consciousness which is inseparable from thinking, and, as it seems to me, essential to it: it being impossible for any one to perceive without perceiving that he does perceive.” I have a set of verdicts about personal identity cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them. I think that if a prince’s consciousness were transferred into a cobbler’s body, the person in the cobbler’s body would be the prince, not the cobbler. I think a sober man and the same man drunk are one person even if the drunk man remembers nothing afterward. I think someone who suffers total amnesia is still the same person as before. I also accept the brave-officer case that Reid raises against Locke in Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man: an old general remembers commanding as a young officer, and the officer remembers being flogged as a boy, but the general does not remember the flogging—yet the general, the officer, and the boy are all the same person. On the other hand, I think that if a person’s memories are destroyed and then reconstructed in another body after the original body dies, the original person has not survived; and in a fission case where two later people each remember being one earlier person, I think neither later person is identical with the earlier one, though the earlier person is responsible for what each later person does. Is there one account of personal identity that delivers all these verdicts, and what does it predict about a case in which a person’s memories are gradually replaced one at a time? If no single principle can accommodate them, which verdict should I overturn and why?
