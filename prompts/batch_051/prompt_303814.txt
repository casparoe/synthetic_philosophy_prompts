I run a philosophy of language club at my school, and I want to produce a sorting quiz for our next meeting. The members are roughly fourteen to eighteen years old and have no formal training in semantics, so the quiz must work from concrete judgments about sentences rather than from technical labels.

The question the quiz should sort people on is: what makes a generic statement such as "ducks lay eggs" true or false? I would like the quiz to sort members into five broad positions, described in plain terms:

- a statistical-prevalence view, on which a generic is true when most members of the kind have the property;
- a normalcy view, on which a generic is true when the property is normal or expected for the kind, even if most members lack it;
- a kind-attribution view, on which the sentence attributes a property directly to the kind itself rather than to individual members;
- a prototype view, on which a generic is true when the property belongs to the typical or ideal representative of the kind;
- a contextual view, on which the standards of truth for a generic shift according to the topic, the context, and what is at stake.

Please build the quiz as a decision tree with eight to twelve questions. Each node should present one concrete case (for example, "dogs have four legs", "ducks lay eggs", "mosquitoes carry malaria", "sharks attack bathers", "lions have manes", "books are paperbacks", and "teenagers are reckless") and ask whether the sentence is true, false, or true only relative to a context or topic. None of the questions should mention the names of the positions or ask directly which view sounds right.

I am especially interested in two kinds of hard cases. First, cases where most members of the kind have the property but the generic is still false, such as whether "books are paperbacks" should come out false even though most books are paperbacks. Second, social generics such as "teenagers are reckless", where normalcy, statistics, and negative stereotype may pull apart. Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first lay out which patterns of answers the questions allow, then locate the five positions in that space, and then flag the combinations that no position occupies, saying briefly whether each unoccupied combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag any pairs of positions that the tree cannot cleanly separate by truth-value questions alone, and explain what an extra question would need to get at in order to separate them.

Please write your answer in British English.
