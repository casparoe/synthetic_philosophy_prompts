I'm a postdoc with three years to point at the adaptationism debate, and I'm trying to figure out where to point it. By adaptationism I mean roughly the research program that assumes a trait is probably an adaptation—shaped by selection for its current job—until shown otherwise. The spandrels critique (Gould and Lewontin) pushed back by saying lots of traits are byproducts of other features, or are constrained by development and history, and that the program tends to produce just-so stories.

Here are five open questions I could work on. Rank them by how much they'd actually move the debate if done well:

1. In practice, can we tell a spandrel from an adaptation when experiments aren't possible, e.g. for human bipedalism or the human chin (often suspected of being a byproduct of other jaw changes)? What evidence would actually count either way?

2. Does the spandrels critique hit evolutionary psychology harder than organismal morphology, or does evo psych have better inferential tools (reverse engineering a known environment, cross-cultural data, modeling)?

3. Is "spandrel" even a stable category once you allow exaptation (a byproduct later co-opted for a new job)? If any current function can be called an exaptation, what's left of the adaptation/spandrel distinction?

4. What should the default model in comparative biology be: near-optimal adaptation, neutral drift of traits (random change by sampling, not selection), or something else? How much does the answer depend on the trait and the organism?

5. How often do developmental constraints, rather than selection, explain traits that look well-designed?

Rank them, give me your top two or three and your bottom one, and defend the ranking using importance, tractability, and neglectedness. Also say what new data or argument would most change the ranking. Put the final ordering in the last line, exactly as \answer{...} with something like \answer{2 > 5 > 1 > 4 > 3}.
