Suppose a person at forty is offered a genuine open-ended immortality: no aging, no disease, memory intact, and a world that supplies an unbounded sequence of genuinely new worthwhile projects, relationships, and pleasures. The novelty is real — nothing literally repeats — but the person's basic character and their standards of what counts as worthwhile do not change. This is a variant of the Makropulos case that Bernard Williams used against the desirability of endless life, modified to remove the easy explanation that literal repetition is what makes immortality boring.

I want your credence in the following proposition:

PROP: Over a sufficiently long stretch of such a life, the person would become bored in a specific sense: their engagement with new projects and relationships would progressively flatten; they would lose what, following Williams, can be called categorical desires — desires whose objects matter to them independently of merely staying alive and which give them a reason to go on at all; and they would eventually prefer death to continuing, though their life contains no unusual pain, illness, or incapacity.

Give me a number for PROP, but make the reasoning explicit by decomposing the estimate. I expect at least these terms, with a rough probability and a one-line justification for each:

1. Fixed-character satiation: the probability that a fixed character with fixed standards of worth, facing endlessly novel inputs, will eventually satiate; the goods keep their kind but lose their grip.
2. Finitude-dependence of categorical desire: the probability that categorical desires depend essentially on the finitude of a human life — on the shape that death gives to projects and relationships — so that they cannot be indefinitely replenished in an open-ended life.
3. Character revision is not a rescue: the probability that the only way to restore engagement would be to deliberately revise one's standards and desires, and that doing so enough to matter would amount to becoming a different person in the sense relevant to whether the original offer was good for the person who accepted it.
4. No boredom-free successor state: the probability that there is no stable successor state — for example calm contemplation, aesthetic appreciation, or serene contentment — in which a finite human psychology could go on indefinitely without the kind of project-centered engagement in (1) and (2) and still correctly regard its continued life as worth living.

Then explain how these combine. They will not be independent; state the dependencies and estimate the joint as best you can, rather than multiplying as if they were independent. Finally, tell me which term your overall number is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument — from the psychology of reward and habituation, from fiction, from first-person reports of aging or retirement, from reflection on the role of death — would move that term the most.

If you think PROP is not well-defined as stated, say precisely where the ambiguity is and give conditional credences under the two or three most reasonable precisifications, rather than refusing to answer. If your answer would be different for a religious heaven in which the person's nature is transformed, give the conditional number as well and say which assumption does the work.
