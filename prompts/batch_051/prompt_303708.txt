For a graduate seminar on scientific realism, write a sorting decision tree that takes a student from a single starting acknowledgment to one of six named positions, using only questions the student can answer without already knowing which label an answer leads to. The starting acknowledgment is this: the student grants the pessimistic meta-induction its historical premise, namely that many past theories that made successful novel predictions were later discarded, and that some of their central unobservable posits, such as caloric and the ether, turned out not to refer, while much of the surviving mathematical and predictive content carried over into successor theories. The tree should therefore begin from the fact of theory change rather than from a prior realist or antirealist commitment.

The six leaves must be standard scientific realism, selective realism, epistemic structural realism, entity realism, constructive empiricism, and instrumentalism, each used exactly once and defined in a sentence when it is reached. Your diagnostic questions should force the positions apart through concrete cases rather than through allegiance statements. Open the tree with a question that divides those who think a successful theory's achievement licenses belief in what it says about unobservables from those who think it licenses belief only in empirical adequacy, phrased without using the labels realism or antirealism. At minimum, the tree must also include the following probes: a question using the caloric theory of heat and the survival of Fourier's heat equations, where the central entity was eliminated while the mathematics survived; a question using Fresnel's ether and the survival of his optical equations in Maxwellian theory; a question using electron manipulation in the laboratory, as a test of whether experimental interactivity licenses existence claims that passive inference does not; a question about whether parts of a successful theory that played no role in any of its novel predictions are licensed to the same degree as the parts that did; and, on the empiricist branch, a question about whether claims concerning unobservables are literally true or false while merely not believed, or are instead only a convenient way of talking. You may add further questions if the six leaves require them, but keep the tree to at most eight questions. For each question, say which leaves it separates and which combinations of answers the tree takes to be empty; where a combination is empty, say briefly whether it seems incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Finally, flag any pair of leaves that your questions cannot cleanly separate and explain why. I suspect selective realism and epistemic structural realism will be hard to pull apart on the historical cases, since both agree that only part of a discarded theory earned belief; my hypothesis is that they differ only over whether what earned belief was entities and mechanisms or structures and equations, and I want the tree to test whether a concrete question can force that difference to the surface. Also address whether entity realism really separates from constructive empiricism on the electron case, since both can agree about observable phenomena while differing only over whether manipulation crosses the observable/unobservable line.
