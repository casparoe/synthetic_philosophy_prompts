I'm putting together a sequence of posts on social ontology, and I keep circling an argument about money that I can't fully defuse, so I'd like a careful audit rather than a quick yes-or-no. The argument runs as follows:

(1) A coin is money in a community exactly when the community collectively treats it as money — accepting it in settlement of debts, quoting prices in it, and so on.
(2) If the community's attitudes changed and everyone ceased to accept the coin, the very same piece of metal would persist, but it would no longer be money.
(3) So money is not identical with the piece of metal; it is a status, or more precisely a set of collective attitudes.
(4) A set of attitudes is not the sort of thing that can be transferred from one person to another, stored in a drawer, or dropped on the pavement.
(5) But money can be transferred from one person to another, stored, and dropped on the pavement.
(6) Therefore money is not a set of attitudes — which contradicts (3). So at least one of (1)–(5) must go.

I suspect the trouble is in (3) or (4). In particular, (3) seems to slide from "the metal could survive the loss of the money-status" to "the money is the status" — as if the only alternatives to being a piece of metal were being an attitude. And (4) may equivocate on what "transferred" means. But I want a precise diagnosis: is the argument valid as stated? If not, exactly which step or steps fail, under what reading, and how should the false premise be restated so the contradiction no longer goes through?
