I am giving a paper at my department's normativity workshop in a few weeks, and I want to walk in ready for a hostile but competent question period. The paper is my attempt to dissolve the wrong kind of reasons problem for fitting-attitude analyses of value. I argue that the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction should not be drawn in terms of reasons at all—not even in the usual object-given/state-given way—but in terms of the constitutive standards of correctness of the relevant attitudes. Admiration, desire, disgust, respect, and the like each come with a standard that determines when the attitude is correct; a consideration is of the right kind for an attitude just when it bears on whether the attitude meets that standard. On this view, a demon's threat gives me a reason to bring it about that I admire him, but it is not a consideration in light of which admiring him would be correct; so a buck-passing analysis of value can be restated in terms of fitting attitudes rather than reasons to have attitudes, and the threat case no longer forces us to choose between saying the demon is admirable and abandoning the analysis.

The workshop audience is mixed in a way that matters for the objections I will get. About half are reasons-first people, some of them sympathetic to buck-passing but convinced that the wrong kind of reasons problem is the hardest thing their view has to answer; about a third are value-first realists who regard the problem as evidence that fitting-attitude analyses must eventually appeal to an unreduced notion of value; the rest are philosophers of mind and emotion who will be most skeptical that occurrent emotional attitudes really have correctness conditions in the needed sense. The paper's argument runs through three stages: an argument that the object-given/state-given distinction is unstable once we consider how freely the object of an attitude can be redescribed; an argument that attitudes nevertheless present their objects under evaluative descriptions—admiration presents its object as admirable, fear as dangerous—and that these presentations fix standards of correctness; and finally the reanalysis of value claims as claims about which attitude standards an object meets.
