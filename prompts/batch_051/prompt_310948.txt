I have been reading the “Book of the Machines” chapters in Samuel Butler’s *Erewhon* (1872) for a seminar on historical treatments of machine intelligence, and I keep getting stuck on a move the Erewhonian philosopher makes. The treatise is arguing that machines are developing along something like evolutionary lines toward consciousness. To rebut the objection that a steam-engine may have strength but no will of its own, the writer says:

“A man is the resultant and exponent of all the forces that have been brought to bear upon him, whether before his birth or afterwards. His action at any moment depends solely upon his constitution, and on the intensity and direction of the various agencies to which he is and has been subjected. Some of these will counteract each other; but as he is by nature, and as he has been acted on, and is now acted on from without, so will he do as certainly and regularly as though he were a machine.”

I see how this is meant to deflate the objection that machines lack a will by saying that humans, too, lack a mysterious uncaused will. But I am not sure what work the determinism is doing in the larger argument. If human action is just as fixed as machine action, does that lower humans to the status of machines, elevate machines to the status of humans, or both? And is the argument coherent if we accept the determinism: does the analogy license the conclusion that machines might become conscious, or does it only show that neither humans nor machines have ultimate agency? I ask because a contemporary alignment researcher would probably want to say that goal-directedness and agency can be real features of a system even if the system is deterministic; is Butler’s philosopher leaving room for that, or is he denying the very distinction that the alignment framing depends on?
