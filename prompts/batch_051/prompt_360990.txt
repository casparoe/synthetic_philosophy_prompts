I keep running into the claim that Frege's late political diary—with its anti-democratic and anti-Semitic entries—must change how we assess his philosophy. A colleague pressed me into the following argument, and although the conclusion seems clearly too strong, I cannot locate the bad step.

(1) The diary entries are not incidental; they express Frege's settled, considered view about which people are capable of rational political judgment and which are not.
(2) Frege's logic and philosophy of language are built on the claim that thoughts and logical laws are objective, graspable by any rational being independently of private mental states.
(3) A thinker's settled views about who is capable of rational judgment are relevant to assessing that thinker's claims about the objectivity and universality of rational thought, since both concern what reason is and who can exercise it.
(4) So Frege's diary gives us reason to distrust his claims about the objectivity of logic.
(5) But if (3) is sound, then the authority of any philosopher's or mathematician's account of rationality can be challenged by examining their political prejudices; a bigot's proof would be undermined by the bigotry.
(6) That cannot be right, because the validity of a proof does not depend on the moral character of the person who produced it.
(7) Therefore either one of (1)–(3) is false, or the move from (3) and (4) to (5) contains a slide.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I am most unwilling to give up (1), and I am suspicious of (3), but I cannot say exactly why (3) overreaches without also giving up the thought that the diary ought to matter for how we interpret Frege. I would like to know which premise to reject and what rejecting it costs.
