I have been mulling over an argument that surfaces in discussions of behavioral genetics, and I cannot determine whether or where it goes wrong.

(1) The heritability of a trait in a population is the proportion of the total phenotypic variance in that trait that is statistically attributable to genetic differences among individuals, relative to the range of environments actually present in that population.
(2) If a trait's heritability is high, say 0.7, then environmental differences, together with gene-environment interplay and measurement error, account for only the remaining 0.3 of the variance.
(3) If environmental differences account for only 0.3 of the current variance, then changing the environment, even to conditions outside the studied range, can produce only a minor shift in the trait.
(4) Therefore, a high heritability estimate already tells us that environmental interventions on the trait will be of limited effect.

Is this argument valid? If it is, how should we reconcile it with traits that have high heritability estimates yet shifted substantially when environments changed? If it is not, identify precisely which step fails, explain what that step conflates, and state the corrected version of whatever can genuinely be inferred from high heritability about the likely effects of environmental interventions.
