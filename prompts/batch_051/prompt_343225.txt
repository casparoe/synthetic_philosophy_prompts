I've been trying to pin down what a seemingly technical corner of software licensing actually commits people to. Copyleft — the family of free-software licenses of which the GNU GPL is the best-known — grants everyone the right to use, modify, and redistribute a program, on the condition that any modified or derivative version one distributes is licensed under the same terms. That condition only begins to apply once something counts as a derivative or modified version of the covered program, so the license is forced to draw a boundary around each program — to say where one program ends and another begins. In practice the boundary runs through an odd pile of technical distinctions. A program that statically links to a GPL-covered library is treated as a single derivative work, and dynamic linking is widely, though not universally, treated the same way: because the calling code and the library are loaded into a single image and exchange function calls, they count as one work for licensing purposes. But a program that produces the same user-visible behavior by launching the other program as a separate process, communicating over a pipe, or making requests to it across a network is ordinarily treated as two pieces of software merely interoperating — even when the two were developed in tandem and are useless apart. The user cannot tell the difference from the outside; the obligations are entirely different.

What theory of the identity and boundaries of a work does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics rather than defending or criticizing the practice: does it individuate programs by their source text, by the intentions of their authors, by their functional role, by the causal channel through which their parts communicate, or by some unacknowledged mixture of these? Is the resulting account internally coherent, or does the practice quietly invoke one theory when it wants the obligation to spread and another when it wants it to stop? Finally, suppose the presupposed theory were replaced with a strictly behavioral one, on which two processes constitute one work just in case a competent user could not determine, without inspecting the source code, which process is responsible for which portions of the combined behavior. What would have to change in how derivative-work obligations are assigned under copyleft, and would the practice's underlying purpose — ensuring that users who receive combined software can obtain and modify its source — be better or worse served?
