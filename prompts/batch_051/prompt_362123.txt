I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, so I have probably imported some bad habits from my work on distributed systems, and I would rather be corrected than have my framing indulged. Lately I have been trying to work out whether presentism or eternalism about time is right. As I understand it, presentism says that only the present exists; eternalism says that past, present and future events all exist, and 'now' merely marks a position within the whole. I keep returning to the following argument against presentism, and I cannot tell whether it is sound or whether it leans on a physicist's simplification.

(1) If presentism is true, then which events are present is an objective, observer-independent matter of fact. Whether an event is real cannot be relative to a frame of reference; it either belongs to the present or it does not.

(2) An event is present exactly when it is simultaneous with this moment; so presentism requires an objective, frame-independent relation of simultaneity between events.

(3) Special relativity appears to deny any such relation. For two spatially separated events, whether they occur at the same time depends on the inertial frame of the observer; different frames yield different simultaneity judgements, and physics offers no privileged frame.

(4) Hence special relativity seems to tell us that there is no objective, frame-independent simultaneity relation for spatially separated events.

(5) Therefore presentism demands something — an absolute, universe-wide 'now' — that special relativity says does not exist; so presentism is false.

I suspect the failure is in (2), or in the move from (3) to (4), but I cannot state it precisely. Part of the difficulty is that in distributed systems I have been trained to think that a system either has an authoritative global clock or it does not, and that an invisible, unmeasurable clock is no clock at all. I suspect that instinct is doing too much work here.

Please give me a definite verdict rather than a survey: is the argument valid, and if it is not, exactly which step fails, or where does the argument trade on an equivocation? Be specific about the reading of the key terms. If I have mischaracterised presentism or special relativity, say so plainly and correct the framing instead of working within it. I would rather accept an unwelcome conclusion than receive a diplomatic summary of options.
