I first met the value-of-information theorem — I knew it as Good's theorem — in online decision-theory discussions, and I have never been able to make peace between its proof, which looks airtight, and its conclusion, which seems to forbid something I hold dear. The theorem says: for an expected-utility maximizer who will update by conditionalization and then take the act that maximizes expected utility under the posterior, the expected value of any costless piece of information is non-negative. The proof is nearly a one-liner: take the act that is best under the prior; for any possible observation, the act that is best conditional on that observation is at least as good as the prior-optimal act; so, averaged over possible observations, a policy of observing and then acting is at least as good in expectation as acting without observing. I cannot find the mistake, and the theorem seems to leave only two respectable ways to decline free information: admit that you are failing to maximize expected utility, or admit that the information is not actually free — attention, effort, emotional toll, or some other cost smuggled in.

Yet I also hold, with what feels like perfect confidence, that it can be rational to prefer not to know a truth that will change nothing about what you do. A close friend is happy in his marriage, would not leave, and would not alter any decision large or small. I do not think he is making a cognitive error when he hopes never to overhear his wife say, in another room, that she settled for him. I know the two standard reconciliations, and I want them stated at full strength rather than dismissed. The first says the information is not free because the sadness, rumination, and watchfulness it would cause are genuine consequences, and the right utility function prices them in from the start; once it does, the theorem is silent, because the agent's utility is now defined partly over his own state of belief. This is a real move, and I see its force, but I worry it makes the formalism vacuous: any instance of information-avoidance gets redescribed as information with a hidden psychic price, and no behavior could ever count against the theorem. The second says a rational agent may anticipate that he will update or respond in predictably inappropriate ways and may precommit against that by avoiding the trigger, exactly as he might decline a drug that would make him briefly irrational. That, too, has force, but it seems to require him to foresee his own later responses as errors while simultaneously trusting the present self that fears them; I cannot tell whether that is a coherent division of labor within one agent or an attempt to have it both ways.

Present both sides at their strongest and then negotiate. I specifically want to know what it would take for the theorem's scope to be the thing that gives way, what it would take for the intuition to be debunked, and whether there is a reading of free and of should on which both survive. Give me the strongest version of the hidden-costs reconciliation and the precommitment-against-one's-own-irrationality reconciliation, the strongest case that my friend's dread is simply a bias, and then say which move you would actually endorse, and under what conditions you would switch to one of the others.
