I have spent the past few months, as an interested outsider with no formal background in philosophy or law, trying to understand a dispute that keeps surfacing in debates about medical ethics, human rights, and end-of-life care: whether the concept of human dignity is actually doing any work. On one side are writers and campaigners who treat dignity as the foundation that other rights rest on—roughly the idea, often traced to Kant, that persons have an absolute, non-exchangeable worth which cannot simply be overridden by consent or welfare. On the other side are people like Ruth Macklin, who has argued that dignity is a useless concept because any defensible use of it reduces to respect for autonomy, while its remaining uses tend to smuggle in religious or paternalistic conclusions. When I read the two sides next to one another they often seem to be talking past each other, largely because they rarely specify which sense of dignity is in play, or whether it is supposed to add anything beyond autonomy and respect for persons.

Please write the joint statement that a committed defender of the dignity-as-foundation view and a committed defender of the dignity-is-useless view could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Use this structure exactly: first, a numbered list of agreed claims—claims substantive enough that both sides are committing themselves to something, but nothing either would disown; then a disagreements table. For each row of the table, give the residual disagreement stated so precisely that each side would accept the wording of its own position; the crux, meaning the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; and a case, test, or argument that both sides accept as bearing on that crux, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Anchor the statement in at least two concrete settings where appeals to dignity are doing argumentative work but pulling in different directions—for instance assisted dying, the sale of organs, or the legal prohibition of a degrading practice that a competent adult has freely chosen.

As you go, mark clearly which points are settled among people who work on this topic and which remain genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading—for example, of exactly what Kant was committed to, or of what Macklin's argument established—as if it were the consensus. Write in British English.
