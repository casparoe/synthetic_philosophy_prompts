In our seminar on AI and value, we have been studying the argument for what is sometimes called coherent extrapolated volition or, more broadly, idealized preferences. The argument, as I understand it, is that the preferences people actually state or reveal are an unsafe target for optimization. They are inconsistent, myopic, malleable, and often formed in ignorance; if a sufficiently powerful system optimized them as they stand, it would lock in our flaws. A well-designed system should therefore optimize not our current preferences but the preferences we would reach through a suitable idealization — fuller information, reflection, and integration into something coherent.

I have been trying to think this argument through against a development in the field. Many current large language models are shaped by reinforcement learning from human feedback, in which the training signal comes from large numbers of quick pairwise judgments about which model output is better. The judgments are collected without the idealized conditions the argument demands: the raters are not unusually informed, they work under time pressure, their preferences can disagree with one another, and nothing reconciles those disagreements into a single coherent set of values. Yet the resulting models are often described as capturing something like human values, and often better than models trained without this step.

Does the idealization argument survive this development? I am trying to work out which premise is genuinely under pressure: the premise that actual preferences are too flawed to optimize, the premise that idealization must come before optimization rather than being approximated by aggregating many unidealized judgments, or some other premise. One clear reply is that RLHF-type optimization is narrow and low-stakes, while the original argument concerns systems powerful enough to shape the long-run future. But I would like to know whether that reply saves the argument as stated, or whether it quietly replaces it with a weaker thesis about scope and stakes. If a repaired version exists, what exactly should the repaired conclusion say, and what does it give up?
