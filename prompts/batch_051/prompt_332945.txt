Suppose a port authority ordinance from 1902 says: 'No vessel shall enter the harbor of Bellweather nor tie up at any wharf therein without a permit from the harbormaster.' Getting a permit requires at least 48 hours notice and a fee. Last winter a fire broke out on Pier 3 while the town fireboat was in drydock, and a privately owned salvage tug moored just outside the harbor line came in within minutes, trained its pumps on the fire, and saved the pier. Now the port authority wants to fine the tug's owner for entering without a permit. The words plainly cover him, but the drafters in 1902 were thinking about commercial traffic and never imagined a private tug doing emergency firefighting. Is this a case where a court ought to ignore what the text plainly says? Lay out what the plain words, the purpose of the ordinance, and what the drafters would have said if asked each give, and then say which approach should win here and why — and what exactly the absurdity doctrine requires before a court can refuse the text.
