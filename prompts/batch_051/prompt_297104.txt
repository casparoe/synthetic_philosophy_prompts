Ok so I'm in this linguistics class and somethings been bugging me. We were talking about why English spelling is such a mess, and my professor pointed out that English keeps related words looking the same even when they sound completely different: sign and signature, electric and electricity, and even the regular past tense uses a single spelling, -ed, for three different pronunciations (walked, played, waited). Spanish, by contrast, more or less respells words to track how they sound, roughly one letter to a phoneme. She then said, paraphrasing, that a writing system is always a theory of what a language fundamentally is, and that English encodes a theory of morphemes while Spanish encodes a theory of phonemes. I've been turning that over in my head, and I don't know whether to take it literally.

So here's what I want to pin down. If I treat English spelling as an actual existing practice rather than as anyone's stated beliefs, what does it presuppose about the structure of language? The practice seems to treat the morpheme or the whole word as the thing that matters, with pronunciation as something changeable and superficial—that's why sign keeps its g for the sake of signature. But does the practice actually commit us to that view, or is that just a rationalization after the fact? Would a more phonemic system like Spanish really be committed to the opposite, that the phoneme is the fundamental unit and that morphological or etymological relationships are not the spelling's business? Or is the whole thing underdetermined, so you could run an English-style orthography without believing any particular theory of language, and a hardcore morphologist could happily use a phonemic alphabet? And if someone did take the writing-is-a-theory idea seriously, what would that change about how we argue for spelling reform or teach kids to spell?
