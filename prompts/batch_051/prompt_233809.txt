I'm a software engineer and I've never taken a formal philosophy class, but I've been going around in circles on the paradox of blackmail and want to stress-test it with a variation. The way I understand the classic puzzle: revealing true embarrassing information about someone is, by itself, legal; asking someone for money is, by itself, legal; but linking them — pay me or I tell everyone — is a crime. So the puzzle is what makes the combination wrong when both parts are permitted on their own.

Now swap out the human. Suppose someone builds a service, call it LeakEscrow, that works like a dead-man's switch. Someone uploads a secret, sets a recurring payment, and if the payment lapses or the card declines, the service automatically distributes the secret to the victim's contacts. There's no human deciding to make a threat at the moment the pressure is felt; its just code and a precommitment. The victim might even be the one who signed up, as a commitment device, or a third party might do it to them.

Here's the question. If we think blackmail is wrong because of coercion or exploitation, does the dead-man's switch coerce or exploit in the same way, or does the analysis break because there's no agent issuing a conditional demand? If instead we think the wrongness is in the conditional structure itself — do X or I reveal Y — why should it matter whether a human or code enforces the conditional? And separately: is the developer of LeakEscrow a blackmailer, even if they never learn a single secret and never ask anyone for money?

I'm not after legal citations; I want the conceptual hinge. Which feature of blackmail is actually doing the moral work, and does the paradox survive when you replace the blackmailer with a commitment device?
