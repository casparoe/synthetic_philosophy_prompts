I keep coming back to an argument a prof sketched in modal logic, and it seems to prove way to much, but I can't find the bad step. It goes like this:

(1) Necessarily, everything is self-identical. (There's no possible world where some thing fails to be itself — that's just logic.)
(2) If necessarily everything is F, then everything is necessarily F. (I think this is called the converse Barcan formula, though I might have the name backwards.) This also sounds right: if every possible world makes "everything is F" true, then take any actual thing — it's F in every world.
(3) So, plugging in "is self-identical": everything is necessarily self-identical.
(4) If something is necessarily self-identical, then it necessarily exists. Because if it hadn't existed, there'd be nothing there to be identical to itself.
(5) So everything that exists, exists necessarily. Nothing actual could have failed to exist.
(6) But that's absurd. I exist, but my parents could easily never have met; there's a possible world without me. So step (2) or step (4) has to go, but both look pretty hard to refuse.

Where exactly does this go wrong? I'd rather keep the obvious truth that I might not have existed than either of those steps, but I want to know what I'm actually paying when I give one of them up. Walk through the diagnosis, and be specific about what the rejected step was doing underneath the surface.

Whatever else you say, end with a single line that starts "Verdict:" and names the step that goes wrong and, in a few words, what rejecting it costs.
