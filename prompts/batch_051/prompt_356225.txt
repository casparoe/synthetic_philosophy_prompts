Question: "Explain the distinction Chalmers draws between the 'easy problems' of consciousness and the 'hard problem.' Then spell out Jackson's knowledge argument (Mary's room) and what it is meant to show about physicalism. Finally, present one standard objection to the argument and give your assessment of whether the argument survives it."

Student answer: "The easy problems are the problems that neuroscience will eventually solve. They concern how the brain does things: discriminating stimuli, integrating information, guiding action, reporting internal states, and so on. The hard problem is different: even if we had a complete account of all those mechanisms, we would still not see why any of it feels like something from the inside. That's what Chalmers calls the hard problem of consciousness. The point is that no explanation in terms of functions or mechanisms closes the gap between the physical facts and the facts about experience.

Jackson's knowledge argument is supposed to make this vivid. Mary knows every physical fact about color vision before leaving her black-and-white room. When she first sees a ripe tomato, she learns something new: what it's like to see red. Since she already knew all the physical facts and this was not among them, the physical facts do not exhaust all the facts. So physicalism is false.

The standard objection is the ability hypothesis, which says Mary doesn't gain propositional knowledge; she gains abilities, such as imagining, recognizing, and remembering red experiences. If that's right, the argument is in trouble, because physicalism is only committed to accounting for facts. But I think the ability hypothesis fails. Mary can, after the tomato, think a new thought of the form 'so that's what it's like.' That sure seems like a new fact she has learned, not just a new skill. So the argument succeeds, which means we have to accept some form of dualism."
