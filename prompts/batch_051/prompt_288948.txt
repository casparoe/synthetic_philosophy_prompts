I recently joined my hospital's research ethics committee, and at my first meeting a protocol came before us that our terms of reference do not obviously settle. The governing standard, drafted when trials were fixed two-arm designs, reads: "A randomized trial may continue to enroll participants only so long as the evidence, as it now stands, does not favor one arm over the others."

The protocol is a Bayesian response-adaptive trial comparing two chemotherapies for a rare cancer. After each cohort, randomization probabilities are updated: by the fourth interim analysis, the posterior probability that Arm A is superior is 0.84, and new participants are assigned to A with probability 0.9 and to B with 0.1.

Does the committee violate its own rule if it lets the trial continue assigning 10% of new participants to B? The evidence "as it now stands" leans toward A but is not decisive, and the authors of the standard clearly did not imagine a trial that revises its own uncertainty over time. Work through the case from (a) the text, (b) the purpose of the rule, and (c) what the drafters would have said had they known of such designs; then say which reading should govern here, and whether the standard was ever an adequate statement of clinical equipoise — the view that randomizing patients is ethical only while the relevant expert community is genuinely uncertain which arm is better.
