I'm currently taking a course that sits at the intersection of epistemology and the foundations of statistics, and I keep noticing a structural resemblance that I can't quite make precise. In Neyman–Pearson hypothesis testing, the very same set of data can justify rejecting a null hypothesis under one assignment of error costs and fail to justify rejecting it under another: where the evidential threshold sits is set by how bad a Type I error would be relative to a Type II error. Pragmatic encroachment in epistemology seems to do the same thing at the level of knowledge, since it says whether a subject knows that p can change without any change in the subject's evidence, because how much evidence is required depends on what practically rides on p. And epistemic contextualism offers a closely related claim about the word "knows" itself, on which the standard shifts with the conversational context. The mapping seems to run: sample data to evidence, the significance level to the threshold for knowledge, the relative cost of Type I and Type II errors to the practical stakes, and "reject the null" to "the subject knows that p."

But I am unsure whether this is a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or an outright confusion. The most obvious trouble is that a hypothesis test is not factive in anything like the way knowledge is: a rejected null can be true, and a test never forces reality to match its verdict. Relatedly, the costs in the statistical case belong to the statistician or the downstream decision-maker, while in the contextualist case the standard is supplied by the ascriber's situation and in the pragmatic encroachment case by the subject's practical situation. I would like to know exactly where the mapping holds and where it comes apart, what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer, and whether the parallel makes a prediction that turns out false—for example, does it wrongly predict that lowering the stakes should always make knowledge easier to come by, or that contextualism and pragmatic encroachment collapse into a single thesis about decision-theoretic acceptance?
