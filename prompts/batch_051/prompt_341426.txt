i keep going back and forth on whether technology is neutral, and i've started collecting my own gut verdicts on a bunch of concrete cases to see if there's a principle hiding in them. I'm a software engineer, so some of these are from work. sorry this is a bit long.

- The story about the low overpasses on Long Island parkways, built too low for buses to pass under, supposedly to keep certain people out. If the story's even true, that's not just a road, that's a policy written in concrete. I'd call that political for sure.

- A park bench with an armrest bolted in the middle so nobody can sleep across it. Same as the overpass basically: the exclusion is baked into the object. Political.

- A chat app where end-to-end encryption is the default and the server operator literally couldn't read your messages even if ordered to. That feels political too, because a deliberate technical choice changes what power the company and the state can actually exercise. But then again you could also describe it as just the app doing what users want, so I'm not sure it's the same kind of political as the bench.

- A recommendation algorithm tuned only to maximize watch time, and as a side effect it starts recommending more and more extreme stuff to keep people engaged. I want to say this has political consequences, but I feel weird saying the politics is *in* the artifact. Nobody set out to radicalize anyone. Maybe this is just an incentive problem, not a design problem.

- A merge-request setting on our repo that requires approval from one specific senior engineer before anything ships. That's literally a power relation encoded in software, but calling it political feels overwrought. It's just a workflow.

- A hiring screen that filters résumés by keyword and keeps rejecting people with gaps in their work history. Even if nobody intended to filter out people with caregiving responsibilities, I'd say the tool is doing something political, because the discrimination is now executed by the algorithm instead of by a person. But the algorithm doesn't know anything about politics, which bugs me.

- A roundabout versus a four-way stop. The roundabout makes everyone slow down and nobody can jump the queue, so the rule is in the concrete rather than in a cop writing tickets. I think that's also political in a mild way, but maybe I'm just using the word too loosely at that point.

Is there one principle that gets most or all of these, and that would tell me which artifacts have politics built into them and which just have political side effects? And what would it say about a case I keep going back and forth on: a database schema I was asked to design that deliberately has no field for legal sex, so the downstream HR system can't sort anyone by it. Is that the same as the encryption case, or the stop sign, or neither? If some of my verdicts can't all be kept under one clean principle, which ones should I drop and why?
