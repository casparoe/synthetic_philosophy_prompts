I've come to hold a version of the transparency thesis about photography: the view that in looking at a photograph one sees the photographed object through the photograph, rather than merely seeing a depiction of it, so that photographs belong in the same broad category as mirrors, telescopes, and windows rather than paintings and drawings. I want to know what would count against this thesis, in a way that is fair to it rather than hostile. Which cases should press hardest on a careful defender: digitally manipulated or composited images, photorealistic AI-generated images with no optical-causal chain back to a scene, photographs of photographs, accidental exposures, misattributed or misdated photographs, or images one only initially mistakes for photographs? Which technological developments, and which considerations about the role of the viewer's beliefs and background knowledge in seeing, most threaten the strong version of the claim? Please distinguish evidence and arguments that would be decisive from those that would be merely awkward, say how much the view can absorb by distinguishing seeing from knowing-that or from seeing-as, and finish with an honest assessment of whether the thesis, after the necessary qualifications, remains a substantive and testable claim about perception or has been protected into unfalsifiability, and whether that insulation is a problem for a thesis of this kind.

Organize your answer by these sections, in this order:

1. The view, made precise (about 120 words). State the strongest plausible reading of the transparency thesis you will assess and note which everyday intuitions it captures.

2. The case against (about 300 words). Present the most serious counterexamples and arguments, including technological, psychological, and epistemic ones.

3. What a defender should concede (about 200 words). Identify which of those objections count against the thesis even on its own terms, and how a sophisticated defender would grant or absorb them.

4. Ranked defeaters (about 250 words). Rank the main threats from fatal to merely awkward, each with a one-sentence statement of what exactly it would show.

5. Verdict (about 150 words). Say whether the surviving view is falsifiable and informative, or whether its qualifications have drained it of content, and whether that matters here.
