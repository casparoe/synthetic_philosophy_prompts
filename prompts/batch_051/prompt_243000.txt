I follow an epistemic policy and I want a committed assessment of whether it is defensible, not a survey of the options. The policy concerns propositions I take myself to know in the strict sense — not merely believe with high confidence, but know. For any such proposition p, I treat new evidence against p as something to be explained away rather than as a reason to reduce my confidence in p. My rationale is this: if I know that p, then p is true, so any evidence against p is evidence for a falsehood — it is misleading — and evidence that is misleading should carry no weight. I therefore ignore it, even when I have not examined it. I only reopen p when the new evidence gives me reason to doubt something upstream of p, such as my perception, my memory, my sources, or my competence in the relevant domain; I never reopen p directly.

Here is a concrete case. I know my partner is at her office because I dropped her there an hour ago and she has since texted me from her desk. A neighbor tells me he has just seen her at a café across town. Under my policy I do not lower my confidence; I assume the neighbor is mistaken or that something about the situation is misleading, and I do not walk over to check. I regard the refusal to check as part of the policy, not as a failure of nerve: since I know she is at the office, a check would be treating evidence I already know to be misleading as if it were live.

I recognize that this resembles the dogmatism paradox, and I want you to engage the structure of that paradox rather than merely name it. In particular:

1. Does the inference from 'I know that p' to 'any evidence against p is misleading' to 'I may disregard that evidence' go through? If it fails, where exactly does it fail?

2. Is my refusal to perform a cheap, direct check on the same footing as ignoring the neighbor's testimony, or is it worse? If I really know, the check could only confirm what I know, so is refusing it coherent, or does it betray that I do not after all take myself to know?

3. What is the most principled restriction on the policy that would keep whatever is right in it without licensing dogmatism? If no restriction can save it, say why and state what the correct policy would be instead.

Please take a definite position: say whether the policy is defensible, indefensible, or defensible only in a restricted form, and defend that verdict. End your answer with a single final line of the form \answer{...} containing your verdict in a few words (for example, \answer{indefensible} or \answer{defensible with a checking clause}), and put nothing after that line.
