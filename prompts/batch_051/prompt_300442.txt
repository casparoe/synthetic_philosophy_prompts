I'm a novelist, a third of the way into a near-future legal thriller, and Ive hit a wall on the big idea. The premise is that the public court system still nominally exists, but for anyone with a job, a lease, a phone plan, or a credit card, the fine print has routed nearly every possible dispute into binding arbitration — predispute clauses, confidential awards, no real appeal, arbitrators who depend on the same companies for repeat business. The book follows a woman whose employment discrimination claim gets pushed into arbitration, and then she learns the arbitrator regularly works for the employer's law firm. I dont want the novel to be a polemic; I want the worldbuilding itself to carry an argument about the privatization of justice.

So, what are the most important implications of this shift from public adjudication to private arbitration for how I should think about what law is for in the book? Specifically, I'm trying to get at the difference between treating justice as a service people contract for and treating it as a public good, and what a society loses when the reason-giving, precedent-making, publicly accountable side of courts disappears into sealed awards. Conceptual, not policy-wonkish, would be ideal.
