Two friends, Daniel and Priya, have been arguing for weeks about whether gradual replacement mind uploading would preserve personal identity. Daniel thinks that replacing each neuron one at a time with a functionally identical silicon component would let a person survive; Priya says it would merely create a copy while destroying the original. Their exchange went as follows:

(1) Priya asked Daniel whether, after all his neurons had been replaced one by one, he would still be the same person as before. Daniel said yes: there was never any interruption in his consciousness, memory, or personality, so the resulting person is robustly psychologically continuous with him.

(2) Priya then cited Hobbes’s discussion of the ship of Theseus in De Corpore, where Hobbes points out that if the ship’s planks are gradually replaced while the old planks are saved and later reassembled, there seem to be two numerically identical ships, which is absurd. She said gradual replacement faces an analogous problem: if Daniel’s original neurons were preserved during replacement and later reconnected into a working brain, there would be two candidates for being Daniel, both equally continuous with him.

(3) Daniel replied that Hobbes’s case only shows that material continuity cannot settle identity for ships. For persons, identity tracks psychological continuity, not the original material, so the reassembled old neurons, if they produced a duplicate mind, would be another person exactly like him rather than him.

(4) Priya responded that this makes her point rather than refuting it: if the gradually replaced body and the reassembled original neurons could each be psychologically continuous with Daniel, then psychological continuity fails to pick out a unique survivor, which is exactly why she doubts that gradual replacement is survival rather than copying.

Neither would budge, and I honestly cannot tell who is scoring the better points. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: assess whether each move is clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, and say who, if either, has the stronger overall position.
