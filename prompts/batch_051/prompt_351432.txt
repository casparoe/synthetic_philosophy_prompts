I teach physics and mathematics to high-school and beginning college students, and I keep noticing a pattern that I do not have a name for. Students' wrong answers are rarely random: the same incorrect ideas recur across students, across years, and across problems, and when students are asked to explain their answers, they often give a coherent rationale rather than a guess. For example, a student will say that a thrown baseball keeps moving because the force of the throw is still inside it. He then applies the same idea to a coasting car, to a sliding hockey puck, and to a ball dropped from a moving bicycle. When I walk him through a counterexample, he does not give the idea up; he adjusts it, saying the stored-up force drains away gradually, and then uses the adjusted version somewhere else. I see the same structure elsewhere. A student who believes a losing streak makes a win "due" gives reasons for the belief, makes predictions from it, and treats disconfirming cases as exceptions rather than as evidence against it.

I am not looking for the word "misconception," which seems to cover isolated factual slips as well. I want to know whether there is an established category for the specific phenomenon in which a wrong idea is shared, stable, productive of further predictions, and capable of defending itself against counterexamples. Is there a name for that, and how is it distinguished from a plain mistake, a false belief, a bias, and a mere gap in knowledge? If the nearest established notions fit only partially, I would rather hear how each differs and which fits best than be given a bare label. Please write your answer in American English.
