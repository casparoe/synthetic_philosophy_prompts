I was rereading the opening definitions of Newton’s Principia (in Andrew Motte’s 1846 translation) and got stuck on a passage from Definition V, where Newton is explaining centripetal force:

“A stone, whirled about in a sling, endeavours to recede from the hand that turns it; and by that endeavour, distends the sling, and that with so much the greater force, as it is revolved with the greater velocity, and as soon as ever it is let go, flies away.”

I have a set of verdicts about rotation, and I cannot find the principle behind them. Here they are.

(1) Newton’s bucket: at first the bucket rotates while the water stays flat; later the water rotates with the bucket and climbs the sides. I judge that the climbing water is really rotating, even during the phase when it is at rest relative to the bucket. I also think the bucket’s initial rotation is real even though the water is flat.

(2) Two globes joined by a cord in an otherwise empty universe: if the cord is taut, I judge that the two-globe system really rotates, even though there is nothing else for it to rotate relative to. If the cord hangs slack, I judge they do not rotate, whatever their relative motion may be.

(3) A single smooth sphere in an otherwise empty universe: I want to say that if it rotates it bulges at the equator. But I cannot say what “it rotates” means without something to compare it with, and I find this verdict harder to keep than the two-globe case.

(4) A stone whirled in a sling in our actual universe: I judge the sling distends because the stone really rotates, not merely because it rotates relative to the earth or the fixed stars.

(5) A counterfactual case: the stone and sling are at rest relative to the distant stars, and instead the rest of the universe rotates around them in the opposite sense. I judge the sling would not distend; the stone would not be really rotating. But if rotation is just relative motion, I cannot explain why this differs from the ordinary sling.

My tentative principle is that real rotation is rotation relative to some invisible, non-material “space itself” or inertial frame. But I also believe motion is only real when there is something material to move, and I refuse absolute velocities. So I am torn.

Please give me a principle or a small set of candidate principles that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely. Tell me which of my verdicts the principle forces me to drop, especially between cases (3) and (5), and apply it to this further case: a perfectly transparent rigid bucket whose water is exactly level and at rest relative to the bucket, while the fixed stars are observed to rotate around the bucket with a period of one minute. Does the water truly rotate, or not? Also, please say what argument or consideration would change your mind about which principle is right, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
