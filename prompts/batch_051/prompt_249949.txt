I keep finding myself pulled between a principle I find compelling and a reaction I cannot reconcile with it. The principle is roughly this: a society committed to equal opportunity should regard advantages that trace to the accident of birth as morally arbitrary, and inheritance is the cleanest case — a large, compounding, unearned benefit conferred on someone before they have done anything to deserve it. On this view, heavily taxing inherited wealth is not punishment; it is the same logic that underwrites public schooling and other leveling institutions, applied to the purest transfer of unearned advantage. I am drawn to the principle's systematic virtues: it gives a single, general test for which inequalities are suspect — those flowing from unchosen circumstances rather than from choices — and inheritance taxation looks like one of its least intrusive applications, because it taxes a transfer, not the parent's own consumption, savings, or labor.

Yet I also have a firm intuition I cannot shake: when a parent who has earned and already been taxed on their money uses it to give their own child a safer or richer life — paying for tutoring, help with a down payment, or simply an inheritance — this does not feel like the child winning a lottery of birth. It feels like the parent exercising a legitimate, even admirable, form of partiality toward a particular person they love. I find something slightly monstrous in the idea of the state standing between a dying parent and their child to confiscate a large share of that gift on the ground that the child did not earn it. And the principle seems to have no natural stopping point: if the accident of birth is the problem, why is a financial bequest morally worse than inheriting a parent's accent, social network, habits of attention, or the years of bedtime reading that produced a child's verbal ability? Either the principle commits me to leveling all of that as well, which I reject, or the line I want to draw is ad hoc, which would dissolve the principle's tidiness.

So I want to know which should give way. Is my intuition merely a culturally inherited attachment to the family that a sound theory of equal opportunity should debunk and override, the way it overrides other inherited privileges? Or is the intuition tracking something the principle leaves out — that intimate relationships create special permissions that an egalitarian theory must accommodate rather than steamroll? And is there a reading of equal opportunity on which both the case for breaking up dynastic wealth and the legitimacy of parental partiality survive, or does taking either one seriously force me to abandon the other?
