So for my theology class we each have to do a fifteen minute presentation defending a position and then take questions, and I got the evolution one. My position is that animal suffering and death during the long evolutionary history before humans is compatible with a good God and with creation being called good, so the fossil record and predation before humans are not a serious problem for Christianity. My argument is going to run something like this: the goodness described in Genesis is about the whole system being alive, ordered, and fruitful the way a healthy ecosystem is good even though lions kill zebras, not about every individual animal having a pain-free life, so creation being good never meant zero animal pain or zero death. I also want to say that the death Paul talks about as entering through sin is human death, or the broken relationship between humans and God, not every death ever, so I dont have to deny that animals died earlier. And I want to hold the line that a good creator is not required to make a world with no animal pain at all; being good as a creator seems more about giving creatures the right kind of existence as the kinds of things they are, not making a pain-free theme park.

The room is not a random room. My teacher is a young-earth creationist who is going to think I'm quietly giving away the authority of scripture. There are three kids who accept evolution and are Christians but this exact issue is the biggest thing shaking their faith, and they want me to give them an actual answer rather than a dodge. There are two kids who will come from the angle that a God who used a process this cruel and wasteful just isnt good, full stop. And there is one kid who mostly cares that death before sin seems to mess up the whole fall-and-redemption plot.

I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense paper. Give me the objections in roughly the order I'll actually get them, worded the way each of those people would word them, not the versions a debate bro would use. For each one, tell me how much damage it really does, what I should concede on the spot, what I should hold, and the two minute reply I need to land. Also tell me the weakest part of my own argument and how I should prepare for it instead of hoping they miss it, and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.

Finally: keep separate the parts of this that are settled among theologians and philosophers who have actually worked on this from the parts that are genuinely contested, and don't present a contested reading of Genesis like it's just the consensus.
