In Dennett's rejoinder to Searle's Chinese Room, he accuses Searle of being a "two-bitser": someone who, after admitting that a system does everything the right way functionally, still demands one extra ingredient — original intentionality, real understanding, intrinsic aboutness — to turn it from a mere syntactic engine into a genuinely semantic one. Dennett's claim is that this is a cheat, because all intentionality is ultimately derived, and the boundary between original and derived is not a fact about the system but a feature of the interpretive stance we adopt toward it. Now consider the development of large language models. These systems are trained on vast corpora of human text to predict the next token; they have no sensory organs, no embedded goals, no robust world model of their own, and their outputs are generated without any explicit representation of what their words are about. Yet they produce long stretches of prose that, to many observers, look like the product of understanding, belief, and intention. Some critics say this shows exactly what Searle was worried about: syntax without semantics can go a surprisingly long way, which suggests there is a real difference between derived and original intentionality after all. Others say it vindicates Dennett: the more we see how far mere statistical pattern completion can go, the less reason there is to posit a special original intentionality that humans have and machines cannot.
