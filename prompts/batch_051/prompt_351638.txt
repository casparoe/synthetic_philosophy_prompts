I edit a small journal at the boundary of evolutionary biology and machine learning, and I have to rule on a terminology dispute in a paper I'm handling. The authors train a deep neural network with Adam (a form of stochastic gradient descent) and, in several places, describe the run as "evolution by natural selection": each gradient step is a "variation", they say, and the network that survives from one epoch to the next is the "individual" whose profitable variations have been preserved. A referee has objected that this is a misleading misuse of Darwin, and has pinned the objection to this passage from the sixth edition of the Origin of Species, Chapter III, "Struggle for Existence":

"Owing to this struggle, variations, however slight and from whatever cause proceeding, if they be in any degree profitable to the individuals of a species, in their infinitely complex relations to other organic beings and to their physical conditions of life, will tend to the preservation of such individuals, and will generally be inherited by the offspring."

The authors have replied that Darwin is here stating a perfectly general principle — roughly, that whatever changes are retained were advantageous in the environment that retained them — and that a training run satisfies that principle read literally. The referee says the passage cannot be lifted out of its setting: it presupposes a population of distinct individuals, heritable variation among them, and differential success in leaving offspring, none of which a single network's weights undergoing backpropagation have. I want you to arbitrate this, not by announcing your own view of evolution, but by working through what this passage does and does not license. Give me the verdict each of three readings yields: a strict reading of the words; a reading that treats the passage as a step in Darwin's argument in the Origin, with the function it has there; and a reading that asks what Darwin would most plausibly have said if he had been shown a modern gradient-descent run and asked whether it fell under this principle. Then say which reading should govern the journal's decision, and why — in particular, whether the authors are using "natural selection" in the passage's sense or only by analogy.
