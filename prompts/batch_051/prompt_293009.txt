Hey, I'm in a philosophy of perception seminar and we've spent the last two weeks on disjunctivism. I'm trying to collect the standard objections to it — the view that veridical perception and hallucination are fundamentally different kinds of mental state, even when their subjectively indistinguishable. So far I have four:

1. The best-explanation/common-kind objection: a veridical perception and a subjectively matching hallucination are introspectively indistinguishable, and the obvious explanation for that is a shared experiential kind. Disjunctivism denies the shared kind, so it owes us some other account of the indistinguishability.

2. The causal argument: if the same proximal neural state can produce either a veridical perception or a hallucination, then same cause, same immediate effect, and the two can't be different fundamental kinds after all.

3. The problem of illusion: illusions like the bent stick in water involve a real object but a wrong appearance, so they sit awkwardly between the good case and the bad case. Critics say disjunctivists either have to shove illusions into one of those boxes or give a really contorted account of what the erroneous appearance consists in.

4. The no-positive-account-of-hallucination objection: disjunctivism defines the bad case purely negatively, as the absence of the good case, and never tells us what a hallucination is in its own right.

I keep feeling like there's a fifth standard objection I'm forgetting, maybe something more epistemic. What is it, or what's a good candidate? It has to be genuinely different from these four, not a restatement or a special case of one of them. And be honest: is it actually any good?
