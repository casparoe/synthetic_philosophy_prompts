I'm a philosopher presenting a paper next month to a state sentencing commission's working group. The paper defends the thesis that sentencing's treatment of defendants with severe mental disorders should be organized around Strawson's distinction between the participant stance and the objective stance: in the participant stance we hold one another to expectations and feel reactive attitudes such as resentment, indignation, and gratitude, while in the objective stance we treat someone as an object of management, treatment, or control rather than as an addressable participant in moral life. My argument is roughly this: the law's existing cognitive and volitional tests keep trying to settle exemption or mitigation by asking whether the defendant understood right from wrong or could have done otherwise, but Strawson's framework shows that what actually guides judges in these cases is a prior question — whether the defendant is a possible target of the reactive attitudes at all. The participant/objective distinction, I claim, explains why psychosis and severe intellectual disability standardly exempt while personality disorder does not automatically exempt, and it does so without making sentencing depend on any metaphysical verdict about free will. The audience is about fifteen people: eight judges, four forensic psychiatrists, and three criminal-law scholars with philosophical leanings. The judges want workable rules and distrust vague standards; the psychiatrists regard psychopathy and impulse disorders as graded clinical realities that do not sort cleanly into two stances; the criminal-law scholars worry that letting reactive attitudes set the standard licenses retributive anger to pass as legal principle. Prepare me for the Q&A. Give me the objections I am actually likely to get, attributed to the kind of audience member who would raise them, phrased as that person would phrase them, and ordered by how likely and how damaging they are. For each, give a concise reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. Mark which objections have real force and tell me what I should concede about them up front rather than waiting to be pressed. Include the question I should be praying no one asks, and explain why it is the dangerous one. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — for this sheet, that means naming the one concession or the one reply that must land for the paper to survive this room — and then give the full prep sheet with no preamble and no restating of the setup.
