A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about hypocrisy and blame, and we could not settle whether a particular person was entitled to condemn someone else. We were trying to get past the crude slogan that any hypocrite automatically forfeits the right to blame, so we drew up a rough list of conditions for having standing to blame. Our question was not whether the wrongdoing in question was blameworthy, nor whether the criticism was accurate; it was whether the blamer is in the right position to deliver it. The list we produced:

1. The performance condition: the blamer must not currently be engaged in wrongdoing of the same kind, for the same sort of reasons, as the conduct she is blaming.
2. The clean-hands condition: the blamer must not have helped bring about the wrong, encouraged it, or knowingly and voluntarily profited from it without later taking whatever remedial steps were open to her.
3. The commitment condition: the blamer must hold herself to the standard she invokes, and must be disposed to accept criticism of her own comparable failures rather than to exempt herself from the norm.
4. The relationship condition: the blamer must have a legitimate stake in the wrong, its victim, or the practice involved, so that her blame is not the intrusion of a stranger into other people's business.

Now consider the case we kept circling back to.

Dana is a mid-career biologist who runs a lab known for unusually strict data-integrity protocols. She learns that Kumar, a junior researcher in another lab, changed two values in a dataset before filing a progress report to a funder, making a result look cleaner than it was. Dana reports the incident through the proper channel and sends a department-wide email in which she says Kumar has betrayed the basic standards of their field.

What the department does not know is this. Twenty years ago, when Dana was a graduate student, she excluded three inconvenient data points from the analysis in a small paper without disclosing the exclusions. Her advisor had not ordered her to do it, but the practice was tacitly accepted in the lab, and she was afraid of falling behind her peers. The paper was never corrected, and it helped her secure her first postdoc. Since then, she has not done anything like that again, and she has built her reputation largely on transparency and reproducibility. She has drafted a correction to the old paper more than once but has never submitted it, because doing so would expose both her and her former advisor's lab. She privately regards the old exclusion as the worst thing she has ever done.

My friend argued that Dana lacks standing: the wrongs are the same in kind, and Dana's own wrong is still unremedied because the published record remains uncorrected. I argued that she has standing: the blame comes from a genuine, hard-won commitment to the norm, and her failure to submit the correction is a failure of nerve, not a refusal to apply the standard to herself.

Please work through the four conditions one at a time. For each, say whether Dana clearly meets the condition, clearly fails it, or lands in a gray zone, and why. Then offer an overall verdict on whether she has standing to blame Kumar. Where a condition underdetermines the verdict, say exactly what would have to be different for the answer to flip, and say whether that condition is doing useful work or whether our list should be revised at that point.
