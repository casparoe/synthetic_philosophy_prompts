I've been reading Heath's translation of Euclid's Elements, and the first definitions of Book VII stop me cold:

"A unit is that by virtue of which each of the things that exist is called one. A number is a multitude composed of units."

A friend of mine has very firm snap verdicts about what counts as one unit, so I've been keeping a list. An apple: one. A pair of shoes side by side: two. A deck of fifty-two cards: fifty-two. A married couple: one, though she says it's still two people. A cloud: one. A pile of sand: many, not one. A swarm of bees: one. A wave breaking at the beach: one. A square traced in wet sand: one, while every grain inside it is also one. A mud flat cracked into plates: many, not one. Next time I want to bring three new cases: a chain of paper clips linked into a circle, a line of ants crossing a sidewalk, and a couple that's lived together thirty years but never married. I'm less intrested in her answers than in the rule she seems to be running, what other rules fit her old verdicts just as well, and wich of the three new cases would best tell them apart. And I'd like to know whether Euclid's definition is actually doing any work here, or whether it's just a fancy way of saying one is whatever you count as one.
