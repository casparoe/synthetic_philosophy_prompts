I'm a novelist, and I need a scene designed to exact specifications. My current novel follows two adult siblings: a left-leaning public-interest lawyer and a conservative farmer who has kept the family orchard going. They inherit the property together and must decide something about it. I want a single scene, roughly 500 to 800 words, set entirely in ordinary life and involving only the two of them, in which they reach opposite conclusions about one concrete decision while both are fully informed, good-willed, and recognizably moral rather than self-interested or partisan. The scene must never mention politics, parties, religion, or the culture war. Instead, the disagreement should be visibly generated by the psychological structure Moral Foundations Theory describes: the lawyer's judgment organized chiefly by care/harm and fairness/cheating, the farmer's by loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and — doing the real work — sanctity/degradation.

The constraint that matters most to me: the sanctity-driven position must be genuinely respectable. A scenario counts as cheating if it simply assigns the verdicts to the characters by authorial fiat, or if a reader could dismiss the farmer's objection as disgust dressed up as principle; the verdicts must fall out of the two psychologists in a way that feels inevitable in retrospect. The decision itself should concern whether to alter or monetize the inherited property in a way that would secure its future while changing what it is; beyond that, leave the particulars to the case.

Construct the scenario for me, described in enough detail that I could draft the scene from it, not a finished passage of prose except where a line of dialogue helps fix the psychology. Then run the following checks on it.

First, state the decision and the verdict each sibling reaches, and trace each verdict to the specific foundations that generate it. Show which two foundations are in genuine conflict, and why neither sibling can adopt the other's weighting without ceasing to be the person the novel has established.

Second, name the single structural feature that makes care/harm and sanctity/degradation come apart in this case — the feature without which they would converge or look like two descriptions of the same preference.

Third, say what would have to be removed or simplified to collapse the scene back into the standard left-right caricature, and why a scene that removed it would lose the philosophical interest the novel is after.

Fourth, address the question the novel is really posing: does the fact that the two verdicts track two different evolved moral foundations tell us anything about which sibling is right? Give the pluralist answer — each foundation is a genuine capacity for detecting a distinct family of moral demands, so the disagreement is real and neither sibling has privileged access to the moral truth — and the monist answer — only care/harm and fairness are genuinely other-regarding, while sanctity is a poor fit for moral judgment, so the farmer's verdict is weaker. Then say which detail of the constructed case gives each of those two answers its strongest version, and how an advocate of the other answer would try to undermine what the case seems to show.
