I work as a software engineer on a team that builds large-scale recommender systems, and next week I'm giving a 45-minute talk to my company's machine learning reading group. My thesis is that if we call natural selection an optimizer at all, it is a strikingly stupid one: it runs without gradient information against a moving target, with enormous selective waste and regular trapping in local solutions. The standard analogy between evolution and gradient descent is misleading in both directions, and machine learning practitioners should stop using evolution as rhetorical cover for SGD. The genuine lessons from evolution are narrower and mostly concern population-based, zeroth-order search, path dependence, and the regularizing role of developmental and structural constraints.

I'll argue it in roughly this order:

- SGD follows gradient information estimated from a sample against an explicit, effectively fixed loss function. Natural selection has no loss function and no gradient signal. Fitness is relative, frequency-dependent, and environment-dependent, and organisms partly construct their own environments, so the landscape is not fixed.
- The places where the analogy is mathematically respectable, such as certain replicator-dynamics results, only work under assumptions that fail in the cases people actually care about, including fixed adaptive landscapes and additive genetic variance.
- Evolution is better described as a population-based, zeroth-order search: many variants evaluated in parallel, hard selection bottlenecks, no foresight, and strong path dependence. It succeeds at planetary scale despite enormous waste, not because it is an efficient optimizer.
- The practical upshot is not that we should abandon gradient descent. It is that citing evolution as evidence that gradient-based optimization is sufficient gets the lesson backwards. The cases where evolution does things SGD so far cannot are precisely the cases where evolution least resembles gradient descent.

The audience is roughly 25 people, mostly ML engineers and research scientists who train deep models every day. Two attendees know population genetics well, and one comes from evolutionary computation and has implemented genetic algorithms and evolution strategies; he will defend the idea that evolutionary algorithms are really just clever finite-difference gradient estimators.
