I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a interactive piece about a classic puzzle in metaphysics, the clay statue. I'll set the scene for the piece the usual way: someone shapes a lump of clay into a statue, and we're supposed to ask whether the statue is the same thing as the clay. At first it seems like an easy yes, but then the differences keep popping up. If you flatten it tomorrow, the clay is still there, but most people want to say the statue isn't; if the clay is slowly replaced bit by bit while the statue stays on its pedestal, your answers might flip depending on whether you track the matter or the shape. And yet, right now, the statue and the clay occupy exactly the same place, have the same parts, and the scale doesn't read double. That's the tension I want to build the interactive around.

What I need from you is a decision-tree quiz for general readers that sorts people into positions on material constitution based on what they actually believe, not on what labels they recognize. The diagnostic questions should be concrete cases a layperson can answer without jargon: a squashed statue, a slowly replaced statue, the question of whether you'd count one thing or two in the same spot for purposes like weight, insurance, or a museum catalogue, and maybe a case where someone promises to sell the clay but keep the statue. Make sure the leaves include at least a one-object view (the statue just is the clay, and the apparent differences are differences in how we talk or what concepts we apply), a constitution view (the clay constitutes the statue but is not identical to it), an eliminativism or nihilism about statues (only the underlying clay, or the simples arranged statuewise, really exist), and a four-dimensionalist view (the statue and the clay are distinct things that overlap now but have different whole careers). Include a sortal-relative-identity leaf if your questions can actually reach it.

Build the tree from the logical space first: lay out the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the named positions inside that space. For any combination with no one in it, flag it and say briefly whether it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or just underexplored, and be honest about any two positions your questions cannot separate rather than pretending the tree is finer than it is. I'd rather a reader end up at 'the questions don't settle this bit' than get sorted by a distinction the cases don't track. Keep the tone playful but not dumb, so someone who finishes as a monist or a colocation pluralist feels they got a fair hearing, not gotchaed.
