So I'm not sure this is the kind of thing you can help with, but here goes. I grew up in Ohio and moved to Copenhagen about a year and a half ago, and the thing I can't shake is that my Danish coworkers and I have completely different first reactions to the state. When a letter from the tax agency arrives, they open it like its from a family member; I open it like its from a process server. I used to be confident that my suspicion was the reasonable attitude and theirs was just naive. Then last month the tax office here noticed an error I'd made in a filing, corrected it on their own, and sent me a small refund — no fine, no threat, just a note saying what they changed. It honestly made me cry a little, which I hate admitting. And it made me realize I can't even state what "reasonable trust in government" is supposed to be, so I can't defend my own reaction.

So here's the ask. Give me a working definition of "reasonable trust in government" — the attitude a citizen has when they treat a government communication, program, or official as on the level without checking, hedging, or lawyering up first. I've got some cases, and I want the definition to classify them the way I'd defend on a good day:

1. Two days after a contamination notice, the city health department texts that the tap water is safe again, and you drink it without boiling.
2. You fill out a municipal needs survey asking about your religion or ethnicity, assuming it won't be used to profile you.
3. A cop at a traffic stop says searching your trunk is "standard procedure," and you agree even though you'd rather not.
4. You enroll your teenager in a free city counseling program, knowing the school district gets anonymized summaries of the data.
5. You vote to raise your own property taxes because a city engineering report predicts it will fund better storm drains.
6. You call the building department about your neighbor's sagging retaining wall, expecting they'll make him fix it rather than fining him into bankruptcy or doing nothing for a year.
7. During a pandemic, you take a vaccine distributed by the state even though the approving agency let a dangerous drug through a decade ago.
8. In a neighborhood with a long history of redlining, a family refuses a free municipal lead-testing program because they think the results will be used against them in housing or custody disputes.

Here's where I'm at: 1 and 5 feel like clear cases of trust, 3 feels like it isn't trust at all (it feels like fear or submission), and 7 is where I'm genuinely torn. I also suspect 8 is more reasonable than it looks even if it doesn't come out as "trust," and I'd like the definition to say whether reasonableness is part of trust or a separate judgment about it. I keep suspecting the phrase is doing two jobs — one about whether the institution has earned it, and one about whether it's smart for me to extend it — and if you think that's right, split the definition and say which cases fall under one reading but not the other. Propose the definition, run it through all eight, tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and then either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. And flag the classification you're least secure about.
