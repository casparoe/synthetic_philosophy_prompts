I'm putting together a unit for an undergraduate course on social epistemology, and I want to build a session around Peter Weir's The Truman Show and the distinction between epistemic bubbles and echo chambers that C. Thi Nguyen has made influential. As I understand the distinction, an epistemic bubble is a structural lack of exposure to relevant outside voices: contrary information never reaches you, so the problem is an absence. An echo chamber is something more active and harder to escape: it shapes whom you trust, so that when contrary voices do reach you, you have already been trained to dismiss them as unreliable. My working hypothesis is that the film is usually misread as a pure bubble narrative. Truman's world does omit nearly all evidence of an outside, but what interests me is the machinery that switches on once anomalies begin leaking in—the falling studio light, the intercepted radio transmission, the scripted reassurances from his wife Meryl and his best friend Marlon, and the way Sylvia, who tried to tell him the truth, is hurried away and treated as delusional. I want to argue that the film shows a bubble being reinforced, once it springs leaks, by echo-chamber tactics aimed at managing trust rather than merely filtering information.

I would like a close reading that tests this hypothesis rather than simply illustrating either concept. Where does the distinction genuinely illuminate the film—for instance, what separates the Marlon scene from ordinary lying, or why Sylvia's testimony has to be neutralized rather than ignored? Where does the film fit the bubble-versus-echo-chamber distinction cleanly, where does it blur it, and where does it resist being captured by it? I am especially interested in what the film adds to the philosophical account: I suspect it dramatizes the role of brute sensory anomalies and firsthand evidence in breaking a managed epistemic environment, since Truman ultimately cannot be talked back into the fold and must instead trust his own eyes over the people closest to him. I will be teaching this to students who know the film well but have not studied epistemology, so I want an account that stays close to widely remembered scenes and the two concepts rather than assuming scholarly background or requiring the students to look anything up.
