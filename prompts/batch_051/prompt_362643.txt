The other day I was deep in hyperparameter tuning and went down a rabbit hole on the no free lunch theorem: averaged over all possible objective functions, every optimizer, including random search, has the same expected performance. The only way an algorithm ever beats random search is by carrying an inductive bias that matches the structure of the particular class of problems it actually faces.

Then a podcast I listen to did an episode on Hume, and the host described his problem of induction roughly like this: any inference from past experience to the future has to assume nature is uniform, but that assumption can't be justified by experience, because that would be circular, or by pure reason, because denying it isn't a contradiction. And it hit me that these sound like the same point: data by itself can't prefer one generalization over another; you have to bring something like a prior over which patterns or worlds are likely before evidence can teach you anything. My Bayesian friend says yes, obviously, that's just the point that you need a prior and philosophers were slow to notice.

But I can't tell whether the no free lunch theorem is actually a formal version of Hume, or whether I'm just pattern-matching two half-understood ideas. If the mapping is genuine, what exactly has to hold on both sides for the theorem to say anything deep about induction? If it's superficial, where does the analogy break, and what's a concrete prediction the analogy makes that turns out false? I keep going back and forth on whether this is deep or just trivially true.
