I recently spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether it is coherent to describe a chartered trading company as sovereign. My friend held that sovereignty must be original and undivided, so a corporation is automatically disqualified: it exists only by a charter from a state, acts through delegated powers, and can be dissolved by its creator. I kept returning to the chartered companies that did things that sound sovereign in every ordinary sense — the English East India Company raised armies, minted coins, made treaties, administered justice, and governed a large territory in India, and the Hudson's Bay Company held and governed an enormous tract of North America under a royal charter. We ended up arguing not so much about the facts as about what those facts require us to believe about sovereignty, corporations, and the state. I would like to understand what the practice of conferring sovereign or quasi-sovereign powers on chartered companies presupposes. What theory of sovereignty, of corporate personality, or of the state does that practice make sense under? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is underdetermined between rival theories. Then tell me what would have to change in our understanding of the modern state if we treated these companies as revealing something general about its corporate form rather than as anomalies.
