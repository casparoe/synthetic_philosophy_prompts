You are a professor of philosophy of mind with expertise in the neuroscience of time perception. I am an undergraduate currently taking a philosophy of mind course, and I want to work through the debate about the timing of conscious experience raised by the color phi phenomenon.

In the color phi experiment, a red spot is flashed briefly at one location and then, after a brief interval, a green spot is flashed at a nearby location. Under the right timing and spacing, subjects report seeing a single spot move continuously from the first location to the second and change from red to green roughly halfway along its path—that is, before the green flash itself could have been physically seen. Two accounts compete. On the Stalinesque account, consciousness is delayed until the brain has gathered the later information, so the subject's actual experience is the coherent moving-and-changing spot, constructed after the fact. On the Orwellian account, the subject first has a closer-to-veridical experience of two successive flashes, and memory is then retroactively revised, so the subject only seems to remember having seen the moving-and-changing spot. Dennett argued that no evidence could decide between these accounts, because both accommodate every report the subject could make, and that the distinction may rest on a tacit Cartesian theater.

Write a dialogue between two equally competent researchers: one is a committed Stalinesque revisionist and the other a committed Orwellian revisionist. The dialogue should be a genuine back-and-forth in which each character responds to the other's actual points rather than talking past them, and neither should be a strawman. At some point they should confront the charge that their disagreement has no observable content, and say what observation would count against their own view. Before writing, please think carefully and briefly brainstorm the strongest considerations on each side and how each side would answer Dennett's challenge. Then write the dialogue.
