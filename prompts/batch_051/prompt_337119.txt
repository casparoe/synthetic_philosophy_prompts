I would like you to act as a philosopher of mind and language who also has a good feel for experimental design. A colleague and I have been arguing about Moore's paradox, and while each of us can tell a tidy story, neither of us can say what would actually decide between the two stories. I want your help thinking through whether they can be separated empirically at all.

Here is the datum we are trying to explain. A sentence such as 'The café is closed, but I don't believe that it is closed' is bizarre to assert, though it can easily be true and is not a formal contradiction. The third-person counterpart ('The café is closed, but she doesn't believe that it is closed') and the past-tense counterpart ('The café was closed, but I didn't believe that it was closed') are unremarkable, so the oddity attaches to the present-tense first-person combination rather than to the content as such. The closely related sentence 'The café is closed, but I don't know that it is closed' seems just as defective, so an adequate account should handle both.

My colleague defends what we have been calling the assertion view. On this view the defect is a violation of a norm on the speech act of assertion. In asserting something, one represents oneself as believing it, or as knowing it; the second conjunct then explicitly cancels the self-representation that the first conjunct has just made. The corresponding mental state is odd only derivatively, because asserting the sentence publicly performs that cancellation.

I am inclined towards what we have been calling the state view. On this view the defect already lies in the first-person present-tense mental state. A thinker cannot be in a correct state of believing the whole sentence, because believing the first conjunct would falsify the second conjunct's self-ascription; the sentence merely tries to make public a state that could not be correctly held. Assertion, on this account, is the messenger, not the culprit.

I would like you to design the strongest test you can of the claim that the defect is located in assertion rather than in belief. Be concrete about the conditions, measures and comparisons: what would participants hear, read or do, and what pattern of results would count as evidence for the assertion view and what pattern for the state view? If the best evidence is observational rather than experimental, say so. Then say what a clean result in either direction would and would not show, and be explicit about what the operationalisation of a conceptual claim like this inevitably leaves out. If the strongest case requires several converging conditions rather than one critical experiment, say plainly why a single experiment cannot do the work.

Please think through two or three candidate tests before settling on your final proposal, and reject the ones that would not actually discriminate between the two views. Your final response should be written as continuous prose, the way one would write a careful email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
