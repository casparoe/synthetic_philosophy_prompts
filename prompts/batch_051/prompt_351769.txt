I keep coming back to a parallel between Émilie du Châtelet's account of happiness and the social-psychology literature on positive illusions, and I cannot decide whether it is a real convergence or a false friend. In her Discours sur le bonheur, Du Châtelet argues that much of what makes a human life happy — above all love and ambition — depends on systematic illusion about the beloved, about one's prospects, or about the rewards the world will actually deliver; she observes, for example, that love tends to last only as long as the lover's flattering picture of the beloved survives. Her position is not to strip such illusions away but to govern them so that they do not become a source of misery. The positive-illusions research program associated with Taylor and Brown claims something similar at the level of individual self-appraisal: mildly self-enhancing and over-optimistic beliefs function as supports of well-being rather than as defects to be corrected, and the surrounding work on depressive realism suggests that greater accuracy about oneself is not on the side of happiness. So both accounts put false or unwarranted belief in a causal supporting role for well-being, and both make accuracy look like a threat to it.

But I suspect the parallel may be only verbal, and I want it tested. The two positions seem to differ in at least three ways. First, Du Châtelet writes prescriptively about what a reflective person should do to become happy, whereas the psychology is largely descriptive and correlational. Second, her illusions are mainly directed outward at other people and future outcomes, whereas positive illusions are usually self-referential beliefs about one's own traits, capacities, and degree of control. Third, Du Châtelet ends by ranking the passion for study above love and ambition as the most reliable route to happiness because its satisfactions depend least on other people, which seems to introduce a source of happiness whose value does not lie in illusion. Is the resemblance deep, as two independent articulations of one idea about the relation between well-being and epistemic accuracy? Is it superficial, with the shared notion of illusion doing the real work? Or should we read the psychology as vindicating Du Châtelet's descriptive psychology while leaving her normative ranking untouched? Give me a precise mapping of where the two accounts coincide and where they come apart, and a verdict on what the comparison actually teaches about the supposed trade-off between happiness and seeing things as they are. If the parallel were exact, name something we should expect to find in one domain but do not — and say whether that mismatch is fatal to the parallel or merely a difference of emphasis.
