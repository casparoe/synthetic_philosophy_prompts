I've recently quit a job doing UX copywriting and conversion optimization because I couldn't shake the feeling that a lot of my best work was just manipulation with cleaner typography. Now I'm sensitized to it everywhere, including at home: my partner keeps saying I "steer" conversations, and I genuinely can't tell anymore which of the things I do are fair persuasion and which are manipulative. Before I go read the usual theories, I want the desiderata first. What does any adequate account of the manipulation / legitimate persuasion line have to be able to do? I mean: the cases it has to get right, the edge cases it has to explain rather than wave off, the distinctions it has to respect, and the demands people make that are really just one camp's prejudices or nice-to-haves. Give me the case or consideration behind each requirement, flag anywhere you suspect two desiderata can't be jointly satisfied, and say which requirements are negotiable and which aren't.

In particular, I want to know whether a decent theory is obliged to handle three things, and if so whether they pull in different directions. First, manipulation that isn't deceptive: a true emotional confession timed to disarm someone, or a friend who uses your own stated commitments against you without lying or hiding anything. Second, manipulation without any manipulative intent, like an app whose defaults exploit loss aversion because the early design team just copied a pattern and nobody ever meant to exploit anyone. Third, self-manipulation: me setting up my own environment so I'll cave to certain pressures later, or scheduling an email so my future self has to send it. If a theory has to cover all three, does that make it impossible to keep the distinction crisp? And which of these demands does the most work in ruling out otherwise tempting accounts?
