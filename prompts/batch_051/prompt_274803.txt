I'm a software engineer, not a biologist, and I'm building the data ingestion layer for a long-term ecological monitoring dataset. The field protocol from the early 1990s that governs how observations were recorded contains this counting rule, which I have to implement in code:

"Count as one individual each living body that has developed from a single fertilized egg and is not attached to any other living body of the same genetic constitution. Where several living bodies of the same genetic constitution are attached to one another, count the attached group as a single individual."

The protocol was written around surveys of birds, small mammals, and solitary insects, but the dataset I'm integrating now contains observations the authors clearly never anticipated, and the rule gives strange or unstable results on them.

First, there is a stand of quaking aspen: thousands of above-ground trunks connected underground by a single shared root system, all genetically identical and presumed to be one clone from one original seed. A literal reading makes the whole stand one individual, provided the roots are still connected; where a connection has been severed, the same clone becomes two individuals on paper.

Second, there are records of the Portuguese man o' war (Physalia physalis), which develops from a single fertilized egg, yet its body is a colony of attached zooids — a gas-filled float, stinging tentacles, feeding parts, and reproductive parts — each homologous to a whole animal in a related free-living species. The literal rule makes the whole colony one individual.

Third, there is a rock with several hundred sea anemones, all clones of a single founder produced by repeated fission and physically separate from one another. The rule's attachment condition treats each polyp as a separate body, but its developmental condition excludes everything except the original founder from the count, so a literal application would count almost none of the animals actually present.

Fourth, there are lichens: stable, integrated composites of a fungus and an alga or cyanobacterium (what many biologists would now call holobionts). Whether one treats a lichen as one composite body or as two attached partners, neither the developmental condition nor the shared-genotype condition is met, so a strict reading of the rule seems to put no individual in the record at all.

I'm less interested in a number for each case than in what the different approaches to interpretation give, and which should govern. For each case, tell me: what does the rule as written require; what would the rule's evident purpose in a population-monitoring program require; and what would the authors of the protocol most plausibly have said if they had been shown these cases before finalizing the text? Where those readings diverge, which should I actually implement, and why? If I amend the rule to handle these cases cleanly, what does that amendment quietly commit me to about whether a clonal stand, a colony, or a lichen is one organism, and could any amendment stay neutral on that question?
