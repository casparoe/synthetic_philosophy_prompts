I am a novelist, and I have been trying to work out what it would mean, after Orwell, to write political prose rather than prose that merely touches political subjects. Suppose I extract from Orwell a working set of criteria for prose that is political in the strong sense: it is concrete and visualizable; it is built from fresh or at least honest imagery rather than the exhausted figures he catalogued, the dying metaphors and pretentious diction and verbal false limbs that no longer make anyone see anything; it closes the gap between a writer's real and declared aims instead of papering over it; and it keeps thought capable of dissent because a reader can check what is actually being said. Now consider a book I am planning, and tell me honestly whether it qualifies under those criteria. Its narrator is a communications officer at a large humanitarian organization, and the entire novel is written in that working voice: monitoring reports, funding proposals, impact statements, internal emails dense with the words stakeholders, outcomes, capacity-building, sustainability. The prose is deliberately made almost entirely out of the ready-made abstractions Orwell condemned; nothing is parodied, because there is no outside voice mocking from above, and the style remains grammatically transparent, even elegant in a corporate way. The author's stated aim, which appears nowhere on the page, is diagnostic: to show by inhabiting the language how it makes certain thoughts unsayable, such as the thought that the organization is doing harm or that the narrator does not believe a word she writes. The criticism would exist only in the accumulating evasions and dead spots of the voice itself. Is this book political prose in the Orwellian sense my criteria try to capture, or is it a clear case of the failure those criteria exist to name? Go through the criteria one by one, mark where they underdetermine the verdict, and then say whether the case shows that my criteria need revision, for instance whether they should demand a clean prose that stands outside the criticized language, or whether they should credit irony and formal inhabitation as an alternative route to the honesty Orwell wanted. Please answer in continuous prose only, as in a letter or email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
