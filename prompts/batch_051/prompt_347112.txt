I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy or law background, so stop me if I've mangled the idea. I keep running into this while we write the governance docs for our DAO. The rule of thumb I was taught for legal personhood goes something like: a thing counts as a legal person if it can hold rights and duties in its own name — own stuff, enter contracts, sue and be sued, and keep existing when the humans behind it change. A corporation gets this by filing papers with some state and then the law treats it as a person. Fine.

Now here's the borderline case. Our DAO is a set of smart contracts on a chain with a treasury the code controls. Token holders vote on proposals and the code pays out automatically once a proposal passes. There's a written governance charter. It can hire a dev shop, pay out grants, and if every founding member quit the whole thing would keep running, because the contracts don't care who calls them as long as the call follows the rules. What it can't do in its own name is show up in court or sign a paper contract; some human or a wrapped LLC has to front for it.

So: is the DAO a legal person under the criteria above, or close enough that the gap is just theater? Go through the checklist and tell me which criteria are doing the real work and which are decoration. And here's what actually bugs me: if a corporation is itself a legal fiction, a thing the law pretends is a person because the pretense is useful, then isn't the DAO just another useful pretense, and the real question is only whether that pretense is worth maintaining? I want to know wich way to push — wrap the DAO in a traditional entity, or argue it already is one in every sense that matters — and where exactly the definition runs out and someone just has to decide.
