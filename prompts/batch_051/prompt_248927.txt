I've been retired a few years now — I spent my career in physical chemistry, so I'm used to thinking about evidence and thresholds and false positives, though not in this vocabulary. Recently I've been reading epistemology, and I've got a bit stuck on the debate between pragmatic encroachment and contextualism about knowledge attributions. As I understand it, contextualists say the word "knows" picks out different standards in different contexts, while pragmatic encroachers say the word means the same thing everywhere, but whether someone actually knows can depend on how much is practically at stake — high stakes demand better evidence.

I keep thinking about Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men. A boy is on trial for murdering his father, and if convicted he faces the death penalty; the evidence initially looks overwhelming, and most of the jurors say they know he did it, though one of the jurors insists they can't be sure. Its a natural test case for these theories, but I'm not sure which way the film actually cuts. I'd like a careful reading of the film through this debate. Does it dramatise the idea that the stakes — a boy's life — should raise the evidential bar for claiming to know? Or is the real story more about contextual shifts in what "reasonable doubt" demands, or about group psychology, prejudice, and the difference between legal proof and knowledge? Where does the film support a pragmatic-encroachment or contextualist reading, and where does it resist it?

I'm leading a discussion on it for a local philosophy group, and I don't want a film review. I want to know which scenes a philosopher would point to, whether the film ends up vindicating one of these two views or neither, and whether it shows something about knowledge that the journal articles miss.
