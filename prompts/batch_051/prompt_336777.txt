I'm a PhD student in political philosophy working on labor, and I've been roped in to teaching a 75-minute session for an undergrad/grad seminar on work and politics. About 18 students, mixed backgrounds. I want the session to be a game/role-play rather than a lecture. The thing I want them to feel before anyone names it is this: a strike isn't just a bunch of individual decisions not to work. Once there's a bargaining unit or union, majority decisions bind the minority, solidarity has a coercive edge, and that bindingness is both what gives the workers power and what makes the right to organize hard to defend to an individualist. I also want them to hit the coordination/threshold problem themselves—everyone will strike only if enough others do, but no one wants to be the one who walks out alone.

Could you design the activity? Please include roles (workers with different outside options and vulnerabilities; maybe a union rep; a manager), a points-based payoff scheme that doesn't need real money, a secret willingess-to-strike ballot that then feeds into a binding majority vote, a strike phase where workers can secretly keep working or cross the line, a management response, and a timeline for 75 minutes. The rules should be specific enough that I can run it without improvising to much. No lecture up front, and the students shouldn't hear labels like 'collective action problem' or 'assurance game' until the debrief.

Then give me a debrief that maps what happened during the game onto the ideas: exit vs voice, assurance and threshold effects, free-riding, the majority binding the minority, and how an individual opt-out right looks both attractive and like it dissolves the power the group just built. Say what's likely to go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it—I suspect some groups will collude in the debrief, or nobody will strike because the stakes feel fake—and be honest about what the game can't reproduce, like the real fear of losing your job, legal protection or its absence, and union culture built over years. The point isn't to get them to a pro-union view, it's to get them to see the tension between binding collective action and individual exit without flattening either side.

Oh, and please write the whole thing in American English.
