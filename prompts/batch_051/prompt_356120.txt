I was assigned part of Aristotle’s On Sophistical Refutations for school, and I was struck by how he starts by saying he will treat arguments that look like refutations but are really fallacies. He treats this as a general skill, not just as a mistake that bad arguers happen to make.

That made me think about a contemporary idea I had read about in AI safety. Some researchers have proposed that we could evaluate the claims of very capable AI systems by having two AIs debate each other in front of a human judge. The thought is roughly that if one AI gives a false answer and another gives a true answer, and the human judge can ask questions, the true side will be able to defend its position while the false side will eventually get tangled up or exposed.

But Aristotle’s opening observation seems to put this proposal under pressure. The debate setup assumes that a human judge can tell the difference between a real refutation and something that merely looks like a refutation. Modern language models, however, are often trained to produce text that human raters find plausible, satisfying, and persuasive, even when the underlying reasoning is flawed. That sounds very close to the skill Aristotle describes. So it seems possible to build systems that are exactly good at producing sophistical refutations—arguments that win the debate without being sound.

My question is whether the debate-based safety argument survives this development. Which premise does Aristotle’s point actually threaten? And is there a repaired version of the AI safety via debate argument that says something weaker but still important, while taking seriously the possibility that systems will be optimized for persuasive fallacies?
