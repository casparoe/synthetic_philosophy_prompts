I am a PhD student in philosophy working on practical rationality, and I am preparing a talk on weakness of will and why it is so often modelled with hyperbolic rather than exponential discounting. I would like to open the talk with a poem that lets the audience feel the structure before I name it. Please write a sonnet in which the philosophical content does structural work. In the octave, the speaker should be at a temporal distance from two goods, a smaller-sooner one and a larger-later one, and should plan, with steady conviction, to take the larger-later good; the rhythm and mood of these eight lines should be regular and settled, as a plan feels when all the relevant outcomes are still remote. The volta should be the reversal: the sooner good has drawn close, and its present value has steepened until it outweighs the later good, so the choice flips even though the speaker's judgement about which outcome is better has not changed. The sestet should render the akratic texture of that moment — the speaker still regards the later good as the better one while choosing the sooner one — and it should keep this distinct from the reversal itself: the reversal is the work of the discount curve, while the stable judgement is what makes the episode akratic rather than a mere change of preference. The closing couplet should say, without jargon, exactly what has occurred: not a change of mind about what matters, but the inflation of a nearby good until it occludes a larger one further off. To make the form itself track the idea, I would like the final six lines to shorten, syllable by syllable, so the verse visibly steepens the way a hyperbolic discount curve does. It should be precise enough that a philosopher working on akrasia and temporal discounting would recognise the mechanism rather than wince at the rendering, and good enough that someone who has never encountered the literature would still feel the reversal. Begin with the poem itself, with no preface or commentary, and write in British English.
