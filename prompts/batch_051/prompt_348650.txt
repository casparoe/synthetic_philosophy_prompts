As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I keep coming back to a puzzle that sits awkwardly between formal epistemology and the psychology of reasoning, and I would like you to work through it with me as rigorously as you can. You are a philosopher of logic and formal epistemology; treat this as a thinking exercise rather than a literature question, and reason from the puzzle itself rather than from named authors.

The puzzle is the surprise exam. A teacher announces on Friday that next week there will be exactly one exam, and that the students will not know the night before which day it will be held. The students reason backward. If the exam has still not happened by Thursday night, then only Friday remains, so Friday would be fully predictable and the exam would not come as a surprise; Friday is therefore ruled out. But once Friday is ruled out, Thursday becomes the last possible day, and by Wednesday night it is ruled out in the same way; and so the elimination marches backward until no day remains. The argument is finite, simple, and apparently airtight, and it has exactly the virtues that make a piece of theory feel irresistible: a small set of premises about knowledge yields a definite verdict through a tidy backward-induction procedure.

Against this stands a firm and very ordinary intuition: teachers make announcements of roughly this sort all the time, and students are surprised. A teacher who sets the exam for Wednesday has not done something logically impossible. The conviction here is not that students sometimes feel startled; it is that a rational student, fully apprised of the announcement, can still fail to know the night before which day has been chosen, even when the range of possible days is finite and common knowledge. I find the argument compelling, and I find the intuition compelling, and I do not want to dissolve the conflict too quickly by saying that the word surprise is vague.

I want a negotiated verdict, not a winner declared by fiat. Before you commit to anything, brainstorm at least three or four distinct diagnoses of where the tension could be coming from, and state each fairly. Then do three things. First, set out as precisely as you can what the backward-induction argument must assume: what must the students know about the teacher's announcement, which rules must their knowledge obey (for example, closure under known consequence), and whether they may legitimately treat the announcement as something they know will hold, for the elimination of Friday to go through? Identify the weakest set of assumptions under which the conclusion follows, and say whether any single assumption is quietly carrying the weight. Second, put the intuition in its strongest form: not as a claim about actual classroom surprise, but as a claim about what a rational student could fail to know. Third, run through the live resolutions and price each one. On one resolution, the intuition should be debunked: the argument is sound for a particular idealized reading of knowledge, and our sense that a surprise exam is possible tracks a weaker psychological state, or rests on an ill-formed announcement. On another, the theory should be amended or abandoned: one of the premises fails, and the argument is invalid. On a third, both survive under a distinction that has not yet been drawn, such as a gap between knowing a day and being able to act on or assert that knowledge, or between the students' information and the students' ability to make that information explicit. Tell me which resolution you think is ultimately correct, and what a defender of each rival position would need to demonstrate to change your mind. If you judge the argument sound, say exactly what you think is true on Tuesday night of the week in question; if you judge it unsound, identify the precise inference at which it fails and explain why the backward induction nevertheless felt so convincing.
