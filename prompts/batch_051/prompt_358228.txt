I have spent a while trying to pin down what changes when two or more people stop merely doing the same thing at the same time and start genuinely doing something together. I can feel the difference, but I cannot state it, and my attempts keep falling apart when I press them. Here are the cases I have been using to test any definition.

Two friends agree to carry a heavy bookcase down a flight of stairs, counting down together before they lift. A man hired to load boxes and a friend helping out of affection do not know each other's motives, but they still coordinate their timing while carrying a table. Two strangers at a station both step forward, without speaking or making eye contact, to help a third person lift a pram up the stairs, and each adjusts to the other mid-lift. A conductor leads an orchestra through a piece; several players privately despise the conductor and would play otherwise if they dared, but they follow all the same. A crowd panics and runs for the same exit, and the pressure of the mass pushes the door open. Two pedestrians heading along the same street unconsciously match pace for a block, each lost in thought and unaware of the other. A corporation pollutes a river through the routine work of dozens of employees under a policy written by managers who left years ago. Two people agree to rob a bank together, but one is an undercover officer who only pretends to agree and plans to stop it before anyone is hurt.

I want a working definition of acting together, or a principled explanation of why no single definition will do, that separates the cases in which there is one activity shared between people from cases of parallel, coincidental, or merely caused-together behaviour. I am fairly sure the first case is acting together and the two pedestrians are not, but I want the definition to settle the others and to show me which of my preliminary judgements I should revise. Please answer in British English.
