I spent my working life in the physical sciences, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science with a local discussion group. The reading has made me reconsider something I used to take for granted: nearly every model I ever relied on was built on assumptions I knew to be false, and in practice that rarely seemed to matter. Our group now divides, roughly, into two camps about a question I never had to answer explicitly while working: when is a false assumption harmless, and when does it cost a model its right to count as explanatory? One camp takes an instrumentalist line, close to what is sometimes called Friedman's F-twist: a model's assumptions are to be judged not by whether they describe the world but by whether the model predicts well enough for the purpose at hand, and the demand for realistic assumptions is a leftover piece of essentialism. The other camp holds that idealizations can properly figure in explanations only when they are controlled and in principle removable — when one can say how much an idealization costs, show that the omitted factor is inert or small in the relevant regime, or derive the idealization as an approximation. On that view, a model whose false assumptions cannot be so disciplined is a predictive instrument, not an explanation, however excellent its forecasts.

Two members of the group — call them the F-twist instrumentalist and the controlled-idealization theorist — have now been arguing for months, and I have become convinced that they are partly talking past one another. I would like you to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between them: the document both could sign after honest negotiation. Please include, first, a numbered list of substantive claims about models, idealization, prediction, and explanation that both camps can accept without reservation — concerning, for example, frictionless surfaces, idealized billiard-ball gases, and black-box predictive models with no mechanistic content whatsoever — where the claims should be concrete enough that each side is at least mildly uncomfortable signing them. Then include a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is first stated in neutral wording and then restated in wording each side would accept for its own position. Then set out the cruxes: the sub-questions a verdict on which would move at least one side, and for each crux a concrete model or case that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction or interpretation recorded in advance.

Finally, indicate which parts of their dispute, if any, are merely verbal — for instance, whether the word "explanation" has quietly become the contested term — and say what would remain genuinely open once that is cleared away. The aim is not to rule in favor of either camp. It is to separate what they can jointly assert from the exact places where they still part company, so that a reader learns the real architecture of the disagreement rather than another caricature of it.
