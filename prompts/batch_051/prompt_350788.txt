I'm a PhD student in social ontology and African philosophy, and I keep getting stuck on the Ubuntu idea that you become a person through other people rather than just being one by virtue of being born human. The literature treats this as a relational vs. individualist conception of the person, but I've never seen a working definition sharp enough to actually run through cases.

Give me a working definition of "person" (or "personhood," your pick) that classifies the following cases. I have firm intuitions about some and none about others:

- a healthy newborn
- a 40-year-old in a persistent vegetative state
- an elder who has been formally expelled from their community for a serious offense and is now shunned by everyone who used to know them
- a corporation
- an LLM that people consistently treat as a conversational partner
- an ancestor who died decades ago but is remembered, spoken to, and appealed to in rituals
- a human adult raised with no human language or social contact (a feral child, not a thought experiment)

I'm confident the newborn is a person and the corporation isn't. I flip-flop on the ancestor and the expelled elder. The feral child feels like the real test for the relational view: biologically human, zero community.

I suspect the word "person" is doing at least two jobs — one descriptive (a certain kind of organism or mind) and one normative/relational (something you can wrong, or something constituted by recognition). If you agree, give me both definitions and say which cases move between them. Either way: propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, flag any case where it gives a verdict I'd reject or no verdict at all, and then revise it or defend the suprising verdict.

Format: exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
