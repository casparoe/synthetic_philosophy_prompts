I'm helping open a small two-day workshop called Geometry and the State, and I've got to give the welcome remarks. The room will be maybe forty people: about half are historians of political thought who can pretty much recite the relevant chapters of Leviathan, and the other half are working geometers who've never read Hobbes and don't want to feel like they're being assigned research. I want the speech to use Hobbes's views on geometry as the bridge between the two camps, not as a way to show off. The idea I'd like woven through is that Hobbes took geometry to be the one science God has given mankind, because only in geometry do we start from definitions we've made ourselves and so avoid the endless disputes over words that plague other fields — and that this is why he wanted politics to reach the same kind of certainty. Could you write me a five-minute opening speech that makes that point land as an invitation to the workshop rather than a lecture? Please don't talk down to the geometers about what geometry is, and don't explain to the Hobbes people what Leviathan says. Also, if you can avoid the cliché of using squaring the circle as a cheap metaphor for failure, that would be a relief.

At the very end, give a one-sentence summary as the last line.
