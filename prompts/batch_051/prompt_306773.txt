I'm writing a philosophy of law paper on desuetude — roughly, the idea that a statute can lapse or lose its force through prolonged non-enforcement — and I keep getting stuck on a question one step before the one I'm supposed to answer. The paper asks whether desuetude is compatible with the rule of law, or whether it is just a polite name for officials quietly ignoring statutes they no longer like. But to answer that, I need a working definition of what it is for a law to be in force. The obvious definition, duly enacted and not yet repealed, would make desuetude impossible by stipulation, and it also seems to misdescribe dead-letter statutes that everyone treats as law in name only. So I'd like you to propose a definition of a law's being in force and test it against the cases below. A confident yes or no is fine, and a hedged "partly" is also fine, provided you say why.

Here are the cases: (1) an adultery statute that has been on the books for eighty years and was last enforced sixty years ago, and that no current official would dream of prosecuting; (2) a jaywalking ordinance that is enforced only a handful of times a year, unpredictably; (3) a statute held unconstitutional by the highest court years ago, never repealed and still printed in the code; (4) a law enacted yesterday whose effective date is six months in the future; (5) a facially neutral law that is enforced steadily but in practice almost exclusively against one ethnic group; (6) a pollution regulation meant seriously but allocated no enforcement budget and never once applied; and (7) an inheritance custom that everyone in a community follows and enforces through social pressure, though no legislature ever enacted it. I suspect the definition will need to appeal to enforcement expectations or official dispositions, but I cannot make that precise without either losing case (4) or collapsing law into case (7). Propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, say where it gives surprising or wrong answers, and revise it as needed. Tell me how confident you are in the final definition and which of your seven verdicts you are least sure of. End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states the final definition in one sentence.
