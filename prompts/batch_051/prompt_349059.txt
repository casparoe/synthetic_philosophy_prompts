Next month I'm presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop, and I want to spend my prep time on the Q&A rather than the talk itself. My position, roughly: the historical expansion of the moral circle — kin, tribe, nation, all humans, then the messy partial push toward nonhuman animals and future people — is real, but the usual explanations have the causal direction backwards. It wasn't mainly better arguments that widened concern, and it wasn't an evolved switch toward impartiality doing the work on its own. It was mostly falling costs: trade made strangers useful instead of dangerous, literacy and mass media made distant suffering cheap to see, cities made difference ordinary, and states forced people into repeated cooperative contact with out-groups. Moral argument mostly came after, rationalizing and consolidating concern that had already become affordable, and it rarely led the expansion. The audience is mostly evolutionary moral psychologists and a couple of anthropologists who are convinced moral judgment is a cluster of emotion-backed intuitions shaped by group selection, and they're suspicious of any claim that institutions do the normative work. There are also two economic historians who think moral language is mostly cheap post hoc justification for material interests, plus one Kantian philosopher who thinks the expanding circle is just what you'd expect if practical reason were doing its job. So I'll be getting it from three directions. I need objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way each camp would actually put them, with a two-minute reply for each, a note on what to concede and what to hold, and a flag on the weakest point in my position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Include the question I'm privately hoping nobody asks.
