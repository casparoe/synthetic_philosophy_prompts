I have been turning over a structural analogy between Amartya Sen's capability approach in the equality-of-what debate and J. J. Gibson's notion of affordances in ecological psychology, and I cannot decide whether it is deep or superficial. On Sen's side, what matters for distributive justice is not resources or achieved utility but capabilities: the real freedoms a person has to achieve functionings (beings and doings), given personal and social conversion factors. On Gibson's side, an affordance is a possibility for action the environment offers relative to an animal's abilities—a surface is walk-on-able for an adult human but not for a toddler, independently of whether anyone uses it or wants to. Both ideas seem to locate the relevant facts in the fit between an agent's constitution and its environment, rather than in the object or the outcome alone; both are relational, and both concern possibilities rather than actual performances. Does this resemblance reflect a common underlying structure, or does it trade on the vague word possibility? In particular, affordances are standardly treated as value-neutral descriptive facts, whereas capabilities are normative freedoms to achieve functionings one has reason to value. Is that a fatal disanalogy, or does it just mark the place where an affordance-based account would need a supplementary theory of value to do distributive work? If the analogy were deep, the capability approach would amount in effect to equalizing central human affordances. What would that restatement capture, and what does the analogy predict about one side or the other that turns out false?
