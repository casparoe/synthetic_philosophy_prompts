Something I keep coming back to: the Stanislav Petrov incident, September 26, 1983. A Soviet early-warning satellite reported what looked like an inbound US missile launch, then a handful more, and Petrov, the duty officer at the Serpukhov-15 command bunker, decided it was a false alarm rather than passing it up the chain. It turned out to be sunlight hitting high clouds and fooling the satelites. The standard line is that he saved the world, but I want to think through it with the theory, and think about the theory too.

Use the framework of nuclear deterrence and brinkmanship — MAD, second-strike credibility, launch-on-warning, Schelling's idea of threats that leave something to chance, the rationality of irrationality. Walk me through the Petrov incident in those terms: who or what the 'players' are, what the system was designed to make a duty officer do and why, what the payoffs looked like in that room, and whether by the theory's own lights Petrov not escalating was rational or a dangerous lapse. Then tell me where the framework stops making sense of what actually happened. I suspect it has something to do with deterrence theory wanting the state to be a single rational actor, while what we actually got was one tired officer making an epistemic judgment about a sensor he had reason not to trust. Then run it the other direction: what does the incident do to the theory? Is it an anomaly, a confirmation of the worry that deterrence is less stable than planners assume, or a case the theory was never built to handle? Be precise about what the theory can and can't extract from this rather than forcing it to explain everything.
