Here's a short answer question I gave on my intro philosophy of biology midterm, plus one student's response. Can you grade the response and give me the point-by-point comments I should write for the student?

The question was:

"A study of a large, genetically diverse sample in the contemporary US reports that the heritability of height is about 0.8. A journalist writes that this means 80% of a person's height is caused by their genes and the other 20% by their enviornment, and that since genes are fixed, environmental interventions like childhood nutrition programs can only ever matter for that 20%. Identify at least two things wrong with the journalist's interpretation, and say what the 0.8 does and doesn't tell us."

The student wrote:

"The journalist is wrong on both counts. Heritability is the proportion of phenotypic variance in a population that is attributable to genetic variance, so the 0.8 is a claim about the sample as a whole (really the population it represents), not about how much of any one person's height comes from genes. The journalist also mistakes heritability for immutability: even if a person's genes were completely fixed, a trait with high heritability could still respond strongly to a new environment—think of height rising under better childhood nutrition. So the 0.8 does not set a cap on what a nutrition program could do. What it does tell us is that, under current conditions, genetic differences account for more of the variation in height than environmental differences do. It doesn't tell us about the causes of average differences between populations, and it doesn't tell us how much a specific intervention would move the mean. Still, if only 20% of the variance is environmental, then there isn't much environmental variance to work with, so I'd be surprised if a nutrition program moved the mean much."
