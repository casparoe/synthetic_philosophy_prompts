I follow a rule about fine-tuning arguments, and I want to know whether it is defensible or a bias dressed up as a rule. When someone argues that the constants of physics are so finely tuned for life that they are strong evidence of a designer, I refuse to update until they can specify which values the designer would have had reason to choose, and on what grounds. Last month a friend pressed the usual cases: if the cosmological constant had been even slightly larger, the universe would have expanded too quickly for stars to form, and so on. I told him I could not count any of this as evidence until he told me what the designer was aiming at, and why that aim rather than some other aim should be expected. My reasoning is that if a life-permitting universe ends up being whatever universe we happened to get, then the observation cannot discriminate between design and chance; a target has to be specified in advance for a miss to be meaningful. But I suspect my rule may be too strong. A proponent could reply that the design hypothesis says only that the universe was produced by an agent who intended life, and that the observed constants are exactly what such an intention would lead one to expect. On that reply, my demand for a more precise specification of the agent's aims refuses to let the hypothesis be what it is. So I want to know: is my rule a sound application of the demand that a hypothesis be stated before its likelihood is assessed, or does it fix the game so that design cannot win? And is there a defensible version of the rule — for example, one that asks for a comparison class rather than a full specification of motives?
