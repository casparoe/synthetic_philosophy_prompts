I am writing the take-home final for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in contemporary moral philosophy, and I want one essay question on what is sometimes called the paradox of deontology. The students already recognize agent-relative constraints and side-constraints, so the question should not reward them for merely defining those terms; it should require them to work through a hard case under time pressure.

Please build the question around the following stylized scenario: an agent can prevent five strangers from each being murdered only by murdering one stranger herself. The deontological constraint against murder seems to forbid her from acting, yet forbidding her appears to guarantee that the very harm the constraint is meant to mark is realized five times over rather than once. The essay question should force candidates to go beyond both of the easy responses — that constraints simply are not to be minimized, and that obviously fewer murders is what matters. A top answer must state the apparent paradox precisely; explain why framing the situation as a choice about which quantity of constraint-violations is smaller may already beg the question against the deontologist; give a substantive account of why the agent's own violation could have a different normative status, rather than treating agent-relativity as a bare label; and engage with the uneasy fallback that the minimizing act is wrong yet all-things-considered what she should do.

Please deliver three things: the exam question itself, answerable in roughly ninety minutes; a model answer of about 700 words; and a marking scheme out of 25 marks that identifies five must-hit moves, assigns the mark for each, and adds a one-line note on the characteristic confusion each move is intended to catch. The question should not ask students to cite any literature or produce a reading list; it should be fully self-contained apart from the shared background concepts.
