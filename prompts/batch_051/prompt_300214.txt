When people defend economic growth, they usually cash out its value in terms of what it buys: longer and healthier lives, greater consumption, more secure incomes, better insurance against shocks, and the expansion of individual options. But there is a recurring residue in these discussions: a conviction that a society with a large accumulated stock of wealth has a kind of value, or moral worth, over and above every one of those channels. Call this the residual-abundance claim. It is not the psychological claim that people feel that abundance matters — a survey could test that — but the axiological claim, the claim about what is valuable, that the value is really there and should show up in how we compare states of affairs. I want to know whether this claim can be brought under empirical test at all, and if so what the strongest test would be. Design it concretely: what natural experiment, comparison of otherwise similar societies, or measured outcome could separate the hypothesis that abundance contributes something beyond the welfare it finances from the hypothesis that we have merely failed to measure all the consumption, security, insurance, and option value that wealth delivers. Say which pattern of results would count in favor of the residual-abundance claim and which against. Then be exact about what a favorable result would establish and what it would not: which component of the claim, if any, remains a bare value judgment that no observation could reach, and whether that is a defect in the claim or a fact about its subject matter. Write the answer in American English.
