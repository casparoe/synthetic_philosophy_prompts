I am writing a novel whose central character, a lapsed seminarian, keeps returning to Spinoza’s *Theological-Political Treatise*. A philosopher friend gave me this compact account of Spinoza’s position: “Spinoza shows that miracles are just events whose natural causes the biblical writers could not identify. Therefore the miracle stories in Scripture have no evidential value for revelation, and the Bible should be read strictly as a human historical document; any claim to divine inspiration in it is a pre-scientific illusion.” I then read this passage in the Elwes translation:

“The masses then style unusual phenomena, ‘miracles,’ and partly from piety, partly for the sake of opposing the students of science, prefer to remain in ignorance of natural causes, and only to hear of those things which they know least, and consequently admire most.”

I want to know whether my friend’s summary is fair, because it will shape how the character speaks. Please go claim by claim: what is accurate, what is overstated, what a careful reading of Spinoza would add about how he distinguishes the Bible’s historical reports from its moral teaching, and whether the conclusion that revelation is an illusion follows from anything Spinoza actually says here.
