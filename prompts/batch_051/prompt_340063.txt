I'm a retired physicist, and lately I've been amusing myself with logic paradoxes. Two things keep nagging me, and I cant find the right names, or even whether they're really the same underlying beast.

The first is a sentence that says of itself: "If this sentence is true, then the moon is made of cheese." You can put any statement in the second slot. If you run the reasoning in the natural way, you seem to be able to prove the second statement, whatever it is, without knowing anything about it. Suppose the sentence is true. Then the conditional it asserts is true, and its antecedent is also true, so the moon is made of cheese follows. But that line of thought also shows the sentence itself is true—since what it says is exactly that conditional—so then the moon is made of cheese, full stop. That feels different from the ordinary liar sentence, which just yields a contradiction and stops. I want to understand what's doing the work in the conditional version. Why does adding if-then make the thing so much more permissive?

The second is a trick a friend showed me. Take an infinite stack of sentences. Sentence 1 says all the sentences after it are false. Sentence 2 says all the sentences after it are false. Sentence 3 says the same, and so on forever. You can derive a contradiction, but no sentence ever mentions itself—each one only talks about the ones after it. My friend says thats the whole point: a liar-style paradox with no self-reference. But I've also heard people say there's a circle hiding in there anyway, just in a less obvious place.

So my question isn't really what these are called, though that'd be nice. It's more whether these two patterns are cases of the same thing. What exactly makes a sentence or a family of sentences like this genuinely paradoxical, and where does a self-referential-looking trick stop being harmless and start letting you prove anything? I'd rather have the edges of the concept than a label.
