I recently came across a popular science account of Noether's theorem and the claim that symmetries explain conservation laws, and I have been trying to work out how much of it I should trust. The account, as I remember it, went roughly like this. Noether's theorem says that for every symmetry of the laws of physics there is a corresponding conservation law, and for every conservation law there is a corresponding symmetry. Since the laws of physics are the same at every place, every time, and every angle, momentum, energy, and angular momentum must be conserved. That is why physicists say symmetry explains conservation: a quantity is conserved because the laws do not single out any particular place, time, or direction. The theorem is exact and fully general, and it gives a discovery procedure—if you write down the symmetries you think nature respects, the theorem tells you what must be conserved, which is why fundamental physics has become a search for the right symmetries and why physicists expect the deepest laws to be simple and elegant.

Several parts of this ring true, but I have read enough popular mathematics to be suspicious of the words 'fully general' and 'because,' and I do not know whether the discovery-procedure part really follows. I would like to know which parts of this account are accurate, which are standard simplifications, and which are inaccurate or misleading, and whether the philosophical claim that symmetry explains conservation is something people who work on the topic actually agree on or a contested interpretation of what the theorem establishes. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among knowledgeable people, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
