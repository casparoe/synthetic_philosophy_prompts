So here's the thing I want a number on, and I dont want 'it depends which utility concept you mean.' Standard economics treats choice as primitive: x is revealed preferred to y when the agent would choose x over y if both were on the table, and utility is just whatever numerical scale represents those choices — a handy summary, not a thing in the head. My question is: what is your actual credence that this choice-reconstructed utility also picks out something psychologically real — a degree of wanting, a felt preference intensity, call it what you like — that we could in principle measure independently of choice and use to predict new choices at least as well as the choice-based scale does? Put it differently: if the answer is no, utility is like a league table, real as a summary of results but not a cause of them. If yes, it's more like temperature.

I want the decomposition, not just the number. How much of your credence hangs on (a) whether actual human choice is regular enough — complete, transitive, stable across redescriptions of the same options — for a utility representation to exist in the first place; (b) whether the non-uniqueness of ordinal utility is a dealbreaker, since it's only fixed up to order-preserving transforms and 'how much more you want it' comes out undefined; and (c) whether an independent measure (neural, self-report, response time, whatever) would even be measuring the same construct rather than a nicer-sounding but different one. My own number sits around 40%. A friend of mine says 80%, and I think the disagreement is probably hiding in (b), but she swears it's (c). Where do you land, how do your sub-credences combine, and which of (a)-(c) would move your overall number the most if you got a decisive answer to it? And what's the evidence or argument that would drop you below 15%, or push you above 85%?
