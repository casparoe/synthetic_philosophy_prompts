In preparing it I read a trade book that summarizes the experimental philosophy literature, and I want to check the book's summary before I repeat any of it. The relevant passage, as I remember its substance rather than its exact wording, runs roughly like this:

Over the past two decades, experimental philosophers have shown that philosophically significant intuitions differ systematically across cultures. The flagship result involves a well-known case from the theory of reference: when told a story in which a person does not satisfy the description most people associate with a name, Western participants mostly give the causal-historical answer that Kripke's theory predicts, while East Asian participants are significantly more likely to give the descriptivist answer. Comparable variation has been reported for intuitions about knowledge, moral luck, and free will. These findings show that the intuitions philosophers treat as evidence are not universal deliverables of reason but local cultural habits, so a theory built on such intuitions has no more authority than any other culture's folk theory. The traditional assumption of a shared intuitive competence has been empirically refuted, and this is one of the most robust and widely replicated results in the field.

Act as an expert in experimental philosophy and philosophical methodology. Think carefully, and begin by separating the descriptive findings from the methodological and normative conclusions.
