I've spent the last few weeks reading whatever I can find on how terrorism gets defined, mostly legal statutes and encyclopedia-style overviews, and I keep tripping over the same pattern that I don't have a word for. Nearly every definition starts with features of the act itself: violence against noncombatants, intent to spread fear, a political or ideological aim, trying to coerce some wider audience or government. Fine. But then, usually near the end, they add a clause saying the actor has to be a non-state group, or the act has to be illegal under the laws of war, or they quietly hand "state terror" off to a seperate category. The result is that the very same sort of event — a bomb in a market meant to make a population press its government, say — gets classified as terrorism when an underground movement does it, but as something else (a war crime, "strategic bombing", an act of war, etc.) when a recognized government's air force does it. I'm not primarily after the moral question of whether those two are equally bad; what I can't name is the definitional move itself. A definition can present itself as picking out a kind of act, but then carry a buried clause about who the actor is — and that clause does all the normative work, turning the category into one that can't criticize states at all.

Does that move have an established name in the philosophy of political violence? I know "state terrorism" exists as a phrase, and there's the old line about one side's terrorist being the other side's freedom fighter, but those aren't quite it; I'm after the trick of smuggling a contested agent- or legitimacy-boundary into a supposedly descriptive definition so it stops being contestable. If no standard term fits, I want you to say so plainly and coin one, with a definition and a couple of edge cases. And please don't just lay out four candidate labels and stop — take a position: is the non-state restriction something a good definition of terrorism ought to keep, or is it a normative judgment dressed up as an act-type distinction? Commit and give me your reasons.
