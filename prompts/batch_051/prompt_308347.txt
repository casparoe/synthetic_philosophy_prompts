I've been arguing with a friend who works in environmental history, and it's left me suspicious of a phrase we both keep using: "historical actor." She said something like the volcano did more to shape nineteenth-century Europe than Napoleon, and I half-agreed before wanting to say no, a volcano is a cause, not an actor — an actor makes choices. But then she pointed out that historians write about the Little Ice Age or the plague bacterium as "actors" all the time, and it isn't just sloppy shorthand; it changes what counts as doing the explaining. I genuinely can't settle where the line goes, and my intutions keep sliding around.

Could you work up a definition of "historical actor" that classifies these cases as well as possible? Here they are: (1) a general who decides to invade; (2) the 1815 Tambora eruption and the crop failures and riots that followed; (3) Yersinia pestis during the Black Death; (4) the Little Ice Age, taken as a slow climatic shift over centuries; (5) a corporation or a state; (6) a market crash; (7) a river that determines where cities get founded; (8) a technology like the printing press. I'd say the general is clearly an actor and the river clearly isn't, and for the rest I want a definition that forces a yes or no even where I can't make up my mind. I'm also worried that wherever the line lands, you either hand too much explanatory power to non-human forces (hello environmental determinism) or make humans the only real actors and miss the way slow, inhuman processes genuinely move history. Propose the definition, run it through all eight, and tell me where it gives you a surprising verdict or no verdict at all — and revise it or defend the surprise as needed. While you're at it, say how confident you are in each part of the answer, and single out the specific claim you're least sure of.
