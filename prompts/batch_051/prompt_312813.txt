In a seminar on the moral limits of the criminal law, I put together an argument that I now find uncomfortable, and I cannot locate where it goes wrong. A colleague who works on online speech finds the conclusion absurd but cannot find the bad step either. The argument runs as follows. (1) Punishment, whatever else it involves, is the deliberate imposition of a burden or hard treatment on a person in response to a supposed wrongdoing, where that treatment expresses condemnation of the person. (2) Deplatforming—removing someone from a platform, with the attendant loss of audience, income, or status—imposes hard treatment in response to what the deplatformers take to be wrongdoing by the speaker, and it is typically experienced by the target as a public verdict of condemnation. (3) So deplatforming is a form of punishment, even when carried out by private platforms or loose coalitions of users. (4) Punishment may be justified only when certain conditions are met: there must be a prior determination of wrongdoing by a fair process that gives the accused a genuine opportunity to answer; the sanction must be proportionate to the offense; and the punisher must have standing or authority over the person punished. (5) Deplatforming by platforms and informal online coalitions often meets none of these conditions: there is no hearing, no reliable opportunity to answer, the sanction frequently outstrips the alleged offense, and the deplatformers lack established authority over the speaker. (6) Therefore most deplatforming is unjustified, and, by the same reasoning, so are boycotts, firings, public shaming, refusing to associate, and the rest of ordinary social sanction. (7) But (6) seems unacceptable: if correct, it would make everyday social life impossible—we could not permissibly stop associating with someone whose company we find intolerable—and it would condemn forms of collective action, such as consumer boycotts and labor strikes, that many of us regard as paradigm cases of justified social pressure. So one of the premises must go. I am most reluctant to give up (2) or (5); deplatforming really does feel like punishment to those subjected to it, and it is often procedurally grim. But then I am left deciding whether the analysis in (1) or the normative constraints in (4) should be weakened, and I do not know which choice is less costly. Which step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost?
