Okay so I was arguing with a friend who does machine learning, and he brought up the no-free-lunch theorem. The way he explained it to me: if you average over every possible sequence of observations, any prediction rule is exactly as good as any other — an advantage on one kind of sequence is exactly paid for on another, so for every neat regular world where induction works there's a matching weird world where it fails horribly. His conclusion was that this is just Hume's problem of induction with the math done properly: induction's great track record in our world gives no real reason to prefer it, because across all possible worlds the counter-inductive rule (always expect the opposite of what the past suggests) does just as well on average. So preferring induction is just picking an arbitrary prior.

I'm not convinced that's the right reading of the theorem, and I want to know how much weight to actually put on it. So here's the claim I'd like a number for: the no-free-lunch-style argument shows that induction has no epistemic justification that counter-induction lacks — that preferring induction over counter-induction is just an arbitrary prior preference, not something rationally required. What's your credence in that? Give me an honest number and then the decomposition. For instance: how much hinges on whether 'averaging over all possible worlds' is the right way to model having no evidence, versus caring mostly about worlds that are simple, computable, or relevently like ours? How much hinges on whether a rule needs to do well across all possible worlds, versus just doing well in the actual one? And how much hinges on what 'justification' even means here — non-circular proof, better in practice, admissible relative to a prior? Then say how those pieces combine into your number, which sub-question your credence is most sensitive to, and what argument or evidence would move it the most.
