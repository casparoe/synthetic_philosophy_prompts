I follow a rule about how I form beliefs: when a teacher, a coach, or a doctor tells me something within their area of expertise, I accept it and act on it without independently checking it. I make deference the default, and I override the default only when I already hold specific, concrete evidence that the person is mistaken. My reasoning is that such people know more than I do, that my own checking would be slow and unreliable, and that constantly second-guessing experts would leave me worse off. Recently, though, I read about the Milgram obedience experiments, in which ordinary people followed an authority's instructions even when they believed they were harming another person, and it unsettled me: I began to suspect that my rule is a small, quiet version of the same tendency, handing my judgment to someone else and calling it humility. Is my rule defensible as an epistemic policy, or is it obedience dressed up as deference? If it is partly defensible, how should it be restated so that it keeps the benefits of trusting experts while blocking the Milgram-style failure? From the inside, how can I tell the difference between virtuous deference and excessive obedience?
