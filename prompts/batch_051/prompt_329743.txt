I've been rereading Bagehot's *The English Constitution* (1867) and I'm not sure wether I actually believe his central move any more, or maybe I believe it too much. His whole thing, as I understand it, is that a constitutional monarchy works by being two constitutions at once: the dignified parts, which are the crown, the ceremonies, the royal family, and all the stuff ordinary people feel loyalty toward, and the efficient parts, which are the Cabinet and Parliament and civil service that actually get things done. The point of the dignified part is to absorb the public's imaginative attachment so the efficient part can govern without being understood or loved. He says something like the mass of people can only follow a simple, visible, almost theatrical thing, not a committee of tired lawyers. So the monarch is a piece of stagecraft, but load-bearing stagecraft, because if you took away the theatre the public would start poking at the machinery.

Take the abdication crisis of 1936 as a test case. Edward VIII wanted to marry Wallis Simpson, the government and the Church and the empire governments said that was impossible for the King to do, and he chose to abdicate. On Bagehot's model, the crisis is weird: the dignified side and the efficient side are supposed to be cleanly separated, but here the King's private life—which is supposed to be the most dignified, least efficient part of all—became a genuine constitutional problem that the efficient side had to solve by removing the King. So I want a careful mapping: who were the players, what were the actual options and payoffs, what was the equilibrium the system was trying to preserve, and what does the fact that the system could swap out a defective symbol tell us about Bagehot. Does the abdication confirm his two-constitutions story—the machine protected itself and public loyalty transferred to the new King without a hitch—or does it show that the dignified/efficient split is too clean, because the person filling the symbolic role can act as an efficient player just by being stubborn about their private life? I also want an honest claim about where the model is silent: is there anything in the actual dynamics of the crisis that Bagehot simply cannot describe without forcing it?

Then maybe do the same thing for Japan's 1947 constitution, because that looks like Bagehot made into statute law: the Emperor becomes a purely ceremonial symbol of the state, with no government power at all, and the real efficient machinery is moved entirely into the Diet and Cabinet. But Japan wrote that into a constitution under military occupation, and the Emperor had to also make a separate declaration renouncing his divinity. Does the Japanese case show Bagehot's account generalises beyond its British source, or does it show that his explanation of why ceremonial monarchy works was always a bit of Victorian cleverness that doesn't transfer? I'd like a comparison that doesn't just list similarities but says what each case reveals about the other in light of the theory.
