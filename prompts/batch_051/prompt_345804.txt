My philosophy club meets Thursdays in the library, and for the last few weeks we've been arguing about whether the fact that you can picture something is any kind of reason to think it could actually happen in some world. Last Thursday it got weird because I realized we were using the rule in only one direction. When we talked about whether a universe could run without any laws at all, someone said "I can vividly imagine a world where stuff just happens with no regularities, so that world has to be possible, right?" and everyone basically agreed that counts as evidence. But ten minutes later someone said he couldn't imagine how time could have a beginning, and the whole group jumped on him: "you not being able to picture it doesn't mean it's impossible, that's a limit of your imagination, not of reality." Nobody but me seemed to notice that we'd just used "I can conceive of it" as evidence for possibility and refused to use "I can't conceive of it" as evidence against it.

The part that still bugs me is that inconceivability does sometimes seem to count. I genuinely can't conceive of a four-sided triangle, and that really does feel like a reason to say such a thing can't exist. So the rule can't just be "conceivability is evidence, inconceivability isn't." I want to know whether the asymmetry is defensible. Is there a principled reason why successfully imagining X tells us more about X's possibility then failing to imagine Y tells us about Y's impossibility? If there is, where exactly is the line between the four-sided triangle case (where not being able to imagine it seems to prove something) and the beginning-of-time case (where it doesn't)? If there isn't a good principled reason, then which way should we go: treat failures of imagination as evidence too, or stop treating successes as evidence? And what would that cost us?
