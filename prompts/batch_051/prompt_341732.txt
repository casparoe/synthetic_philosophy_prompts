I am a retired natural scientist — most of my career was spent in the earth sciences — who has lately turned to philosophy of history and environmental history, and I find that discussions of non-human factors in history are habitually compressed into one over-coarse question, namely whether the environment determines human affairs. I should like a classification instrument that sorts people's actual commitments rather more finely than that.

Please write a decision tree (it may double as a sorting quiz with a scoring key) for positions on the role of non-human factors in historical explanation, concentrating on three kinds: pathogens such as Yersinia pestis, the plague bacterium; multi-century climatic episodes such as the Little Ice Age; and discrete geophysical events such as the 1815 eruption of Tambora. The leaves should be the positions people actually hold, or could coherently hold, about whether and how such factors act in history. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of labels: each branch should be labelled by a diagnostic question, phrased as a concrete claim or case that a person can answer without knowing the philosophical vocabulary, so that the instrument sorts by belief rather than by self-description.

Include as leaves at least the following five positions: a hard environmental determinism on which geography or climate drives large-scale historical outcomes; a possibilism on which the non-human world supplies constraints and affordances while human choice carries the explanatory burden; a human-centred view on which pathogens, climate shifts, and eruptions are mere background or triggering conditions, with explanatory agency reserved for human intentions and institutions; an entangled-agency view on which such non-humans are actants — entities that make a difference within a network — on a par in explanatory structure with human actors even though they lack intention; and a causalist view that grants non-human factors genuine causal importance while treating agency language as at most metaphorical, locating explanatory priority either in the society that is struck or in the coupled human-environmental system.

The diagnostic questions should prise apart four things that are habitually run together: causal importance, explanatory priority, the literalness of agency talk, and type of non-human factor. In particular, ask whether a pathogen, which replicates and evolves, deserves different treatment from a single volcanic event or from a centuries-long climatic trend. Use at least three of these cases within the questions: the fourteenth-century Black Death; the 1815 Tambora eruption and the unusually cold, wet summer of 1816; and the Little Ice Age and its effects on early modern European societies.

For each combination of answers that has no occupant, say briefly whether it is empty because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any two leaves the tree cannot separate, and state what further question would separate them if it were added. Each leaf should carry one sentence naming the position's most awkward commitment, and the instrument should make visible where environmental determinism is being quietly assumed rather than argued for.

The instrument is for a mixed reading group of scientists and philosophers who already know the historical cases; it should be usable in roughly twenty minutes and have between six and ten leaves. I should be grateful if the answer were written in British English.
