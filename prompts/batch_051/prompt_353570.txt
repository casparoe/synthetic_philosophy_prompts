I am a third-year undergraduate working as a grader for a course on nineteenth-century political thought, and I have to mark a short-answer question on Mill's later engagement with socialism. The course covered Mill's earlier writings on liberty and political economy together with the late essays on socialism published after his death, and the students were explicitly asked to reconstruct the qualified position Mill actually held rather than treating him as either a full convert to socialism or an unchanged individualist. Below is the question and the student's answer.

Question: "Mill is usually read as a defender of private property and individual liberty, yet his later writings express considerable sympathy with socialism. Reconstruct his qualified position: which parts of the socialist critique did he accept, which institutional remedies did he favor, and what did he continue to reject?"

Student's answer: "Mill's late sympathy with socialism was not a conversion to state ownership. He accepted the socialist charge that the existing distribution of property is a human arrangement rather than a natural necessity, and he came to see wage labour as a temporary stage that would eventually be superseded by worker-owned cooperatives, in which the labourers collectively own the capital and manage production. He remained hostile to nationalizing the means of production, on the ground that it would concentrate power in the state and endanger individual liberty. His disagreement with the socialists therefore concerned production: he argued that the laws by which wealth is produced are themselves matters of human convention, alterable in the same way as the laws of distribution, so there was no economic obstacle in principle to a cooperative reorganization of industry. He further concluded that inheritance has no natural basis and should be abolished outright, though he allowed that this could be approached gradually."
