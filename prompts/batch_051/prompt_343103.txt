I'm one of seven members on the planning commission in a small city — call it Alder — and we vote next week on a rezoning appeal. The site is a mostly empty strip mall at the edge of an older single-family neighborhood, ten minutes' walk from a light-rail stop. A developer wants to rezone it to build a six-story, 180-unit apartment building: 15% of the units below-market, almost no parking, and a small ground-floor grocery. The neighborhood association has packed every hearing. Their objections run from the usual stuff (traffic, shadows, school crowding, property values) to something more principled: people bought into a quiet, low-rise neighborhood, raised kids there, and kept the place up, and now they're being told that someone who doesn't even live there gets to change what their block is. I keep coming back to the fact that the people who would live in those 180 units can't show up to public comment because they don't exist here yet, and that if every neighborhood in the city successfully blocks this kind of project, the housing shortage becomes everyone's problem and no one's fault in particular. To me the real issue isn't just whether 180 units is too many; it's whether current residents have a legitimate say over land they don't own, and whether 'neighborhood character' is a good that should carry weight in a land-use decision or just a preference we're supposed to evaluate rather than defer to. I also can't shake the difference between a neighbor's concrete stake (noise, parking, crowded classrooms) and a neighbor's wish to control what the neighborhood becomes. The first feels like a reason; the second feels like the very thing under dispute.

Draft the memo I'd circulate to the other six commissioners before the vote. It should recommend one of three options — approve the rezoning, deny it, or approve with conditions — with an actionable bottom line. Please steelman the neighbors' position rather than treating it as mere NIMBYism: give the strongest version of their claim, then say honestly why it does or doesn't win. Use these section headings in this order, with rough lengths: Summary (~120 words), Background (~150), The case for approving the rezoning (~250), The case for denying it (~250), Analysis (~300), Recommendation (~120), Conditions (~80). Don't hedge — actually pick.
