Hope has recently become personally important to me: someone close to me is seriously ill, and I have been both relying on hope and urging others to remain hopeful even though the evidence is genuinely ambiguous. I do not doubt that this is morally good; I doubt that it is intellectually honest. The following argument keeps returning to me:

(1) To hope that p is, in part, to let the possibility that p weigh more heavily with one — in attention, affect, and action-guiding deliberation — than the available evidence alone dictates.
(2) To let a possibility weigh more heavily with one than the evidence dictates is to fail to proportion one's belief to the evidence, which is exactly what evidentialism forbids.
(3) A disposition that systematically involves failing to proportion one's belief to the evidence is an epistemic vice.
(4) Therefore, hope is an epistemic vice.

This conclusion seems too harsh, yet each step strikes me as at least initially plausible. Is the argument valid? If not, identify the exact step at which it fails and the reading under which it fails; if it is valid, identify which premise is most plausibly false and explain why.
