I am a retired physicist — I spent my career doing atomic and condensed-matter experiments, where the quantum formalism was a daily instrument — and I have only recently begun reading the philosophy of physics seriously. The question that has come to trouble me is an old one that I had thought I had put aside: whether the wavefunction is part of the furniture of the world, what is now called the psi-ontic view, or a compact summary of our knowledge, as the psi-epistemic family of views would have it. I find myself increasingly persuaded by the ontic side. The wavefunction is not an idle computational crutch; it governs interference, entanglement, and the way systems are prepared and transformed, and the impossibility results of recent decades aimed at purely statistical readings of the quantum state have made epistemic interpretations look strained to me. And I have never been able to see saying that the wavefunction is just information as much of an explanation, because information has to be encoded in something and to be about something. The simplest reading of our best theory, it seems to me, is that the formalism's central object is central because it stands for something real.

But I cannot shake a much older conviction: that before and after a measurement there is some determinate fact about the apparatus — the pointer was here or there, the detector fired or it did not. If the wavefunction is the complete real state, then an isolated atom in a superposition of decayed and not-yet-decayed seems, on that view, to have neither property determinately; and if one keeps the standard linear dynamics, the world threatens to become a set of branching histories rather than the single record of definite events I spent my laboratory life producing. I know enough not to think that collapse or hidden variables come free, so this is not a request for a solution. What I want is a clear account of what would have to yield. What would it take to show that my conviction about determinate records is a macroscopic prejudice that should be given up? What would it take to show that the ontic reading of the wavefunction is too strong and should be amended or abandoned — for instance, toward a view on which the wavefunction is real but nomological, more like a law of nature than a substance, or toward a more carefully drawn epistemic account? And is there a reading on which both survive, perhaps one in which decoherence explains why a single record is an excellent approximate description within each history while the wavefunction remains the real state throughout? I would rather be shown the costs and benefits of each move than be told which camp to join.
