I'm leading a two-hour discussion for a mixed reading group of non-specialists — intelligent adults with no formal philosophical training — on the idea that reasons can exist without a reasoner: that a consideration can genuinely count in favor of an action or attitude even when no one has actually taken it up, considered it, or felt its force. I want to teach the concept itself rather than survey positions about it, and I want the group to leave able to state the difference between a reason's being operative in someone's deliberation and its being there to be had.

Plan the session's explanatory arc for me. Specifically: (1) what conceptual equipment has to be in place before the core idea can even be stated, and in what order the pieces must be introduced — what depends on what; (2) the single carrying example or case you would build the whole session around, and what features it must have and must lack to do that work; (3) the misconceptions or resistances the group predictably brings, in the order they are likely to surface, and where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than merely corrected afterwards; (4) the check for understanding at the end that would show they have grasped the idea itself — the possibility of unowned reasons — rather than having merely learned a slogan or registered the example as odd; and (5) what you would deliberately leave out at this level, and why.

Commit to one definite sequence, one carrying example, and one final check, and give the reasons for those choices — including why your example is better than the more obvious candidates, such as cases of someone simply forgetting or being ignorant of a fact.
