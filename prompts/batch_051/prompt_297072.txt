At a lab meeting, two colleagues of mine went back and forth about whether it is ever correct to say that a large language model really believes things, and I found myself unable to sort out who was right.

Rhea opened by saying that we should simply take the intentional stance toward the system. When we predict and explain what the model says, treating it as believing certain things about the world is far and away our best strategy; that, on Dennett's view, is what taking the intentional stance is. If the pattern is robust and the stance buys real predictive power, then calling the attribution "merely as if" is asking for an inner essence that we do not require even when we attribute beliefs to one another.

Theo replied that taking a stance toward a system does not by itself make the stance true. We can get some leverage by adopting the intentional stance toward a thermostat or a chess-playing program, but that does not commit us to thermostats having beliefs. A real belief, he said, has a functional profile: it combines with desires to guide action, it updates in response to evidence, and it is answerable to the world through perception and action. The model has no desires, no actions in the world, and no way of checking its outputs against the world, so the regularities that make belief-talk useful are regularities of text production, not regularities of believing.

Rhea answered that this functional profile is too parochial and does not even fit Theo's own cases. We attribute beliefs to people who are temporarily unable to act, and we attribute beliefs on the strength of testimony alone. The model is not closed off from evidence either: the prompt supplies new information, users accept or reject its outputs, and, precisely insofar as attributing beliefs improves prediction, the attribution is doing the same work it does for human subjects. She added that Dennett's real-patterns point is that a pattern which earns its keep in prediction is not "merely" anything; if it is that useful, it is as real as the other patterns cognitive science posits.

Theo said the predictive value of belief-talk is doing different work in the two cases. Human text is produced by believers, so a system optimized to imitate human text will be predictable in belief vocabulary even if it has no beliefs of its own. That tells us about the training distribution, not about the model's relation to the facts it writes about. When the model says that Paris is the capital of France, there is no internal state that tracks that fact and controls what it says and what it would do with the information in other contexts. The tracking runs through the corpus, not between the model and France.

Rhea replied that Theo was helping himself to an unobservable "real" relation to the facts. For humans too, beliefs are inferred from words and behavior, not inspected. If someone answers correctly that Paris is the capital of France, we infer the belief directly, without checking their causal connection to France. Moreover, a trained model plausibly encodes world structure in its weights, so there is a causal channel from facts, through the training texts, into the model. That is exactly the causal dependence Theo said was missing.

Theo said it is not the right kind of causal dependence. A fossilized footprint is causally downstream from the foot that made it, but the footprint does not believe anything about the foot. For a state to count as a belief, the system must be capable of being wrong in a sense it can itself register: there has to be some possibility of correction by the world. The model's training signal is prediction error against text. It can be wrong relative to us, but there is no point at which the world tells it so.

Rhea responded that this is a very strong requirement and one Theo would not apply uniformly; plenty of human beliefs are insulated from direct correction, yet they are still beliefs. And the model's training does not consist only of next-token prediction: fine-tuning uses human ratings of whether outputs are true or useful, so there is an error signal that represents correctness, not merely likelihood.

Theo granted that this complicates the picture but denied that it settles it. An error signal about what human raters will approve is still an error signal about human approval, not about the world; a model trained to track whether people would endorse a string can be systematically wrong about the world, and its behavior tracks the endorsement, not the fact. He allowed that the line may be gradual and that some constrained subsystems might approach the functional profile of believing, but he insisted that the intentional stance by itself does not license a move from "useful to attribute" to "literally true."

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: at each stage, say who has the better of it and why; identify the strongest and weakest moves on each side; and say what, if anything, each party has actually established by the end. Is either of them more right overall, or does the dispute rest on a distinction neither has drawn precisely enough?
