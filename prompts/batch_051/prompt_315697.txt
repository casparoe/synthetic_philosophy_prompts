I got into an argument with a colleague who works at one of those forecasting outfits that puts numbers on elections, civil wars, sovereign defaults — the sort of thing that only happens once in anything like a cleanly repeatable way. He insists that when his model says there is a 72 percent chance that a particular country will default in the next year, the 72 percent is a fact about that country right now: a real objective tendency in the world, neither a summary of how often similar-looking countries defaulted in the past nor merely a measure of how strongly he'd bet. I want to say that a single event can't have a probability in that chunky sense — that all you can mean is a frequency in some reference class you chose, and the choice of reference class is always a judgment call, or a degree of belief that is only as good as its calibration. The argument kept coming back to the same seam: does his number describe something out there, or does it describe the state of his evidence, and could anything in principle tell the difference? It felt like the old split between propensity, frequentist, and subjectivist readings of probability, but with a concrete prediction sitting on the table.

So here is what I'd like. Design the best test you can of the claim that a single event can have an objective probability, a propensity, distinct from both long-run frequency and rational degree of belief. Be concrete about what data you would collect, across what kinds of events, and what pattern of results would push you toward or away from the propensity reading. Then say what the test could never settle even if it went perfectly, since all you ever get to observe is that the event happened or it didn't. If the propensity reading turns out to be strictly untestable, I'd also like you to say whether that is a problem for the view or just a sign taht asking for a test is the wrong way to approach the concept. No headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — just continuous prose, like an email. American English, please.
