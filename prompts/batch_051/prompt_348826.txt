I'm in a philosophy of law course, and we're doing the moral relevance of time in punishment — whether statutes of limitations track anything real about blame, or just about decaying evidence. I've got a pile of gut verdicts and I'm trying to pull a principle out of them.

Luke is 19 and is the lookout in a convenience-store robbery that goes bad and leaves the clerk with a permanent limp. He runs, moves away, and for the next 45 years works steadily, raises a family, and has no contact with the law. At 64 he's identified. I don't think he should be prosecuted now. Same facts, but identified after two years: obviously prosecute. So time itself seems to do some moral work for me.

Marta is 52 and embezzles from a charity hospital for years. It comes out 20 years later, after she's retired wealthy and unrepentant. The crime is nonviolent and most of the direct victims are dead or hard to identify, but I think she should still be prosecuted.

Daniel is 46 and a teacher; he has a sexual relationship with a 16-year-old student, with all the manipulation and power imbalance that involves. She comes forward 30 years later. He's now retired, was respected, has no later misconduct, and by everyones account is genuinely remorseful. I keep going back and forth — part of me says time shouldn't matter given the seriousness, part of me says the guy to be punished no longer exists.

A 24-year-old commits a murder during a drug deal and hides the body. He lives as a model citizen for 35 years, is apparently racked with guilt, and confesses the moment he's caught. I lean toward some punishment, but much less than a fresh murder, like the lived guilt already did some of the work. But that seems weird if we can't verify inner states.

Is there a single principle about when time should matter that captures these, or close? And what does it say about these ones I haven't settled: a wrongdoer who spent 20 years in a coma after the crime (no change of character, no lived guilt); a fraud where all the victims are dead but the offender is still the same kind of schemer; and an assault where the victim has healed and asks for no prosecution but the offender is unchanged and remorseless?

Please structure your answer as: (1) Case map — restate my verdicts and flag which two are hardest to hold together (about 150 words). (2) Candidate principles — two or three serious principles that get close, stated precisely, with the cases that motivate each part (about 400 words). (3) Stress test — run those principles through my three extra cases and say where they break (about 250 words). (4) Your proposal — the principle you'd actually defend and which of my gut calls it forces me to drop (about 350 words).
