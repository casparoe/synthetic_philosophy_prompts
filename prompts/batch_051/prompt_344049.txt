I am a doctoral student in moral psychology, and I have been arguing with a colleague in forensic psychology about a claim that sits awkwardly between an empirical hypothesis and a conceptual stipulation. She holds that people with psychopathy, or elevated psychopathic traits, do not know that what they do is wrong: they can supply the usual justifications, saying that an assault is wrong because it harms the victim or because it is against the rules, but this is, as she puts it, parroted moral language rather than genuine moral knowledge. She is careful to say the dispute is not about motivation, since many such individuals are plainly unmoved by the rules they can recite; the question is specifically whether they know. I suspect the claim collapses into one of two trivialities. If knowing that an act is wrong requires only the ability to apply the shared criterion correctly, then many people with psychopathy do know it, and the claim is straightforwardly false; if it requires the affective response that typically accompanies judgments of wrongdoing in others, then the claim is true only because knowing has been quietly redefined to include feeling, and no observation could refute it.
