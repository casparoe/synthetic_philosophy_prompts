In Plato's Meno, Socrates offers an argument for why knowledge is worth more than true belief. True belief, he suggests, is like an untethered statue of Daedalus: it may be correct, but it does not stay put, because the mind cannot reliably retain a truth for which it has no account. Knowledge is true belief that has been fastened down by reasoning about the explanation, and it is this fastening that makes the knower a more reliable guide, both to herself over time and to others. The argument thus locates the extra value of knowledge in a specific property: stability that comes from the agent's own grasp of the reason why.

Now consider a development that loosens the link between stability and the agent's own understanding. Suppose an agent holds a merely true belief, but the explanation, the supporting evidence, the checks, and the relevant records are stored in an external system — a practice, an institution, or a device — that reliably corrects or re-certifies the belief whenever needed. The belief is now stable in practice: it does not slip away, and it continues to guide action accurately over time. Yet the agent herself cannot give the account, and in that respect the belief is not knowledge in the Meno's sense.

Does the stability argument survive this change? Identify precisely which premise of the argument the scaffolded-belief case bears on, and say whether that premise can be repaired so that the argument still shows something distinctively valuable about knowledge. If the argument cannot be repaired in its original form, explain what the real difference between the knower and the externally stabilized true believer now comes down to.
