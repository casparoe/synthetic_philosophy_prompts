I'm a PhD student in political theory working on secession, and I've been writing down my gut verdicts on cases because they seem to cut against each other and I can't find a priniciple that gets them all. I want you to do reflective equilibrium on my behalf: find the best principle (or small set of candidate principles) that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, say clearly which ones it can't accommodate, and tell me which verdicts I should probably drop.

Here are the cases.

(1) Scotland 2014. The referendum was lawful because Westminster agreed to it. If Yes had won narrowly, I think independence would have been legitimate, and the UK government would have had an obligation to negotiate a settlement in good faith. But I'm also weirded out by the idea that a one-day vote with 50.1 percent could dissolve a 300-year-old union. I can't tell if that's a principle or just a feeling.

(2) Catalonia 2017. The Spanish constitutional court ruled the referendum illegal, the Spanish state sent police to stop it, and the Catalan parliament then declared independence and immediately suspended it. My verdict: the declaration wasn't legitimate. And yet I can't point to the exact feature that makes it different from Scotland. If the only difference is that Madrid didn't agree, that feels like handing the central state a veto over the self-determination of a nation with a sustained independence movement.

(3) Quebec 1995. The No side won by a hair, and the Canadian federal government later legislated that the House of Commons would decide whether a question and a majority were clear enough for it to be bound to negotiate. My verdict: a bare 50-plus-one would not have been enough for Canada to be forced to negotiate partition, and some kind of clear-majority threshold is defensible, though I can't say why 50.1 in Scotland bothered me less.

(4) Kosovo 2008. Kosovo declared independence without Serbia's consent, after the repression and mass displacement of the 1990s and after years under international administration. My verdict: legitimate, and Serbia's consent was not required. I don't think I can hold this while saying all secession from a democracy needs constitutional approval, but I want to hold both.

(5) A rich region of a consolidated democracy holds a lawful, agreed referendum and votes 56 percent to leave. The main motive visible in the campaign was that it is a net payer into the national budget and doesn't want to subsidize poorer regions. It has some distinct institutions but no real national identity. My verdict: this shouldn't be recognized, and at minimum the rest of the state has no obligation to cooperate. But I can see that "motive" is doing a lot of surface-level work, which makes me uncomfortable.

(6) A small island territory, colonized in the living memory of many residents, with a distinct language and no elected representation in the central legislature, votes 89 percent to leave. The colonial power says the vote has no legal effect. My verdict: clearly legitimate, and unilateral action is fine here.

(7) The case I keep going back and forth on: a region inside a functioning liberal democracy, with a genuine and old national identity, a long history of self-government before it was absorbed, and stable supermajorities for independence over several decades. The constitution says independence is unconstitutional unless the whole state agrees. The central government allows protests and regional elections but refuses to allow a binding referendum or any legal path to secession. No repression, no recent injustice. Do they have a right to leave unilaterally, and would the outside world be right to recognize them? Half the time I think yes, because otherwise the whole state's voters can keep a nation in against its settled will forever. Half the time I think no, because it would let any disciplined regional majority rewrite jurisdictions as it likes.

Is there a stable and reasonably simple principle behind all this, or close? If it can't capture everything, say which verdicts I should overturn and why. Tell me what your preferred principle says about the back-and-forth case, and give me one more case of your own that separates the candidate principles and show how each candidate handles it.
