Before I read any more of the accuracy-first literature, I want the specification written out first. I keep seeing particular scoring rules — Brier, log, spherical, and the cruder ones they are meant to replace — defended or attacked one criterion at a time, and I would rather begin from a full list of what a measure of inaccuracy for graded credences actually has to satisfy.

The measure is supposed to serve the accuracy-first program: treating accuracy to the truth as the fundamental epistemic value, and using accuracy-based arguments (dominance and expected-accuracy) to defend norms on credences — at minimum probabilism, and ideally a rule such as conditionalization as well. So the measure must be good enough for that job, not merely a plausible scoring rule for an isolated forecasting contest.

Write the desiderata. For each requirement, give the consideration or case behind it: what candidate measures slip through if it is dropped, and what breaks in the accuracy-first arguments if it is weakened. Separate the requirements into non-negotiable ones — those a measure must meet to count as a measure of closeness to the truth at all, or that the accuracy arguments cannot run without — and negotiable ones, such as additivity/separability, continuity, or particular symmetry assumptions, which are modeling choices, idealizations, or the preferences of one camp. Say why each lands in its category.

Three things I most want to see worked out.

First, the distinction between what a measure needs in order to be a measure of accuracy, and what it needs in order to function as an epistemic utility function inside expected-accuracy and dominance arguments. In particular: where strict propriety belongs in that divide, why propriety does not simply fall out of truth-directedness, and whether truth-directedness and strict propriety together discipline the space of admissible measures in a way neither does alone.

Second, whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, or satisfiable only with an idealization that costs something elsewhere; name the tension and how it is usually traded off.

Third, which single requirement does the most work excluding the tempting but inadequate candidate measures — for instance, a summed absolute-distance rule, a 0/1 step rule, and a calibration-only score — and why it excludes each.

Keep the scoring of the named measures (Brier, log, spherical) brief and at the end; the criteria themselves should carry the weight.
