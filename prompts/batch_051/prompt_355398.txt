I have been developing a view I call valence architecture, and I would like an honest assessment of it. The central claim is that moral considerability is a matter of degree and is fixed by two features jointly: an entity must have positively or negatively felt states, and those felt states must be handled by a single system that trades them off across time, so that a present harm can be exchanged for a later benefit. A dog has both features and is owed a great deal. An insect appears to have faint felt states but only minimal cross-temporal integration, so it is owed a little. A plant responds to injury but has no felt states, so it is owed nothing; a thermostat has neither feature and is owed nothing; a trading algorithm that forgoes short-term gains for long-term ones has integration of a sort but no felt states, so it too is owed nothing. I worry that the view may just be hedonism with an extra step, and that it draws the insect/plant line in a way that is either too generous or too stingy. Reply as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the sharpest charitable statement of the view; (2) the existing position it most resembles or collapses into; (3) the strongest objection; (4) the second-strongest objection; (5) whether the objections are fatal or merely uncomfortable.
