Recently I had to decide whether to keep my daughter's school bus route open after an automated flood sensor issued a conditional alert: if the levee had overtopped, then the Mill Road underpass was flooded. Before the alert I had placed the probability of the levee having overtopped at 0.6. A colleague working the same emergency updated the way I was once taught: she treated the conditional as ruling out the conjunction overtopped-and-not-flooded, eliminated those possibilities, and renormalized. That mechanically lowered her probability that the levee had overtopped. I kept mine at 0.6. My standing policy is this. When I receive a purely conditional message—one saying that if A then B, where I believe the channel would have sent some conditional of that form whether or not A obtained, though I cannot be certain—I do not allow it to move my probability of A. I revise my conditional probability of B given A, adjust whatever is strictly entailed, and otherwise change my distribution as little as possible. My colleague says I am protecting my priors from evidence. I think her method imports information about A that the conditional did not supply. Is my policy defensible as a standing epistemic habit? Under what conditions is her renormalization correct, and under what conditions is mine? Is there a precise statement of my rule—perhaps a minimal-change update—that captures what I am after without simply shielding my earlier probabilities? And how would I tell, from the inside, whether I follow the policy because it is the right way to handle conditional information or because it conveniently leaves my probability of A alone?
