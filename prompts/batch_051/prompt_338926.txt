I am a third-year undergraduate currently enrolled in a seminar on the origins and historiography of the First World War, and I am scheduled to present a paper next week on the Fischer controversy. My thesis, put briefly, is that the long-running dispute between those who follow Fritz Fischer in assigning primary responsibility for the outbreak of the war to Imperial Germany and those who emphasize shared responsibility, miscalculation, and a slide brought on by the structure of the alliance system rather than a deliberate German bid for hegemony is not, at bottom, an empirical dispute about the documents. I argue that it is a conceptual dispute about what it means to hold a nation responsible, as distinct from holding a cabinet, a general staff, or a population responsible, and that participants in the controversy quietly shift among several senses of the word Germany — the state as a corporate actor, the small decision-making elite of July 1914, the nation as a broad cultural community, and ordinary Germans — depending on which stage of the argument they are at. My further claim is that each side's reading of the archival record is largely downstream of unarticulated commitments about collective agency, about whether historical blame should function like a legal verdict or like a moral lesson, and about where causal contribution ends and culpability begins. I close by suggesting that making these commitments explicit would not settle the controversy, but would move it to a place where it can actually be argued.

My difficulty is that the room I am presenting to is not naturally friendly to a theory-heavy argument of this kind. Most of the audience are diplomatic historians of modern Europe who know the July Crisis documents in detail and are broadly inclined toward the view that the war was a tragedy of misperception and entangling commitments rather than a deliberate German project; they are impatient with abstraction and will want to know what the documents say. There will also be two political philosophers who work on collective responsibility and transitional justice, one of whom is skeptical that anything as diffuse as a nation can be a bearer of fault at all. Please prepare a Q&A prep sheet for me rather than a general defense of the thesis. I need the eight or so objections I am most likely to receive from this specific audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased in the idiom of the person who would raise them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me which objections have enough force that I should concede ground on them up front, which I should hold, and which question I should be quietly hoping nobody asks.
