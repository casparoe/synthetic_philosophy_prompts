And I don't just mean they let the belief run; they build evidence. They eat the cookies, leave half-drunk milk, write labels in a strange hand, slip money under the pillow, and when the kid asks straight out whether Santa is really real, they deflect or answer evasively. Then the reveal comes, and everybody treats it as sweet rather than as a broken trust.

I'd like you to reconstruct the theory of trust, honesty, or childhood that this practice presupposes. Does it presuppose that trust is fundamentally about good will or care rather than truthfulness, so benevolent deception doesn't count against trust? Or does it presuppose that kids are not yet full participants in a trust relationship, so the anti-lying norm doesn't directly apply? I want you to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say where it's philosophically underdetermined. Also tell me what would concretely have to change in the practice if we replaced the operative view with the view that trust is mainly about truthfulness and that manipulative deception always harms it.
