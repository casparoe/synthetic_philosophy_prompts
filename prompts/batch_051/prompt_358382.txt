A friend and I recently had a long argument about whether the evolutionary history of life is compatible with the doctrine that creation was originally good, and I want to think through the structure of the argument more carefully than we managed to. The traditional position we were debating runs roughly as follows: God created the world good; death and suffering entered the world only through human sin; therefore the animal suffering we now see is a consequence of the Fall. The development that puts this under pressure is basic to modern biology: predation, parasitism, disease, and extinction have been part of the history of life for hundreds of millions of years before any human existed, so animal death in particular appears built into the mechanisms by which life has developed.

What does this development actually do to the argument? Does it kill the argument outright, does it need repair, or does it merely defeat one premise—and if the last, which premise, and why? Can the argument be repaired without becoming trivial? For instance, one might say the doctrine was always only about human spiritual death, or that the goodness of creation never excluded predation, or that the Fall had retroactive cosmic effects; but those moves seem to change either the content of the doctrine or the meaning of its central terms in substantial ways. Assess whether any of these repairs, or another one, preserves enough of the original argument to remain worth making, and say explicitly what must be given up for the repair to work. I am interested in the logical anatomy of the argument and the cost of each repair, not in recommending any theological view. Please answer in American English.
