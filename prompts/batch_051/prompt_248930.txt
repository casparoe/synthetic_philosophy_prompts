In my work as a philosopher of science I sometimes advise a flood-defence planning group on how to read climate model projections, and I have developed a standing epistemic rule that I now apply quite generally. I treat agreement among climate models as evidence for a projection only to the degree that the agreeing models are structurally independent of one another: when several models share substantial code, parameterisation schemes, or calibration data, I discount their agreement accordingly, sometimes almost to zero. I likewise refuse to take the unweighted multi-model mean as my central estimate, and I will often lean on two or three models whose assumptions I can inspect rather than on a larger ensemble majority. Is this a defensible epistemic habit, or a rationalisation for favouring models that suit my prior expectations? Under what conditions would correlated model error make a thirty-model consensus genuinely weaker than three independent models, and is there a principled rule for weighting models by independence that would keep the benefits of my policy without its arbitrariness? Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, and write in British English.
