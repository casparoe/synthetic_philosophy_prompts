You are a philosopher of intellectual virtue teaching a seminar on the ethics of belief. Consider the following pattern in ordinary judgment. A manager accepts a flimsy, easily checked story that flatters her — that she turned the project around single-handedly — and repeats it to her superiors; we blame her as self-deceived. A manager accepts an equally flimsy, equally checkable story that condemns her — that she alone wrecked the project — and repeats it; we are more likely to call her honest or hard on herself than epistemically negligent. Now keep the evidence fixed and flip the belief: a manager who refuses to accept solid evidence that she deserves credit is seen as admirably modest, while one who refuses to accept solid evidence that she is to blame is seen as in denial and is blamed. So the same epistemic failure — fixing one's level of belief in a way the evidence does not warrant — is condemned when the error favors the thinker and excused or even praised when it disfavors the thinker. Is there a defensible principle that underwrites this asymmetry, or is it an inconsistency our critical-thinking norms should give up? Brainstorm the strongest justifications, develop the best one, and then test it against a case where a self-flattering error benefits other people and a self-condemning error harms them. End with a clear verdict on whether to keep, reverse, or abandon the asymmetry.
