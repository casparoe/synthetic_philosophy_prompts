I've been teaching the KK principle and the anti-luminosity argument for years, and I've finally admitted to myself that my own judgements on the cases don't add up to a principle. I'd like you to help me find one, or tell me which judgement I should give up.

By KK I mean the claim that if you know something, you know that you know it. By luminosity I mean the idea that being in a mental state is enough, on its own, to put you in a position to know you're in it. My gut is that neither holds across the board, but they don't fail together, and I can't see the pattern.

Here are my verdicts, as honest as I can make them.

1. In full daylight I look out the window and know the car outside is red. Asked whether I know that I know, I say yes without hesitating.
2. I've counted the till twice and got £17.40 both times. I know there is £17.40 in the till. But I don't think I know that I know it; two counts could easily go wrong in the same way.
3. I feel feverish, and I am feverish. I'd say I know I'm feverish. But I don't know that I know it—the feeling sits too close to ordinary warmth, and my inner sense of temperature feels exactly like the sort of thing that can feel definite and still be wrong.
4. I wake up and know I was just dreaming. But I want to say I don't know that I know it. I might still be half asleep, and the feeling of being awake is just the kind of state that can feel clear without being reliable.
5. I'm a radiologist looking at a scan. My first glance says the mass is there; the glance is right, and I have an 80% track record on such first glances. I'd say I know the mass is there, but I don't know that I know—inside the glance I can't tell the veridical 80% from one of the 20%.
6. I know that 7+5=12, and I know that I know it, and I can't even imagine being wrong about it while still understanding what it says.

So: is there one principle about when knowledge iterates—about when knowing p brings knowing that you know p—that gets all six? If not, tell me which verdict to overturn and why. State the principle precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and then apply it to a case I keep going back and forth on. I've driven this road a hundred times and I know the next turn is left, but today the hedges look unfamilar and I start doubting whether I know it at all. Do I still know the turn is left, and do I know that I know?

No citations or reading lists, please—just the reasoning from the cases. And please write the whole answer in British English.
