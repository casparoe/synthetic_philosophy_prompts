I've been keeping a list of times self-reflection seemed to help people I know and times it clearly made them worse, and I can't find the line between them. Here are my verdicts, going by gut.

A friend spent most of a winter replaying why his long-term relationship ended: rereading old texts, asking "what is wrong with me," writing it all down every night. Verdict: not fine, it hollowed him out.

Same friend, months later, sat down for one hour and wrote out two concrete things he wanted to do differently in his next relationship and what he'd watch for in himself. Verdict: fine, it actually settled something.

My cousin loses a tennis match and for the next week he is mentally replaying every point and calling himself a choker. Verdict: not fine.

Same cousin, after the next loss, sat with the video once, wrote down three technical errors and the drills for each. Verdict: fine.

Another friend, after any party, spends the drive home scanning the evening for signs people were annoyed with her. Verdict: not fine, and its worse because there is no answer and she can't stop.

But the same friend once tried a ten-minute version: "what's one thing I'd do differently at the next party?" and she had an answer and stopped. Verdict: fine.

Now the pair that breaks my tentative rule. One friend with panic attacks checks his pulse and breathing every hour to see whether one is coming on. Verdict: not fine, it feeds the thing.

Another friend does a ten-minute body scan each morning where she notices tightness or a fast heartbeat with no attempt to interpret or fix anything, and then moves on. Verdict: fine.

The principle I was going to propose is: self-examination is fine when it's aimed at changing a future action and has a stopping point, and not fine when it's aimed at settling what kind of person you are, or when it can't conclude. But the body-scan case isn't aimed at future action and doesn't exactly conclude either, and it still feels fine. And the party-scan case is about others' reactions, not about "what kind of person," and it's still not fine.

The one I'm torn on: a friend whose partner complained he interrupts too much, so he started logging every interruption right after it happens in a notes app, planning to review weekly and adjust. Half of me thinks that's exactly the kind of concrete, change-focused, stoppable self-examination that works. Half of me thinks he's turning every conversation into self-surveillance and will get worse at listening, not better.

Is there a principle that gets all of my verdicts, or close? If I have to give one up, which should it be? And what does the principle you'd defend say about the interruption-logging friend?
