I have been reading about statutes of limitations, and I keep noticing a distinction I don’t have a name for. In the syllabus of United States v. Marion, 404 U.S. 307 (1971), the Court says: "The relevant statute of limitations provides a safeguard against possible prejudice resulting from pre-accusation delay, and here appellees were indicted within the applicable limitations period." That sounds like an epistemic rationale: time degrades evidence, so the law protects defendants from trials in which the truth may no longer be recoverable. But some cases seem to rest on something else—not that the evidence has gone bad, but that after enough time the defendant simply has a right to be free of the claim, even if we stipulate that the plaintiff or prosecutor could still prove everything. I have seen the word "repose" used, but I cannot tell whether it is a legal term of art, a moral concept, or just a metaphor for the same epistemic worry. Is there an established name in philosophy for the idea that elapsed time has intrinsic moral significance—that a wrong can cease to be actionable or punishable, not because our knowledge has degraded, but because time itself changes the moral relation? If there is no established name, what distinction would you draw between the epistemic and non-epistemic justifications for time bars, and what would you call the latter? Please answer in American English. If my question rests on a false dichotomy or a confused premise, say so plainly instead of forcing a label.
