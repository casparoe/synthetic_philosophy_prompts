The way particle physicists actually work is kind of funny when you think about it. They'll happily compute low-energy quantities like pion scattering using an effective field theory whose Lagrangian just has the light degrees of freedom and some couplings, while explicitly refusing to say what the underlying high-energy theory is. And this isn't usually treated as a stopgap until someone finds the real theory; the low-energy calculation gets treated as a genuine explanation of the phenomenon, even if the ultraviolet completion is unknown or, in principle, might turn out not to exist. Weinberg describes the early version of this attitude in his talk "Effective Field Theory, Past and Future" (2009), talking about Nambu's approach:

"In this work it was not necessary to assume anything about the nature of the broken symmetry -- only that there was some approximate symmetry responsible for the approximate conservation of the axial vector current and the approximate masslessness of the pion."

So here's what I'm trying to figure out. If this is a legitimate practice and not just intellectual laziness, what philosophy of science does it presuppose? Does it commit physicists to some kind of antireductionism, where the low-energy level is causally or explanatorily autonomous from whatever is happening at high energies? Or can it be read as fully compatible with the orthodox reductionist view that the fundamental theory does all the real explaining and the effective field theory is just a way of organizing our ignorance? I want the implicit theory reconstructed carefully: what does the practice strictly presuppose about the relation between scales, and what is merely compatible with it? And if someone insisted that no low-energy description genuinely explains anything until you know the ultraviolet completion, what exactly would have to change in how physicists talk and work?
