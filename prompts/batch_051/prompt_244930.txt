For a seminar I am preparing on Marx's account of technology and automation, I plan to pair the machinery chapter of Capital with Karel Čapek's R.U.R. (Rossum's Universal Robots). I want the session to centre on this passage from Capital, Volume One, Chapter Fifteen, in the Moore–Aveling translation:

"Like every other increase in the productiveness of labour, machinery is intended to cheapen commodities, and, by shortening that portion of the working-day, in which the labourer works for himself, to lengthen the other portion that he gives, without an equivalent, to the capitalist. In short, it is a means for producing surplus-value."

For context, the play concerns a firm that manufactures artificial workers, its robots, as a cheaper substitute for human labour; the robots eventually acquire something like feeling, revolt, and destroy humankind apart from the works architect Alquist, who is spared because he, too, works with his hands. I would like you to write me a critical reading of the play through the lens of the passage, as the centrepiece of the seminar. The passage treats machinery not as a relief from toil but as an instrument for producing surplus-value: it cheapens commodities and extends the portion of labour the worker gives without an equivalent. Read R.U.R. against that claim. Where does the play dramatise it exactly, given that Rossum's robots are owned outright and are manufactured precisely to cheapen the labour of others? Where does the play complicate or resist the claim, since Čapek's robots are not simply machines but manufactured workers who suffer, organise, and issue demands? And what does the play add to Marx's analysis that the chapter's argument alone does not supply, especially in the closing scene in which two robots' willingness to sacrifice themselves for each other leads Alquist to send them out as a new Adam and Eve? I want a reading faithful in both directions: one that does not reduce the play to an allegory of Capital, and one that says plainly where the fiction and the theory part company. Please write in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as you would in a letter or a seminar note.
