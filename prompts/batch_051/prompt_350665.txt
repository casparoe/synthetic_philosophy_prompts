I recently read an article in a popular ideas magazine that summarized the philosophical debate over whether "better than" is transitive, and I want to know how much of it to trust. As best I can reconstruct it, the article's account ran as follows. We ordinarily assume that if one outcome is better than a second, and the second is better than a third, then the first is better than the third. The article said spectrum arguments have shown this assumption to be false. You construct a series of possible lives that start clearly excellent and end clearly miserable, where each step differs from the previous one by only a tiny loss of well-being. For any two adjacent lives, the article claimed, the difference is so small that it would be arbitrary to say one is better, and most philosophers now conclude that adjacent lives in such a spectrum are equally good. But the first life is plainly better than the last, so "better than" must fail transitivity. The article then drew the moral that there is often no fact of the matter about which of two similar outcomes is better, and that comparing policies by asking whether one would leave people better off is unreliable, since any comparison can be sabotaged by constructing a long enough chain of small changes. I am inclined to trust the article because the author has a background in philosophy, but the closing claim about policy comparison strikes me as too sweeping.

Please audit this account the way a philosopher working on value theory and spectrum arguments would. In exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences and with no introduction or conclusion, go through the account's main claims in order and say for each whether it is accurate, roughly right but simplified, or misleading, and whether the article's final conclusion would survive correction of any errors you find.
