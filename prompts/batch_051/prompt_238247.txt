I keep coming back to a parallel that I can't tell is deep or just a pun on the word freedom. In the Grand Inquisitor chapter of The Brothers Karamazov (Ivan's poem, as he tells it), the Inquisitor's whole case is that human beings can't really stand freedom; if you give it to them they'll be miserable and will eventually beg someone to take the burden of deciding off their hands. What they actually want, he says, is bread, and wonders, and someone to bow to — so the church is doing them a kindness by making their choices for them. Meanwhile there's this whole consumer-psychology literature on choice overload: past a certain point, more options make people less happy, more likely to put off the decision, and more regretful afterwards, and well-designed defaults usually leave people better off by their own report. Product design has basically internalized this — hide the settings, limit the menu, choose for them. So both stories seem to say: removing choice increases felt well-being, and people will even be greatful for it. But I suspect the Inquisitor is saying something much stronger — not just that decision costs make freedom unpleasant, but that people actively want to be mastered, to hand over their conscience, to be told what to want. Is that a difference in degree or in kind? What would the consumer-psychology results have to show, exactly, for them to count as evidence for the Inquisitor's view? And where does the parallel actually break — what would the Inquisitor's version predict about how people behave around choices that the choice-overload account gets wrong? I know the choice-overload effect itself is contested and pretty context-dependent, so maybe that matters for how seriously to take the analogy.
