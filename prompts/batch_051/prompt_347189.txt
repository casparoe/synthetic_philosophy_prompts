I cover science and ideas for a regional paper, and I am writing a long piece about whether a public body can attach one coherent value to a statistical life when it weighs treatments for identifiable patients against programmes whose beneficiaries cannot yet be named. I have been reading the published decision summaries of the Averdale Health Priorities Commission, which pays for treatments and prevention programmes out of one fixed budget; I have anonymised the treatments. Its framework sets a standard ceiling of about £30,000 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY), but its recent decisions depart from that ceiling in both directions.

Its record includes these five decisions. It approved Elaventis, a gene therapy for an ultra-rare and otherwise fatal paediatric metabolic disease, at roughly £212,000 per QALY, on the grounds that it converted an early death into something close to a normal lifespan for a catastrophic condition with no alternative treatment. It rejected Cardistatin, a daily preventive pill for otherwise healthy 55-year-olds with moderately raised cholesterol, at about £13,500 per QALY; although that falls well below its ceiling, the Commission said the absolute risk reduction for any one person was small and that its duty ran first to those already seriously ill, not to marginal risk reduction across large numbers of the currently well. It approved Panvecta, an immunotherapy for advanced pancreatic cancer in patients with less than six months to live, adding roughly 2.6 months of life and some symptom relief at £94,000 per QALY, under an end-of-life adjustment and with no other treatment available. It rejected Rheumotrex, an add-on for moderate rheumatoid arthritis offering modest symptom improvement at £47,000 per QALY where cheap, effective alternatives already existed, judging the incremental gain too small to justify the price. And it approved Lingua-Recover, an intensive post-stroke speech and language therapy package at £22,500 per QALY, citing strong evidence of substantial and durable functional recovery within its standard cost range.

The Commission has not yet decided on KardioScreen, a proposed regional programme of genetic screening and preventive treatment for hereditary dilated cardiomyopathy in adults aged 20 to 50. Asymptomatic people would be tested; those carrying the mutation would receive medication and, where indicated, an implanted defibrillator. Modelling suggests the programme would prevent roughly four sudden cardiac deaths and eighteen cases of severe heart failure each year at £29,000 per QALY. The people it would help are currently healthy and cannot be identified without screening, though someone who tests positive has about a 40 per cent chance of serious disease within fifteen years. On its own precedents, should the Commission fund KardioScreen? I am less interested in your independent view of the programme than in what consistency with the Commission's track record requires. Tell me which earlier decision is the closest analog, which candidate rules fit all five precedents, what each of those rules predicts about KardioScreen, and the principle the Commission would be adopting under each possible decision. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
