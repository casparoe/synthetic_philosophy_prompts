I've been tinkering with one of those little open-source rover kits that's supposed to learn to navigate around my apartment, and I keep catching it in this loop: it rolls up to the threshold between the hallway and the kitchen, decides something is blocking it, backs up, then immediately tries the exact same approach again. Repeat until the battery dies. My officemate called it sphexish, which sent me down a rabbit hole about the wasp that drags a cricket to its burrow, pops inside to check things, and if you nudge the cricket a few inches, it comes back out and starts the whole inspection-and-drag routine from the top.

The thing I can't shake is this. People sometimes talk like sphexishness and free will are two ends of one spectrum: the wasp is rigid, so it isn't really choosing; a person would notice the cricket moved and just grab it. But I want to know how much of that talk is empirically load-bearing. Suppose I make a specific claim: sphexish behavior is a triggered routine that is insensitive to a relevant change in the task environment, while flexible behavior alters its procedure when that change matters, and only the latter kind of behavior counts as evidence for genuine agency (whatever else free will is). How would I test that claim? Design the strongest experiment you can, with a concrete manipulation and a concrete predicted difference between the sphexish and flexible outcomes. Say which result would support the claim and which would undermine it. And be strict about the residue: if a system passes your test and really does respond flexibly, what would that still not show about whether it has free will or agency, and does the notion of sphexishness buy us anything beyond just behavior that looks dumb?
