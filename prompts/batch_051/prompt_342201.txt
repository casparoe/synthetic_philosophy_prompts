For several years I have been arguing with two friends about a question that will not leave me alone: could any of us have come from a different sperm and a different egg? We have settled into two stable camps, and I have been pulled back and forth between them. The first camp, which I will call the strict-origin view, says that each person is tied from the very start to the particular sperm and egg he or she actually came from, and that a child formed from a different pair would simply be a different person: a sibling, not you, no matter how similar the life. The second camp, which I will call the life-pattern view, says that this makes too much of the starting cells. What makes you the individual you are is a pattern that grew out of them, a continuing body, mind, and history, and the answer to whether you could have come from a different pair depends on how much of that pattern we are holding fixed in the comparison. If the same parents, the same kind of upbringing, and the same broad course of life are held fixed, this camp says, there is no further fact about the gametes that forces the answer to be no.

I would like you to draft the joint statement these two camps could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Please give it three parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: statements both camps accept, phrased so that neither side is simply surrendering while the other side quietly wins, including whatever they can jointly say about what a question like "could you have come from different gametes?" is really asking for. Second, a disagreements table: for each remaining disagreement, state the issue in neutral wording, then give the strict-origin camp's formulation of its own position, then the life-pattern camp's formulation of its own position, each phrased so that the camp itself would sign it, and then a note on whether that disagreement is substantive or partly a difference in how the two camps use words such as "could," "you," or "same person."

Third, and most important, a section on cruxes: identify two or three sub-questions whose settlement would genuinely move at least one camp. For each crux, describe a concrete case or argument that both camps would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts the case shows. Make the camps work through at least these four cases rather than talk around them: identical twins, who come from one sperm and one egg yet are plainly two people; a person whose body grew from two separately fertilized eggs that fused into one embryo; an imagined medical procedure that produced a child with the same genetic makeup from a different sperm and a different egg; and the ordinary case in which my parents had conceived a child two years earlier. My own suspicion is that the first two cases put more pressure on the strict-origin view and the last two put more pressure on the life-pattern view, but the statement should test that suspicion rather than assume it, and each camp should be allowed to revise its wording only by making its commitments clearer, never by changing the topic.

Keep the statement self-contained: no references, citations, or reading lists, only claims, arguments, and cases.
