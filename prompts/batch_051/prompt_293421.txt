I am a novelist working out the social world of a near-future novel, and I want a reasoned prediction rather than narrative invention. The novel is set in a wealthy liberal democracy, thirty years after the introduction of a cheap, safe, and reversible intervention that removes the felt badness of suffering. A treated person still registers the sensory signals of injury, still forms the belief that something is bad for them, and still takes ordinary steps to avoid harm; what is gone is the raw, gripping awfulness of physical and emotional pain. Need, effort, failure, rejection, competition, and deprivation all remain. The inner sting alone is subtracted. The treatment is voluntary, and by the time the novel opens, most adults use it.

I have been reading Schopenhauer to think through what such a change would do to institutions and character. In "On the Sufferings of the World," from Arthur Schopenhauer's Studies in Pessimism (trans. T. Bailey Saunders), he writes:

"But misfortune has its uses; for, as our bodily frame would burst asunder if the pressure of the atmosphere was removed, so, if the lives of men were relieved of all need, hardship and adversity; if everything they took in hand were successful, they would be so swollen with arrogance that, though they might not burst, they would present the spectacle of unbridled folly—nay, they would go mad. And I may say, further, that a certain amount of care or pain or trouble is necessary for every man at all times. A ship without ballast is unstable and will not go straight."

That passage bundles need, hardship, adversity, pain, and trouble together. My imagined treatment removes only one strand of that bundle: the felt badness itself. I want you to test Schopenhauer's warning against that divergence rather than accept it. Thirty years after the treatment becomes widespread, what do you predict for politics, family life, work, innovation, and the arts? I want the mechanisms explicit and your confidence graded at each step: which effects follow robustly from the removal of felt suffering, which depend on further assumptions about what motivates people, and which would shift if the treatment were mandatory instead of voluntary? Settle for me what the passage actually requires: does Schopenhauer's "ship without ballast" argument do its work through the felt pain it removes, or through the resisting world that remains after the removal? And if the latter, should a committed negative utilitarian, who cares almost exclusively about minimizing felt suffering, regard my fictional country as a success rather than a slow catastrophe?
