I help mediate a network of forty independent patient-advocacy groups that share a public alert system. Any one group can issue a network-wide systemic safety alert about a treatment; once one is issued, regulators, insurers, and hospitals treat it as the considered position of all forty. The groups are now split over whether to issue an alert about an obstetric drug that some of them believe causes rare but severe harm, while others regard the evidence as too confounded to justify a public warning. There is a nonbinding convention that a group should consult at least two others before publishing, but it is routinely ignored during scares, and no one can enforce it. Everyone grants that the system allows any single group, on its own reading of the evidence, to put the entire network on record no matter what the other thirty-nine think.

As I understand it, the unilateralist's curse is roughly this structural trap: when any one of many actors can take an action on everyone's behalf, the effective decision-maker is whichever actor has the lowest threshold for treating the evidence as sufficient, rather than the group's median or most careful judgment. Take that gloss as enough shared background; I want its applied implications, not a recounting of the idea. Please give the most important implications of the unilateralist's curse for our situation, at two levels: (a) how the network should redesign its governance of the alert system, and (b) how any one of the forty groups should reason about its own discretion in the next few days, before any redesign can be adopted.

Structure your answer in named sections, in this order, with the indicated rough lengths:

(1) 'The trap we are in' (~150 words): restate our case in unilateralist terms, noting where we do and do not fit the standard structure.

(2) 'Why majority caution is not enough' (~200 words): explain the mechanism by which one low-threshold actor can set policy for everyone, and say whether the risk worsens as the network gets larger or more heterogeneous in evidential standards.

(3) 'What this implies for the alert system's rules' (~250 words): give concrete procedural changes and their tradeoffs, not general appeals to caution.

(4) 'What this implies for an individual group this week' (~200 words): under the existing rules, how should a single group decide whether to issue the alert, given that others are reasoning in the same situation?

(5) 'Bottom line' (~100 words): the single most important lesson for a flat, trust-based network with unrestricted publishing power.
