As a journalist who covers risk, institutional design, and emergency powers, I have spent the past year interviewing people who plan for the continuity of government. I have accumulated reactions to a series of concrete cases, and I would like to know whether a single principle explains them or whether I am simply inconsistent. I will set out the cases and my verdicts.

The practice of sending one cabinet secretary to an undisclosed location during the State of the Union, so that at least one constitutional officer survives a catastrophic attack on the chamber, strikes me as appropriate and, within broad limits, something the government ought to do, notwithstanding that the chance of such an attack is negligible; the visible continuity of the constitutional line is itself part of what the state is owed, and the burden on the chosen secretary is modest. Similarly, when a state emergency-management director asks the lieutenant governor not to attend the same packed football game the governor is attending, I think the request is legitimate, even obligatory absent special burdens, and I am not troubled by the fact that no specific threat has been made.

I would, however, object to a city council that bans more than two of its five members from eating in the same restaurant at the same time, on the theory that a quorum should survive some improbable mass-casualty event; that strikes me as security theater and an unjustified intrusion on ordinary association. I would also side with a pediatric surgeon who resists a hospital rule that the only two surgeons trained for a particular rare operation may never fly together: the hospital may ask, coax, or insure against the risk, but it may not make her off-duty travel a disciplinary matter. Two further cases pull in opposite directions. If a cabinet secretary refuses to serve as the State of the Union designee because her child is in intensive care, I do not consider her blameworthy, and I do not regard the state as wrong for passing the assignment to the next eligible official. But if a confirmed missile launch forces the government to relocate, and the official next in line refuses to go unless her family is admitted to the relocation convoy against the rules, I think she is plainly wrong; in a genuine emergency, the duty to preserve continuity overrides even strong duties to family.

The distinctions I reach for instinctively—public versus private, ceremony versus genuine emergency, federal versus state versus local—seem to do real work, but when I push on them I cannot state the principle without building it out of the very conclusions it is supposed to explain. Is there a coherent principle that yields all of these verdicts? What would it say about further cases: a private utility that forbids its only two grid-restoration engineers from flying together; a rule that the designated survivor's location must be kept secret from her spouse; a proposal that ceremonial continuity assignments require the designee's formal consent? And if no one principle can accommodate every verdict I have listed, tell me which ones I must abandon, and why the surviving principle is the one I should keep.
