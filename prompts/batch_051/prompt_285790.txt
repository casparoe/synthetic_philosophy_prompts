Act as an expert in democratic theory and in the design of experiential classroom activities. Think through several candidate mechanics before settling on one, and be concrete about rules, roles, materials, timing, and facilitator scripting. I teach an upper-level undergraduate political philosophy seminar—sixteen students, one eighty-minute session—and I want to give them a worked, felt encounter with the boundary problem: the puzzle of who should be included in the demos for a given collective decision. I do not want a lecture up front, and I do not want the phrase "boundary problem" mentioned until the debrief; the problem should surface from what the students are made to do.

Here is a seed scenario to refine, replace, or complicate. At the start of class each student draws a card assigning them one of several roles, but most are "current members" of the student common room's governing body. The class is told they must, within the session, pass two binding decisions: how to allocate a small renovation fund, and whether to adopt a new after-hours access policy. Both decisions plainly affect people who are not in the room—first-year students who have not yet arrived, part-time students who use the room only at night, the cleaning staff, and recent alumni who still use the space—but only the current members have seats. After an initial vote, representatives of those outside groups arrive and petition for a voice. The students then have to decide, under their own inherited procedure, whether and how to include them; the inherited rule says any expansion of the franchise requires a two-thirds vote of current members, and no one can say where that rule came from. Please design the activity so that at some point the group is forced to choose between treating every affected person as a stakeholder and relying on a decision procedure that can only be justified by presupposing who already counts. I want a runnable package: the full cast of roles and how they are revealed, rules of proposal and voting (including which meta-rules are amendable and how), materials to print, a minute-by-minute timeline, a debrief that maps what happened onto the competing principles—roughly, all affected interests, all subject to the rules, and existing membership—and that distinguishes first-order decisions from the second-order decision about who decides. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, with a fix for each failure, and be honest about which feature of the real-world problem a low-stakes classroom simulation cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. As the final line of your reply, give a one-sentence summary of the core insight the activity is meant to teach, formatted as \answer{...}.
