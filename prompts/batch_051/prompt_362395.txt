I have been working with a distinction in the epistemology of uncertain evidence, and I should like a candid assessment of it before I rely upon it in further work. I distinguish eliminative from redistributive evidence. Eliminative evidence, when taken up, drives at least one previously live possibility to credence zero. Redistributive evidence merely moves probability mass among the cells of a partition, leaving every cell with some positive residual probability. Ordinary Bayesian conditionalisation on a proposition is the updating rule suited to the first kind of evidence; Jeffrey conditionalisation is the rule suited to the second, since it takes the agent's new probabilities over a partition as inputs and recomputes the remainder of the credence function while holding the old probabilities conditional on each cell fixed.

My thesis is that, outside mathematics, eliminative evidence is not an independently existing species of evidence at all. I suspect it is redistributive evidence whose distribution has been pushed close enough to an endpoint that the agent imposes a zero by convention. No observation, report, or experimental result strictly eliminates every rival; some residue always remains for the possibility that the instrument has misfired, the witness has dissembled, or one's own perception has confabulated. I call the act of converting such near-certainty into a zero the elimination convention. If the thesis is correct, Jeffrey conditionalisation is the general rule for empirical evidence, and orthodox conditionalisation is the special case in which the convention has already been applied.

Consider a clinician who sees a fracture on an X-ray. She does not, strictly speaking, eliminate the hypothesis that the image is a projection artefact; she lowers its probability to something negligible and then treats the matter as settled. My suggestion is that the treating-as-settled is not justified by the evidence itself but is a practical decision about how much residual risk may be ignored.

I should like to know three things. First, can the distinction be drawn precisely, or does 'live possibility' do too much work? Second, have I merely re-described a familiar position—perhaps one about the difference between acceptance and belief, or between probability one and certainty—under a new name? Third, what are the strongest objections to the view, and is any of them fatal rather than merely uncomfortable? I am particularly uncertain whether the move from 'the residual probability is negligible' to 'the zero is conventional' is defensible, or whether it trades on a conflation of belief with policy. Please write your answer in British English.
