Write a dialogue of roughly 1,000–1,200 words between two philosophers, Maya and Julian, who have just left a public consultation on a proposed statute. The statute would permit the gradual replacement of a patient's entire brain by functionally equivalent artificial neurons — never more than one small neural tract at a time — but only on the condition that the patient remains continuously conscious throughout the procedure. Maya holds that the patient survives this procedure, because what matters for survival is the right kind of causally and functionally continuous psychological life, not the material substrate in which it is realized. Julian holds that the patient does not survive, because a person is a living biological organism, and a perfect functional replica, however gradually introduced, is at most a successor. The dialogue must show real back-and-forth rather than trading speeches. Julian must press the branching challenge: if psychological continuity is sufficient for survival, why wouldn't the patient also survive as two equally continuous successors if their mental life were continued in two substrates? Maya must answer that challenge directly, not change the subject. Maya must press the threshold problem: does Julian's view force him to say that the last replacement kills the patient, or that there is a sharp cutoff somewhere? Julian must answer directly. The two should also discuss whether the statute's continuous-consciousness condition tracks anything metaphysically important, given that people routinely survive sleep and anesthesia, or whether one of them should regard it as mere theater. Each character should concede or refine at least two specific sub-points in the course of the exchange while the central disagreement remains unresolved. End with each stating, in one or two sentences, the precise issue that now separates them.
