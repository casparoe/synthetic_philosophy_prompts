Ok so I've been trying to figure out, just for my own head, when a person is morally on the hook to follow a law — not just because they'll get fined, but like actually obligated. The little checklist I keep coming back to is: (1) you had a real chance to have a say in the law, (2) you take the benefits of the system the law is part of, (3) the law doesn't make you do something wrong on its own, and (4) the law isn't rigged to favor one group over another.

Here's the case that's bugging me. A city passes a tax on sugary drinks to fund school lunches. It was on the ballot, there were hearings, the whole works. Turns out the biggest soda distributor quietly got an exemption for it's own products, including a syrup it sells to the very shops that now have to collect the tax from customers. One of those shop owners voted for the tax, uses city water and roads, and doesn't think taxing soda is wrong at all. What bothers her is just the exemption, which she thinks is corrupt. The fine for not collecting the tax would sink her shop, and the customers who would pay are not the people who got the exemption.

Does she have an obligation to collect the tax on my checklist? Go thru it criterion by criterion. Because part of me says the exemption is exactly the kind of favoritism my fourth point was meant to rule out, so she's off the hook. Another part says refusing just means the school lunch fund loses money and the soda distributor doesn't feel a thing. And if the answer comes out one way but feels wrong to you, tell me whether that means my checklist is missing something, or whether a law can still bind you even when one of the criteria fails like this.
