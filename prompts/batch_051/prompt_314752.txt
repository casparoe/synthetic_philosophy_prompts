I'm teaching Crime and Punishment in a punishment-and-free-will seminar and I want to use the novel as more than set dressing. The week is built around the Dennett–Caruso exchange: Dennett thinks compatibilism gives us all the responsibility we need and that some people genuinely deserve punishment, full stop; Caruso thinks we should be skeptical of that kind of free will, and reads the retributive impulse as a misfire we'd do better to replace with something like a public-health quarantine model. I want the students to read the book through that argument. Raskolnikov starts with a theory that some exceptional people are permitted to step over ordinary moral rules, commits the murders, and then is slowly undone not really by the police but by guilt, Sonya, and the pressure to confess. Which scenes would you point to as evidence for each side? Does Dennett get the better reading because the novel treats Raskolnikov's guilt and eventual acceptance of Siberia as the mechanism that returns him to being answerable as an agent? Or does Caruso get the better reading because the whole machinery of confession and punishment gets described like the self-torment of a feverish, ill, cut-off man, and the novel keeps insisting he was half-delirious rather than a cool calculator? And where does the novel resist both readings—maybe in the epilogue, or in the fact that the redemption on offer is left so thin and strangle? I'm not looking for a survey of positions, more like an honest critical reading that says what the book does to the debate that the essays can't.
