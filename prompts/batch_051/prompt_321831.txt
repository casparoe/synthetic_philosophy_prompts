Define a predictive shortcut for one of these systems as an algorithm that (a) produces long-horizon predictions at accuracy comparable to or better than explicit step-by-step simulation, (b) does so at substantially lower computational cost, and (c) does not itself amount, in effect, to running the full simulation under another description. Let P be the proposition that for at least one of the three systems, no predictive shortcut exists even in principle: any accurate long-horizon predictor would essentially have to trace the system's own causal steps. My credence in P is around 0.65. My colleague's is around 0.25, on the grounds that coarse-graining and effective laws routinely supply exactly these shortcuts at the scale we actually care about.

I would like your own credence in P, but more importantly I want the decomposition. Give me the sub-questions you take to determine the answer. They will plausibly include something like: whether the fine-grained physics of these systems is computationally irreducible, in the specific sense that no algorithm can determine the final state without tracing through the intermediate states; whether that irreducibility, if it holds at the fine-grained level, survives coarse-graining up to the level at which we actually make predictions; whether the practical unavailability of a shortcut should count separately from its nonexistence in principle; and whether P is precise enough to have a determinate truth value at all, given the vagueness of substantially lower computational cost and comparable accuracy. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, state how you combined them, and tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to.

Finally, tell me what single observation, theorem, or methodological development would move your credence the most, and whether you think my 0.65 and my colleague's 0.25 can both be reasonable. If not, say where the disagreement between us most likely lives.
