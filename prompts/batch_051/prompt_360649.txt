In my former life I spent a lot of time inferring things I couldn't see—particle physics, nobody ever saw a neutrino, you saw tracks and missing energy and posited the neutrino to make the numbers come out right. Since I've been reading philosophy I keep meeting the same move about other minds: we infer that other people have minds much the way physicists infer unobservables. Behavior plays the role of the tracks in a bubble chamber, and the mind is the unobserved something that best explains the observable pattern. The move is usually deployed to conclude that the problem of other minds isn't a special skeptical scandal, just ordinary scientific inference, and we should quit treating it as deeper than that.

I'm sympathetic, but I suspect the analogy is being made to carry more weight than it can. I'd like you to audit it. Give me the correspondence table: what in the other-minds case plays the role of the tracks, the background theory, the detector, the unobservable entity, the prediction, and the rival hypotheses? Then lay out where the mapping breaks. Off the top of my head: I have first-person access to my own mind in a way I never had to a neutrino; the inference to another mind has ethical and emotional stakes that particle physics doesn't; and the "unobservable" in the other-minds case is the kind of thing I already am, not a new kind of entity. There may be more. Tell me which of these disanalogies actually matter for the intended conclusion—that our knowledge of other minds is just abductive science—and which are mere decorations. Does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing the real work all along? And wether the analogy is bad for that purpose, is there a better one, or should the whole comparison be retired?
