a friend who's deep into the foundations of arithmetic keeps telling me one is not a number, properly speaking — its a unit. his favorite argument is the letters-to-words analogy: units are to numbers as letters are to words. a single letter isnt a word (he waves off 'a' and 'I' as accidents of english orthography), so a single unit isnt a number; the number two is like a word built from two repetitions of the unit-letter. he uses this to prop up the old euclidean picture where a unit is that by which each thing is called one, and a number is a multitude made from units, so the unit is prior to number and not itself a number. our other friend says this is hopeless because 1 behaves like every other number in arithmetic — successor, multiplication, primes, all of it.

i want you to audit the analogy. what actually corresponds to what (letter, word, alphabet, spelling rules — do they all have counterparts?), what the analogy quietly imports from written language that the arithmetic case may not have, and where the mapping breaks. then tell me which breaks matter for the conclusion and which are cosmetic, whether the analogy is doing the real work in making 'one is not a number' look obvious, and whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are in view. if theres a better analogy, i'd want that too.
