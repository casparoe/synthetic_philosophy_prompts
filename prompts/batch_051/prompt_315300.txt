At a reading group, a colleague repeatedly used the proverb ‘there’s no accounting for taste’ to end a dispute about whether a moral conviction — the certainty that a particular practice is repugnant — is a defensible moral outlook or merely a bias picked up from upbringing. From the proverb he moved to the general claim that moral disagreement is just clashing tastes. I want to know what that proverb could actually amount to. Please lay out the distinct precise claims it might be making (for instance, that immediate sensory preferences cannot be mistaken, that preferences are not rationally corrigible, that tastes are private and incomparable, or that evaluative standards never apply across persons), and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false. Then say which of these readings would actually support the move from harmless differences of taste to the conclusion that no moral view is better or worse, and which reading people who deploy the proverb in such debates are probably leaning on.
