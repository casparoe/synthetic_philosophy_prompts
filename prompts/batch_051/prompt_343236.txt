I have been working for a while on a small theory I call mnemonic camouflage, and I would like an honest assessment of whether it captures a real distinction or is just a vivid metaphor.

The central claim is that some true and useful ideas become hard to remember and hard to transmit for a distinctive reason. They are not rejected, forbidden, too complex, or boring; rather, their content directs attention to something ordinary cognition is built to skip—an absence, a slow background trend, a counterfactual, or any state that has no event-shaped episode. Holding such an idea in mind requires repeatedly rebuilding an attentional frame that the mind has no standing incentive to maintain, so the idea loses the competition for rehearsal even when everyone involved would agree, if prompted, that it matters. Consider two examples. A team rehearses responses to a specific disaster, but the real risk is the gradual decay of the drills themselves; the decay is a non-event spread across months, not a scenario that can be imagined in a vignette, so it never becomes shared memory. After a near miss, the lesson that luck rather than design carried the day tends to vanish from institutional memory, because what must be remembered is the absence of a cause of failure, and an absence does not supply a retrievable image. I also have a tentative prediction: such ideas spread mainly by attaching themselves to something vivid and positively valenced—a slogan, a mascot, a named hero—which pays the transmission cost the idea itself cannot pay.

What I want to know is whether the mechanism is coherent enough to be more than a metaphor. I am aware it is adjacent to salience bias, availability, and organizational forgetting; my claim is meant to be narrower and more mechanical than those. Does it earn that narrowness? Has something like it already been worked out under a more precise name? And where does it break—what ordinary forgetting or poor communication does it wrongly classify as camouflage, and what would have to be true to distinguish it from the banal fact that non-events are harder to notice than events?
