I'm chairing a review of our university's examination regulations, and the committee is stuck on whether to abolish the oral viva for research degrees. The reformers say it's an expensive, anxiety-inducing bit of theatre that mostly tests performance on the day, while the old guard say it catches weaknesses that written examination can't surface in advance. One colleague keeps pressing Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France, specifically the passage where Burke contrasts the small private stock of an individual's reason with the larger accumulated fund that a whole society builds up across generations, and warns against acting as if one's own bit of reason were equal to the whole. His claim is that the viva is exactly the kind of inherited practice whose full rationale we can't reconstruct from first principles, so we ought to leave it alone unless we can say positively what harm it does. I can see the force of that in constitutional matters, but it seems to let any old institution veto reform forever, and I'm not sure university examinations are the right object for that kind of caution. How would you apply the passage to a concrete institutional practice like the viva, rather than to a whole constitution? Is the right conclusion that we should keep the viva, or just that we should change it more slowly than the reformers want? And do you think the reformer's case is itself an example of the private stock of reason Burke warned against, or is that just a cheap way to dismiss criticism?
