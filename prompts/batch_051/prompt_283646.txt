I'm working on a longread about how philosophers actually argue — not which conclusions they hold but what they're willing to count as evidence for a view. I mostly cover science and ideas, and I've been sitting in on a months-long email thread between two philosophers I met at a summer workshop on method. One is what I'll call a case-first philosopher: she thinks the bedrock evidence is our intuitive verdicts about particular scenarios, and principles are downstream summaries that should break when a vivid case pushes hard enough. The other is a principle-first philosopher: he thinks case verdicts are noisy, culturally local, and too easy to flip with framing, so the real evidence is how well a principle sits in a system — simplicity, coherence, coverage, not having to gerrymander. They've actually agreed to do an adversarial-collaboration write-up for the peice, but neither of them has time to draft it, and I'm not enough of a philosopher to write something both of them would sign.

So: draft the joint statement for them. Use this format: (1) a list of agreed claims, substantive enough not to be "both sides care about reasons"; (2) the remaining disagreements, in wording each side would accept as a fair statement of its own view; (3) a list of cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case, argument, or test both agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction recorded in advance. Work in the two places where they genuinely got stuck: the transitivity of "better than" (one treats a long chain of tiny improvements, where each adjacent step seems fine but the endpoints clearly aren't, as a reductio of trusting case verdicts over a transitivity axiom, and the other treats it as evidence that transitivity itself is not sacrosanct), and what simplicity is doing (is it evidence of truth, or just a cognitive convenience?). If some apparent disagreement is actually terminological — say, they mean different things by "intuition" or "simple" — say so and give the disambiguation rather than leaving them to talk past each other.
