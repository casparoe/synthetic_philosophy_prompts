I'm helping organize a two-day adversarial collaboration at a workshop on interpretability, and I need the draft of the joint statement two participants would put their names to. Their positions are representational realism about network internals and an instrumentalist view of features. The realist holds that when a sparse autoencoder trained on a model's activations recovers stable directions that track, say, syntactic category or stored factual associations, those directions are genuine constituents of the network's computation. The instrumentalist holds that such recovered latents are one of many possible summaries an interpreter can build from the activations, with no privileged claim to being 'in' the network, and that 'representation' is doing more work than the measurements will bear.

To anchor the discussion they have agreed on a shared case: a single language model, a single layer of its residual stream, and a sparse autoencoder whose latents both predict downstream behavior and shift it in the expected ways when amplified or ablated. They also agree that a different autoencoder with similar reconstruction quality yields a different basis of latents that intervenes about as well, and they agree that this underdetermination is the point where their disagreement becomes concrete. They do not agree on what the case licenses one to say: whether the latents are discovered or invented; whether retraining underdetermination shows that features are instrumental fictions or only that 'the representation' was the wrong unit; and whether there is any fact of the matter about what a network represents.

Please draft the document such a collaboration would produce: the claims both can sign about the measurements and about what would count as evidence; the most precise available statement of the residual disagreement, worded so each side would accept the phrasing of its own position; and the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — each paired with a test, manipulation, or argument both agree would bear on it. Work hard to separate a merely verbal dispute over what counts as 'really in the network' from any substantive dispute that remains, and don't hide a verbal disagreement behind vague wording. If the task cannot be done well as posed — for example, because it needs a theory of content or a criterion for when two learned decompositions encode the same representation — say so plainly and say what would have to be fixed first, rather than producing a polished but empty document. Format: exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, spending the five items on some combination of jointly signable claims, precise statements of the disagreement, and cruxes with their agreed tests.
