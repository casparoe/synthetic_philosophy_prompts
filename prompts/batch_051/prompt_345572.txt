A colleague who writes about video games for a living recently summarized a long essay on the ethics of depicting war in games. His summary was roughly this:

"War games are ethically suspect because they take the worst thing human beings do to one another and turn it into recreation. A typical shooter rewards players for killing: points, kill-streaks, slow-motion kill-cams, all designed to make the player enjoy the violence. Enjoying simulated killing just is glorifying war, and it cannot help but make players more callous about actual violence. Even when a war game tries to be serious — mournful scores, tragic storylines — the surrounding gameplay is still fun, which shows the seriousness is only decoration. A medium whose aim is fun cannot represent war seriously. The only defensible war games, therefore, are the ones that refuse to be fun."

I suspect this slides between several different criticisms, and that the final claim outruns whatever preceded it, but I don't know the debate well enough to pin down exactly where. Could you audit the summary claim by claim? For each claim, I want to know what a careful critic of how games depict war would be right to accept, what a defender of serious war games would object to, and where the summary conflates positions that should be kept apart. Finally, tell me whether the closing claim — that only war games which refuse to be fun are defensible — follows from the corrected versions of the earlier claims.
