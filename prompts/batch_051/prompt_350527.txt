I have been trying to build a workable distinction between a moral preference and a moral bias, and I want a candid assessment of the criterion I have settled on. I call it the origin-transparency criterion. The idea is this: hold fixed all the object-level facts someone has about the issue, then expose them to the full, accurate causal history of how they acquired their evaluative disposition—who taught them, which examples moved them, what incentives and emotional levers shaped them. If the disposition would survive that exposure, count it as a preference; if its hold on them depends on not having that history in view, count it as a bias. Equivalently, a bias is an origin-fragile evaluative disposition, and a preference is an origin-robust one.

An example. Consider someone who became vegetarian after watching a graphic documentary, then learns that the film systematically selected the most disturbing footage and arranged it to maximize revulsion. There are two ways to respond. She might say that this fact about the film undermines her confidence, and her conviction quietly weakens. Or she might say the editing was manipulative but irrelevant to whether her conclusion is right, and her conviction persists. My criterion says the first response reveals that the disposition was, at least in part, a bias, while the second reveals a preference. The object-level evidence changes in both cases; the point is to separate the person whose conviction tracks the arguments from the person whose conviction tracks the machinery that installed it.

The worry I keep hitting is that the criterion may be circular or self-defeating. Persisting after learning one's origin could itself be a bias toward consistency, sunk costs, or the status quo, rather than evidence that the preference was genuine. And collapsing upon learning one's origin could reflect the new evidence contained in the history, not any vulnerability in the disposition itself. I am also unsure the criterion avoids the genetic fallacy: why should the causal source of an evaluative disposition matter to its authority, once the object-level facts are fixed? Tell me the three strongest objections to the origin-transparency criterion, and whether any of them is fatal rather than merely embarrassing. I am less interested in whether it matches existing terminology than in whether it is coherent as stated.
