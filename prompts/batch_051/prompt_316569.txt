I am giving a research seminar to a department of positive psychology next month. My thesis is that the standard tripartite operationalization of meaning in life — coherence, purpose, and significance — captures typical byproducts of a meaningful life rather than its core. Following Moritz Schlick's 1920s account of the meaning of life, I argue that meaning is fundamentally realized in absorbed activity done for its own sake, what Schlick described as play: activity that carries its value within itself and needs no external goal to justify it. Coherence, purpose, and significance, I claim, are largely the stories people tell after the fact about such activity, and they track it poorly. The audience is mostly empirical psychologists who study meaning in life using self-report measures built on the coherence-purpose-significance model; a few work in self-determination theory and are sympathetic to eudaimonic activity-based accounts; one senior philosopher of well-being is skeptical of questionnaire operationalism in both camps.
