I sit on the clinical AI governance committee of a large teaching hospital. Eighteen months ago we deployed a continuously learning system in the intensive care unit that recommends vasopressor and fluid dosing; it retrains on our own patients' outcomes, and its recommendations are not fully explainable. Last month, during a surge, the system recommended an unusually aggressive escalation for a patient in septic shock. The treating team followed the recommendation, as they had been trained to do, and the patient died. The root-cause review found no protocol violation: the nurses and residents acted on a recommendation that had, until then, been highly reliable; the attending was supervising several simultaneous resuscitations; the vendor's engineers cannot reconstruct why the model produced that sequence; and the exact model version that made the recommendation has since been overwritten by retraining and can no longer be inspected. Underlying the committee's split is a disagreement about whether a hospital may use a system whose harmful decisions can lack any assignable owner. One side wants to suspend the system until every adverse recommendation can be explained and attributed to a person; the other side argues that suspension would predictably cause more deaths overall, because the system's aggregate mortality and complication rates remain better than baseline clinician performance. I need to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting that recommends a concrete course of action — continue, suspend, or continue with conditions — with a timeline, and that engages fairly with the strongest consideration on the losing side. Please draft it.
