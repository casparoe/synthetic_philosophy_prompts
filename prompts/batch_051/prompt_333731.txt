I am a software engineer with no formal background in linguistics or philosophy, and I have been asked to help grade a short-answer question from an introductory linguistics course. Below are the question and one student's response. Please grade the response and provide the specific comments that justify the grade: identify what the student got right, flag any mistakes or unsupported steps, explain briefly why each one matters, and say how heavily it should weigh in the final grade. Where the student has gone wrong, indicate what a corrected version would say. Conclude with a letter grade.

Question: Explain how natural languages tolerate large numbers of homonyms (distinct, etymologically unrelated meanings sharing a single form) and cases of polysemy (related senses of a single word). Your essay should (i) identify at least two mechanisms by which hearers arrive at the intended sense in context; (ii) assess the claim that ambiguity is not a defect but a positive feature of language, because it makes puns and other creative uses possible; and (iii) explain why the boundary between homonymy and polysemy is often hard to draw, and what this difficulty suggests about how lexical meaning is represented. Use the words pitch and tone among your examples.

Student response: Homonymy is common because the number of distinct meanings a language needs is much larger than its inventory of short, pronounceable forms, so unrelated meanings inevitably fall together on one sound sequence. The main coping mechanism is context. In the sentence the pitch was too high for the singer, the adjective high and the noun singer make the musical sense overwhelmingly likely, excluding the throw and tar senses. A second mechanism is frequency. Hearers weight the more frequent sense more heavily, which is why the sentence I need to go to the bank defaults to the financial reading unless the surrounding situation supplies a river or a fisherman. Ambiguity is not purely a defect. Puns, double meanings, and literary ambiguity all depend on one form carrying multiple senses; a language engineered to remove every such duplication would need an impractical number of distinct forms, and it would lose many creative uses. The boundary between homonymy and polysemy is hard to apply because it depends on the history of the word, and most speakers do not know that history. The acoustic, attitudinal, and muscular senses of tone all descend from the same Greek etymon, tonos, whose original meaning was stretching or tension. Because these senses go back to a single source, tone is a true homonym rather than a case of polysemy; the meanings have diverged far enough that speakers now experience them as separate words. This very difficulty shows that the lexicon is stored as a list of discrete entries, and that when a new use appears, a synchronic rule checks etymological relatedness and files the use in the appropriate entry.
