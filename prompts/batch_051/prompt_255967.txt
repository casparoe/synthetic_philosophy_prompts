A friend of mine has been reading Aristotle on money and exchange, and he came back with an argument he swears is just Aristotle put in premise-conclusion form. I can't find the bad step, and the more I stare at it the more it seems to prove too much. So I want you to go step by step and tell me which claim to reject, and what I pay for rejecting it.

Here's the reconstruction.

(1) Money came about by convention — the word itself is tied to nomos, law or custom — to serve as a measure and a medium so that genuinely unlike goods could be equated and swapped.
(2) A thing's natural and proper use is whatever it was brought into being to do: a shoe is for wearing, a house for sheltering people, money for passing around in exchange.
(3) Lending at interest means requiring that money produce more money — you get back the principal plus an extra lump of the same kind — while the original money, just as money, does nothing.
(4) Using a thing against its natural function is unnatural, and acquiring wealth by unnatural use is corrupt, or at least the lowest kind of acquisition.
(5) So charging interest is wrong.

That part already struck me as pretty strong. But my friend then pressed the same reasoning further:

(6) A merchant who buys grain where it's cheap and sells it where it's dear also ends up with more money than he started with, through money circulating rather than through making or growing anything.
(7) An owner who charges rent on a tenement is also using a thing against its natural function — the building's function is to shelter, but the owner is using it to generate income while somebody else does the sheltering.
(8) Even a farmer who sells his surplus crop for cash is turning use into accumulation, which on (2)-(4) looks like the same unnatural pattern.
(9) But (6)-(8) are not all wrong in the way interest is supposed to be; some seem perfectly fine.
(10) So the argument seems to prove too much.

I can't tell whether the flaw is in (2), (3), (4), or in the leap to (6)-(8), or whether I'm just missing a distinction Aristotle would insist on. I'd like to keep the conclusion that there's something specifically objectionable about interest, and I'd also like to keep the general idea that using a thing against its nature can be a real criticism. But I can't see how to keep both without just insisting that money is special in a way I haven't actually said. So: which step goes wrong? And if the fix is to add a hidden premise — say, that money is sterile or merely conventional in a way a cow, a field, or a building is not — does that premise actually do the work, and what does it cost? I want the diagnosis, not just a vote on wether the conclusion is right.
