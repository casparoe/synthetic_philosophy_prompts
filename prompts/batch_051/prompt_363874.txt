I've been arguing with a friend about consequentialist cluelessness, and he pulled out Mill on me. We were stuck on this pasage from Mill's *Utilitarianism*:

"The creed which accepts as the foundation of morals, Utility, or the Greatest Happiness Principle, holds that actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse of happiness."

I get the basic idea, but I don't see how it's supposed to work when someone genuinely wants to do the right thing but can't trace the consequences. Say a person has to choose between taking a job at an oil company or a solar company. They aren't asking what feels best; they want to pick the option that actually tends to promote happiness. But every choice sets off chains nobody can fully predict: who gets hired instead, which technologies get funded, how communities change, what political pressures build, and so on. If the actual downstream effects are unknowable, or maybe even chaotic, then Mill's sentence looks either silent or impossibly demanding. It says rightness depends on the tendency of the action, but it doesn't say tendency relative to what information.

I'm not looking for a survey of views here. I want you to commit. If you had to defend Mill against the cluelessness objection, would you say the principle needs to be read as about expected consequences rather than actual consequences? Or would you say Mill is right to leave rightness partly independent of what the agent can know, and cluelessness is just an epistemic problem, not a moral one? Pick one and defend it, and be explicit about whether your fix actually solves the problem or just moves it one step further along.
