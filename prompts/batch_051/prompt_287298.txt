I've come around to the view that an AI system which can form judgments about what should be done but can't act on them—no actuators, no final say, it just outputs advice a human is free to ignore—is not a moral agent. Not just that it isn't morally responsible for the outcomes, but that it isn't the right sort of thing to be morally assessed at all, except in the way a policy or a weather model are. The core thought is that moral agency requires the capacity to make a difference: to translate your judgment into action, or at least to have the standing to act on it. But I can feel my folk concepts doing a lot of the work here, and I want the view stress-tested.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they'd be, from fatal to merely akward. For instance: do fully locked-in humans or people under total guardianship count as counterexamples? Does the view collapse once you distinguish having the capacity to act from ever actually getting to exercise it? Is it really reasons-responsiveness or judgment-sensitivity that grounds moral agency, with causal efficacy being incidental? Which of these escape routes are live rather than merely logically possible? And a meta question: has the position protected itself with qualifications like "dispositional capacity," "standing to act," or "in principle," and if so, is that unfalsifiability a problem for a view of this kind?

Tell me which defeaters a defender of my view would themselves concede as counting against it, and which they'd dismiss as changing the subject. I'd also like a honest verdict on whether the view risks anything at all, or whether it's been qualified into a tautology.
