A colleague on an AI-governance working group keeps deflating the whole argument about legal personhood for AI with the corporation analogy. Her line is: we already treat corporations as legal persons, and corporations aren't conscious, have no feelings, and can't be punished the way a natural person can; so giving an AI system the same package — holding property, entering contracts, suing and being sued in its own name — shouldn't require settling anything about whether the AI is conscious or has moral status. I suspect the analogy is doing a lot more work than she's letting on, but I can't pin down exactly where it gives out.

Walk me through an audit of the analogy. Please use these sections, in this order, with rough lengths:

1. The analogy restated (~100 words) — the source and target, and the exact conclusion the analogy is being used to license.
2. Correspondence table — a table with at least six rows, mapping features of the corporate form (shareholders, directors, the charter, corporate assets, limited liability, dissolution, etc.) onto their candiate equivalents in the AI case.
3. Where it holds (~150 words) — the real symmetries, and which obvious disanalogies don't matter for her argument.
4. Where it breaks (~300 words) — the disanalogies that do matter, and what the corporate side quietly imports that the AI case may lack (e.g., separate assets with ultimate human beneficiaries, natural persons reachable behind the veil, a business purpose, the ability to wind the entity up). It would also help to say whether the argument depends on a particular picture of the corporation (a nexus of contracts vs. a real entity).
5. What survives of her argument (~150 words) — once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion survive that legal personhood for AI can bypass questions of consciousness or moral status? Distinguish a strong reading from a weaker "personhood as bookkeeping device" reading.
6. A better analogy, if any (~100 words) — or say briefly why an analogy might be the wrong tool here.
