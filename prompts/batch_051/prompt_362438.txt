I live in a city with a large and constantly rotating population of interns, remote workers, and exchange students, and the perennial argument on local forums is how far a visitor's obligation to follow local micro-conventions actually extends. The examples are small and recurring: tipping at counter service, staying to one side in queues, not jaywalking on an empty street, and a dozen other things locals treat as basic respect and visitors treat as friction. A friend who works in municipal policy has proposed replacing the vague when-in-Rome norm with an explicit mechanism. The city would keep a public register of conventions claimed to be load-bearing, and any resident could file a complaint, called a strike, against a registered long-stay visitor for violating a listed convention. A strike would be actionable only if the filer posted a public, non-circular account of the interest the convention protects. Every registered visitor would get three costless strikes per calendar month; from the fourth strike onward, the visitor would choose between a twenty-minute reconciliation session run by the filer or a fine set at the filer's declared hourly wage multiplied by the estimated minutes of disruption caused. Declared wages would be displayed next to each filed strike.

I am not asking whether the scheme is just; I want a mechanism analysis of how it would actually behave. Walk me through who files and why, how the free strikes and the self-declared wage get gamed first, what the public-justification requirement does to the conventions themselves once residents have to defend them in writing, what second-order effects appear in tipping, queueing, jaywalking, and the local-visitor relationship generally, and whether the failure modes could be patched without simply moving the gaming somewhere else. Please give me exactly five numbered items, each one to two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
