I'm putting together a one-pager on my considered views about introspection for a faculty workshop, and I'd like someone to go over it before I'm grilled. I've taught this stuff for years, but I've never written the commitments down next to each other, and I have a nagging feeling the list might pull in a few directions. Here they are, roughly in the order they came to me:

(1) When I sincerely report a current conscious experience — a pain, an afterimage, how bright that red patch looks, a sudden itch — the default is that I'm right. No amount of looking at my brain or my behaviour could put someone in a better position to say what that experience is like than I am. I treat that as about as close to axiomatic as anything gets.

(2) At the same time, I don't think introspection is an inner eye scanning private objects. A lot of what I call knowing my own mind is interpretation: I work out what I believe, want, or feel from evidence — what I said, what I did, the situation I'm in, what I can readily defend — rather the way other people work me out. That interpretive process is fallible, theory-laden, and vulnerable to confabulation, framing, expectation effects, the lot.

(3) I also accept that there are mental states that are genuinely mine but structurally closed to introspection: implicit attitudes, early perceptual processing, suppressed motives, maybe some dispositional states. They influence what I do, I can find out about them only through third parties or brute behavioural patterns, but I can't find them by looking inward.

(4) Yet I'm not a pessimist. When I direct introspective attention to an experience — including the felt quality of an emotion, not just colour patches — my judgements get finer-grained and more reliable, not less. I think the line that introspection is mostly confabulation is either a misreading or overblown; what is mostly confabulated is my story about why I did something, not how things seem to me right now.

(5) Finally, I'm drawn to the transparency story for perceptual experience: to know what my visual experience of the tomato is like, I attend to the tomato — its red, its shape, the light on its skin — not to any inner mental image. There's no extra mental paint I could inspect by turning my attention elsewhere.

So here's what I'd like: an audit. Which pairs of these are genuinely in tension, and which only looked in tension until you got the wording right? For the real tensions, rank them by severity, say what the cheapest repair would be for each pair, and tell me what I'd have to give up. And if there's one revision that would clean up the most conflicts at once, I want to know that too. Don't spare my feelings.
