I sit on the content review board of a mid-size game studio. We are considering a proposal for a single-player historical war game whose centerpiece mission places the player as a soldier during the massacre of civilians in an occupied village. The player may refuse to fire, but the level is built around the possibility of doing so. Killing civilians is never rewarded — no score, no achievement, no loot — and an end-of-mission screen reports the number of civilian dead without commentary. The lead designer argues that the game is anti-war in intent: the atrocity must be playable rather than shown in a cutscene, because the design goal is to make the player feel complicity rather than spectatorship. Two board members object that, whatever the framing, the studio would be selling the experience of committing a massacre as a consumable interactive product, and that a game whose systems are built for engagement will inevitably make the atrocity thrilling or pleasurable in the wrong way — a form of glorification under the cover of seriousness. Draft the board's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a concrete recommendation the team could act on, the reasoning behind it, and a serious engagement with the strongest consideration on the losing side.
