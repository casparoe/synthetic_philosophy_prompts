I've been reading around in the historiography debates about history from below and microhistory, and a double standard keeps bugging me. When a historian works with an elite source — say a monarch's correspondence or a minister's memorandum — the natural thing is to treat it as evidence of that person's aims and choices: the letter is someone deciding, weighing options, acting on the world. But when the same historian works with a non-elite source — an artisan's diary, a weaver's testimony before a church court, a servant's complaint in a municipal record — the natural thing is to treat it as evidence of the beliefs, fears, or resentments of a group: it gets read as a specimen of popular religion, popular resistance, or popular memory, and the individual becomes a representative rather than an agent. So the queen gets read as a person who makes history; the weaver gets read as a sample of a culture. I have a suspicion this isn't just a pragmatic response to thin records — because the asymmetry persists even when the non-elite source is unusually rich, say a hundred-page private journal, where a historian would still ask what it shows about the popular experience of industrialisation before they asked what it shows about this particular person's plans.

What I want you to do: take the asymmetry at its strongest, where both readings are competent historians doing normal work rather than some crude error, and ask whether anything justifies it. Find the best candidate principle that would generate both treatments — something about causal power, typicality, genre, about who actually had choices — and test it against nearby cases (a general's diary, a merchant's ledger, a slave's testimony, a nineteenth-century worker's autobiography, whichever help). Then give an honest verdict: should we keep the asymmetry, narrow it, invert it, or give it up? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, i.e. state the verdict immediately, and then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating of the question, and write in British English.
