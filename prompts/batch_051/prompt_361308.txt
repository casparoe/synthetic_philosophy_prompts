I am a novelist, and in the book I am currently drafting my protagonist keeps a journal that she has quietly rewritten from the inside — a self-help gratitude journal of the usual kind, except that she has been working her way through Spinoza and is trying to reconcile the daily practice of giving thanks with her growing conviction that nothing whatever could have been otherwise. I would like you to write the actual text of that journal page, or a short sequence of pages, as it now stands, which I may insert verbatim into the novel as an interlude: the prompt, the instructions in the bland, encouraging second person of the genre, and her deadpan annotations at the points where the exercise collapses under its own premises.

Let it be a parody of a guided gratitude journal, roughly 700 words, in the voice of the wellness genre but written by a thoroughgoing Spinozist — someone who holds that free will is an illusion born of inadequate ideas, that God is simply nature understood under the aspect of eternity, and that the highest thing available to us is the intellectual love of God: an active joy in seeing why each thing follows necessarily, rather than thanks addressed to a person who could have done otherwise. The jokes should come from getting Spinoza right. Gratitude, as ordinarily felt, is directed at an object imagined as a free and external cause; once the journal-writer attends to the actual causes, that object dissolves into the whole order of nature, and her thanks have nowhere local to land — they must end up addressed to everything or to nothing. Work through that real question, whether necessitarianism leaves gratitude standing or converts it into something else, without ever stating it as a thesis. A specialist should recognize the Ethics underneath; a reader who has never opened it should still follow and laugh.

Make definite choices rather than offering alternatives: choose the structure — for instance, a five-day sequence of daily entries with the pseudo-legal fine print the genre loves — the running jokes, and the ending. After the piece, add a short note of a hundred words or so on where the satire takes liberties with Spinoza or leans on a contested reading of him; I would rather be told which moves are controversial than have them smoothed over.
