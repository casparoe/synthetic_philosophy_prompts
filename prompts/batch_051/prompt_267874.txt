One of my sisters has spent her career defending companies against fraud, sanctions, and bribery charges, and she has developed unusually firm, consistent intuitions about when a business is complicit in a wrong that another party ultimately commits. She is careful to say that these are her moral judgments, not predictions about what a court would hold. For years I have been collecting her verdicts on invented cases, and I still cannot state the rule she is tracking. So far she has said the following. (1) A precision-machining firm knowingly manufactures bespoke firing pins for an arms broker who is illegally supplying a sanctioned militia, and the broker's buyers in fact use the pins; the firm is complicit. (2) A forklift operator moves crates that, unbeknownst to him, contain smuggled ivory, and that shipment reaches its buyer; not complicit. (3) An office manager discovers that her employer is laundering money through the firm's accounts but does nothing at all about it, neither helping nor reporting it; not complicit. (4) A courier firm, warned by a whistleblower exactly which parcels are part of an ivory-smuggling operation, transports those parcels anyway and the shipment gets through; complicit. (5) A bank processes a large wire transfer for a customer it knows from leaked records to be evading sanctions, charges its ordinary fee, and the transfer clears; complicit. (6) A landlord renews a lease at market rates after learning that the tenant is running an illegal gambling operation from the premises, and the operation continues; complicit. (7) A shareholder who knows that the company she holds shares in is dumping toxic waste keeps her shares but takes no part in management or governance; not complicit.

The new case I want your help with is this: the same precision-machining firm from the first case contracts to supply a further batch of the same bespoke pins to the same broker, knowing exactly what they are for, and ships them; but the shipment arrives after the broker's last sale to the militia has already been completed. The broker never uses the pins, and its sales and the militia's operations would have gone exactly the same had the firm never taken the contract. I can see at least two rules consistent with everything she has said so far. On one, complicity requires that a person knowingly performed an act that in fact furthered the wrong, even if that was not their aim. On the other, complicity requires that the person knowingly performed an act of the sort the wrongful venture relies on and understood it to be part of the venture's machinery, whether or not it in fact made a difference is, on this view, no more decisive than it is for a criminal attempt. I want to know which rule, if either, better captures her seven verdicts; what each rule predicts about the new case and what your best guess is about her verdict; which of her earlier cases is the closest analog; what principle she would be endorsing by each possible verdict on the new case; and what further hypothetical would most cleanly settle which rule she is actually running.
