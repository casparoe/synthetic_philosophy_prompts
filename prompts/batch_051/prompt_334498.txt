I am a novelist. In my current book, a mathematician character tries to persuade a nonspecialist that the sentence "2 is an element of 4" is not a straightforward fact about numbers but an artifact of how the numbers get encoded as sets. Before I put her speech in the book, I want to test the reasoning, so I have laid it out as a numbered argument. Here it is:

(1) Arithmetic characterizes the natural numbers as a sequence built from an initial 0 by a successor operation, subject to the Peano axioms; any system satisfying those axioms (with every element obtained from 0 by finitely many successions) is a fully adequate realization of the natural numbers.
(2) The finite von Neumann ordinals form such a system: 0 = ∅, 1 = {0}, 2 = {0,1}, 3 = {0,1,2}, 4 = {0,1,2,3}. In this system, 2 ∈ 4.
(3) The finite Zermelo ordinals form another such system: 0 = ∅, 1 = {0}, 2 = {1}, 3 = {2}, 4 = {3}. In this system, 2 ∉ 4.
(4) If "2 ∈ 4" were true in virtue of the numbers themselves, then every fully adequate realization of the natural numbers would agree on it; a fact about the numbers cannot hinge on an arbitrary choice among equally adequate encodings.
(5) The two systems in (2) and (3) are equally adequate yet disagree about "2 ∈ 4," so the sentence is not made true or false by the numbers themselves.
(6) Therefore the unqualified claim "2 is an element of 4" has no determinate truth value until a particular set-theoretic encoding is stipulated; at most it is true of the von Neumann encoding and false of the Zermelo one.

I half-suspect that step (4) is doing the philosophical work and that a defender of the claim that numbers literally are sets would reject it, but I cannot say exactly how. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and under what reading of it? In answering, please separate what is settled in this area from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of mathematics, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
