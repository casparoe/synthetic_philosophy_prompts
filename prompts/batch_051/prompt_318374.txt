I am a high school senior writing a paper for a philosophy of mind and music elective, and the paper grew out of a recurring argument with a friend. We had been listening repeatedly to the same slow, minor-key piano piece with a long descending melody. She maintained that the piece's sadness is not something we merely project onto the sounds; the sadness, she said, is a property of the music in roughly the way its tempo is. I took the other side. When I call the music sad, I argued, I am describing something about the kind of response it tends to elicit in listeners who share my auditory system and musical upbringing, not reporting a feature of the acoustic signal itself.

She had a reply for that, and I now think it deserves more than casual argument. If sad were only in the listener, she asked, why do the same basic features—slow tempo, minor mode, falling contour—keep being identified as sad across quite different audiences and periods? And she added that a listener who heard the piece and felt nothing sad would be missing something about the piece, not merely differing in taste. I find that partly persuasive, but I also suspect that agreement across listeners might be explained by shared physiology or shared conventions rather than by sadness sitting in the sound on its own.

I want to treat her claim as a partially testable hypothesis rather than a verbal standoff. Please set out the strongest test you can design of the claim that a piece's sadness is in the music itself, and not only in listeners' learned or species-typical responses. Tell me what observations or experiments would count, what pattern of results would push toward each side, and exactly what the test could never settle even in principle. You may take for granted that a sad piece and a sad person are sad in different senses; I am not looking to dissolve the question by defining it away, but to know how much of it can be answered by evidence.
