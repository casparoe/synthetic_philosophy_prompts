I keep going around in circles on an argument that seems to show something stronger than the usual skeptical worry about induction, and I want someone to locate precisely where it fails.

Fix a future time t. Suppose that every emerald observed up to t has been green.

(1) That body of evidence inductively supports the hypothesis that all emeralds are green.
(2) The underlying principle seems to be this: a property found in every examined member of a class may be projected onto the unexamined members of that class.
(3) Define 'grue' as follows: an object is grue just in case it is first observed before t and is green, or it is first observed at or after t and is blue.
(4) Every emerald observed before t is grue, since each was first observed before t and is green.
(5) Therefore, by the very same principle, the same evidence equally supports the hypothesis that all emeralds are grue.
(6) The two hypotheses make incompatible forecasts about the first emerald observed at or after t: green, on the first, and blue, on the second. The same evidence cannot genuinely support both.
(7) Nor is this an artifact of green and blue. For any past regularity and any arbitrarily chosen future divergence, one can build a grue-like predicate that fits the past evidence and then diverges in exactly that way. An unrestricted projection rule therefore licenses every possible continuation of the evidence, which is to license nothing.
(8) So either the projection rule in (2) is worthless, or it must be restricted to a favored class of predicates — and the evidence itself cannot justify that restriction.

Both horns trouble me. The obvious move is to reject (5) and say 'green' is a natural, kind-tracking predicate while 'grue' is gerrymandered. But my interlocutor replies that in a language whose basic color predicates are 'grue' and 'bleen' — where an object is bleen just in case it is first observed before t and blue, or first observed at or after t and green — 'green' itself can be defined as 'grue if first observed before t, and bleen if first observed at or after t'. The gerrymander is symmetrical, and preferring 'green' begins to look like a choice of vocabulary rather than a constraint on induction.

Where, exactly, does the argument go wrong? If you reject a premise, name it, explain why it looked true, and say what rejecting it commits me to. If you think the argument is valid, tell me which horn of (8) I should take and what the other horn would cost. I would most like to keep (2), the plain thought that observed greenness supports greenness; I am prepared to give up (5), but only if that does not turn out to be giving up (2) in disguise.
