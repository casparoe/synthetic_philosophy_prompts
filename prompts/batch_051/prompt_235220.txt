A friend and I recently argued late into the evening about whether an actual infinite could exist. He defended a finitist conclusion with the following version of Hilbert's Hotel, and I could not say where his reasoning went wrong, if it did.

(1) A hotel is full if and only if every room is occupied.
(2) In Hilbert's Hotel, before the manager acts, every room is occupied; so the hotel is full.
(3) The manager instructs each guest to move one room up: the guest in room n moves to room n+1. No guest leaves, and no guest arrives; the same guests and the same rooms are present throughout.
(4) After the move, room 1 is empty; so by (1), the hotel is no longer full.
(5) A hotel cannot change from full to not full unless the number of guests or the number of rooms changes.
(6) But by (3) neither number changed, while by (2) and (4) the hotel changed from full to not full. That is a contradiction.
(7) Therefore the description of Hilbert's Hotel is incoherent; and since the only unusual feature of the story is the actual infinity of rooms, actual infinities are impossible.

My friend then said the only real alternatives are finitism or accepting that a full hotel can become not-full merely by rearranging its occupants, and he finds the second obviously absurd. I came away convinced that something in the argument must be wrong, but I could not say where.

Please tell me whether this argument is valid. If it is invalid, identify the first step that fails, explain precisely why it fails, and then say whether finitism can still be defended once that step is rejected. If you think the argument is valid, say whether we should accept the conclusion or give up one of the premises. End with a last line of the form \answer{...} containing either 'valid' or the number of the first false step (for example, \answer{5}).
