I've been going down a rabbit hole on direct democracy (California ballot propositions, Brexit, Swiss referendums) and came up with a mechanism I want stress-tested. Here's the proposal: any citizen-initiated ballot measure has to be voted on as a package. Voters get two yes/no boxes on the same ballot. The first box authorizes the policy change. The second box is the paying-for-it side: if a nonpartisan state office estimates the measure will cost money, the second box triggers a specific new tax, fee, or budget cut, naming the actual tax form or budget line, that raises or saves the estimated amount; if the measure would reduce revenue, the second box cuts a specific line item by an equal amount. Both boxes pass or nothing does. Also, the first page of the official voter guide has to show the cost estimate, and a randomly selected citizens' panel reviews and signs off on that summary before it goes out.

Don't just tell me whether this is a good idea in the abstract. Walk me through how it actually plays out once people adapt to it: what it incentivizes among initiative authors, and whether it changes who can afford to run an initiative; what kinds of measures get on the ballot and what kinds quietly never get filed; how voters actually use the two boxes, and whether the package rule actually disciplines the dynamic where voters approve spending while rejecting the taxes to pay for it, or just makes ballots harder to parse; how interest groups, parties, and legislatures route around it; and what happens when the cost estimate is wrong. Where does it get gamed first, and which failure modes could be patched without gutting the mechanism, and which are just baked in?

Also, please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who study referendums and direct democracy, and don't present a contested take as if it were the consensus. American English, please.
