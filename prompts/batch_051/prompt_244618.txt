I've been tinkering with a reconstruction of Popper's argument against laws of history for my philosophy of social science lecture, and I cant tell anymore whether its airtight or held together with tape. Here is the version I put on the board:

(1) If there are laws of history, then the future course of history is in principle predictable from present conditions.
(2) The future course of history is in principle predictable from present conditions only if the future growth of scientific knowledge is in principle predictable.
(3) But the future growth of scientific knowledge is not in principle predictable. To predict today that a particular idea will be known tomorrow, we would have to be able to state that idea today, and stating it today just is knowing it today.
(4) So the future course of history is not in principle predictable.
(5) Therefore there are no laws of history.

My worry is that step (3) is doing something sneaky: it seems to slide between predicting that an idea will be discovered and predicting what the idea is. But then I also worry that this is exactly the distinction Popper would reject, so maybe the argument is fine and I'm the one confused. And there is a second worry about step (2): even if future science is unpredictable, a law of history might still predict the parts of history that dont depend on the content of future science. So tell me: is this argument valid? If not, which step fails, and under what reading? If it is valid, are the premises true, and is the conclusion as strong as Popper needs?
