I'm writing an essay for a seminar on the history of ideas, and I'd like feedback on an argument I'm developing before I commit it to paper. The essay is about what connects Darwin's and Turing's two famous inversions of reasoning. In Darwin's case, the pre-Darwinian assumption was that complex functional design — the eye, the wing, the orchid's mimicry — required a designer with foresight and understanding of the end being produced. Darwin flipped this: biological competence can be generated by a process, natural selection, that has no aim, no foresight, and no understanding of what it is producing. In Turing's case, the analogous pre-Turingian assumption was that intelligent-seeming behavior — doing arithmetic, playing chess, holding a conversation — required a mind that understands what it is doing. Turing flipped this: a machine can produce such behavior by following rules, storing and manipulating symbols, without understanding them in any human sense. So both cases replace an explanation that appeals to a comprehending agent with an explanation that appeals to a blind, mechanical, competence-producing process. My essay will argue that the two inversions are not just parallel rhetorically but are unified in a deeper way: both are instances of what you might call the design-without-a-designer principle. I also want to argue that the standard objection to each — that the process must have comprehension smuggled in somewhere, whether in the fitness function or in the programmer — is the same objection in two different costumes, and that it is no more fatal in one case than in the other.

I'd especially like comments on three points. First, is my assimilation of the two cases too strong? Some people say Darwin's inversion eliminates the need for any intelligence in the system, while Turing's inversion merely relocates the intelligence from the machine to the programmer; if that distinction is sound, my thesis is in trouble. Second, does my account of comprehension — as I use it, roughly, the capacity to grasp what one is doing and why — do enough work, or is it just a placeholder? Third, can you suggest a concrete example or thought experiment that would make the unity or disunity of the two inversions vivid for a reader who is not already steeped in the literature?
