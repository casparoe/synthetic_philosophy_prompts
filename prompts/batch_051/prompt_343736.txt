Consider a classic argument that generational categories such as Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z are not merely arbitrary labels, but pick out something real. In rough form: a generation is a birth cohort whose members pass through the same formative public events — wars, recessions, technological shifts, widely shared media moments — at the same developmental stage, and these shared events stamp the cohort with distinctive attitudes, values, and habits of mind. On this view, Gen Z is real in something like the way a biological cohort is real: its members share a causal history that makes them systematically more similar to one another, across a range of socially relevant traits, than they are to Millennials or to the cohort that follows.

Now consider a development that postdates the classic version of this argument. When it was formulated, there was a broadly shared mass-media environment, so most members of a cohort genuinely were exposed to many of the same events at roughly the same age. Today the information environment is personalised. Recommendation systems, algorithmic feeds, and fragmented attention mean that two people born in the same year can spend their formative years in nearly disjoint information environments: different news, different cultural references, different norms presented with different valences, sometimes different versions of the same public event.
