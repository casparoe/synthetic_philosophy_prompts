I am a machine learning engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I have been working through the reference class problem on my own, and I want to test my understanding by forcing it through the vocabulary I actually use every day. Here is the target vocabulary I have in mind: features and covariates, conditioning sets, bins and subgroups, class priors, model capacity and regularization, calibration, expected loss, and the choice of a model family.

As I understand it, the problem runs like this. To assign a probability to a single case — this patient's tumor responding, this applicant defaulting, this defendant re-offending — one has to put the case in some reference class of similar cases. But every single case sits in indefinitely many classes at once: all patients with this disease, all patients with this subtype, all patients with this subtype at this age and with this particular mutation, and so on downward. These classes yield different frequencies, and nothing in the world seems to determine which class attaches the probability to the case itself. The usual repair I have seen is to choose the narrowest class for which reliable statistics can be compiled, but that only relocates the arbitrariness, since the narrowest reliable class depends on how much data one has, on how one trades bias against variance, and on what one is willing to count as reliable.

In my day job I do not worry about any of this, or I tell myself I do not. I choose features, fit a model, and report P(y | x). A model's prediction for an individual is itself a frequency over a bucket, namely everyone who shares that feature vector, or is close to it under some metric once smoothing and regularization enter. If the feature vector is nearly unique to the individual, the bucket has effectively one member and I regularize, which is itself a quiet reference-class decision. And the feature set is chosen to minimize expected loss over a population, not because it is the right class for this individual. To sharpen the puzzle: two models with different feature sets can both be well calibrated on a population and yet assign materially different probabilities to the same individual, which looks, at least on the surface, like two reference classes disagreeing.

I want the faithful translation, and then the residue report. Please translate the reference class problem into the ML vocabulary above as precisely as you can, without quietly adopting the ML framework's assumptions and then presenting them as a translation. Then sort what you find into four piles: which parts of the problem map cleanly onto things like the choice of a conditioning set, a subgroup, or a bin; which parts you had to distort or approximate to force them into the vocabulary; which parts have no expression in my vocabulary at all; and whether the residue indicates that the original problem is mainly an artifact of thinking of probability as objective frequency rather than as a model's conditional prediction, or indicates that ML is suppressing a question it ought to be asking about single cases. I would rather receive an honest report of what does not survive than a forced equivalence.

Finally, please tell me how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the specific point you are least sure about.
