I've come to think that the deepest objection to penal substitution is not the familiar moral complaint that it is unjust to punish an innocent person, but a conceptual one: the doctrine seems to treat desert, guilt, or liability to punishment as a transferable item that can be moved from the guilty to the innocent, when in fact legal and moral deserts are attached essentially to the person who acted. David Lewis's way of pressing this thought makes it especially sharp—penal substitution appears to require either that two persons somehow share one moral status, or that one person be punished for what another person did while still counting as a genuine case of punishment rather than mere harm.

I want a rigorous map of what would count against this conceptual objection. Which considerations about the nature of punishment, retribution, representation, or personhood would undermine it? Please sort the considerations into those that would be decisive if accepted—showing that the notion of transferred desert is coherent after all—and those that would merely soften the problem or push it to a different location. Also address whether a defender of penal substitution can accept that the doctrine is conceptually anomalous and treat that anomaly as theologically central rather than as a defect, and whether that move sacrifices the claim that the doctrine is rationally intelligible. At the end, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or case would lead you to a different overall verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
