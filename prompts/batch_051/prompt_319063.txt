I'm a software engineer, so mostly I think about symmetries when I'm trying to make a UI layout behave under rotation or whatever, not in physics. But I fell down a rabbit hole on Noether's theorem and whether symmetry actually *explains* conservation laws or just labels them. I started reading the Tavel translation of Noether's 1918 paper *Invariant Variation Problems*, and near the beginning she says:

“By a ‘group of transformation,’ familiarly, is meant a system of transformations such that for each transformation, there exists an inverse contained in the system, and such that the composition of any two transformations of the system in turn belongs to the system.”

Then I found a blog post that reconstructed her argument like this:

(1) Noether's theorem is about an action that is invariant under a continuous group of transformations.

(2) By her own definition above, a group is just a system of transformations closed under inverses and composition.

(3) So saying "the action is invariant under the group" means nothing more than: if a transformation is allowed, its inverse is allowed, and composing two allowed transformations gives another allowed one.

(4) Noether's theorem says that whenever the action has that closure property, a conserved quantity falls out.

(5) So the conservation law is explained by symmetry alone, not by any particular dynamical details.

(6) Therefore any two systems whose actions are invariant under the same group must have the same conserved quantity.

I can't tell weather the problem is step (3) subtly changing what "invariant under a group" means, or whether step (4) overstates what the theorem actually says. Could you go through it step by step and tell me if the argument is valid, and if not, exactly which step fails and what the corrected version of that step would be? If it is valid, does (6) really follow?
