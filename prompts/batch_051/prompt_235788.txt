I'm a novelist and I keep running into an asymetry in how people judge two kinds of deception. Charlotte tells her husband a direct falsehood about where she was last night, and everyone inside the novel and out treats her as a liar. But in the next chapter she lets him assemble the same false belief out of strictly true sentences, a strategic pause, a half-correction that only sharpens the wrong conclusion. Readers and editors both say well, she didn't actually say it — even though she meant it just as much and worked harder to make sure he'd land exactly there. I want to know whether there's a real principle that makes the outright lie worse, or whether this is just a convenience that keeps plausible deniability in working order. What's the strongest candidate justification you can find? And give me an honest verdict on whether it survives the case where the two deceptions are identical in every way — intent, harm, certainty the victim will be fooled — except that one is said and the other is only implied.
