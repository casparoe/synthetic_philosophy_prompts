I have been defending a hinge-epistemological picture in my reading group — roughly the view associated with Wittgenstein's On Certainty — according to which all evidential reasoning and rational doubt operate against a background of commitments that are not themselves adopted on evidence: propositions such as that there is an external world, that our memories are broadly reliable, or that the world has existed for a long time. On this picture, these commitments are neither ordinary pieces of knowledge nor open to the kind of doubt that applies to ordinary empirical claims. Not being confirmed by evidence, they are not justified in the usual way, yet the practices of giving reasons, checking sources, and demanding grounds all presuppose them. They show themselves in what we unhesitatingly do and in what we treat as too settled to count as a genuine question.

I am trying to figure out what would genuinely count against this view, not what people already disinclined toward it dislike about it. I have in mind things like: cases where a hinge is revised over time, disagreement between people whose hinges differ, the problem of telling exactly which commitments are hinges and which are ordinary empirical beliefs, the role of hinges in epistemic criticism or intellectual conversion, and whether the view ends up licensing epistemically or morally bad certainties. For each candidate challenge, I would like to know whether it is decisive, seriously awkward, or merely appears to be a problem that a careful hinge epistemologist can absorb without cheating — and, in that last case, whether the absorption is a legitimate theoretical move or a sign the view has been made unfalsifiable. I would also like an honest assessment of whether the view, properly stated, risks anything at all, and whether it matters if it does not.
