I keep noticing a parallel between price controls and a rare medical condition, congenital insensitivity to pain, and I can't tell if it's deep or just word-matching. Take a binding rent ceiling: the price can't rise to reflect scarcity, so the signal that would normally bring in new supply and ration the existing apartments gets muted. You get shortages, waiting lists, landlords letting units rot because maintenance doesn't pay, and discrimination stepping in where price would have done the rationing. Same structure, roughly, with a minimum wage as a price floor in labor markets: the signal that a worker's marginal product is low gets suppressed, and you get unemployment queues or non-wage rationing enstead. Now the medical case. People with congenital insensitivity to pain don't feel the signal that reports tissue damage, so they don't shift their weight, don't pull back from a hot surface, don't notice a joint being ground down — and they end up with much worse injuries than someone whose pain system was working. In both cases, suppressing an aversive signal seems to make the underlying problem worse rather than better, because the signal is the report of the problem, not the problem itself. Is this a genuine structural identity — both are feedback systems where a cost is the information carrier — or does the analogy break because a market contains agents who know the signal is suppressed and can substitute other mechanisms (queues, side payments, reputation), while a body's subsystems can't deliberate about the missing signal? And here's what worries me: medecine does sometimes suppress pain on purpose — palliative care, anesthesia, chronic pain management — and we regard that as good, not as a mistake. If the parallel were deep, wouldn't it predict that suppressing the price signal is sometimes good too? Does that disanalogy kill the parallel, or does it actually show the conditions under which price controls might be justified?
