I keep coming back to the two canonical cases on naked statistical evidence. In the first, a plaintiff is injured by a bus and can prove only that a single company operates 80% of the buses on that route—enough to satisfy the civil preponderance standard, yet most people resist letting that fact alone justify full damages against the company. In the second, an event promoter knows that 501 of 1,000 attendees entered without paying and sues a randomly selected attendee; the probability that this particular person is a gatecrasher is just over half, so the arithmetic favors the plaintiff, but the verdict again feels wrong. The unease is not about the math; it is about making an identified defendant bear full liability on the strength of a reference class.

Suppose a jurisdiction stops trying to resolve this all-or-nothing and instead creates a statistical liability pool for civil claims. The rule: when a plaintiff's case against an identified class of potential defendants rests on a base rate and no individuating evidence, the plaintiff recovers p times the assessed damages from the pool, where p is the base-rate share attributed to that class. Every member of the class is assessed quarterly in proportion to its estimated share, but a member may reduce its assessment—or remove itself from the class entirely—by submitting auditable, individuating evidence: dispatch logs, telematics, serial-number registries, ticket stubs, entry timestamps. The shares are recomputed by a public model, with adversarial hearings over the model's inputs. Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a moral position. Trace what records actors start keeping and deliberately stop keeping; how the class boundary gets contested once money follows it; whether the burden lands hardest on those who cannot cheaply prove their own non-involvement; how plaintiffs and their lawyers game the base rates; and where the system fails first. Then tell me whether the standard objection to naked statistical evidence—that it treats a person as a draw from a reference class rather than as an individual—survives this reform, or whether the reform quietly turns that objection into a payment problem and dissolves it.
