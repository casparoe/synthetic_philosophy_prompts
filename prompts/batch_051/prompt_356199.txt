I spent the past year on the board of a volunteer-run food cooperative, and I keep noticing a dynamic I had previously only read about: the members who shirk still get the same share as the members who carry the work, the most reliable working groups keep the whole organization alive, and groups with a few shirkers quietly stop functioning. What now strikes me is that the real puzzle is not why people free-ride but why cooperation persists at all, and I want a group of nonspecialists to feel that puzzle in their own choices as well as in the arithmetic.

I run an adult discussion circle on evolution and cooperation, usually 14 to 18 people, and we meet for two hours in a room with several small tables. Design a low-tech simulation — using nothing but pens, index cards, and a large bag of beans or counters — in which participants repeatedly choose between a locally profitable move that slightly harms their own table and a locally costly move that benefits their table as a whole. The rules must be arranged so that, within any given table, the selfish move is the winning choice, while tables with more of the costly, cooperative choices survive, divide, or seed new tables and tables with few of them die out, with whatever reshuffling or group-level structure is needed to connect the generations. The goal is for the numbers to make one fact visible: cooperation can decline within every table over the course of a single generation while the overall frequency of cooperation in the room rises from one generation to the next. There must be no lecture up front, and the facilitator must not use the phrases "group selection," "multilevel selection," "gene's-eye view," "vehicle," "replicator," or "altruism" until the debrief.

In the debrief, map what happened onto three things. First, the difference between a trait's effect on its bearer's success within a group and the same trait's effect on the group's collective output or survival. Second, the fact that total evolutionary change can be decomposed into a within-group component and a between-group component, and that the between-group component is what allows a locally costly trait to spread — together with why a gene's-eye redescription of the same results may be formally adequate but can hide the between-group level at which the pressure appears. Third, the gap between showing that group-level selection is possible in principle and showing that it actually explains any particular human or nonhuman trait. Be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce: true heritability, continuous variation, migration, and any empirical claim about how much of human cooperation actually comes from this process; say what the facilitator must supply in words instead. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run — for example, participants agreeing at a table to soften the conflict, or treating the exercise as team-building rather than as competition both among and within tables — and how the facilitator should handle it.

Begin your answer with a single sentence stating the bottom-line insight the activity is built to implant — the thing participants should come away seeing — then give the rules, schedule, and debrief with no preamble and without restating my request.
