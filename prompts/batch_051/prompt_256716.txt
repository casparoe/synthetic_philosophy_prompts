I've been reading a popular science book about genetics and it has a chapter that takes aim at the whole "DNA is a code" way of talking. The author summarizes the view they're attacking something like this (I'm paraphrasing from memory, but I think it's close): molecular biologists talk about the "genetic code" and about genes "carrying information" for building an organism, as if DNA contained messages that get read out by the cell. Then the author argues that this is all a metaphor that has outlived its usefulness. Information in the strict sense requires a sender, a receiver, and a shared code — something that means something to someone — and DNA is just a molecule; transcription and translation are chemical reactions, not communication. A gene's sequence is correlated with a protein, but correlation isn't information; if it were, a footprint would carry information about a foot and a crystal would carry information about its lattice. Shannon's theory doesn't rescue the idea either: Shannon information is just a measure of how unlikely a sequence is, so a random string would be maximally informative, which is the opposite of what biologists mean. So when we say DNA "codes for" a protein, we're projecting our own interpretive practices onto a chemical system. The information isn't in the genes — it's in the whole developmental system, the cell, the organism, the environment, if it's anywhere at all.

I find this pretty persuasive, but I'm not sure how much of it is the author's own spin and whether the view being attacked is being represented fairly. I'd like you to go through it claim by claim and tell me what a philospher of biology would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong or overstated. In particular: is "information requires a sender and a receiver" a definition or a substantive claim, and does it rule out genes carrying information by stipulation? Is the Shannon point fair, or is it attacking a strawman? And does the last sentence — that the information is in the whole developmental system — actually follow from the rest, or is that a separate move? Basically, how fair is this to people who talk about genetic information, and how much of the author's conclusion should survive if the unfair parts are corrected? I want to know how much of this I should trust, because I've caught myself repeating the "DNA is just chemistry" line and I'm not sure I could defend it if someone pushed back.
