Write a dialogue between two philosophy grad students who disagree about whether being maximally morally good is actually a thing anyone should want. One of them defends something like Wolf's moral saints line: a person who is as morally good as possible would have to run every choice through such a moral lens that they'd lose the nonmoral stuff that makes a life rich, like sharp humor, pointless hobbies, or enjoying a nice meal without checking whether the money should have gone to bed nets, and they'd come out flat and kind of inhuman as a person. The other one pushes back that this only works against a rational saint, someone acting from duty and keeping score, not against a loving saint whose moral life just flows from love and who can still play music and tell a real joke. Make the first character hold their ground: they should press the point that leaving room for serious nonmoral goods, when those goods compete with moral ones, is itself incompatible with maximal moral goodness, and that the existence of supererogatory acts (the optional, better-than-required stuff) makes the very idea of a maximum weird. Have the second character answer that this gets supererogation backwards, because it shows morality does not demand maximizing at every moment, so a genuinely excellent person would integrate nonmoral excellences into one life rather than sacrificing them wholesale. They should both be equally competent, neither a strawman, and they should respond to each others actual arguments instead of taking turns giving speeches. Somewhere in there they should notice that their disagreement partly turns on whether nonmoral excellences are merely permitted for a good person or are themselves part of what makes a life good. Keep it around 900 words, plain continuous prose like an email or a letter, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
