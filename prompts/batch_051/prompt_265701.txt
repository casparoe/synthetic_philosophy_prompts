There's something that's been bugging me about how people talk about democratic backsliding. The main tools used to date backsliding episodes -- the expert-coded indices like V-Dem and Freedom House -- don't just track whether elections happen and are competitive. The scores that get quoted for Hungary, Poland, or Turkey fold in rule of law, media independence, minority rights, judicial constraints, and so on. V-Dem even keeps an Electoral Democracy Index seperate from a Liberal Democracy Index, but the backsliding story keeps reaching for the liberal one, and Freedom House's score has civil liberties baked in from the start. Hungary still held elections that observers treated as genuinely contested for years while its liberal-democracy score was already sliding. So the practice seems to presuppose that democracy just is liberal democracy, or at least that a drop in liberal protections automatically counts as a drop in democracy, even when the electoral core is intact.

I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory of democracy that this practice presupposes, and be careful to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Is it coherent to treat democracy as a single continuum running from autocracy to liberal democracy, with backsliding as movement back along that line? What would an elections-only, minimalist conception say about the same cases, and would it date the backsliding in Hungary or Poland differently? Finally, say what would have to change in how the indices are constructed, and in which countries get labeled backsliding, if we replaced the presupposed liberal view with a more participatory or egalitarian conception of democracy.
