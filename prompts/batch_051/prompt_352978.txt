For the past two years I have been trying to keep a small family manufacturing firm alive while our industry has been reorganized by forces none of us foresaw — new regulation, a broken supply chain, a much larger competitor entering our niche. I spent the first year proceeding as I always had: carefully, with long planning horizons and incremental adjustments. For a while that worked. Then the ground shifted, the careful plan became obsolete, and a rival I had privately dismissed as reckless moved faster and took the position I had been preparing to occupy. A friend handed me Niccolò Machiavelli's The Prince, and this passage from chapter 25 brought me up short:

"I believe also that he will be successful who directs his actions according to the spirit of the times, and that he whose actions do not accord with the times will not be successful. Because men are seen, in affairs that lead to the end which every man has before him, namely, glory and riches, to get there by various methods; one with caution, another with haste; one by force, another by skill; one by patience, another by its opposite; and each one succeeds in reaching the goal by a different method. One can also see of two cautious men the one attain his end, the other fail; and similarly, two men by different observances are equally successful, the one being cautious, the other impetuous; all this arises from nothing else than whether or not they conform in their methods to the spirit of the times."

I find myself inclined to accept this view, and not only as a description of my own circumstances: it seems to explain why the same temperament succeeds in one period and fails in another, why cautious institutions collapse when the climate changes, and why boldness looks like wisdom only after the times have ratified it. But I am aware that a view which can explain both success and failure after the fact by an unobservable "spirit of the times" may be explaining too much. Before I reorganize my own conduct around it — abandoning my caution, acting before I feel ready, pressing an advantage instead of consolidating it — I want to know what would count against it. Treat me as someone looking for genuine reasons to withhold assent, not as someone who needs the position explained back.

I want the evidence, arguments, concrete cases, and conceptual objections that should move me, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. What would have to be true for the claim in this passage to be false rather than merely unhelpful? Can defenders always absorb an apparent counterexample by relocating the error into a misreading of the times? Are there outcomes that the view rules out, or does "conforming to the times" become a redescription of whatever happened to work? And is the most obvious difficulty — that one's temperament cannot be swapped at will, and that the spirit of the times may be legible only in retrospect — a defect in the view or a candid limit that tells in its favor? I also want to know where a committed defender of Machiavelli would honestly concede ground, as opposed to where a critic would merely score points; and whether, once qualified, the position leaves any testable guidance in my hands or only a warning.
