In my seminar on political violence, we have spent several weeks on the definition of terrorism, and the discussion keeps stalling in the same place. One side treats terrorism as a descriptive category: politically motivated violence directed at noncombatants, usually by non-state actors, carried out to signal to a wider audience. The other side objects that every such definition either excludes state terror or builds condemnation into the criteria under the guise of neutral description, since the word already functions as an accusation. Once the canonical cases are on the table, nobody moves; we just cycle through boundary disputes about coercion, noncombatants, and the state/non-state distinction. I have begun to suspect the impasse is not really about where to draw those boundaries. It may be that the two sides are using the word 'terrorism' to do different jobs—one to identify a recurring tactic, the other to mark an act as beyond legitimate politics—and that they disagree about whether an evaluative concept can also be a useful analytical tool. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this is: a substantive dispute about the criteria of political violence, a verbal dispute about the function of the term, or a deeper dispute about how evaluative and descriptive content should relate in political concepts. Be as concrete as you can about what each side is actually committed to, and say what would count as progress given your diagnosis—a distinction, a change of question, a better formulation—or whether we should expect the stalemate.
