i keep coming back to the puzzle people call imaginative resistance: we happily imagine talking horses or faster-than-light travel, but when a story tells us that, in killing her baby, Giselda did the right thing because it was her duty, most of us balk. I want to read Nabokov's Lolita in terms of that puzzle. The whole novel is Humbert trying to pull us into his evaluative frame — Dolores as a nymphet, his love as real, the adult world as the actual corruption. Read the book against the puzzle: which of Humbert's evaluations do we go along with, which do we refuse even inside the fiction, and where does the line fall? Does the book show the resistance is graded rather than all-or-nothing, or does it show that Humbert gets through mostly by keeping his frame descriptive instead of explicitly moral? I'm teaching it and want a reading that's fair in both directions — not reducing the novel to an illustration and not flattening the puzzle to fit the book.
