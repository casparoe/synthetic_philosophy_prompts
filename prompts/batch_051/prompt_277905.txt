I am a statistician whose working vocabulary is Bayesian modeling: priors, likelihoods, posteriors, conditionalization, credences over a fixed event space, zero-probability and probability-one events, Cromwell's rule, and model checking. I have been reading about hinge epistemology, the family of views that grew out of Wittgenstein's late notes in On Certainty: the idea that beneath ordinary empirical beliefs lie "hinge" certainties, such as that the world has existed for a long time or that one's name is what it is, which are not arrived at by evidence and which make ordinary inquiry and doubt possible. I am aware that some hinge epistemologists deny that hinges are ordinary propositions with truth values at all, and I want that tension surfaced rather than papered over.

Please restate hinge epistemology in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. Say what a hinge commitment becomes in a Bayesian setting — an event assigned probability one, a zero-measure event, an assumption built into the event space or the prior, or something else — and say what the difference between a hinge and an ordinary empirical belief becomes. Be explicit about Cromwell's rule: if rational credences should not sit exactly at zero or one for anything that could in principle be overturned by evidence, what does the Bayesian say about hinges that inquiry never treats as open to revision? Then give me the inventory I am after: which claims of hinge epistemology translate cleanly into this vocabulary, which translate only if you distort them (and where exactly the distortion enters), and which have no counterpart in the vocabulary at all. I especially want your verdict on whether what fails to translate is a defect in the Bayesian framework — a sign that it is missing a real distinction that inquiry needs — or a sign that hinge epistemology is less precise than it claims to be, or some of each.
