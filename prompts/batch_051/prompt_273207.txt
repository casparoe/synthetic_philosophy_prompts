As a novelist, I find the ring of Gyges scenario — a person able to act unobserved and without the usual social consequences — less interesting as a test of whether anyone would remain just without fear of punishment, and more interesting as a test of whether one could go on narrating one's own life as the life of a decent person. My working view is that for many people the ring chiefly removes not sanction or reputation but the audience, including the internal audience before whom one must live with what one has done; much moral restraint would then be a kind of narrative self-consistency, a refusal that keeps one's story tellable rather than a responsiveness to principle or to social consequence. I would like this claim tested rather than merely debated. Design the strongest test you can of the hypothesis that moral restraint often depends on being able to keep narrating oneself as a moral protagonist, as against the view that it depends on feared sanctions or damaged reputation. Specify what result would favor each reading, and be precise about what the outcome would still leave unresolved. If you think the alternatives are badly drawn, the notion of an audience is doing too much work, or the claim collapses into ordinary socialized conscience, say so plainly instead of building a test for show.
