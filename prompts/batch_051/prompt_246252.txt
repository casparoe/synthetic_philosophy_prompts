I teach an upper-level undergraduate module on value theory and I'm setting the final essay. I'd like one question on achievement and excellence that can't be answered just by summarising perfectionism — you know, the line that it's good to develop your capacities. It should make students really work out whether an excellent activity is still good when it produces no benefit to anyone, not even to the person doing it.

Build it around this case. Anamaria is a composer who spends thirty years in a remote cottage writing a set of piano études. The pieces are genuinely excellent — extraordinary originality, craft, and musical understanding — but no one else ever hears them, and she burns the scores before she dies. She also found the composing mostly gruelling and never felt any satisfaction about the results, because she doubted the pieces were any good.

Please write: (1) the exam question itself, suited to a 90-minute closed-book essay of about 1,000 words; (2) a model answer of about 800 words; and (3) a marking scheme with five must-hit points, marks out of 20, and a one-line note on the typical way students miss each point. The crux is that a top answer has to seperate at least three things: whether Anamaria's activity is good full stop, whether it is good for her, and whether the value of excellent activity lies in the exercise of the skill itself or in some product that someone could benefit from. A student who just trots out 'excellence is intrinsically valuable' without noticing these distinctions should lose a lot of marks. And the model answer shouldn't rest on named authors — the paper is closed book.
