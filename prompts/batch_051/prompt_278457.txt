I'm in a philosophy of math reading group and we've been stuck on the opening of Euclid's Elements, Book VII. The translation we're using renders Definition 1 as: "A unit is that by virtue of which each of the things that exist is called one," and Definition 2 as: "A number is a multitude composed of units." One guy in the group keeps pushing the line that this is exactly what set theory does with singletons — {cow} is the unit by virtue of which the cow is called one — and that therefore Frege and the set theorists didn't actually solve the problem of what numbers are, they just rebranded Euclid's unit in new notation. The conclusion he wants us to accept is that arithmetic bottoms out in an unanalyzable notion of unity, and that the whole nineteenth-century reduction of number to sets was a notational victory rather than a conceptual acheivement. How far does that analogy actually hold? I want the correspondence table — what in Euclid maps onto what in the set-theoretic picture — then the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion, and an honest verdict on whether the conclusion survives or whether the unit/singleton analogy was carrying it the whole time.
