I am a PhD student in theology, working on the reception history of apocalyptic and wisdom texts. Last term a visiting speaker at our research seminar derived a precise date for a future upheaval by taking the one thousand two hundred and sixty days, the forty-two months, and the three and a half years that appear in Daniel and Revelation and simply adding them as though they were independent quantities; I noticed that I had stopped taking notes well before he reached the conclusion. That episode crystallised a habit I have followed for years without really defending it. When a scriptural interpretation turns on the symbolic meaning of a number, I begin with a rough assessment of its plausibility and then discount it further for each stage of arithmetical or letter-number manipulation the interpretation needs in order to reach its conclusion. A reading that treats seven or twelve or forty as a conventional sacred or round number I weigh like any other claim about the text. But once the argument starts adding digits, multiplying counts from separate passages, or converting names into numbers and back, I tend to set the conclusion aside unless exactly the same point can be made without the number. In practice, I am sympathetic to readings that connect seven with completeness, and almost entirely dismissive of readings that derive a date or a hidden name from the one hundred and forty-four thousand of Revelation.

A colleague in my department tells me this is sound epistemic hygiene: the space of possible numerical patterns is enormous, the constraints on matching are loose, and such constructions are nearly always unfalsifiable, so a steep prior against them is appropriate. A statistician friend puts a different gloss on it. She says the real problem is multiple comparisons and degrees of freedom, but that my discount schedule is arbitrary, and that whether a numerical pattern is evidence rather than coincidence depends on factors a blanket rule cannot capture: whether the interpretive procedure could have been specified in advance, whether the numerical convention is independently attested in the source culture, and whether the resulting reading explains features of the text that would otherwise look unmotivated. I see force in both responses.

I would like to know whether my habit is defensible in approximately its present form. Is a graded discount against increasingly elaborate numerical interpretation a reasonable epistemic policy for a scholar who cannot chase every numerological claim to the ground, or is it a bias dressed up as method? If it is defensible, how should the discount be set or constrained, and under what conditions ought an elaborate numerical reading be taken seriously despite the standing presumption against it?
