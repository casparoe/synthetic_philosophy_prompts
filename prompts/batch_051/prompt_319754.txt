Orwell's essay Politics and the English Language argued that political language was not just ugly — it made bad thinking easier. Prefabricated phrases, dying metaphors, and euphemism let people and governments avoid the thoughts they would have to face if they wrote plainly, so he thought tightening up your prose was a political act: you can't defend mass killing by calling it pacification once you force yourself to say what happened.

Here's the development I want to test against it. LLMs now produce that kind of language on demand — hedging, dead metaphors, prefabricated phrases — but there is no mind behind it avoiding a thought. A model is just predicting the next token, so the link Orwell relied on, between the words and the state of the person producing them, is gone. Maybe the argument then collapses, because there's nobody whose thought is corrupted. Or maybe the danger was always what the language does to readers and to public debate, and LLMs make that part of the argument stronger, not weaker. Take a definite position: does Orwell's argument survive this development, does it need a new premise, or is it dead — and say exactly which step the development breaks or leaves standing.
