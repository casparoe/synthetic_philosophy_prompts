Since being appointed to my city's planning commission, I have noticed a policy I keep using. When a proposal to increase state control is justified mainly by benefits expected a century or more from now, I discount that justification almost to zero—partly because the projections cannot be checked, partly because far-horizon reasoning has often been used to license present domination. A colleague on the commission objects that this throws away the strongest reasons we have for acting on nuclear waste, climate change, or pandemic preparedness, where the worst consequences lie far in the future. Is my discounting rule a defensible epistemic safeguard or a bias against long time horizons? Could it be restated so that it keeps its protection without excluding my colleague's cases, and how should I, in practice, tell the two kinds of far-future argument apart?
