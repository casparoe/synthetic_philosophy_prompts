In the long-running debate over interpersonal comparisons of utility, the same impasse keeps recurring. One side holds that such comparisons are not scientifically legitimate: utility is revealed only as an individual's ordering of options, and there is no observable fact of the matter about whether one person's gain outweighs another's loss. The other side replies that every distributional or policy judgement already presupposes some interpersonal comparison, and that declining to make the comparison is itself a substantive commitment, not neutrality. The two sides work with the same formal apparatus of individual preference orderings, and neither denies that ordinary life is saturated with such comparisons. My working hypothesis is that the dispute is not really empirical, about what could in principle be measured, but turns on what 'meaningful' is being asked to do — whether it requires a mind-independent fact to be discovered, a convention to be adopted, or a normative stance to be defended; if so, the disagreement is partly verbal and partly methodological rather than factual. Is this diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden empirical or formal question still doing the work? And on the correct diagnosis, what would count as progress: a measurement technique, a theorem, a distinction, or a change of question? Please write your answer in British English.
