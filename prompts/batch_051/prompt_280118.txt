I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I've been trying to reconstruct what a friend of mine actually thinks marriage is for. She has strong verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I keep flipping between "there's a clean principle hidden in here" and "she's just running on vibes."

Her track record, as best I can pin it down:
- Same-sex marriage: obviously yes, and it should just be marriage, not some second-tier civil-union thing.
- A same-sex couple who plan to raise kids together: yes.
- A same-sex couple who definitely don't want kids: also yes, no hesitation.
- An 80-year-old opposite-sex couple: yes.
- A couple who know in advance that they're infertile: yes.
- A three-person triad, all romantically involved, who want a single three-way marriage: no, even though they say it just extends marriage from two people to three.
- Two adult siblings in a romantic relationship, no possibility of children: no.
- Two people who marry purely for citizenship or tax reasons, with no intention of sharing a life: no, that's not a marriage even if the state approves it.

Now here's the case I actually care about. Two close friends, not romantically or sexually involved, are raising a child together as co-parents — one is the biological parent and the other has been in the kid's life since before birth. They want to marry, partly for legal security and partly because they regard the co-parenting arrangement as a permanent partnership. Given her track record, what should I expect her to say? Which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analogue? More importantly, what are the two or three candidate rules about what marriage is for that fit the record so far, and which of those rules gets selected by whatever she says about the co-parent case? If that case still leaves more than one rule standing, what further case would cleanly seperate them?

And one more thing: say how confident you actually are in the prediction, and tell me which part of the reconstruction you're least sure of.
