Ok here's one I keep coming back to. If Marx got dropped into 2025, what would he actually, specifically say about large language models and the current wave of automation? I dont just mean "capitalism bad, machines good under communism." I want a grounded prediction that works through his own categories, not a vibe.

Draw on whatever bits of Marx you think do the most work: constant versus variable capital, relative surplus value, the reserve army, real subsumption, the tension he saw between rising productivity and the source of value shrinking. There's also the passage where he gets a bit utopian—in the Grundrisse, where he floats the idea that society's accumulated knowledge becomes the decisive force of production and that direct labor time stops being able to measure wealth. But in Capital he mostly treats machinery as something capital uses to discipline and displace workers. So which Marx are we getting: the one who thinks automation breaks capital's own accounting, or the one who thinks capital just absorbs every new machine into the same old squeeze? Does an LLM count as constant capital that creates no new value, and does the labor theory of value start to buckle when the cognitive work is done by a machine? Would he see algorithmic management of delivery drivers and warehouse workers as the old factory despotism with better sensors, or as a genuinely new kind of subsumption of labor? And would he think AI just intensifies the contradiction he already described—more and more wealth with less and less living labor—or does it change the story in ways his categories can't handle?

I also want you to be honest about what would surprise him and where he'd have to stretch. Don't just put a 2025 take in Marx's mouth.

Structure the answer like this, in this order, roughly these lengths:

- The toolbox (150-200 words): which parts of Marx you're using, and how you're weighing the early humanist stuff against the later critique of political economy.
- The read (400-500 words): the actual prediction, worked through the categories as carefully as you can.
- The cracks (200-300 words): where the categories strain, what would surprise him, and what he'd likely get wrong.
- Bottom line (100-150 words): the one-paragraph version I can use in a seminar.
