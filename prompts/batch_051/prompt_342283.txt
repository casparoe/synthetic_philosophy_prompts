Ok, here's one: Paul Grice dies in 1988, so he never saw a large language model. Try to work out what he would have said about the claim that an LLM's outputs mean things in his sense, and that a chat with a chatbot is governed by the usual cooperative principle and full of run-of-the-mill conversational implicatures. I don't want boilerplate about how LLMs don't have intentions. Actually push through his commitments.

For Grice, speaker meaning (non-natural meaning) involves the producer of an utterance intending to produce some effect in an audience partly by means of the audience's recognition of that very intention. A base model obviously has no such intention, but it's outputs sure look like they were produced by someone who had one. Would Grice say the model lacks speaker meaning entirely, so no implicatures ever arise in a chat, or would he relocate the meaning to the designers, or to the user prompting the model, or treat the model as a kind of instrument the user means through, or something else? Say which of these he'd pick and why, given the rest of his views.

Also: models routinely flout the maxim of quality—they make things up—but users mostly treat the false statements as errors or hallucinations, not as a signal to recover some deeper implicature. For Grice, flouting a maxim is supposed to trigger exactly that search for an implicature. How would he describe what's different in the LLM case: is the model not actually flouting because it isn't an utterer, or is the user simply bad at the inference, or does the model occupy some third category he'd need a new term for? Be specific about which parts of his framework bend and which parts he'd insist are still needed.
