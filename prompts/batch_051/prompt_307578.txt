I'm in a seminar on the ethics of autonomous weapons, and the phrase "meaningful human control" keeps doing a lot of work while remaining conspicuously undefined. The rough idea is that a weapon system that selects and attacks targets without direct human intervention is acceptable only if a human remains in meaningful control over it. But it is unclear what exactly that requires. A human "on the loop" might hold a veto with three seconds to react to a blurry image, and it is hard to see what such control is control of. At the other end, a point-defense system that automatically intercepts incoming missiles has no human deliberating over individual shots, yet rejecting it on those grounds would rule out a use of automation that may be safer than the human-dependent alternative. I want the standard fixed before I read further.

Please write the desiderata that any adequate theory of meaningful human control over lethal autonomous weapons must satisfy—adequate, that is, for judging whether a system may be fielded and for ascribing responsibility when its actions would otherwise amount to a violation of the law of armed conflict. The desiderata should cover the cases the theory must get right, the distinctions it must respect (selecting targets vs. engaging them; human-in-the-loop vs. human-on-the-loop; individual engagements vs. mission-level goals; fixed vs. learning systems), and the constraints it must honor from international humanitarian law, in particular distinction, proportionality, and precautions. For each desideratum, give the scenario or consideration that motivates it. Flag which desiderata look non-negotiable, which might be specific to one camp in the debate, and which pairs likely cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude views on which control is merely nominal.
