I'm taking a historiography seminar and we spent a week on Herbert Butterfield, the guy whose 1931 book The Whig Interpretation of History turned Whig history into a standard criticism. He died in 1979, so he never saw any of this, but I can't stop noticing that a lot of what he complained about sounds like a checklist of what a language model does when you ask it for history.

His charge was that historians keep writing the past as a story of progress that flatters the present: they pick out the people and movements that look like us, treat them as the real story, moralize about the losers, and smooth over the contingencies so the outcome comes to feel inevitable. Now picture Butterfield being shown a pile of LLM-generated narratives—say, the history of science from Galileo to Darwin, or the road to modern democracy, written in the confident style these models fall into. What would he say is the same temptation he diagnosed in human historians, and what would he think is genuinely new or worse because of the training data, the push toward coherence, or the fact that the model has to produce a story on demand? I want you to reason from his actual categories—the way he criticized abridging the past, casting present-day concerns backward, and turning people into heroes and villains—rather than just ventriloquize a generic crank. And where would he maybe be wrong about the technology? Is the problem fixable with prompts or better data, or would he think its baked into any machine that can't refuse to narrate? I'm more interested in a genuine extrapolation with tension in it than in a tidy answer.
