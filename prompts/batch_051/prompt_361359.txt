I have been working through the antinatalist literature and keep getting stuck on the asymmetry usually attributed to Benatar. As I understand it, the asymmetry says: when a possible person never comes into existence, the absence of the pain they would have had is good — not good for anyone, since there is no one there, but good in some impersonal way — whereas the absence of the pleasure they would have had is not bad. That pair of judgments is offered as a datum of considered evaluative thought, and it does a great deal of work in arguments that coming into existence is always, on balance, a harm. What I cannot tell is whether the asymmetry is a real feature of our evaluative outlook or an artifact of how the two cases are usually described. The two judgments are almost always presented in asymmetrical language, and I suspect that wording, ordering, and what the person is invited to focus on may be doing much of the work.

I want the strongest investigation you can design of the claim that the asymmetry is a genuine and stable feature of people's considered judgments about bringing persons into existence, rather than a framing effect or a local habit of the examples. I have tried to imagine matched vignettes — one about a child who would have a painful life and one about a child who would have a happy one — and choice tasks in which people must weigh preventing a possible child's suffering against creating a possible child's happiness. But each attempt seems to me to presuppose part of what it is trying to test, so I am asking for a design cleaner than my own. By an investigation I do not mean only a survey. Set out the exact comparisons: what participants would be shown; how the absence-of-pain case and the absence-of-pleasure case would be matched so that no asymmetry in the results could be charged to the prompt itself; what order, wording, and substitution controls would be needed; and what pattern of results would count as evidence that the asymmetry is robust. Say how, if at all, the design can distinguish considered judgment from whatever people say on a first pass. Include, if you think it is stronger, a behavioral version in which people must trade the two absences off against each other or against other goods, and tell me whether that behavioral version measures the same thing as the verbal one.

I can imagine all of this being run, and I still would not know what philosophical conclusion to draw from the results, so I am asking you to draw that line as well. What would a result in either direction actually settle, and what part of the claim would remain untouched by every observation, choice experiment, and survey? In particular, could a finding that ordinary judgments are symmetric refute the asymmetry, or would it only show that people are confused; and could a finding that they are asymmetric show that the asymmetry is correct, or only that it is common?

Be blunt where the setup deserves it. If testing an evaluative asymmetry in this way is a category mistake, or if the contrast between the asymmetry's being real and its being a framing effect gives out under scrutiny, or if no empirical result could rationally move someone who accepts the asymmetry, say so plainly and tell me what question I should be asking instead.
