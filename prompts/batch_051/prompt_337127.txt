I am a high school student, and in the middle of a unit on evolution I have ended up with a set of firm judgments about what counts as a biological function, but I cannot find one rule that holds them together. I want you to help me extract the principle behind my verdicts, or else tell me which verdicts I should abandon.

Here are the cases and my honest reactions to them.

1. A healthy human heart pumps blood, and it also makes a thumping sound that a doctor can use to detect valve problems. I have no hesitation saying the heart's function is to pump blood, and that making the sound is not a function of the heart. The fact that the sound is diagnostically useful does not make it something the heart is for.

2. A patient has heart failure so severe that the heart cannot pump enough blood to keep the patient alive without a machine. I still say that heart's function is to pump blood. It is a malfunctioning pump, not an object that has acquired a new function.

3. A penguin's flipper is used for swimming, but it is descended from wings that were used for flying. I say its function is swimming, not flying. Whatever a function is, it appears capable of changing over evolutionary time.

4. A cave fish has degenerate eyes that cannot form images and play no role in its behavior. I say those eyes have no function. They are vestiges, even though the same structures in ancestor populations once had the function of seeing.

5. Suppose a new heritable mutation appears in a plant population and, as a side effect of the altered protein, makes the plant slightly more resistant to a common herbivore. The mutation has not spread by natural selection; it is still rare, and whether it persists is mostly a matter of drift. Here I genuinely do not know what to say. I lean toward thinking that defense against the herbivore is not yet the mutation's function, because the mutation has not been selected for anything. But then I notice that the resistance is doing the very same work in this plant that a selected resistance trait would do, and I start to think it should count as a function. This is the case I keep going back and forth on.

6. Now imagine that a fully formed rabbit appears out of nowhere by an absurd cosmic accident, assembled molecule by molecule by chance, with no evolutionary history and no ancestors. Its heart is chemically and structurally identical to a normal rabbit's heart, and the heart pumps blood. I find it very hard to say that the heart is not for pumping blood. If the heart is doing the job of pumping blood and the rabbit would die without it, I want to say that is its function, even though the heart was never selected for anything.

When I try to state the rule behind my confident verdicts, I keep coming back to something like this: a trait's function is the effect that explains why the trait is there, namely the effect it was selected for, or is currently maintained by selection for. That rule handles cases 1 through 4 and fits my lean in case 5, but it seems to force me to say the cosmic rabbit's heart has no function, which I resist.

The other rule I can see is that a trait's function is whatever important contribution it makes to the organism's staying alive or reproducing. That rule handles the cosmic rabbit and the mutation immediately. But it appears to make the heart's diagnostic thump a function, or at least lets any lucky side effect count just because it is useful, and I am not willing to accept that.

Is there a single precise principle that captures the verdicts I am confident about, and what does it force me to say about cases 5 and 6? If no simple principle works, I would rather see two or three candidate principles stated precisely and compared honestly: which of my verdicts does each candidate save, which does it overturn, and what does each predict about cases I have not listed? And do you think my two rules are actually answering different questions, or is the word "function" doing two different jobs that only pull apart in strange cases like the cosmic rabbit?
