Write the joint statement that could be signed by both parties at the conclusion of an adversarial collaboration on the epistemic force of majority rule.

Background. One standard version of the Condorcet jury theorem runs as follows. A group faces a binary choice between two options, one of which is, on some fixed standard, better than the other. Each member votes for the better option with probability p greater than one-half, and each member votes independently of the others conditional on that fact. The probability that a simple majority selects the better option then increases with the number of voters and tends to one as the group grows.

The two camps. The robustness camp holds that the theorem's guiding conclusion—that larger groups can outperform smaller ones at tracking some target—survives realistic weakening of the assumptions, including unequal competence, limited dependence among votes, and some forms of strategic or expressive voting. The fragility camp holds that the theorem is a toy result whose force depends almost entirely on two conditions that fail in the cases that matter: there is rarely a well-defined better option for contested practical questions, and voters' errors are not effectively independent because people share evidence, narratives, interests, and deference to common sources. On the fragility view, increasing group size often amplifies correlated error rather than independent judgment.

The two parties have spent a year arguing about this. They are equally sophisticated; neither is to be written as a strawman. The document must isolate their disagreement rather than manufacture a false compromise. Each residual disagreement must be stated in wording each side recognizes as fair to its own view, with the two phrasings kept distinct where they diverge.

Produce the document with the following structure.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of substantive claims both parties can sign. Include claims about the formal theorem, about what particular weakenings of the assumptions do and do not change, and about what would count as evidence one way or the other. These should be nontrivial, not restatements of definitions or of the theorem itself.

2. Disagreements table. A table whose rows give the residual disagreements. Each row should contain a neutral label for the topic; the narrowest shared description of the question; the robustness position; and the fragility position. Positions should be precise enough that evidence could bear on them.

3. Cruxes. The sub-questions such that settling at least one of them would move at least one party. At minimum, address the following two, but replace them if you can defend better candidates.

   a. The source of dependence. Whether dependence arising from shared evidence and dependence arising from shared bias have materially different consequences for the accuracy of large majorities, or whether the distinction is largely cosmetic in realistic cases. The robustness party holds that shared evidence preserves much of the truth-tracking effect, because each observation still carries information about the target. The fragility party holds that realistic dependence is mostly shared bias, which does not wash out as the group grows.

   b. Contestability. Whether the model's better-option assumption requires a mind-independent fact accessible to the voters, or only an operational standard against which the group could in principle be scored. The robustness party holds that many real group decisions have such a standard even when the question is normatively charged. The fragility party denies that the standard remains stable when the disputants differ in values or interpretive frame.

4. Tests and recorded predictions. For each crux, give at least one concrete case, simulation specification, or natural setting that both parties accept as bearing on that crux. One test must be an extension of the formal model: specify the information structure, the form of dependence, and the outcome variable that both parties agree measures accuracy. Compare at least a shared-evidence structure and a shared-bias structure. Another test must be a real-world setting with a verifiable target, such as an aggregate judgment about a quantity later measured independently; describe the setting directly rather than citing any named study. For each test, record each party's prediction in advance in measurable terms. Do not include a test that either side has not already accepted as relevant.

Use formal American English throughout. Do not include citations, reading lists, or footnotes.
