When people ask who started the Cold War, they almost never mean "whose earlier moves, taken all together, made the confrontation most probable." They mean: which side took the first open, official, easily datable step toward confrontation — a doctrine announced, a blockade imposed, an alliance extended across a line that had previously been informal. The earlier quiet steps are narrated as mere context even when they were just as deliberate: arming and advising clients, financing the friendly local faction while publicly deploring interference, excluding a rival from the administration of occupied territory, refusing the forum where a disagreement might have been aired before it hardened. The result is a stubborn asymmetry in how blame gets located. An act counts as "starting" a conflict in rough proportion to its public legibility and formal commitment, not in proportion to its causal weight, its deliberateness, or its foreseeability. A state can shape the board for years through deniable, low-visibility moves; the first side to move in the open, and thereby become quotable and datable, gets named as the one who started it.

I want you to consider whether anything principled justifies this asymmetry, or whether it is a recording artifact that has hardened into historiography. First, formulate the strongest justification you can find for locating initiative at the open, officially avowable act rather than at the earlier quiet acts that narrowed the alternatives. Does it rest on an epistemic principle (only legible moves create a situation to which others can react), on a commitment principle (only an avowed, official act binds the state as a whole, while a deniable act binds only the official who carried it out), on a control principle (only a side that knows it is crossing a line can be said to initiate the crossing), or on something else? Then test that justification against nearby cases: a family feud conducted for years in private letters before the first public insult; a hostile takeover assembled through years of quiet share purchases before the public tender offer; a marriage that had ended in all but paperwork long before the divorce filing; an economic sanctions regime whose first public announcement came only after years of quiet capital restrictions. In each case, ask whether the principle still places the "start" at the moment of public legibility, and whether that placement is right. Conclude with an honest verdict: should this practice of assigning initiation be kept, reversed, or given up? And say specifically whether your verdict revises the ordinary question "who started the Cold War" — whether we should keep the question but answer it differently, replace it with a better question, or stop asking it.
