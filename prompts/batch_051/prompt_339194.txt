I'm a software engineer, mostly working on infrastructure, and I've been reading a little Pascal in translation. My rough understanding of his remarks on justice and force is this: a rule that cannot be backed up is impotent, power that is not tied to a legitimate rule is tyranny, so the only workable arrangement is to fuse the two. But he seems to add a darker claim — that because we can't reliably make the just side the strong side, societies do it the other way around: they take whichever side is already strong and dress it in the language of justice until it looks as though the strong side was just all along. (That's my reconstruction from memory; if I've gotten him wrong, correct me, but the application is what I'm after.)

What I keep thinking about is content moderation at platform scale. Enforcement there is close to pure force: an automated pipeline flags an account and applies removal, downranking, or suspension at negligible marginal cost, while the user is left with an appeals queue. At the same time, the platform spends heavily on making that force look just — rulebooks, transparency reports, appeals, and even an external oversight body. So the force keeps running ahead of the justice, but the company also insists the justice is real.

What would Pascal make of this arrangement? Would he see it as his darker thesis realized — force successfully dressed as justice after the fact? Or would he see the rulebooks and appeals as genuine but poorly coupled justice? Would he think these accountability mechanisms repair the gap he cared about, or mainly make the force more tolerable? And would he draw a principled line between a platform that enforces its rules this way and a state that claims to hold legitimate force?

Please keep two things separate while answering: what Pascal is clearly on record as holding, versus what you are extending or venturing on his behalf. And where serious readers of Pascal divide — for instance, over how far his cynicism is meant to go, or how much the view depends on his theology — tell me there's a division rather than presenting one reading as the obvious consensus.
