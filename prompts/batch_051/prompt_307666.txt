I teach an upper-level course on Scripture and its interpretation, and I am designing a session on the doctrine of inerrancy and the hermeneutics of apparent contradictions. I want to give students a case in which the standard distinctions—difference of standpoint, difference of idiom, different theological vocabulary—have to do genuine work rather than merely get gestured at. The case I have in mind is the double account of who incited David to take a census of Israel. 2 Samuel 24:1 (NIV) reads: "Again the anger of the Lord burned against Israel, and he incited David against them, saying, 'Go and take a census of Israel and Judah.'" 1 Chronicles 21:1 (NIV) reads: "Satan rose up against Israel and incited David to take a census of Israel." On their face, one text says the Lord incited David and the other says Satan did.

I can see several ways an inerrantist might try to reconcile these verses: reading Chronicles as naming the proximate agent through whom the Lord's incitement was carried out; treating "Satan" in the Chronicles passage as a common noun meaning "an adversary" rather than as the proper name of the devil; distinguishing divine permission from direct commission; or claiming that "incited" carries different senses in the two contexts. I want you to think through which, if any, of these reconciliations can be pressed without special pleading, and which merely restate the surface tension in more abstract vocabulary rather than resolving it. Is there a reading on which both verses truthfully report the same event without making one of them say less than it plainly says? And how should one distinguish a principled hermeneutical constraint from an ad hoc harmonization in a case like this? If you conclude the verses cannot be honestly reconciled while preserving inerrancy, say precisely which commitment—factual consistency, a particular doctrine of inspiration, or a particular rule of interpretation—has to yield. Do not worry about citing secondary literature; the question is about the conceptual moves, not the scholarship.
