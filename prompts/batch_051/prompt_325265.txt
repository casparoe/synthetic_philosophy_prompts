I recently read a chapter on a puzzle called the MU-puzzle, and a friend who had read the same chapter offered me what he called its real lesson. His summary went like this. The puzzle starts from the string MI and four rewriting rules: you may add a U to the end of any string ending in I; you may double everything that follows an M; you may replace III with a U; and you may delete any UU. The task is to reach MU. No amount of applying the rules to letters will get you there, he said, but the famous move is to stop working inside the system and count I's instead: the rules preserve the fact that the number of I's is never a multiple of 3, while MU would need an I-count of zero. From this he concluded that every system of rules, in mathematics or anywhere else, has questions it cannot settle on its own terms, and that genuine progress always comes from jumping out of the system rather than following its rules. He added that this is the point Gödel's theorem makes rigorous, and that when a rule-system seems to be trapping you, the reasonable thing is to treat its rules as optional and search for a larger frame.

He is bright and usually careful, and the first parts feel right to me. I have since seen the jumping-out move nicknamed JOOTSing, and it seems often to be treated as a deep lesson. But the leap from "this string cannot be produced" to "every rule-system has questions it cannot settle on its own terms" leaves me uneasy, and the advice to treat any trapping system's rules as optional sounds as if it could justify almost anything. I would like to know whether his summary is fair. Could you go through it and separate what is accurate, what is being quietly overstated or conflated, and whether the practical moral he draws actually follows from the parts that survive correction? I am less interested in what some book literally said at each point than in whether the ideas, as he has put them, hold together.
