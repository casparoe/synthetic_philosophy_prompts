I write fiction, and I am drafting a novel set in a federation of historical nations whose politics I have loosely borrowed from the secession debates in Scotland, Catalonia, and Quebec. The country's constitution contains an exit clause of my own invention, and before I build a plot around it I want to understand it as a piece of political machinery rather than as an abstract position on the right of self-determination. Under the clause, a constituent nation may begin the process only if its parliament passes a declaration of intent by a two-thirds majority. Between eighteen and thirty months later, a referendum must be held; independence wins this first vote only if it secures both a majority of votes cast and the support of at least forty percent of all registered voters in the nation. If the first vote passes, a five-year transition begins, during which the nation gains full internal self-government but remains inside the federation's currency union and pays an annual exit contribution set by a joint tribunal. At the end of those five years, a second referendum is held, and a simple majority of those voting is then enough to confirm independence. If the first referendum fails, no new declaration of intent may be tabled for fifteen years. A nation that completes the process must also accept a per-capita share of the national debt, and every resident retains the right to keep the old federation's passport for life if they wish.

I am less interested in whether this clause is just or wise than in how it would actually behave. I would like a walk-through of the incentives it creates for secessionist leaders, unionist parties, ordinary voters, and the central government at each stage; where it would be gamed first, whether in the drawing of the electoral register, the timing of the first referendum, or the negotiation of the exit contribution; what second-order effects the five-year gap between the two votes is likely to produce; and which of the resulting failure modes could be repaired without gutting the design.
