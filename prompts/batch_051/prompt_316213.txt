I'm a retired physicist—fluid dynamics, mostly—who's been reading Leibniz for a year or so. Lately I've been going back and forth between the Monadology and the Theodicy, trying to make sense of the claim that this is the best of all possible worlds. My problem is that an argument I keep building seems to prove too much, and I can't find the exit.

Here's the argument as I've formalized it.

(1) God is perfectly wise and perfectly good, so whenever he acts he chooses the option with the greatest degree of perfection.
(2) Creation is an act, so God chose the possible world with the greatest degree of perfection.
(3) If the best world is unique, then given God's nature, he could not have chosen any other world.
(4) A free act requires that the agent could have chosen otherwise.
(5) So creation is not a free act—or at least not in any ordinary sense.
(6) But Leibniz insists that God is free in creating, and that this freedom is real, not just a turn of phrase.
(7) Therefore either (1), (4), or Leibniz's view of divine freedom must be adjusted.

The part that feels like it proves too much is step (4 to 5). If acting according to one's nature always removes freedom when one could not do otherwise, then not just God but any rational agent acting on their best judgment would fail to be free. That conclusion is absurd enough that I suspect the argument equivocates somewhere, maybe between different senses of necessity.

I've come across Leibniz's distinction between absolute necessity and hypothetical or moral necessity. I get that he wants to say God is morally necessitated to choose the best but not absolutely necessitated, because the opposite implies no contradiction. But I don't understand how that distinction blocks the argument, since a moral necessity that makes the alternative impossible for a perfect will still seems to destroy freedom. Is the right move to reject (4), and say freedom doesn't require the ability to do otherwise, or to weaken (1) so that God could have chosen a less perfect world? Or is there a third way I'm missing? I'd like to know which premise you'd give up, and what it costs the rest of Leibniz's system.
