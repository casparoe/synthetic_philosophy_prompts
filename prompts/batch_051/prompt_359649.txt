I edit a small regional newsroom, and last night I argued for hours with a friend about whether there is any principled line between privacy-invading journalism and responsible reporting. I work as the editor who approves publication of local stories involving arrests, lawsuits, family disputes, and health records. We make these calls several times a day, with no in-house counsel, often on deadline. Our current practice is essentially: if a detail is lawfully obtained and accurate, the only remaining question is whether it is too indecent or gratuitous to publish. I think this is not a standard; my friend thinks any further restriction is paternalistic and unworkable.

The argument kept returning to a passage from Samuel D. Warren and Louis D. Brandeis, "The Right to Privacy," Harvard Law Review, Vol. IV, No. 5 (December 15, 1890):

"The intensity and complexity of life, attendant upon advancing civilization, have rendered necessary some retreat from the world, and man, under the refining influence of culture, has become more sensitive to publicity, so that solitude and privacy have become more essential to the individual; but modern enterprise and invention have, through invasions upon his privacy, subjected him to mental pain and distress, far greater than could be inflicted by mere bodily injury. Nor is the harm wrought by such invasions confined to the suffering of those who may be the subjects of journalistic or other enterprise. In this, as in other branches of commerce, the supply creates the demand. Each crop of unseemly gossip, thus harvested, becomes the seed of more, and, in direct proportion to its circulation, results in the lowering of social standards and of morality."

I want to translate this passage into a pre-publication procedure my desk editors can actually use. It should state the questions in order; specify what counts as a sufficient answer; say where the procedure ends in a definitive no, where it ends in a yes, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is genuinely a value judgment; and mark the points where judgment is merely relocated rather than eliminated. The procedure should not simply conclude that private details are acceptable whenever a story has reader interest, but it should also give real content to the distinction between public interest, harmless gossip, and prurient curiosity.

Then run the procedure on three recurring types of decisions we face: publishing a city official's adult child's recent psychiatric hospitalization in a story about the official's opposition to a mental-health facility; publishing a local business owner's long-ago misdemeanor conviction during a profile about his restaurant expansion; and continuing our police-blotter practice of publishing names and addresses of everyone arrested, before conviction, now that the blotter is indexed by search engines. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I will resist. End by saying what this procedure will not solve.
