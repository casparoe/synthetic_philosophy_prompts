Two of my coworkers got into a long argument at lunch about a demand our company had received, and I ended up unable to sort out which of them was right. I work as a software engineer on a discussion platform that serves users all over the world. A government in one country sent us an order to remove a post written by a user in a second country, take it down everywhere, because the post is illegal under the first country's laws, even though it is plainly lawful where the user lives and where our company is headquartered. I have no formal background in law or philosophy, and I mostly listened.

Dana argued that online speech should be governed by the law of the place where the speaker published it, which in practice means the user's country or at most the platform's home country. Speech is an act carried out somewhere, she said, and if every country that can load a page gets to suppress what it calls illegal, then each of the world's speech codes becomes a global speech code. She was willing to geoblock the post inside the requesting country, since that country has jurisdiction over its own residents, but she would not remove it globally. Amal replied that she was treating the internet as though it took place in one location when the relevant fact is that the post is delivered to people in the requesting country, where the harm the law aims at actually occurs. A platform that deliberately serves users in a country, collects their data, and makes money from them is operating there, he said, and cannot claim to be outside that country's laws. Dana answered that his principle would make the strictest speech law on earth the law for everyone, because a platform reachable from nearly every country would have to satisfy every one of them at once. Amal said her principle had the same flaw in the opposite direction: platforms would cluster in the most permissive jurisdictions, and any country's local speech protections could be hollowed out from the outside. By the end they were arguing over whether it even makes sense to speak of a single place where an online act of speech happens, and whether a distributed network can be governed one country at a time at all.

I would like someone to walk through this exchange and say who is right about which specific point, and what both of them are missing.
