Rewatching Breaking Bad, I keep getting stuck on how Walter White's slide into Heisenberg never has an obvious point of no return—each choice feels like the only move available given where he already is, but by the end he's someone the season-one Walter would have been horrified by. In my rational choice class we just read Quinn's self-torturer: someone turns a dial up one notch at a time, each notch makes the pain imperceptibly worse while he's paid for every step, so each individual advance looks worth it, yet the endpoint is a setting he would never have chosen in a single jump. I want to read the show through that lens for a paper I'm writting. Walk me through where Walter's descent genuinely has the self-torturer's structure—cooking once to leave his family money, letting Jane die, working for Gus, killing Gus, ordering the prison murders—and where the analogy breaks down, either because his steps aren't actually imperceptible or because the show seems to think he was always choosing the endpoint. Does the show end up taking a position on whether a whole sequence of choices can be irrational even when every step in it is rational from where he stands? And does it add anything to the philosophical puzzle that the bare thought experiment doesn't—for instance by dramatizing intransitive preferences, or the self-deception and identity drift that let someone cross a threshold they would have refused outright?
