Pain asymbolia is a condition in which a person can report that a stimulus is sharp, burning, and located in a specific part of the body, yet is entirely untroubled by it, showing no distress and no desire to withdraw or make it stop. Now compare two people who receive the same noxious stimulus—a hard pinch, say. One minds it intensely; the other, who has pain asymbolia, registers the same sensory qualities but does not mind it at all. Most of us would judge that there is something wrong with inflicting the pinch on the first person and nothing, or much less, wrong with inflicting it on the second. Is that asymmetry in our moral judgments defensible? If the badness of pain is intrinsic to the sensory state itself, the two cases should be equally bad; but if the badness consists entirely in how the subject reacts to the stimulus, it is hard to see how ordinary pain is bad in itself, as we usually suppose. Give the strongest account you can of what makes pain bad, test it against these two cases, and say whether the asymmetry in judgment should be kept or given up.
