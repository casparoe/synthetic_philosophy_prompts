I help run an independent, volunteer-moderated discussion forum with members spread across roughly forty countries. We host text posts and comments; we don't run ads, we don't have a legal department, and we're not incorporated anywhere with deep pockets. A few times a month we receive a demand that some post be removed because it is allegedly unlawful in a particular country: defamation in one, glorification of a banned organization in another, insult to a foreign head of state in a third, blasphemy in a fourth. Each time, the poster lives in one country, our servers sit in another, and the complainant or allegedly harmed reader is in a third; often the content is legal everywhere involved except the complainant's jurisdiction. Our de facto policy has drifted toward: comply when any country's law plausibly applies, because that feels safest, and let the strictest law win by default. But I keep reading thoughtful arguments that this is the wrong principle — that an online forum isn't really "in" a place, that only the law of the server or the law of the speaker should matter, that users consent to the platform's rules rather than their state's, or that deferring to the most restrictive regime is just extraterritorial censorship by other means. These arguments are appealing in the abstract, but I can't see how to run a moderation queue on them.

Please turn the defensible core of whichever view you think is actually right here into a reusable decision procedure for us: a checklist or flowchart in words, with the questions in order, what counts as a good-enough answer to each, where the procedure instructs us to leave a post up or take it down, and where it stops and hands the decision to a human because the question is one of judgment or values rather than anything a rule can settle. It needs to be runnable by a trained volunteer in under an hour, without a lawyer per case. Be explicit about which steps are doing real work and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, which distinctions from the underlying theory must survive the simplification and which you dropped as unusable, and where the procedure will systematically misfire.

Then run the procedure on three cases and tell me if any of the answers it gives are ones we won't like. Case one: a member abroad posts a satire of a religious figure that is lawful where they live and where we host, but a group of members elsewhere reports it as blasphemy under their country's law. Case two: a member in a country with harsh defamation law posts a damaging but true claim about a local official; the claim is lawful where it was posted and where most readers are, but the official threatens suit under his own country's law. Case three: a member posts material that is legal in their country and in the country where our servers sit, but prohibited in a third country where a few members live and one of them complains.

If the honest answer is that there is no principled procedure here — that the choice between these principles is ultimately a political or fiduciary one, and that any checklist would just be aesthetics laid over a coin flip — I would rather you say that plainly than give me something that looks rigorous but isn't. Likewise, if I've framed the problem badly — for instance, if "which law applies" is the wrong question to ask, or if my sense of extraterritoriality is muddled — tell me directly rather than answering the question as posed. And please mark your own confidence: which steps of the procedure you would vouch for, which rest on a contested view of jurisdiction over speech, and which are your own best construction rather than anything settled.
