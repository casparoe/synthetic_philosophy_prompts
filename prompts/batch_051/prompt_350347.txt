I'm in an epistemology reading group, and we've been stuck on the anti-luminosity argument for months now — long enough that I've started to think the impasse is the interesting part. We all agree on the shape of the argument: you take a condition that comes in degrees, run a sorites series from a case where it clearly holds to one where it clearly doesn't, and then use a margin-for-error premise (roughly: if you know the condition holds at one point, it can't have failed at the immediately adjacent point) to show that the condition can't be luminous — that is, it can't be such that whenever it holds you're in a position to know it holds. As a piece of formal reasoning, nobody disputes that it goes through. The fight starts when we ask what it's supposed to show about the KK principle, the idea that if you know p then you know that you know p. One camp treats it as a clean refutation: knowing isn't luminous, so KK fails. A second camp says it only shows that no non-trivial mental condition is luminous, and that KK was never the claim that knowing has a sort of introspective glow; they say KK is about reflective accessibility — being able to know that you know by attending to your grounds, not about some felt difference between adjacent cases. A third camp says the whole thing is just the sorites paradox wearing an epistemic costume, so the real action is in your theory of vagueness: reject bivalence or go contextualist about 'knows', and the anti-luminosity conclusion stops being so alarming. We go round in circles, and I've stopped expecting anyone to change there mind, and from what I've read the published debate looks just as entrenched.

My working theory is that the three camps are answering different questions — one about a formal operator in epistemic logic, one about introspective access, one about how to handle vagueness — and that the phrase 'the KK principle' is doing three jobs at once. But I'm not sure if that's right, or just a flattering way of dissolving a debate that's actually substantive. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal, or about which cases count as data, or a hidden formal question about safety or margins for error? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a theorem, a cleaner distinction, a new kind of case, or an argument that the question was ill-posed all along?
