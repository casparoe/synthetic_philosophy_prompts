I retired from a career in molecular biology a few years back and have got into philosophy since. I keep bumping into "infinite regress" arguments, and I cannot work out when their meant to be damning and when their just a harmless fact about how things are. Let me give you my gut reactions to a handful of cases, because I suspect I'm being inconsistent.

1. If every event has a cause, the chain of causes goes back forever. An infinite past doesn't bother me in the least. I don't see that anything is left unexplained by saying the chain just doesn't have a first link.

2. If you justify a belief by another belief, and that one by another, you're told you either stop somewhere or go in a circle. But as a working scientist I always thought justification is provisional and you can keep asking "why" indefinitely; the chain is open-ended and that's fine. I don't feel I need foundations.

3. Lewis Carroll's tortoise: if I need a rule telling me to apply modus ponens, and then a rule telling me to apply that rule, and so on, something has gone wrong. That regress seems genuinely vicious to me, even though each extra rule is harmless by itself.

4. When physicists ask what explains the laws of nature, and the answer is a deeper law, and then what explains that, I don't think the regress refutes anything. It just shows explanation is never finished. That seems benign.

5. The first-cause argument for God: I've never understood why a first cause helps. If God can be uncaused, why couldn't the universe be uncaused? So as a regress argument it strikes me as weak.

6. But here's one that does feel vicious, and it bothers me because it looks structurally similar to the justification one. If the meaning of a word is fixed by an interpretation, and an interpretation is just more symbols that themselves need interpreting, then no amount of interpreting ever pins the meaning down. I want to say that regress is bad in a way the causal and explanatory ones aren't.

Is there a single principle that sorts these into vicious and benign? Or am I just giving physics a pass and being stricter with semantics? And what would your principle say about the homunculus regress in explanations of vision — the little man in the head who needs another little man — which I've always suspected is more a rhetorical trick than a real argument?
