I've ended up running a small research program — three people for three years, mix of political philosophy, anthropology, and comparative politics — on what's actually going on when someone calls a practice corruption. The remit is broadly: when NGOs, international bodies, or just voters slap the label on things like clientelism, vote-buying, gift-giving to officials, patronage hiring, paying for access, or customary "dash," what is doing the conceptual work? Those are all pretty different transactions, and I keep oscillating between two unsatisfying poles. Either corruption is just whatever violates an officeholder's formal duty, in which case the Western/bureaucratic standard is definitionally correct and the debate is over before it starts; or corruption is just a local/cultural category, in which case transnational anti-corruption norms look like they have no grip at all. I figure the interesting work is somewhere in between, but I'm not sure which part of that in-between is most worth funding.

If you were setting the research agenda for that group, which two or three open conceptual questions in this area would you put at the top, and why those rather than the alternatives? I don't want a literature review or a reading list. I want a dependency-aware ordering: which questions are upstream of the rest, which are actually tractable for philosophers and social theorists as opposed to ethnographers or lawyers, and which would genuinely change how one should think about pushing anti-corruption norms across borders. For instance, I have a half-baked hunch that the public/private distinction is doing too much work, and that the more useful variable might be whether a transfer creates an exclusionary obligation — jobs, contracts, protection only for insiders — or a personalized channel of accountability between official and constituent. I'm not sure its not just a red herring, though. Also say a bit about what evidence or argument over the next few years would make you demote one of your top questions.
