A familiar argument in the study of writing systems can be reconstructed as follows. An alphabet's great advantage lies in its phonemic transparency: because a small set of letters corresponds, however loosely, to the sound segments of the language, a writer can encode almost any spoken word by ear and a reader can recover a plausible pronunciation directly from the marks. A morphosyllabic script such as Chinese operates at a different level of analysis: its characters standardly encode syllable-sized morphemes rather than individual sound segments, so a competent writer must accumulate a large inventory of signs, and even a sign's internal phonetic component is often an unreliable guide to its pronunciation. Call this the phonemic-transparency argument for the practical and cognitive advantages of alphabets. Now consider a development that postdates the argument's standard formulations: in modern Chinese, most writing is produced by typing a phonological spelling in pinyin and selecting among candidates supplied by an input method editor, which takes over much of the memory-and-retrieval work that the non-alphabetic script itself used to impose on the writer. The displayed script has not become alphabetic; the phonemic layer has instead migrated out of the script and into the software interface. Does the phonemic-transparency argument survive this change? Does it still show that alphabetic writing systems have an enduring advantage, or does it now show only that some layer of the total writing-plus-input system must make sound segments explicit, with the script's own level of encoding left unsettled? Identify which premise of the original argument is actually under pressure, and say whether a repaired version can preserve anything important from the argument without the original conclusion about the superiority of alphabets themselves.
