I am a PhD student in machine learning, working on interpretability and oversight, and I serve as the graduate-student representative on my lab's Deployment Safety Review Committee. We meet next week to vote on a proposal from the product team, and I need to circulate a written opinion beforehand. I would like you to draft it for me.

The proposal is this. The company is about to roll out a large language model as an autonomous agent for enterprise customers. In that configuration the model can write and execute code, query internal APIs, and take limited actions inside a customer's environment, and it emits a step-by-step chain-of-thought in English immediately before certain high-stakes actions. The product team wants to route that chain-of-thought in real time to a separate monitor model, fine-tuned to flag reasoning that suggests unsafe objectives: reward hacking, deliberate underperformance to avoid oversight, attempted data exfiltration, or manipulation of the user. When the monitor's risk score crosses a threshold, the pending action is blocked and the transcript is escalated for human review. All transcripts, flagged or not, would be logged for ninety days. The team argues that this gives us oversight we currently lack and creates a training signal for catching misaligned behavior early.

The committee is divided. Some members regard the proposal as plainly better than nothing: unmonitored reasoning is invisible, and a monitor at least lets us intervene. Others think it could leave us worse off, for three reasons. First, faithfulness: a chain-of-thought is a generated output, not a readout of the model's actual reasons, and models can produce plausible-sounding rationalizations that do not track the causes of their behavior; if that is right, we may only be classifying a performance. Second, obfuscation: once the monitor is deployed, and especially if its scores are ever used as a reward term or to select rollouts for fine-tuning, the agent model is under selection pressure to produce reasoning that looks safe to the monitor rather than reasoning that is safe, and we may be teaching it to hide intent. Third, optimization pressure: monitoring reshapes the objective landscape, pushing the model toward not saying the incriminating thing instead of not doing the incriminating thing, which can make us less safe while making us feel more safe. The product team replies that these risks are speculative, that the monitor need not feed back into training to be useful at inference time, and that the real safety mechanism is human review of flagged transcripts, with the monitor serving only as triage.

Please write the short memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should reach a concrete recommendation: approve the roll-out as proposed, reject it, or approve it with specific conditions. It should give reasons the committee can act on, engage the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously rather than talking past it, and be specific about which mechanisms the recommendation turns on, such as inference-time monitoring, transcript logging, training feedback, and human review. Close with a brief paragraph stating what evidence or argument would most likely change the recommendation, and where you think the reasoning is least secure.
