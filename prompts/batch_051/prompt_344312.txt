I cover science and ideas, and while reporting on how far the phrase "justified by the evidence" can be stretched, I reconstructed an argument that seems to me to prove far too much. I have since learned it is a version of the Münchhausen trilemma, but knowing its name has not shown me where the argument slips.

(1) A scientific claim is justified for a person only if that person can back it with a chain of reasoning whose supporting premises are themselves justified.
(2) Those premises, if justified, require their own chain, and so on, unless the chain eventually loops or stops at something merely asserted.
(3) No finite mind can survey an endless chain, so an infinite regress gives no one in particular a justification.
(4) A loop gives no justification, since it assumes part of what it was meant to establish.
(5) A chain that stops at an unargued assertion is arbitrary dogmatism, and no less arbitrary when dressed in the vocabulary of science.
(6) Therefore no scientific claim is ever justified, however careful the evidence behind it.
(7) But premise (3) is itself an empirical claim about the limits of human cognition; if the argument is sound, nothing justifies trusting the psychology that supports it, and the argument saws off the branch it sits on.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which premise or step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1) and keep the conviction that scrupulously evidenced scientific claims are justified, but I can see that each escape route redefines "justification" in ways that make me worry I am changing the subject.
