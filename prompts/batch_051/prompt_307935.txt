I'm trying to understand a puzzle about letters and typefaces that I've seen described in terms of the "Letter Spirit" idea. Suppose I have a constrained font design task: all letters must be drawn on the same coarse grid, using only a small set of allowed line segments, yet each letter must remain identifiable as itself. I get that there's a distinction between a letter's categorical role in the alphabet and its stylistic realization in a particular typeface. What I don't have a clear handle on is the conceptual structure of how role and style interact. Specifically, in a well-formed grid font, the style seems to be more than a layer of aesthetic decoration applied after the fact to each letter's core shape: it can change which local strokes are available or acceptable, and it constrains how far a particular 'a' can depart from some prototype before it stops reading as 'a' and starts reading as, say, a 'd' or a degenerate mark. Could you explain the conceptual issue of what makes every 'a' an 'a' in that setting? I'm less interested in computational implementation details and more in how to think about the mutual pressure between preserving a letter's identity and maintaining a consistent global style.
