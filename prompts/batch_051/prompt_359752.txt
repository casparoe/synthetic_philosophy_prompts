So I've been chewing on the standard automation line you hear in tech circles: machines are supposed to eat the boring work first, and humans keep the meaningful stuff — writing, coming up with ideas, making art, taking care of people, learning for its own sake. The bet goes back at least to Keynes, who thought productivity gains would carry us down to something like a 15-hour week and we'd spend the extra time on the good life. The unspoken premise is that what gets automated first is the drudgery, and what's left over for humans is the meaning.

Except now LLMs and image models are automating exactly the writing, drafting, summarizing, coding, and image-making — a lot of the parts of jobs people actually liked and found meaningful. Meanwhile a bunch of the truly bad jobs aren't gone; they're still there, just surveilled or paid worse. So the thing the old argument needed might not be true anymore.

Here's what I want: does the old "automation frees us for the good life" argument survive this, or does it collapse? Pin down which premise the new development actually puts pressure on, and whether that premise is dead or just in need of a bunch of conditions. Then try to repair it — something weaker, like automation makes the good life possible but not automatic, and we only get it if we rebuild status and meaning around something other than having a job. I want to know whether that counts as a patch to the original argument or a whole different argument wearing the old ones clothes. Walk me through where the original argument snaps and what would need to be true for any repaired version to hold.
