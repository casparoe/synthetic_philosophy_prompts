While reporting a piece on how historians argue about causes, I heard a historian lay out an argument that seems to prove too much, and I cannot find the misstep. It runs: (1) A factor caused an event only if the event would not have occurred without it. (2) The factors historians single out are almost always redundant in exactly this way: the surrounding circumstances held other conditions, each sufficient on its own. Take a government that fell amid a fiscal crisis, a military mutiny, and a grain shortage—on the historian's telling, the fiscal crisis alone would have brought it down, but so would the mutiny alone, and so would the shortage. (3) By (1), then, none of these caused the fall; and when the point is generalized, nearly every factor cited in nearly every historical explanation was not a cause. (4) But that cannot be right: historians do sometimes identify genuine causes, and a collapse like this was not an uncaused event. (5) So the argument must fail somewhere, even though each premise looked sound when I accepted it. Which premise or step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I would most like to keep (4), and I am tempted to abandon (1), but I worry that then any list of causes becomes a matter of narrative taste. If the task is somehow badly posed, tell me that plainly rather than resolve it as if it were well formed.
