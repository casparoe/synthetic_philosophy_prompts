After retiring from a career in the physical sciences, I have been reading political philosophy and trying to state exactly what I find attractive in communism as an ideal, setting aside questions about whether it could be made to work. I have arrived at a principle I call the unconditional-dependence principle, and I would like a candid assessment of it. The principle says that a society is communist to the degree that one person's material dependence on another is not treated as a commodity: hunger, illness, childhood, and old age should call forth care, food, and shelter, but should not function as income opportunities for those who supply them. The example I keep returning to is that, in a market society, the fact that many people are old and frail makes their care a growth sector, so that somebody's vulnerability is somebody else's business plan; if communism worked, the same facts about vulnerability would still elicit care, but no one would have a financial stake in the need's persisting. My claim is that the desirability of communism lies in this change in the character of dependency relations rather than in the abolition of scarcity, strict material equality, or the elimination of every market as such. I am aware that this resembles older formulas according to which people should contribute as they are able and receive as they need, and it equally resembles the social-democratic idea of decommodifying certain services. But I think the principle differs by locating the value of communism in removing profit from vulnerability itself, rather than in abundance or distributive equality. Is this a distinct position or a restatement of a familiar one under another name? What does it get right, and where does it break — for instance, is it genuinely objectionable for others to earn a living by meeting my needs if they meet them well, and why would a generous welfare state not already realize nearly everything the principle asks for?
