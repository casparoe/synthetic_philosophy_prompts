For a feature I am writing on the way informal online sanction now operates as a parallel system of punishment outside the law, I want to work through one episode in detail and use it to test the standard theories of punishment. Take the case of Justine Sacco, the public relations executive who in December 2013, just before boarding a long flight to South Africa, posted a tweet widely received as a racist joke about AIDS in Africa. Without phone reception for the duration of the flight, she could neither see nor answer the reaction, and by the time she landed she had been pilloried worldwide and fired by her employer. I am deliberately setting aside whether the tweet was racist and whether she should have lost her job. What I want instead is a rigorous case analysis of the episode as an instance of social sanction: punishment administered by an unorganized public, with a private employer acting under its pressure, rather than by any legal authority.

Begin by saying whether the episode counts as punishment at all on a standard definition — the intentional imposition of a burden by some authority in response to a perceived wrong — and, if it fails the definition, which element is missing and why that matters. Then work through the main theories of punishment and say what each would prescribe here. The retributivist asks whether the penalty was proportionate to the offense and whether the offender received no more and no less than she deserved; the deterrence theorist asks what conduct, in whom, was being deterred and at what collateral cost; the expressive or communicative theorist asks what the sanction communicated, from whom to whom, and whether the target was in any position to receive and answer the message; and an incapacitation account must say what further offense the firing actually prevented. Then tell me where the analogy between this episode and state punishment breaks down. I am especially interested in the absence of adjudication and any proportionality check, the way thousands of small individual sanctions aggregated into a single crushing penalty, the permanent and searchable character of the record, the asymmetry of power between the target and the crowd, and the fact that the most consequential punisher — the employer — was not the party most directly wronged, if anyone was wronged at all.

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: what does the Sacco case show about the idea that social sanction is punishment? Is it best understood as a defective instance of punishment, as a different social practice that borrows punishment's vocabulary, or as evidence that the classical theories assumed features of formal legal punishment that do not transfer to decentralized online sanction? I want an honest account of which features of the episode each theory explains, which it misdescribes, and where each falls silent, rather than a forced mapping.
