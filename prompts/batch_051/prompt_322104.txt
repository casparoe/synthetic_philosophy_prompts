Here's a debate in moral philosophy that has been spinning its wheels for as long as I can remember, and I want a diagnosis of the dissagreement itself, not another attempted settlement.

On one side are the systematizers: utilitarians, Kantians, contractualists in their reductive moods—people who think a moral theory earns a real point in its favor if it derives many duties from one master principle (or a very small set). They treat parsimony as a genuine theoretical virtue, the way good science prefers not to multiply basic forces; a one-principle theory is supposed to explain the moral data instead of just listing it. On the other side are Ross-style pluralists, intuitionists, and at the far end particularists, who say ethical reality is just messy: a list of irreducible prima facie duties tracks the phenomena better than an elegant monism with a hundred ad hoc epicycles bolted on. For them, parsimony in ethics is at best a pragmatic or aesthetic preference and shouldn't override fit with considered judgements. Both camps will happily use reflective equilibrium, but each accuses the other of rigging what gets equilibrated: the monist says the pluralist lets unprincipled intuitions veto every theory, and the pluralist says the monist lets a taste for elegance veto the data.

My working hunch: this isn't fundamentally a first-order moral disagreement. I suspect it's a disagreement in moral epistemology about whether simplicity/parsimony has evidential weight in normative theory at all, rather than being mere cognitive convenience or good pedagogy. But I keep flipping on that. Maybe there's instead a hidden substantive question about moral realism or about how reliable case intuitions are; or maybe 'parsimony' just means different things in the two camps and they've been talking past each other.
