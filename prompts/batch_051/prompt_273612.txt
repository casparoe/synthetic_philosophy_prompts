A mid-sized software consultancy decides to redesign its performance-review process around the dichotomy of control popularized by the modern Stoicism revival. At the start of each quarter, every employee submits two lists. The "up to me" list may contain only actions the employee can perform: preparations, attempts, processes, choices of technique, messages sent, skills practiced. Outcomes, metrics, and market conditions are expressly forbidden from this list. The "not up to me" list contains everything else that matters to the business: client decisions, deal outcomes, colleagues' and subordinates' contributions, product performance, economic conditions. At quarter's end, pay and promotion depend solely on whether the employee did the actions on their own "up to me" list, verified against lightweight evidence such as logs, drafts, or meeting attendance. Anything on the "not up to me" list is never evaluated and cannot be held against anyone, even when an outcome was foreseeable and the employee plausibly had some indirect influence. Employees write their own lists, a manager must approve them, and managers are in turn assessed on their own "up to me" list, which includes the action of thoughtfully approving or challenging each subordinate's lists.

Analyze how this system would actually behave in practice: who gets promoted and who leaves, what kinds of work migrate onto the "up to me" list and what quietly gets neglected, how employees game the boundary between the two columns, how managers game the approval step, what second-order effects appear in hiring and internal politics, and where the system fails first. The founders want to know specifically whether the failure modes are accidental implementation bugs that a small patch could fix, or whether they are built into the underlying idea that only what is up to each person should count.
