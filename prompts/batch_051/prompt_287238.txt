I read a summary of "order and harmony as political values" that's been nagging at me, and I want to know how fair it is. The summary runs something like this:

"For Confucians, harmony is the overarching goal of politics. A harmonious society is one where conflicts, strong emotions, and open disagreement are kept out of sight, everyone settles into their proper station, and the ruler maintains order from above. That is why Confucian political thought has always been more comfortable with authority and stability than with individual rights. Western conservatives like Burke are after basically the same thing—order and settled hierarchy rather than the endless contestation liberal pluralists celebrate—they just don't share the Confucian metaphysics."

I know the broad outlines of Confucianism, so the "harmony" part sounds half right to me, but I suspect its flattening something. Id like you to audit this summary and the leap it makes to "Confucians are anti-pluralist." Go roughly sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful reader of the Confucian texts would accept, what they'd object to, and what survives the correction. The passage the summary leans on is from James Legge's 1893 translation of *The Doctrine of the Mean*:

"While there are no stirrings of pleasure, anger, sorrow, or joy, the mind may be said to be in the state of Equilibrium. When those feelings have been stirred, and they act in their due degree, there ensues what may be called the state of Harmony. This Equilibrium is the great root from which grow all the human actings in the world, and this Harmony is the universal path which they all should pursue.

Let the states of equilibrium and harmony exist in perfection, and a happy order will prevail throughout heaven and earth, and all things will be nourished and flourish."

How much does this passage support or undercut the summary's picture of harmony? And at the end, say how confident you are in your reading and which point you're least sure of.
