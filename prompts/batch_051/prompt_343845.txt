I am a high school senior preparing for a national Model United Nations conference, where I will represent a state in a committee that reviews the international refugee system. In drafting my position paper, I have found that I cannot write a single sentence about asylum policy without leaning on a contested definition of refugee, so I would like a working definition before I go further.

The 1951 Refugee Convention, on which the committee's work is based, protects people who have a well-founded fear of persecution because of their race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group. I do not want to simply recite that formula, because most of the committee's hardest cases fall at its edges.

Here are the cases I want the definition to classify:

1. A family crosses a border after government soldiers burned their house and threatened to kill them because of their religion.
2. A young man leaves his country for a factory job abroad because the wages at home cannot support his family.
3. A farmer leaves after three successive droughts destroyed his crops and the government, which had promised relief, has stopped distributing food aid in his region.
4. A woman flees after years of beatings by her husband; she twice reported the abuse to the police, who dismissed it as a private family matter.
5. A teenager flees a city district controlled by a criminal gang that threatened to kill him for refusing to join; the police rarely enter the district and are widely believed to be compromised by the gang.
6. A gay man flees a country whose government has just made homosexual conduct a crime punishable by imprisonment and has begun raiding bars and nightclubs known to serve gay customers.
7. A family flees a civil war because artillery shells have fallen on their neighborhood for months; no armed group has threatened them personally.
8. A soldier deserts and crosses a border rather than take part in a war of aggression his own government launched; he would be imprisoned for desertion if returned.

I am confident that the first case is a refugee and the second is not, and I am genuinely unsure about the third and the eighth. I suspect that the word refugee is doing two jobs—one legal and one moral—that no longer overlap perfectly. Please propose the best single working definition you can, run it through all eight cases, and tell me where it breaks down or gives a surprising answer. If you conclude that no single definition will do the work, give me the two definitions you think are actually in play and say which cases move between them, and which of the eight verdicts you are least confident about.
