I keep getting stuck on an argument I half believe about the alignment tax. The idea is that making an AI safe costs something—compute, researchers' time, or capability you could've had otherwise—so a lab that's careful either ships a worse model for the same money or the same model for more money. And the competitiveness constraint says that even if every lab is run by well-meaning people, the one that skimps on safety has a cost advantage, so the market punishes the careful lab. That much seems pretty airtight to me.

But here's the chain that's bugging me:

(1) Safety has a cost—call it a tax.
(2) In any competitive market, the firm that pays less of the tax ends up with lower costs or a better product.
(3) So the firm paying less will tend to outcompete the firm paying more.
(4) Over time the market selects for paying as little of the safety tax as you can get away with.
(5) But the exact same reasoning applies to airlines skimping on maintenance, drug companies skimping on trials, nuclear plants skimping on containment, car makers skimping on crash testing.
(6) Those industries look, on the whole, safer than this argument predicts. Planes don't fall out of the sky constantly, and most drugs aren't thalidomide.
(7) So the argument either fails for AI or fails for airlines—it can't be the whole story about competitive pressure.

Which step would you reject, and where exactly does the analogy between AI and those older industries break (if it does)? For what it's worth, I'd rather give up (5) or (6) then (1)-(4), because the economic logic feels undeniable, but (6) also feels obviously true. So: what am I paying whichever way I go? And if the argument were right after all, what would the world have to look like for it to be true that planes are safe?
