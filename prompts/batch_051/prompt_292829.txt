Exam question:
"Evaluate the claim that forgiveness can be neither unconditional nor morally obligatory. In your answer, distinguish forgiveness from excusing, condoning, and reconciliation, and explain how each distinction bears on the question."

Student's answer:
"Forgiveness presupposes that a responsible agent has wronged the victim. To forgive is to overcome, or forswear, resentment towards that agent while continuing to regard what was done as wrong. It must therefore be distinguished from three neighbouring phenomena. To excuse is to hold that the agent was not fully responsible — because of ignorance, compulsion, or incapacity — and so to withdraw the charge; if the agent is excused, there is nothing left to forgive. To condone is to stop treating the act as wrong, whereas the forgiver continues to condemn the act while releasing the resentment it produced. To reconcile is to restore the relationship; this is a further step, which forgiveness does not guarantee, since one can forgive and still refuse to resume a relationship that has been destroyed by distrust.

Given these distinctions, the debate about obligation is straightforward. Forgiveness is the voluntary remission of something the wrongdoer has no right to demand; it is, in short, a gift. No one can be under a duty to make a gift, because a performance done from duty would not be free, and what is not free cannot be a gift. Forgiveness, therefore, cannot be obligatory. Duties can govern our outward treatment of wrongdoers, but not the inner act of forgiving. And since forgiveness cannot be obligatory, unconditional forgiveness cannot be obligatory either.

The only remaining question is whether unconditional forgiveness — forgiveness in the absence of repentance or apology — is so much as possible. It is not, in the strict sense. Overcoming resentment requires a reason, and the only adequate reason is the wrongdoer's repentance and apology. Where repentance is absent, what looks like forgiving is really something else: forgetting the wrong, or a merely therapeutic effort to be rid of one's resentment. In either case the change is made for the victim's own sake, not for the wrongdoer's, and so neither amounts to forgiveness. An unconditional forgiver would therefore either be condoning the wrong or mistaking self-therapy for forgiveness. It follows that only conditional forgiveness, granted in response to repentance, deserves the name."
