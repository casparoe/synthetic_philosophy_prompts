I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been turning over something I noticed about the way debugging actually gets done. There is a standing list of things no one treats as live hypotheses when something breaks. When a service misbehaves, the first assumption is that the defect is in our own recent changes, or perhaps in a dependency we just updated; no one begins by suspecting that the CPU is miscomputing, that the compiler miscompiled a line, that integer addition glitched, or that memory stopped being coherent. Those are real possibilities—processors ship with errata, compilers have bugs, cosmic rays flip bits—but if we admitted them as ordinary suspects, work would stop. They are held fixed, not because anyone has tested them in this context, but because the rest of the investigation has to stand somewhere. Only after local explanations are exhausted do we reluctantly promote an "impossible" cause to "unlikely." A friend told me this sounds like something Wittgenstein was getting at in On Certainty: the idea that some certainties are not known or verified in the ordinary way, but form the unmoving backdrop against which doubt and testing become possible at all. I tried the book, but I don't know what to do with it.

What I want is an account of what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes. Is it merely an engineering heuristic for managing time under uncertainty, or does it carry an actual epistemology with it—something like the view that not everything can be doubted at once, or that certain things are held in a fundamentally different way than empirical beliefs about the code? Could a thorough fallibilist who says every technical claim is in principle revisable describe exactly the same practice without contradiction, by telling a story about costs and expected value? And what would daily engineering look like if we deliberately ran on the other picture, treating the language specification, the compiler, and the hardware as ordinary suspects from the first minute? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined. Also, if I have misread On Certainty, or if the premise that a debugging practice "presupposes" an epistemology is confused, tell me that plainly instead of working around it.
