I help run a small family foundation whose younger members are deep in the effective-altruist world and whose older members suspect we now only fund interventions that come with a clean randomized evaluation attached. The argument is always the same: the EA side insists that unmeasured impact is indistinguishable from no impact, and the skeptics reply that this quietly starves policy advocacy, coalition-building, norm-shifting, and institution-construction — the work whose causal chains are too diffuse and slow to show up in a trial. We are considering a concrete compromise, and I want a hard-nosed analysis of how it would actually behave rather than a debate about whether it is principled.

The proposal: our foundation will place a fixed 30% of each year's payout into a separate Opaque Impact portfolio. To receive money from that portfolio, an organization must first pass an unmeasurability screen: it has to convince a review panel that no feasible RCT, natural experiment, or defensible quantitative counterfactual could estimate its per-dollar effect within a decade, so the usual EA yardsticks are structurally inapplicable rather than merely inconvenient. Once certified, an organization gets unrestricted multi-year funding and reports only on process — what it did, whom it convened, what it changed about its own strategy. As the single safeguard, each Opaque grantee must publish one falsifiable leading indicator of progress that a skeptical outsider could check in five years, and the other Opaque grantees vote on whether that indicator is too mushy; a vote of too mushy reduces the next tranche by 20%.

Walk through what this setup incentivizes, what kind of organizations and behaviors it selects for, where it gets gamed first, and what it does to the foundation's relationship with measurable, evidence-backed causes. I especially want to know whether the unmeasurability screen itself becomes a gameable asset — a credential organizations will optimize for — and whether the leading-indicator check can be made sharp without quietly smuggling measurability back in. Consider whether the failure modes are fixable by adjusting the rules, or whether the fix itself creates new distortions. Then take a definite position on the most important failure mode: does the mechanism mainly fail because grantees game certification, because the Opaque portfolio becomes a low-accountability subsidy, or because of a second-order effect not obvious from the rules as written? Defend that verdict rather than laying out the options.
