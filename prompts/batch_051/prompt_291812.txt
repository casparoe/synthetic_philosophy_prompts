So I've been going back and forth with a friend about AI art, and he keeps reaching for the old objection people usually credit to Ada Lovelace: a machine can't be genuinely creative because it only does what its programmers told it to do, and whatever originality shows up in the output really belongs to the programmer, not the machine. I know the line mostly through Turing's discussion of it, and the standard reply used to be that a computer can still suprise its programmers by exploring combinations nobody explicitly wrote into the code.

But it seems like the argument has shifted in a way the old replies don't quite cover. A diffusion model that generates images isn't a set of hand-coded rules about composition, color, and style. It gets trained: it compresses millions of captioned images into a latent space and learns to reverse a noising process, so it produces images that were never in the training set and can be steered by prompts nobody anticipated. The engineers choose an objective and a dataset, but nobody sits down and encodes 'how to paint.' So the classic Lovelace-style objection—machines only execute the explicit instructions of their makers—looks like it's aimed at a kind of computing that doesn't exist anymore.

Here's what I'm stuck on. Does Lovelace's objection actually survive this change, just with a shifted target? Maybe the system still isn't creative: it's only optimizing a loss function and sampling from a distribution, so the creativity in the output was inherited from the human artists in the training data and from the engineers who picked the objective. Or is that a defensive retreat—if 'programmed' gets stretched to mean 'trained on human data with a human-chosen objective,' then the objection becomes so loose that nothing a machine did could ever count against it? I want to know what happens to the argument itself, not whether you happen to like AI-generated images. Which premise does the shift from explicit programming to training bear on, and can the objection be repaired into a form that still does real work, or is it basically dead?
