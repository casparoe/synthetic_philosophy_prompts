Lately I keep noticing my own views about punishing people who did nothing wrong are a bit of a mess, and I'd like to know if there's actually a principle holding the mess together. Here are some of my verdicts, roughly as they come to me.

1. Two drivers buy the same car, same year, same brake-line defect that no service check would have caught. Both maintained the car by the book. One's brakes fail on a curve and he kills a pedestrian; the other's fail in his driveway with nobody around. I don't want either to go to prison, and the second I'd let off entirely. But I can't shake the feeling that the first should face something—a suspended sentence or a serious fine—even though their conduct was literally identical. I'm not sure whether that's a defensible part of the principle or just me being fooled by the outcome.

2. A food importer uses the standard test on every lot for a contaminant it knows the test sometimes misses. The test catches the contaminant in 999 of 1000 lots; a lot passes anyway, turns out contaminated, and a dozen people get sick. I'm comfortable fining the importer even though they did everything reasonable: the fine is the cost of choosing to put food into commerce, and they can spread it across prices better than the unlucky shoppers can.

3. Same situation, but the importer is a one-woman business and the fine will close her down and take her savings. I'm less comfortable, but I still think the fine is right, or at least the victims shouldn't have to eat the cost themselves. The discomfort doesn't change the verict.

4. A pharmacist fills a prescription that is a near-perfect forgery; the pills get abused and someone dies. He checked everything he was expected to check. I don't want him punished at all—not criminally, and honestly not even personally fined, though maybe the pharmacy should eat some of the insurance risk.

5. A 22-year-old sleeps with someone who shows a flawless fake ID saying she's 21. She's actually 15 but looks the part. I really don't want him on a sex-offender registry. Part of me still wants the law to be strict here—maybe a conviction is unavoidable—but the punishment should be almost nothing, or at least nothing with lifelong stigma attached.

6. A factory CEO signs off on discharge permits. A subordinate fakes the monthly water-quality report, and the company's own audit should have caught it but didn't because the auditor was sloppy. Toxic discharge kills the fish in a river. The law allows prison. I don't want the CEO locked up; I'd fine the company large and maybe even want him fired, but prison feels wrong because he didn't know and the failiure wasn't his.

My tentative principle is something like: strict liability is fine for civil fines and regulatory penalties, especially on businesses that can spread the cost, but never for prison, and never for the kind of punishment that marks someone as a criminal or a predator. The problem is I can feel that rule doing less work than I'd like. It doesn't obviously handle the two drivers—why should the unlucky one pay when the lucky one doesn't, if fault is what matters—it doesn't explain why the one-woman business gets the same fine as a big one, and it leans on a sharp line between fines and punishment that I'm not sure I believe (a big enough fine can ruin someone just as thoroughly as prison). So: is there a coherent principle that captures most of these verdicts, what does it say about each case, which verdicts do I have to give up, and are there further cases I should run it on before I trust it?
