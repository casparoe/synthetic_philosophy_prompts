I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I work on a reasoning system whose domain of objects changes over time: entities are created, modified, and decommissioned, and queries have to be evaluated across states in which different objects exist. We model states, times, and configurations as worlds in a modal logic, and I have been trying to determine which quantifier inferences remain sound when objects enter and leave the domain. That is how I ended up in the debate over the Barcan formulas.

I can write down the two formulas:

BF: ∀x□φ(x) → □∀xφ(x)

CBF: □∀xφ(x) → ∀x□φ(x)

My working understanding is that accepting both is natural only if the domain of quantification is the same in every world, and that once domains vary, at least one of them has to be given up; I have also seen it claimed that the direction of domain change determines which one survives, but I do not trust my grasp of the details. I have run across a cluster of related ideas — constant-domain versus varying-domain semantics, an existence predicate and a free-logical treatment of non-denoting terms, the possibilist/actualist distinction in reading the quantifiers, and the question whether identity across worlds requires something like rigid designation — and I cannot tell which of these are load-bearing for an implementation and which are purely philosophical.
