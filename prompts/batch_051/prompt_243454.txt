I am a PhD candidate in political theory. In our reading group on technology and politics, we have been working through Langdon Winner’s essay Do Artifacts Have Politics? One of my colleagues keeps countering the central thesis with a simple analogy. He says calling a technical artifact political is like calling a hammer political. A hammer can be used to build a house or to kill someone, so its moral and political significance depends entirely on the hand that wields it and the social context in which it is wielded. From that, he draws a stronger conclusion: Winner’s distinction between technologies that are flexible in their political consequences and those that are inherently political collapses. The so-called flexible technologies, he argues, are simply tools whose surrounding social arrangements are visible, while the so-called inherently political ones are tools embedded in rigid social arrangements; in both cases the politics resides in the arrangements, not in the artifact.

I suspect the hammer analogy is doing more work than it can bear, but I want to test that suspicion properly. Treat Winner’s contrast as I have just characterized it, and give me a committed audit of the analogy. What corresponds to what between a hammer and, say, an urban highway or a large technical system? Where does the mapping break? Which features of the hammer as a source domain does the argument quietly import—individual scale, reversibility, transferability between hands, transparency of use, multiplicity of uses—that large technical systems lack? Then tell me whether my colleague’s conclusion survives once those disanalogies are on the table. If the hammer analogy fails for the distinction’s purposes, say what a better comparison would be, and state plainly whether artifacts can be political in a sense that outruns the hammer. I want a definite position with reasons, not a survey of possible readings.
