I'm leading a one-time evening class for adults in a community education program; it runs ninety minutes, there is no assigned reading, and the students have no background in philosophy or psychology. The whole point of the session is to teach one specific kind of conceptual slippage: what I'm going to call a copycat concept. A copycat concept is a term that begins inside a disciplined practice as a diagnostic category with definite application conditions, then migrates into ordinary speech carrying the seriousness and authority of the original while losing the conditions that made it do real work. The running case will be "OCD," which I'll treat as having the diagnostic core of recurrent obsessions or compulsions that cause real distress and functional impairment, and which now gets used for a fondness for an organized desk or a clean kitchen.

The students should come away understanding the mechanism through this one case, not learning psychiatry and not settling who may use psychiatric language. I want the architecture of the session rather than the session itself. Structure your response in the following six parts, in this order:

1. Order of ideas and dependencies (about 300 words): what concept has to be in place before the idea of a copycat concept can even land, and why each later piece cannot be introduced earlier or later than you place it.

2. The carrying example (about 150 words): whether "OCD" is the right case for this job, which features of it do the teaching work, and which feature, if added, would ruin it as a teaching case.

3. Misconception map (about 300 words): the three or four confusions these students are most likely to bring or form, in the order they will arise. I particularly expect two early ones: "language always changes, so nothing here needs explaining" and "the problem is just misuse, which dictionaries could fix." Tell me where in the sequence to head each off rather than clean it up afterward.

4. Closing check (about 100 words): one question or short exercise at the end that would show whether students have grasped the mechanism itself rather than settled for parroting the example.

5. What to leave out (about 100 words): which questions I should explicitly set aside — for instance, whether psychiatric categories are themselves socially constructed, whether the everyday use of "OCD" is harmful and should be policed, or whether a particular person's use is literally false — and how to set them aside without making the session feel evasive.

6. Where the plan is most fragile (about 100 words): which step in the sequence is most likely to fail in the room, what student response would be the earliest sign that it has failed, and what consideration would lead you to replace the running example or invert the order of ideas rather than just patch the plan.
