I recently spent an evening arguing with a friend about Frank Jackson's knowledge argument, specifically over the passage in Epiphenomenal Qualia where Jackson has Mary leave her black-and-white room and see something colored for the first time. My friend kept insisting that the argument collapses once you distinguish knowing a fact from having an ability or being acquainted with something, and I found I could not always say, in the right order, why that reply is too quick. Next month I have to run a two-hour session for a philosophy reading group of scientists and engineers on the knowledge argument, and I want to teach it through that passage rather than through the usual slogans about redness and roses. Plan the session for me: what conceptual pieces need to be in place before the passage can do its work—above all, what physical information must not be allowed to slide toward, and how the thought experiment depends on Mary's completeness—and which misconceptions the group is most likely to bring. I expect at least these three: that the argument is about the limits of current scientific method, that Mary gains only an ability or a new mode of presentation of something she already knew, and that the conclusion is obviously true because you cannot learn what an experience is like from a description. Tell me where in the sequence I should defuse each, and rank them by how damaging they are if left in place. I want the session to make clear what the knowledge argument does and does not need in order to work, and I want a final question or exercise that would tell me whether they have understood the structure of the argument rather than just rehearsed the intuition. Please also say what I should openly leave out at this level.
