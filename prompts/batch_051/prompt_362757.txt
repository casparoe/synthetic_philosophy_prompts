Someone in my reading group gave a confident summary of Libet's work the other day and I've been turning it over since. He put it like this: in the classic experiment, people watch a dot going around a clock face and flex their wrist whenever they feel like it, noting where the dot was when they first felt the urge. With electrodes on their scalp, Libet found that a buildup of activity called the readiness potential starts about half a second before the movement, while the conscious experience of deciding to move only shows up around 200 milliseconds before the action. So, he concluded, the brain had already 'decided' more than a quarter of a second before the person had any conscious say; the feeling of deciding is just a story the brain confabulates after the fact, and free will doesn't initiate anything. He did add that Libet found people can consciously veto an urge at the last instant, and that's where free will hangs on — we can't start actions freely, we can only stop them.

I've seen that basic line repeated a lot, so I'm inclined to trust the broad picture, but the conclusions feel like they're moving faster than the data. Could you do an accuracy audit? Specifically: where is his summary right, where is it subtly misrepresenting Libet's actual findings or what they imply, and does the veto point rescue free will or just relocate the problem? At the end, I'd also like to know what would change your mind — which findings or arguments would make you upgrade or downgrade the whole 'conscious intention comes too late' story, and where your assessment is most likely to break.
