Midterm question (20 marks). Distinguish akrasia from hyperbolic discounting. Then: (i) state the strongest version of the claim that they are the same phenomenon described in different vocabularies; (ii) give the strongest objection to that claim; and (iii) using a concrete case of your own choosing (not one from class), say whether the case is an instance of akrasia, hyperbolic discounting, both, or neither, and why.

Student answer: Both of these involve someone ending up with the worse outcome in a way she sort of saw coming. Akrasia, as we've defined it, is acting against your own all-things-considered better judgment at the moment you act. Hyperbolic discounting is about how people value delayed rewards: the discount rate is higher over short horizons than long ones, so a smaller-sooner option can overtake a larger-later one as it gets close. The standard example is someone who in January prefers $100 in July to $90 in June, but by June 1 prefers $90 now to $100 in a month. At first the distinction looks clean: akrasia involves judgment, while hyperbolic discounting is just a shape of preference over time.

The strongest identity claim isn't that the terms mean the same thing. It's that hyperbolic discounting is the mechanism behind most akratic action, and that what philosophers call the agent's judgment in akrasia is really the agent's long-run preference, the one she'd endorse from a cool, reflective point of view. On this picture, when Julia from the lecture decides to quit smoking and then smokes at a party, her earlier judgment that quitting is best is the cool long-run preference; as the cigarette gets close the discount curve steepens, and at the party she judges that smoking now is better, so she smokes. That, on this view, is the whole phenomenon.

The strongest objection is that akrasia requires the better judgment to be present at the moment of action, whereas hyperbolic discounting doesn't require judgment at all: preferences can reverse in a person who never forms an all-things-considered view, so there can be preference reversal without akrasia. But I don't think this splits the two in any case we care about, because in the Julia case she can say, while lighting up, "I know this is a bad idea." So the judgment is there; it just gets outweighed.

For my own case, take a grad student with a chapter due. Every morning he judges that finishing the revisions is the best use of his day. In the afternoon he tells himself he'll start after a short break, and then he scrolls his phone until midnight. That's akrasia, and it's also hyperbolic discounting, because the near reward of scrolling outweighs the far reward of finishing, which from the morning's perspective looked more valuable. So the case is both, and I think in general the two just are the same phenomenon described at two levels.
