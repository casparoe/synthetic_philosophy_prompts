Keynes held that some of the most consequential economic decisions are made not under risk, where numerical probabilities can defensibly be assigned, but under uncertainty, where the evidence does not determine any such numbers. I want your credence in the following sharpened version of that claim: there is at least one practically consequential real-world question — say, the net long-run effects of a transformative technology, or the economic consequences of an unprecedented change in the climate system — for which no single precise probability distribution is the uniquely rational epistemic state, even for an ideal agent in possession of all the relevant evidence, so that forcing such an agent to supply one would misrepresent the situation rather than merely require an arbitrary but harmless choice. Please give a number, but derive it. Decompose the estimate into the components: whether rational partial belief must always be representable by precise numbers; whether, if precision is not mandatory in principle, it actually fails for some important empirical question rather than only for artificial examples; whether any such failure is a feature of the evidence itself, as Keynes thought, or only of our limited information and computational abilities; and whether treating an imprecise case as if it were precise meaningfully changes what a reasonable decision-maker should do. Assign a rough probability to each component, state how you combine them, and tell me which component your final credence is most sensitive to and what evidence would move it most.
