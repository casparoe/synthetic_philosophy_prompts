Im taking a decision theory course this term and I keep going back and forth on something. The expected utility framework really appeals to me: for any fully specified bet or choice, you compute the expectation and maximize it, and if you violate the axioms you seem to end up with some combination of intransitive preferences or vulnerability to a money pump. It's simple, unifying, and it gives a definite answer in basically every case—until it doesn't.

Here's the case that's bugging me. A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads. If the first heads is on the nth toss, you receive (-1)^(n-1) times 2^n divided by n dollars. So heads on the first toss pays you 2 dollars, heads on the second costs you 2 dollars, heads on the third pays you 8/3, and so on. The expected value, written out the natural way, is 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which converges to ln(2). But it only converges conditionally: the positive terms and the negative terms each sum to infinity, so the expectation in the usual measure-theoretic sense doesn't even exist, and you can rearrange the terms to make the sum come out to anything you like. A friend of mine thinks I'm overcomplicating it—he says the expectation is obviously ln(2), so you just play if the price is below that. But that seems to miss the point: the theory has no canonical number to hand me, so it goes silent on whether to play, refuse, or be indifferent. And my intuition is stubborn: surely one of those three attitudes is rationally better, or at least not worse, than the others. A theory that goes mute on a fully specified bet feels broken, but I'm not ready to throw out expected utility over one artificial game.

So what should give? I can see three moves, and I want to know what would justify each of them and what it would cost. (a) Debunk the intuition: maybe some decision problems really do lack a rational resolution, and "undefined expectation" is just the formal sign of that. (b) Amend the theory: maybe restrict it to bets with finite or integrable expectations, or adopt a regularized notion of expectation like the Cauchy principal value, or add some dominance-style extension for the undefined cases—and I want to know what each amendment rules out or quietly smuggles in. (c) Reconcile them: maybe the theory governs one notion of rational preferability and my intuition tracks a looser, less formal notion, and I have to say which one I actually care about. I don't want a winner declared; I want the strongest version of each move and a sense of what I'd be giving up.
