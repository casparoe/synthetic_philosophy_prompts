I keep stumbling over an asymmetry in the way ordinary people treat expertise, and I can't tell if its defensible or just an unexamined prior. The same person who would never let a random neighbor do their angioplasty, wants their pilot to have a minimum number of hours, and expects the FDA to keep drugs off the market until actual experts run actual trials will turn around and insist that elections should be one-person-one-vote — even on trade policy, pandemic response, monetary policy, and climate targets, where the subject matter is much harder than plumbing and an error kills or immiserates millions. We don't put bridge design to a referendum of the drivers; we put pandemic policy in front of voters who mostly couldn't state the difference between R0 and a case fatality rate. The place with the highest stakes is exactly the place we're most reluctant to let credentialed people decide.

Now, I know this is basically the epistocracy debate, and I also know the obvious move: medicine and engineering have agreed-upon success criteria while politics is contested values, so expertise can't settle it. But that feels too quick to me. A lot of politics is means, not ends — low unemployment, cheap clean energy, fewer overdose deaths, not losing a war are broadly shared ends, and the disagreement is over which policies get there. That is precisely the kind of question where we'd normally ask an expert. So I want the strongest version of the principle, steelmanned properly, that justifies "defer to experts for the technical stuff, but give everyone an equal vote for the highest-stakes collective stuff." Then test it against the nearby cases: juries deciding guilt, parents overriding a doctor's advice for their child, an independent central bank, or letting a professional rather than a friend manage your money. Does the principle survive, and if it doesn't, should the asymmetry be dropped in favor of more political deference, or less deference everywhere else? Please answer in American English.
