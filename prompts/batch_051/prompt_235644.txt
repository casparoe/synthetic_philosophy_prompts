I've been trying to work out what principle actually governs my own judgments about intentional action, using the Knobe-effect materials as a starting point, and I've found that my verdicts resist the simple moral-valence story. The original asymmetry stays with me: when a manager approves a profitable project knowing it will harm the environment and says she doesn't care either way, I have no hesitation saying she intentionally harmed the environment; when the same manager approves a profitable project knowing it will help the environment and says she doesn't care either way, I deny that she intentionally helped the environment. But the obvious explanation of that pair — harmful side effects are judged intentional, helpful ones not — starts to fail once I move to neighboring cases, because my verdicts appear to track more than the valence of the side effect.

Here are the cases. A manager can keep a factory open only by using a process that will release a damaging pollutant into a marsh; she has spent years hunting for alternatives, hates the damage, and proceeds only because every alternative would bankrupt the plant. I don't think she intentionally damaged the marsh — I default to no, with only a faint pull the other way. A manager approves a project that will, as a side effect, clean up a river; she happens to live on that river and has wanted it cleaned for years, so she is privately delighted, although that is not why she approved the project. I think she intentionally cleaned up the river. A third manager approves a project that will improve a river as a side effect, but only by breaching a binding rule written by the regional water authority that prohibits unlicensed alterations to the river even when they are beneficial; the improvement is plainly good, and the manager has no particular view about the river either way. I think he intentionally improved the river, though I am less sure here than I am about the delighted manager. Finally, a manager approves an accounting change that will, as a foreseen but irrelevant side effect, slightly alter the tint of the paint in a break room, and he has no view about this at all. I think he did not intentionally change the paint tint; that one feels obvious.

Is there a single principle — built from valence, norm violation, the agent's attitude, the place the side effect occupies in the agent's practical reasoning, or some combination of these — that yields all of my verdicts, including the original harm/help asymmetry? If no clean principle does, I'd like to know which of my verdicts I should abandon and what the least contrived surviving principle is. Please also say what your preferred principle predicts for a case I have deliberately left unscripted: a manager who brings about a small environmental improvement as a foreseen side effect while genuinely wishing it wouldn't happen and taking steps to prevent it. I care less about having my gut reactions preserved than about understanding which of them commit me to which account.
