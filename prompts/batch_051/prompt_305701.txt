I've been in a little reading group on Bayesian epistemology for the past year, and we keep getting stuck on the same fight: subjective Bayesians versus objective Bayesians about whether there are rationally mandatory priors. On the subjective side, the story is that any prior that obeys the probability axioms is permitted; coherence is the only real constraint, and differences wash out through conditionalization once enough data arrives. On the objective side, people push the maximum entropy principle as a way to pick a prior from the set of distributions consistent with whatever background constraints you have: choose the distribution with the greatest Shannon entropy, the one that is in some sense least committal beyond the constraints you actually accept. That sounds like it settles the question, but then the subjectivists point out that the answer depends on which constraints you decide to include, and worse, that the max-ent prior changes when you reparameterize the problem, so the least committal prior for a parameter isn't the least committal prior for its log or it's inverse. The objectivists reply that this is a defect in the constraints, not in entropy, and around we go.

The debate has looked basically like this for decades, with a lot of technical sophistication but no real movement. My working theory is that it has stalled because the two sides are not disagreeing about a theorem; they disagree about what a prior is for. Subjectivists treat a prior as a representation of an agent's actual beliefs, so there is no sense in asking which one is objectively correct. Objectivists treat a prior more like a default starting point for inquiry or an agreed convention for reporting evidence, so it makes sense to ask which one is least biased. Is that diagnosis right? Or is there a genuine formal question underneath, say whether any representation-invariant notion of noninformativeness is even possible, or an empirical/cognitive question about how people actually update? I want a diagnosis, not a survey: what kind of disagreement is this, and is there anything—a theorem, a distinction, a change of question—that could actually break the impasse?
