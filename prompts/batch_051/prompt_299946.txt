There is a formal result I keep seeing: if two ideal Bayesian reasoners start from the same prior and update by conditioning on their own private information, then once each one's posterior becomes common knowledge between them, they cannot have different posteriors. I can follow that proof. What I cannot tell is how much weight to put on the stronger philosophical claim people usually attach to it — the common prior assumption: that rationality itself singles out one correct starting point before any evidence arrives, so that every disagreement between ideal reasoners must be traceable to a difference in the information they have received, never to a difference in their initial probabilities.

Here is the proposition I want your credence for: before receiving any private evidence, any two ideally rational agents would adopt the same prior probability distribution, and therefore any disagreement between ideal agents could in principle be dissolved by pooling all of their private information. I want an actual number — your genuine credence that this proposition is true — not a range and not an "it depends." Then decompose that number. In particular, how much of it rides on whether the phrase "ideally rational" has enough determinate content to make the proposition truth-evaluable at all; how much on whether, if it does, rationality really forces a unique prior rather than merely a family of permissible priors; how much on whether the common prior assumption is best understood as a modeling convention rather than as a factual claim about rational agents; and how much on what would count as a decisive counterexample, such as two agents with different but apparently defensible priors who still disagree after full information pooling. Show how the components combine into the final number, and say which single sub-question would move your credence the most if you got a definitive answer to it.
