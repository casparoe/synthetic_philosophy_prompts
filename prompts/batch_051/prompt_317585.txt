Not a logician, so forgive the phrasing. I keep seeing a particular argument for the claim that one simple model of computation — the tape-and-head kind, or really any of the usual textbook formalisms — is as general as the notion of a mechanical procedure can get. The argument runs like this. Several very different-looking formalisms (tape-and-head machines, a certain calculus, a certain class of recursively defined functions, even simple string-rewriting systems) all ended up defining exactly the same class of functions. On top of that, nobody has ever found an example of something that feels clearly mechanical — a definate recipe with no judgment calls — but that can't be done by any of these systems. So, the argument concludes, the informal notion has been captured exactly.

But I can't tell what kind of claim that conclusion is. It's not a theorem, because one side is the mushy intuitive idea of "following a recipe mechanically," and no amount of formal result proves a match with an informal notion. But it also isn't being treated as a mere definition or convention, since people point to the independent convergence as if it were evidence. Is there a name for this specific claim, and is there a name for the peculiar kind of support it gets? And how do I keep it seperate from a nearby claim that is an ordinary theorem — that this particular formalism and that particular formalism define the same class of functions? People slide between the two all the time. I'm less after a label than a clean way to say what's doing the work in each claim.
