I'm a PhD student in aesthetics, and in my seminar last week we spent two hours on Hume's "Of the Standard of Taste," and I keep coming back to one line. Early on, after another stretch of him cataloging how much taste varies from person to person and age to age, he says:

"It is natural for us to seek a Standard of Taste; a rule, by which the various sentiments of men may be reconciled; at least, a decision, afforded, confirming one sentiment, and condemning another."

I've basically come to hold a version of this view. I don't think the sheer fact that people disagree about wether something is beautiful settles that it's all just subjective, and I do think that for at least some aesthetic judgments there's a standard — something like the verdict a suitably practiced, unprejudiced, highly discriminating judge would give — that can make one sentiment right and another wrong. Not objective beauty built into the world the way mass or charge is, but a norm of taste grounded partly in how well a response tracks features of the work and partly in convergence among people who are positioned to respond well.

What I want is a serious map of what would count against this. Not the standard objections that taste varies across cultures and that critics disagree — that's the starting point Hume himself grants, not a refutation. I mean: what evidence, cases, or arguments should actually move me? What would a committed Humean have to concede as a genuine defeater rather than something the view can absorb? And at what point does the position get insulated by its own qualifications, where the "true judges" are basically defined as the people who judge correctly, so that the standard couldn't in principle fail? Does that circularity make it unfalsifiable, and is that a decisive problem for a view of this kind, or just the usual shape of a normative standard? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, plus an honest assessment of whether the position risks anything at all.
