Please argue that the precautionary principle is self-undermining when applied to its own adoption. By the precautionary principle I mean something like the standard view that where an activity risks serious or irreversible harm, the absence of full scientific certainty about that harm shouldn't be used as a reason to postpone protective measures, and that the burden of proof falls on the person proposing the risky activity. Now take the activity in question to be: adopting the precautionary principle as a default rule for environmental or health policy. Argue that, on its own terms, the proponent of the principle can't discharge the burden of proof — the net effects of institutionalizing precaution are themselves scientifically uncertain (it might block beneficial innovations, misallocate resources, encourage defensive regulation, etc.), and some of those harms could be serious or irreversible. So a consistent adherent should refuse to adopt the principle outright, or at best adopt it only provisionally and with the same suspicion the principle directs at everything else. Then say where the argument is weakest: is a decision rule an "activity" in the relevant sense, or does the principle only apply to first-order environmental or health decisions? And if the self-application goes through, does it refute the principle or just show you can't adopt it all at once without hypocrisy? Keep it tight.
