An instructor I work with teaches Hume's account of causation almost entirely through a single analogy. He says that for Hume, causal necessity is what color is on the Lockean picture: something the mind contributes rather than a feature of the world as it is in itself. All that exists in the objects, on his telling, is regular succession and contiguity among events. The further element — the felt compulsion, the sense that the second billiard ball must move when the first strikes it — is a determination of the mind, which the mind then projects onto the sequence of events, just as it projects color onto a surface whose intrinsic properties are only primary. He then presses the analogy into service against causal realism. Someone who asks whether there is a real necessary connection in nature, behind the regularity, is, he says, making the same mistake as someone who asks whether the wall remains red after every perceiver has gone. Both questions are supposed to dissolve once we see that necessity and color are contributions of the mind, leaving nothing to be a realist about.

I am not convinced, and I suspect the analogy is doing more work than it can bear — perhaps even supporting the opposite conclusion when pressed. Please give me a full audit of it. Begin with an explicit correspondence table, feature by feature. Then lay out the main disanalogies, and separate the ones that matter for the conclusion that necessity is mind-dependent from the ones that are merely incidental. In particular, scrutinize the Lockean side of the analogy: Locke held that a body's secondary qualities are grounded in real powers belonging to the body and its primary qualities, so that removing every perceiver does not remove the basis for color, even if the perceived redness does not resemble anything in the object. If that is right, does the analogy support the antirealist reading the instructor draws from it, or does it push toward a skeptical realist reading of Hume — a New Hume reading, on which there may be an unknowable ground of the regularity after all? Say whether the instructor's conclusion survives the disanalogies once they are on the table, and what a better analogy, if one is needed, would be.
