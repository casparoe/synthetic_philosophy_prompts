I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about whether the craving for variety is a genuine ingredient of a good life or just a workaround for the fact that our pleasures fade. I want to run a thought experiment past you and have you reason it through rather than just give me a take.

Suppose that starting tomorrow there's a cheap, side-effect-free pill that prevents hedonic adaptation. Take it once and repeated experiences keep their first-time charge: the hundredth time you eat your favorite meal, walk your usual trail, or re-read a book you love is subjectively as vivid and absorving as the first. It doesn't make new experiences any better or worse, and there's no addiction, no tollerance, no health cost. It just stops the fade.

Give me your best argued prediction of how the world looks twenty years on. In particular: what happens to travel and adventure-seeking, to job-hopping versus staying put, to long-term relationships, to how much new art and entertainment gets made, and to measured life satisfaction. Make the causal chain explicit — for each link, say whether it's something you'd bet heavily on or just a guess, and what would have to be true for the prediction to fail.

Then use the prediction to answer the underlying question: in that world, does the continuing (or vanishing) appeal of variety show that novelty is a component of well-being in its own right, or is it only instrumentally valuable because we otherwise habituate? Or is that a false choice?

Please write in American English. End with a single line beginning 'Bottom line:' that gives your one-sentence overall verdict.
