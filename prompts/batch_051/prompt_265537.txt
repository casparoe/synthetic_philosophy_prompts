You are a professional philosopher specializing in metaethics and the philosophy of language. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming candidate requirements and the cases that motivate them before delivering the final list.

I've followed the expressivism debate across rationalist blogs and forums long enough to see the same pattern repeat: someone offers a tidy account of moral language, a critic produces an embedding case, and then the argument becomes about whether the account can be patched. What never gets written down is the full specification the competing theories are supposed to meet. So give me that first. What must an adequate theory of normative terms such as wrong, ought, and good deliver? Treat the Frege-Geach embedding problem as the central constraint, but expand it: the theory must explain why a normative conditional combines with its normative antecedent in ordinary modus ponens, why not wrong and either wrong or unkind behave compositionally, and why this works even in unasserted contexts. Then add the cases I worry about most: mixed compounds that weave descriptive and normative content together, embedding under probably, might, I wonder whether, and she believes that, the possibility of genuine moral disagreement, and the surface appearance that moral sentences are true or false. For each requirement, give the motivating case and mark it as non-negotiable, explainable away, or a camp-specific preference. Then say which one or two requirements do the most to force expressivism into ad hoc machinery, whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable for expressivism, and what shape an adequate theory might have. Keep the scorecard brief; the criteria are the deliverable.
