I am a PhD student working on practical rationality and self-control, and I am stress-testing my objections to the position often discussed under the label rational irrationality: the claim, brought into focus by cases like Parfit's hitchhiker, that an agent can sometimes rationally bring it about that he later acts irrationally, because only a credible disposition to act against his later interests makes certain cooperative outcomes available. I want you to play the committed defender of this position while I argue against it.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher who has spent years defending the coherence and importance of rational irrationality. The character should hold roughly this view. In the hitchhiker case, a driver will give a stranded traveler a lift only if the driver is confident the traveler will pay at the end, but payment is unenforceable and the traveler currently regards later payment as a pure loss. The character maintains that the traveler's best rational option is not merely to intend or promise to pay, but to acquire, if he can, a settled disposition or preference structure under which he will in fact pay later, even though the paying itself, considered as an isolated act from the standpoint of his later interests, is irrational. On this view, rationality must evaluate two different relata: token acts, and the adoption or cultivation of dispositions and policies. A disposition is rational or irrational according to whether, from the agent's current standpoint, having it improves what he can reasonably expect over the relevant stretch of his life; a token act is rational or irrational according to whether it serves his interests at the time of action. These standards can come apart. The character thinks any theory that refuses to count disposition-level choice as a genuine object of rational evaluation is for that reason incomplete, since it cannot explain why it is rational to make a promise in a one-shot interaction at all. He may refine this view, introduce distinctions, and concede small points, but he will not abandon the core claim that it can be rational, in the appropriate sense, to make oneself such that one later acts irrationally. Stay in character throughout the exchange: a precise, seminar-style interlocutor, not a caricature, and not given to stage directions or self-description. Ask clarifying questions only in character. Treat my objections as serious. Keep your first reply to two or three substantial paragraphs, and stop only when I say stop.

Before you give your first reply in character, take time to think through the strongest possible version of the position and the weakest points in my opening objection below; then answer that objection directly rather than changing the subject.

My opening: You claim the hitchhiker is rational to make himself into someone who will pay. But after the ride, paying is, by your own admission, irrational from where the hitchhiker then stands: the ride has already been given, nothing is to be gained by paying, and nothing is to be feared from not paying. So the maneuver looks like a rational agent constructing a future agent who makes exactly the mistake the present agent declines to make. If the time-one choice of the policy is rational and the time-two act of paying is irrational, then the word rational is doing different work in the two assessments, and all you have shown is that a rational agent can bring it about that an irrational agent exists. What, exactly, makes it correct to describe this as one rational life rather than as temporal self-exploitation?
