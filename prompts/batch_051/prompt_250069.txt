I've been covering the "epistemic dependence on AI" beat for a while now — the worry that outsourcing our routine reasoning to chatbots quietly erodes the exact understanding that work was supposed to build, use-it-or-lose-it style. Last week I interviewed someone from a small policy-research outfit that's piloting a rule they call AI escrow, and I can't decide whether it's genuinely clever or just a machine for gaming. The mechanism: staffers can use an LLM as much as they want, but every query gets logged alongside a two-sentence note saying what the staffer thinks they would have done without the model — e.g. "I'd have gone back to the panel-data handbook and probably fumbled the parallel-trends plot." Every quarter, about ten percent of logged queries are drawn at random. The staffer then has one hour, no AI and no notes, to reconstruct the reasoning behind that query's answer in front of a small panel of peers. If the reconstruction is thin, the whole team's AI budget for the next quarter is cut; if they nail it, the team gets extra budget or some shared perk. The pitch is that random auditing can't be gamed in advance and team-level stakes make people police each other. I'm skeptical.

Before I write the piece, I want a cold-eyed walk-through of how this would actually behave. What does it reward on the ground, as opposed to on paper? Where does it get gamed first — logged queries that are conveniently reconstructible, notes written to be unfalsifiable, shadow AI use outside the log, panel politics? What happens to the mix of questions people are willing to run through the model at all? And can any of the obvious fixes survive, or does the whole thing collapse into audit theater?
