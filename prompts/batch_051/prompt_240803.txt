I have a rule I have been following for years and I want someone to check it. Whenever my evidence doesn't settle things outright — a blurry photo that might be of my car, a half-remembered story from a friend, a doctor saying it's probably nothing but she can't be sure — I refuse to update directly on the murky content. I never do Jeffrey conditionalization, where you redistribute your probability over the live possibilities and keep the conditional probabilities fixed. The reason I don't trust it is that it asks me for input probabilities. If I moved my credence that the photo is of my car from .2 to .7, where would the .7 come from? I'd just be picking a number, and I don't think you can get an update out of a free choice. So instead I condition, with certainty, on the proposition that I had exactly this visual experience (or that my friend said exactly these words), and let my old likelihoods do the work.

A friend from my philosophy of probability seminar says I'm throwing information away. He says the content of the blurry photo is probabilistically relevant, and my policy turns it into the cheaper fact that a photo happened to look a certain way to me. He says the whole point of Jeffrey's rule is to let evidence be genuinely uncertain without pretending I learned some proposition for certain. Is my rule defensible, or is it just a way of refusing to let a rule tell me what to believe? Is there a version of it that keeps the good parts without the stubborness? Be honest on both sides. And tell me how confident you are in what you claim, and which specific point you're least sure of.
