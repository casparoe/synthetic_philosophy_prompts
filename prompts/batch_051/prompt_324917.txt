For a reading group I'm writing up a personal position statement on Gould and Lewontin's 1979 spandrels piece, and I'd like a consistency check before I show it to the others. The passage that keeps nagging at me is from the section where they describe the adaptationist programme: they say, roughly, that the adaptationist approach treats an organism as if it could be broken into separate traits, each optimally shaped by selection, when really organisms are integrated wholes and many features are side consequences of how they are built. I know I'm paraphrasing badly, but that's the part I have in mind.

My current commitments:

(1) Natural selection is the only known process that can produce complex, coordinated, function-specific phenotypes, so for a trait like that, "selection shaped it" is a reasonable default.

(2) I find the architectural-constraint/byproduct point from that section largely convincing: you can't atomize an organism into separately optimized bits.

(3) But I also think very few adaptive hypotheses in the literature are actually subjected to serious non-adaptive null models; most people compare a trait only to a strawman and declare selection the winner.

(4) I'm broadly adaptationist about cognition. Language, face recognition, and cheater detection look like decent adaptation candidates, and their early, reliable, complex, functional development seems like real evidence for selection.

(5) I don't think all is adaptation—there are genuine spandrels—but I also think Gould and Lewontin were sometimes unfair to the more cautious post-1960s adaptationists.

I want to know whether this package hangs together. Which pairs are in real tension, which only look like they are, and how severe are the real ones? For each tension, what is the cheapest fix and what does it cost? If I'm using "adaptation" in more than one sense, point that out. Keep the response to at most 450 words.
