I have been circling a gap between the philosophical discussion of akrasia and the behavioral-economics treatment of hyperbolic discounting, and I would like a candid assessment of the way I have been trying to close it. The standard picture is something like this: hyperbolic discounting explains weakness of will by letting a person's momentary evaluation of an option shift as the delay shrinks, so that the smaller sooner reward overtakes the larger later one, while her considered judgment stays put. Akrasia then consists in choosing against that standing judgment. I have come to think that many of the clearest cases have a different structure, and I have been calling the difference judgment lag.

The idea is that the considered judgment is not a fixed reference point that desire moves relative to; it is itself time-indexed and trails behind desire. When a temptation becomes vivid or imminent, the agent's first-order valuation of it rises on the usual present-biased curve, but the pull also bends her all-things-considered judgment — just more slowly. At the moment of choice she does not merely want the tempting option while still holding that she should refrain. She briefly judges, often sincerely and with reasons, that the tempting option is what she should do; the very standard by which she would later condemn the choice has been temporarily re-authored. Once the immediacy passes or the cost lands, the judgment swings back, and she disowns both the action and the interim judgment that licensed it, saying she was not herself or that the standard had simply gone missing. A dieter confronted with dessert does not just feel that the dessert is now worth more to her; she becomes momentarily convinced that the diet was excessive and that eating the dessert is the reasonable thing, all things considered. An hour later the earlier judgment returns, and she cannot quite make sense of how the later one felt so true.

I want to know whether this is coherent or just a confused re-description of the familiar phenomenon. Is it already worked out in the literature under a better name? I can see it is adjacent to the idea of hot-cold empathy gaps and to the thought that temptation infects belief as well as desire, but I have not seen the point posed as a systematic lag in second-order evaluation. What would the strongest, most charitable version of the theory say, and where does it break? In particular, does it multiply parameters only to save the appearances, and what observational evidence could separate genuine judgment lag from ordinary post-hoc rationalization?
