I work on the standards team of a seafood certification body — mostly farmed salmon and trout, shrimp, and increasingly crab and lobster producers that want a "higher welfare" label. We don't really have an animal-welfare standard yet: auditors verify water chemistry, stocking density, disease records, and then just wing it whenever a welfare question comes up, so two farms doing the same thing get different rulings depending on which reviewer looked at which paper. I need to turn whatever is actually defensible about sentience and precaution into a proceedure our auditors can run on any new proposal — a slaughter method, a handling protocol, a physical intervention — in about an hour. Not a position paper. A reusable flowchart in words, with the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a human because the question is values rather than facts. Our auditors are not scientists and are used to binary findings.

You're a philosopher who works on animal minds and on decision-making under uncertainty. Start by brainstorming the ways a producer could game the checklist and the ways an auditor could mechanically misapply it, then design the procedure around those failure modes. Be explicit about the distinctions an auditor must not flatten — nociception (reflex withdrawal, gill flare, escape attempts) versus felt pain versus suffering — and about the two ways to be wrong: certifying a practice that actually causes pain, versus banning or restricting a practice on a false-positive about sentience. I also need you to deal with the expected-value point that farmed crustacean operations run to hundreds of millions of individuals per year, so even a pretty low probability of sentience might swamp an auditor's gut judgment about how much evidence is enough. Then run the finished procedure on three actual hot spots: (1) a shrimp hatchery that wants permission to keep doing unilateral eyestalk ablation on female broodstock — cutting or cauterizing one eyestalk to force spawning, with no analgesia; (2) a salmon producer that wants to call immersion in CO2-saturated water a pre-slaughter "stun" because it's cheaper than a percussive stunner and the workers hate handling fish; (3) a crab and lobster holding facility that keeps animals packed on ice or in near-freezing air because it makes them docile and cuts limb loss, and insists there's no credible evidence crustaceans feel anything.

Be honest about where the procedure breaks down. It should force us to refuse, or at least seriously restrict, something we currently certify if that's what the reasoning actually supports, and I want you to tell me that plainly even though my board will hate it. Also tell me where an auditor is most likely to mechanically misapply the procedure, and which steps only look technical but are actually sneaking in a value judgment. No reading list or citations — I need something we can use while a producer is pushing back.
