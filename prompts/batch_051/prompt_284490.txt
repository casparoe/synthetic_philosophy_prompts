I am a PhD student working on causal inference and its applications to process monitoring, and I keep running into a problem that feels more conceptual than statistical. The systems I study, such as predictive-maintenance alarms on industrial equipment, fraud-scoring pipelines, and early-warning scores in hospitals, are built on the assumption that there is some normal underlying mechanism generating the data and that an anomaly is a deviation from that mechanism. Yet every such system also presupposes a rule for deciding when the model should be re-fit rather than alarmed. If a plant replaces a pump with a different manufacturer's model, or a bank changes its fee schedule, the input-output relations shift. Is that the same mechanism operating in new conditions, or a new mechanism altogether? In practice, engineers make this judgment informally, and whole maintenance and compliance workflows depend on the answer. I would like you to reconstruct what this practice presupposes about the identity conditions of a mechanism: the practice appears to presuppose that a mechanism can persist across changes in its physical components and its observed input-output mapping, but also that it can give way to a distinct mechanism, and it seems to waver among individuating mechanisms by physical parts, by functional or causal role, and by stable conditional distributions. Be precise about what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits comfortably with, say whether it rests on one coherent criterion or on several incompatible ones, and explain what a monitoring system would look like if it consistently adopted a purely functional criterion, a purely component-based criterion, or a purely statistical criterion. I want this written as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a letter.
