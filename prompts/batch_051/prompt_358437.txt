Give me a working definition of loyalty — not a dictionary gloss, but the kind of thing that would let someone classify cases they haven't seen before. Be explicit about it's moving parts: is loyalty an emotion, a disposition, a norm you hold yourself to, or all three? Is it something you can have toward an institution or an idea, or only toward people? Does loyalty require staying when you've lost faith, or can leaving still count as loyal?

Now run that definition through each of the following, and for each say (i) whether it comes out loyal, disloyal, or neither, (ii) why, and (iii) whether that verdict seems right.

- A lab tech who's worked somewhere 15 years and stays quiet about a fudged result because the lab director is also her mentor.
- A different tech who reports that fudged result to a journal editor.
- A fan who switches to a rival team after her favorite player is traded.
- A spouse who stays after an affair and insists the marriage still matters.
- A juror who votes to convict a friend because the evidence is overwhelming.
- A soldier who refuses an illegal order and reports it up the chain.
- A kid who calls the police on a parent who has been hitting a sibling.
- Someone who keeps a dead friend's secret from the friend's family.
- A professor who leaves for a better job and brings her PhD students with her.
- A politician who votes for a party bill she privately thinks is harmful.
- A customer who switches phone carriers because it's cheaper.

Where your definition gives a verdict that seems wrong, or just goes silent, say so and either revise it or defend the surprising verdict. Then tell me which one or two of those verdicts you'd least want to bet on, and whether the cases leave you thinking loyalty is one thing or a family of things sharing a word. Also say whether the definition leaves room for the thought that someone can be disloyal and still be in the right, or whether it builds a positive verdict into the word from the start.
