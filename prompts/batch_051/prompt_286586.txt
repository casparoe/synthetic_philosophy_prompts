A core epistemic habit in the Burkean tradition is that one should not tear down an inherited institution until one can say what it is for and why it was put there—Chesterton’s fence. I have been defending a version of this to policy audiences: a proposal to overturn a long-standing social arrangement carries a burden to explain the arrangement’s function before it is abolished, and one is entitled to keep the arrangement while that explanation remains absent. But I am worried that this slides from an epistemic rule about inquiry into a disguised substantive conservatism, and I want the slide tested against a hard case. Rousseau’s Letter to M. d’Alembert on the Theatre, written to argue against establishing a theater in Geneva, seems to supply the test. He writes:

> On our stage, everything is done by halves. The actor, as I have said, excites the emotions that he does not feel. The spectator, in turn, feels emotions that he cannot act upon. If one pities the wretched, one has no obligation to relieve them; if one admires the virtuous, one has no obligation to imitate them. The heart is moved, the will remains unchanged.

The passage suggests that what drama does is not to make people choose badly but to train them in a form of responsiveness that detaches feeling from action—and that the real danger is that a city of spectators will end up mistaking its own moral sensitivity for civic engagement. My rule would ask the Genevans to identify the function of their existing restraint on theater before lifting it. Is that the same as endorsing the restraint? Or does the rule only force the reformer to say what hidden cost the old arrangement was absorbing, and then leave the practical choice open? At what point does the demand to explain the fence become indistinguishable from a view that fences are generally justified by the mere fact of having lasted? Especially because one of the most common functions of inherited restraints may be to preserve habits of restraint, which are precisely the things a careful cost–benefit calculation is likely to undercount. I want a nuanced assessment of how much of Rousseau’s argument such a rule could genuinely absorb, and where Burkean caution turns into a different kind of unmotivated optimism about the past.
