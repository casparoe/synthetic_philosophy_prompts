In a reading group on scientific methodology, a physicist defended the idea that elegance is evidence by drawing an analogy with data compression. His claim was this: choosing among competing theories is like choosing among programs that each reproduce the same data set; the shorter program deserves more confidence, because a compact description is less likely to match the data by coincidence and is more likely to reflect the structure that actually produced it. On his account, the pleasure mathematicians and physicists take in an elegant argument is a trained sensitivity to exactly this property. Elegance is an imperfect felt proxy for compressibility, and compressibility is a genuine reason to believe a theory or theorem is getting at something real rather than fitting accidents. He treated this as a general license for taking simplicity and beauty as evidence in mathematics and in science, while conceding that aesthetic feelings are noisy indicators.

I find the analogy seductive but suspicious, and I would like you to audit how far it actually holds. Please give the explicit mapping first: what corresponds to the file, the encoding scheme, the compressed representation, and the accuracy of decompression when the analogy is applied to theory choice in science or to mathematical proof? Then mark the places where the correspondence strains or breaks. In particular, address whether elegance is really the same quantity as compression-based simplicity; whether the argument depends on the true theory being among the candidates; how it copes with finite and noisy data, overfitting, and the fact that many short theories can fit the same facts; and whether the conclusion (that a felt sense of elegance is evidence of truth) survives once these disanalogies are on the table. If part of the argument can be salvaged, specify which part, and state what a more defensible version of the compression analogy would and would not claim.
