For an article I am writing about infinite regress arguments, I need a sharp, concrete illustration of the difference between a vicious regress and a benign one. I would rather not fall back on the familiar cosmological material—first causes, unmoved movers, an endless stack of turtles—because readers have encountered it so often that they miss the structural question. Please supply a paired example, drawn from epistemology or the philosophy of mind, in which two chains of dependence of the same general form—each item of a certain kind requiring a further item of that kind—produce different verdicts: one chain looks impossible or ungrounded, while the other looks intellectually unproblematic. In spelling out the example, please explain what is doing the work. Is the regress vicious when the series must actually terminate for the target item to exist, be justified, or be possessed, and benign when the series can instead be discharged holistically, by mutual support, or by an account that never promised a first member? The example should be precise enough to be used in a magazine piece, and it should show a reader why the bare observation that a chain never ends is not by itself an objection.
