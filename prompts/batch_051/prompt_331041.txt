Quick probability exercise. A labor economist has a big observational dataset with years of schooling and adult wages, plus a bunch of controls — test scores, parents' income, region, that kind of thing. She draws the obvious causal graph, assumes there are no unmeasured confounders left once she conditions on those controls, and then uses the backdoor adjustment from Pearl's do-calculus (the intervention layer of his causal hierarchy) to estimate the average causal effect of adding one extra year of schooling on log wages. I want your honest credence — a number between 0 and 1 — that her estimate actually equals the true average causal effect. Dont just say it depends. Decompose the number into the main things it rides on, something like: (a) the probability her DAG actually gets the confounder–mediator–collider structure right; (b) the probability that after conditioning on her controls there really is no unmeasured common cause, like 'grit' or family culture or whatever, pushing around both schooling and wages; (c) the probability that the do-calculus even has a clean intervention to refer to here, given that 'make someone get one more year of schooling' is a fuzzy policy bundle rather than a surgical manipulation; and (d) the probability there's no serious functional-form misspecification or selection problem silently wrecking the estimate. Add any other terms you think carry weight. Put a rough probability on each, show how you combine them, and then tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to, and what kind of evidence would move it most. Please write the whole thing in American English.
