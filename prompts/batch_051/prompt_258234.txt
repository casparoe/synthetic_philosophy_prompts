I'm taking a seminar on gender, science, and social explanation, and we've been circling the gender-equality paradox in educational and occupational choice. As I understand the empirical pattern, several large cross-national comparisons report that countries scoring higher on standard gender-equality indices (attitudes, labor force participation, political representation, sometimes controlling for GDP) tend to show larger, not smaller, sex differences in the share of men and women entering STEM-related tertiary fields, with the Nordic countries often cited as the striking case. The seminar keeps oscillating between people who read this as evidence that sex-typed preferences are substantially innate, people who read it as evidence that gender-egalitarian societies merely remove economic constraints and let supposedly authentic gendered preferences show up, and people who think the whole paradox is an artifact of how STEM is defined, how equality is measured, or what is being compared across countries. Before I commit to any of those readings, I want to step back and fix what a minimally adequate account of this phenomenon would have to get right, independently of which reading one initially favors.

So: write me the desiderata for an adequate theory (or debunking explanation) of the gender-equality paradox in career choice. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it and say whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp. I particularly want you to address: the distinction between a pattern in preferences and a pattern in constraints or opportunities; the fact that STEM is a heterogeneous bundle (engineering versus medicine or the life sciences, where the sex differences often differ or reverse); the problem of whether cross-national comparisons compare like with like in what counts as a STEM degree, what counts as gender equality, and what the relevant baseline is; the difference between explaining the STEM gap and explaining the broader fact that some fields have large sex differences in either direction; and the distinction between how a preference is formed, how freely it is expressed, and how stable it is under counterfactual institutional changes. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement you think does the most to exclude popular readings.
