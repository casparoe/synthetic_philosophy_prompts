I am a legislative drafter in a local authority, and an episode from my work has made a philosophical question suddenly practical. Over the past year I have been revising our building-safety rules, and I kept finding that a provision requiring smoke alarms in rented dwellings had a history but no author. It began somewhere, was copied almost verbatim by other councils, was amended after a court decision, and the amended wording—not the original—has become the version new drafters copy. I have since traced several identifiable lineages of the clause, including a corrupt version in one council, produced by a copying error, which has now been inherited by two neighbouring councils. Some variants spread because they are shorter or because they have survived litigation; others sit unchanged in superseded codes and are copied no further.

I want to know whether this clause is a meme in anything other than a metaphorical sense. My working criteria for a literal Darwinian replicator are these: (1) replication, in that a copy inherits its structure from a source rather than arriving at the same structure independently; (2) copying fidelity, enough that lineages can be traced; (3) variation, meaning copies sometimes differ from their source; (4) differential fitness, meaning variants differ in their rate of being copied as a function of their properties and their environment; and (5) heredity, meaning that a variant's differences are preserved in later copies. By these criteria the clause appears to qualify. Yet I cannot shake the suspicion that the Darwinian vocabulary is doing no work: the copying is deliberate, the variants are edited by lawyers who understand their meaning, and any 'selection' seems to piggyback on the judgments of insurers, courts, and officials. A gene need not be understood to replicate; this clause seems to reproduce only through understanding and institutional authority.

Go through the case criterion by criterion, and tell me whether it qualifies as a replicator in the strong sense. Where the criteria return a yes, say whether that means the criteria are too permissive; where they return a no, say whether the obstacle lies in the criteria or in the case. Above all, I want to know whether 'meme' is doing real explanatory work here, or whether the evolutionary language merely redescribes the ordinary facts about legal borrowing without adding anything. Please answer in British English.
