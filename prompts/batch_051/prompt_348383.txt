ok so I've been chewing on Pascals wager for a while and wrote a short draft thinking through a couple objections. I'd love comments on the draft, specially on whether the reasoning holds up and where its weakest. I dont care about style, more about the argument. Also if I misstate what Pascal actually says please flag it.

Here's my draft:

Pascal says you should believe in God because the payoffs are lopsided. If you believe and God exists, you get infinite happiness. If you believe and God doesnt exist, you loose only some worldly pleasures. If you dont believe and God exists, you get infinite misery. If you dont believe and there is no God, you gain only worldly pleasures. So the expected value of believing is enormous, and you should bet on God.

I think the wager is often dismissed for the wrong reasons. People say you cant force yourself to believe. But Pascal himself seems to say you should act as if you believe, go to mass, take holy water, and belief will follow. So the wager is really a strategy for changing your own habits, not a command to flip a switch in your head. That seems more psychologically realistic to me.

But I have a deeper worry. The wager assumes God rewards belief, or at least rewards the person who makes the bet. What if God punishes people who only believe out of self-interest? Then the payoff table flips. Pascal seems to assume God values faith, but many religious traditions also say God values sincerity. If sincerity matters, then the person who calculates expected utility and then acts religious to get a reward might not count as sincere. So the wager might be self-defeating: the more you use it as a reason to believe, the less your belief is the sincere kind that God would reward.

Maybe Pascal could reply that the wager isnt meant to produce perfect sincerity, just to get you started. Its like going to the gym even when you dont feel like it; eventually the habit changes you. But that only works if God cares about the final state, not the original motive. Is that a coherent picture of God? I'm not sure.

Another issue is the many gods problem. The wager only works if there's one possible god with infinite payoff. But if there are many incompatible gods, each with infinite reward for their own followers, then the expected utility of each option is infinite, so decision theory cant choose. Some people try to fix this by saying you should pick the god with the highest probability or the most evidence. But then the wager is doing no work, because you're back to evaluating evidence. Others say infinity makes the math break down and maybe you need something like lexicographic utilities or infinitesimals. I havent worked through that and would love to know if that's a real fix or just formal handwaving.

I guess my main question is whether the wager is a rational argument for monotheism, or whether it only works if you already accept a specific theological picture that does all the heavy lifting. If the latter, is it still interesting as a piece of decision theory, or just a historical curiosity? I'd appreciate comments that push on the strongest version of the wager, not the straw man.
