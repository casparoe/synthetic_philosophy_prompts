I spent most of my career as an experimental physicist, and since retiring I've been reading philosophy, where I keep recognizing old measurement problems under new names—construct validity, proxies, response biases, that kind of thing. Lately I've been stuck on the World Happiness Report because a friend insists countries should just try to climb its ranking, and I found myself giving him an argument that felt airtight in the moment but has bugged me ever since. Heres the argument, more or less as I made it: (1) you can't rationally treat a ranking as a policy target unless the ranking actually measures the thing you want more of; (2) when we say we want a country to be happier, what we want is more felt happiness in people's day-to-day lives; (3) the headline ranking is based on national averages of people's answers to the Cantril ladder question, which asks them to rate their lives from 0 to 10 against what they imagine as the best and worst possible life for them; (4) rating your life on a ladder is a reflective judgment about how your life is going overall, and that's a different psychological quantity from how happy you feel day to day—the two can and do come apart; (5) therefore the ranking does not measure the thing in step (2), and a government that aims to top it is chasing the wrong target.

I have a nagging suspicion the trick is in step (4), that I've drawn the distinction too sharply, but I cant make it precise. I also know the report's authors fit the ladder averages against six country-level variables—GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceived corruption—and I can't tell whether that strengthens the measurement or just explains variance after the fact. So: is the argument valid? Is each premise true? If a step fails, say exactly which one, give the strongest reading under which it is true, and then say whether the conclusion survives once you repair or replace the weak step. And please mark off which parts of this are settled among people who study subjective well-being and which parts are live disputes—dont present a contested view as the consensus.
