I've been arguing with a colleague about whether cultural items—tunes, jokes, knot-tying techniques, pieces of software—literally replicate, or whether 'meme' is just a vivid metaphor for something better described as reconstruction. The replication camp says that when a variant spreads, later instances stand to earlier ones in a parent–offspring relation: copying transmits a large fraction of the earlier instance's structure, copying errors produce heritable variation, and variants that are copied more leave more descendants. The other camp says this misdescribes the psychology: people do not photocopy what they encounter; they infer a gist, transform it to fit their aims and prior knowledge, and similarities across individuals often arise because many reconstructions converge on the same salient, memorable, or useful form. On that view, what look like lineages are mostly independent arrivals at shared attractors.

Treat this as a partly empirical question, not purely a dispute about how to use the word 'replicate'. Design the strongest test you can of the claim that a particular cultural variant—say a short phrase, an origami fold, or a snippet of code—actually replicates through a lineage of copying rather than being independently reconstructed toward an attractor at each step. Specify the observation or experiment, the result that would favor each reading, and the controls needed to rule out mere convergence on a common form. Then say honestly what the test would and would not settle: if the replication-favoring result came out, would it establish that 'meme' is more than a metaphor, or only that this variant happens to be copied with high fidelity? Are there parts of the replication claim that no finite body of observations could settle, and is that because the notion itself is not fully empirical or because we simply have not been clever enough yet?
