I'm teaching Denett's "Where Am I?" story in an intro philosophy of mind class, and I keep noticing that students will casually say the man is obviously wherever his excised brain is, then treat the visit to the vat as a funhouse moment rather than a real conceptual problem. I want you to make an argument for the opposite view: that in the simple radio-link version the subject is not literally located at the vat, and that the apparent location is a feature of the control loop rather than the self. Argue that moving from "I act through this body" to "I am in this body" or "I am in that vat" is a slide, and that Dennett's point-of-view reasoning is question-begging because it starts from the felt location of first-person perspective. Use the setup: the body is still walking around, the brain only causally links to the body, the subject's perceptual field is the body's surroundings rather than the vat's, and the brain in the vat is as hidden from the subject as it would be in a locked cabinet. Also anticipate the strongest reply: if you stabbed the brain, the subject would shut down, so the brain must be where he is, or at least causally central. End by saying what this implies about teaching the puzzle—should the first-day intuition be pushed toward a distributed self, or toward agnosticism about location? Make the case, don't just rehearse the story.
