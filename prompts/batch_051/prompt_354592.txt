I cover ideas for a living, and I was at a philosophy festival last month sitting in on a small session on American pragmatism when two panelists got into it over a single Peirce line. One of them, Mara, read this out from C.S. Peirce's "How to Make Our Ideas Clear": "Never to fail to recognize an idea, and under no circumstances to mistake another for it, let it come in how recondite a form it may, would indeed imply such prodigious force and clearness of intellect as is seldom met with in this world." Mara said this is Peirce doing something much more radical than people realize: he is saying the whole Cartesian standard of clearness is basically just a subjective feeling of mastery, and the only way out is to define our ideas by their conceivable practical effects. Dan pushed back: that's a point about meaning, she's smuggling in a theory of truth. Peirce's actual account of truth is about the opinion that inquiry would settle on in the long run, not about what the idea does for you now. James, Dan said, took Peirce's method and psychologized it until truth became whatever it pays you to believe. Mara came back: that's a caricature of James. When James says truth happens to an idea, he's not saying private satisfaction makes things true. He's saying truth is a property of the verification process, and Peirce's later re-branding to pragmaticism was mostly about naming, not a deep theoretical break. Dan answered that James's own examples from religious and moral life do license going beyond the evidence when the belief helps you live, and no amount of fine language about verification gets around that. They turned to me and asked what I thought, and I said I'd need to think about it. So here I am. Adjudicate the actual dispute: who reads Peirce's passage correctly, who reads James correctly, and where exactly does this stop being about interpretation and become a real disagreement about truth? I'm not asking for a survey of the field, I want a point-by-point ruling on the arguments they actually made.
