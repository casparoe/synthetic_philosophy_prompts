I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of language, about twenty students, and this term we are working through the question of what it takes for something to count as a language. The unit is built around sign language as the central test: I want the students to see why established sign languages are fully languages and not gesture-plus-pantomime or manual translations of spoken languages, but I also want them to see that the obvious ways of stating what a language is tend either to let in things we do not want to count as languages or to exclude things we do. In our last meeting the class tried the criterion that a language is a conventional system for expressing thoughts, and it counted ASL in, but it also counted Morse code in and gave no real guidance about music or mathematical notation. I would rather hand them a single worked-out definition than a menu of criteria, so I am asking for one.

Here are the cases I care about, roughly from the ones where I am most confident to the ones where I am least confident. An established national sign language such as ASL or BSL should clearly count. Tactile signing as used by DeafBlind signers should clearly count, just in another modality. An emerging village sign language only a couple of generations old should also count, even though its community is small and its history short. A writing system, such as English orthography or Chinese characters, seems to me to be a notation for a language rather than itself a language. A code such as Morse code should not count even though it can transmit any message. Home sign, the gesture system invented by a single deaf child of non-signing hearing parents and used mainly within that family, worries me most on the sign-language side: it plainly has structure, but I suspect it is not a full language, and I need the definition to say why. Vervet monkey alarm calls are referential, but I take it they are not a language. The honeybee waggle dance is informative but not a language. Music is a genuine borderline case for me: it has complex structure and expressive power, but something important is different. Mathematical notation, such as the symbolic apparatus of algebra or predicate logic, is productive and compositional, yet it may be parasitic on natural language; I would like a definite verdict.

Please propose a definition of language that gets these cases right, run it through each of them, and say where it delivers a surprising verdict or no verdict; then either revise the definition or defend the surprises. Tell me which of your verdicts is the least secure and what sort of case would force you to change the definition. If you think the exercise is misframed — because language is a family-resemblance concept that will not bear this sort of definition, or because some of my cases depend on a confusion, or because my ordering of the cases already answers the question I am asking — say so directly and tell me how the unit should be run instead. I would rather give the students a well-motivated diagnosis than a false definition.
