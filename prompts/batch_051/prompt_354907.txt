I'm in a metaphysics of probability seminar and we keep sliding between treating 'possible' as a modal notion and 'probable' as a quantitative one, and I honestly think half the room is using them interchangably. For a revision handout I want to make a 'which view of the possibility/probability link do you actually hold?' quiz — the kind of thing that sorts people by what they say about cases, not by which label sounds best to them.

Can you write me ten questions, each one a concrete case with two or three options: a ticket in a million-ticket fair lottery; a dart hitting an exact point on a board, which has probability zero under the usual continuous model; someone who says 'it might rain' after seeing a forecast that gives 10% versus one that gives 90%; an infinite sequence of fair coin flips that comes up all heads; a coin with an unknown but fixed bias, where you and a friend with the same evidence disagree about how likely heads is; a fair coin that has just come up tails twenty times in a row and someone says heads is 'due'; someone assigning a credence of 0.7 to a mathematical conjecture that is either necessarily true or necessarily false; and anything else you think does real diagnostic work. None of the questions should literally ask 'do you think possible just means nonzero probability?' — they should be answerable by someone who has never seen the labels.

Then give a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the main live positions: possibility as basic, with probability as a measure over already-given possible outcomes; probability as basic, with 'possible' defined as positive probability; epistemic possibility as compatibility with evidence and nothing to do with objective chance; objective chance as the underlying thing, with 'possible' left as a separate notion so that probability-zero events can still be genuine possibilities; and a deflationary view that the two notions answer different questions and shouldn't be unified. Flag which pairs of positions your ten questions can't actually seperate, and for the combinations of answers that are logically possible but that nobody seems to hold, say whether that's because they're unstable, just undefended, or open territory.
