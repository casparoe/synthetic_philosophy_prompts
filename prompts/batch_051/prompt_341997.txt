I'm a science and ideas journalist working on a piece about how unwritten workplace rules harden into things people treat as real regulations even though nobody ever enacted them. I keep coming back to David Lewis's account of convention. As I understand it, a convention is a regularity R in the behavior of a group in some recurring situation S, where nearly everyone conforms to R, nearly everyone expects nearly everyone else to conform, each person prefers to conform given those expectations, there is some alternative regularity R' that would have served just as well, and all of that is common knowledge among them.

Here is the case tripping me up. At a small maintenance hangar for prop planes, mechanics have for decades walked clockwise around an aircraft as they work on it. There is no sign, no manual rule, no training bullet point. New hires get corrected until it becomes second nature. Physically, counterclockwise would work just as well; the point is only that people should not collide while carrying tools. The old-timers are pure Lewisians: they would happily switch to counterclockwise tomorrow if everyone else did; they just dont want to be the one outlier crossing traffic.

But a growing number of the younger mechanics believe the clockwise rule is an actual shop or FAA rule. They would report a counterclockwise walker, and they say they would not switch even if the rest of the crew started going counterclockwise, because they think it is prohibited. They have never seen anyone walk the other way exept as a violation. So the same practice is now sustained by two very different attitudes.

Is the hangar's clockwise circulation still a convention on Lewis's account? Go criterion by criterion and be honest about where the criteria underdetermine the answer. Does this case show Lewis's account misses how conventions can decay into pseudo-rules — or is the younger mechanics' attitude just another layer of convention? And for my piece, should I call this a convention, a norm, or something else?
