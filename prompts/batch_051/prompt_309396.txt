I hold that the distinction between doing and allowing has no intrinsic moral weight. More precisely, I think the distinction is a useful but coarse heuristic: it tracks differences in certainty, in the cost the agent must bear, in the existence and strength of underlying duties, in whether the agent has taken on a special responsibility, and in whether the causal relation passes through another person's choice. But once those factors are fixed, the bare fact that a death is produced by an act rather than by an omission never contributes independent justificatory force. I want to stress what this does and does not claim. I do not hold that killing and letting die are always morally equivalent in practice. I do not hold that a physician who cancels a futile treatment is in the same position as someone who smothers a patient. I hold that where they differ, the difference is fully accounted for by these other factors, and that the act/omission boundary itself is not among the fundamental normative facts.

I would like you to map what would count against this view. What would have to be true, or what would have to be shown, for the doing/allowing distinction to earn a place as an unreduced normatively significant factor? I am asking for the defeaters: the possible cases, intuitions, arguments, formal results, and empirical findings that would bear on the position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. A committed defender of the distinction — someone who thinks that the duty not to kill is simply stronger than the duty to save, or that agency has a structure that makes the initiation of a harmful causal chain specially objectionable — what would they themselves concede as genuine counterevidence to their view? And what would they dismiss as already traceable to another factor, or as depending on a contested description of the case? I also want to know whether my position is in danger of being insulated from refutation by its qualifications, and if so, whether that is a defect for a view of this kind. Finally, distinguish which defeaters would move you personally and which you would resist, and say why.

To make the request concrete, consider how you would weigh at least some of the following: the difference between a physician who withdraws ventilator support from a stabilising but non-recovering patient and one who administers a paralytic; the difference between a bystander who throws a switch foreseeing one worker's death and one who fails to throw a switch that would save five; the difference between poisoning a patient's saline bag and failing to check an already contaminated bag despite having assumed responsibility for it; and the difference between an agent who remotely starts a lethal timer and one who knowingly allows a timer started by someone else to expire. I am less interested in verdicts on these cases individually than in which features of them would have to matter for the distinction itself to survive.
