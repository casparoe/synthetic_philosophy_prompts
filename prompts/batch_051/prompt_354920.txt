Peter Abelard argues in his Scito te ipsum that the whole moral wrong of a sin lies in the will's consent, and that the outward deed adds nothing to the guilt once the consent is present; the act is not a further stage of the sin but only the point at which an interior fault becomes manifest. Modern criminal law, however, punishes a completed murder more severely than an attempt that fails only because of circumstances outside the agent's control, even when the evidence shows the same deliberation and the same resolve. Take a legal system that also maintains, in other parts of its doctrine, that it punishes culpable choice rather than mere harm. What theory of moral responsibility does that sentencing practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separating what the practice strictly entails from what it merely fits well with. Then test it against Abelard's position: could he accept the sentencing difference without abandoning his claim that the sin is complete in consent alone, or would he have to diagnose the practice as inconsistent? Finish by stating what would have to change in the treatment of attempts if an Abelardian view of culpability were adopted consistently. Please write in British English.
