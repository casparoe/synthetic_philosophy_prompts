I'm a doctoral student in philosophical theology working on the impassibility debate, and I've come to think the debate is often less about God than about the word "suffering": the two sides seem to be classifying different kinds of states under one label, and the disagreement becomes merely verbal at exactly the point where it ought to become substantive. Before I take a position on whether God can suffer, I need a working definition of suffering that does not quietly settle the question on one side.

Give me such a definition—of "suffering," or, if you think it necessary, of the underlying notion of being affected or passible—and test it against the following cases:

(1) A child with a severe burn, in acute pain.
(2) A parent six months after the death of a child, no longer crying, but describing the world as flat, wrong, and uninhabitable.
(3) A creature that reflexively withdraws from tissue damage but which, by stipulation, has no conscious experience at all.
(4) A nurse who perceives a patient's agony vividly, is moved to relieve it, yet reports no aversive state of her own—no distress, only clear perception and concern.
(5) A researcher whose lifework is falsified in a single afternoon; she feels no pain but describes the event as a blow from which she has not recovered.
(6) A person in a severe melancholic depression who calls his state unbearable, though it has no object—he is not sad about anything in particular.
(7) A fictional character who, within the novel, is heartbroken.
(8) A bystander who chokes up and feels a pang while hearing a stranger describe a loss, though the loss is not hers.
(9) The God of classical theism, described as one who comprehensively knows every instance of creaturely suffering and responds with perfect love, but whose intrinsic state cannot, ex hypothesi, be altered by what is known.

I take (1) and (2) to be paradigm cases of suffering and (3) to be no case at all. I am unsure about (4), (5), (6), and (8); I suspect (7) involves represented suffering rather than suffering itself. I withhold judgment on (9) because I do not want a definition engineered from a prior verdict on the theological question.

Propose the definition, run it through all nine cases, and say explicitly where it disagrees with my provisional sorting—then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. If you think the word is doing two jobs (one picking out a kind of conscious state, one picking out a normative or evaluative status), give both and say which cases move between them. Finally, use the definition to restate what an impassibilist and a passibilist are really disagreeing about when they deny or affirm that God suffers: is it the facts about God, the concept of suffering, or the criterion for applying it?
