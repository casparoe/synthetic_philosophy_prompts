I'm a PhD student in political science, working on a chapter about how confederal institutions handle their own membership rules, and I keep coming back to a made-up case that my advisor thinks is contrived but I think exposes something real about what "joining" can mean. Suppose a regional confederation—a loose league of small neighboring principalities, city-states, and one or two composite kingdoms, the kind of thing that existed in eighteenth-century Europe—has a founding pact whose accession clause reads:

"Any independent State of these parts may be received into the Confederation with the assent of three quarters of the States assembled in Council; and every State so received shall for all purposes be a Member, bound by this Pact, and entitled to its full protection and privileges."

Now suppose one of the member kingdoms contains a large province that already has its own assembly, courts, and customs posts but no foreign policy. The province secedes. It controls its territory, runs its own militia, collects its own taxes, and has been recognized as a State by four of the confederation's members and by three outside powers—but the mother kingdom refuses to recognize it and calls the secession rebellion. The provincial government then applies for admission under the clause.

The drafters plainly had outside countries acceding voluntarily in mind, not a province splitting off from one of their own. I'm trying to work through whether the clause reaches this case. Does "independent State" make secession irrelevant—the province is now independant in fact, so it may join—or does the pact's background structure, a union of states that guarantee each other's territorial integrity, add an implicit condition that no candidate can be "independent" while an existing member denies its statehood? What would the clause's original purpose (presumably gathering strength against outside powers without meddling in members' internal order) do here, and what would the framers have said if, at the founding, they had been asked to write a secession proviso? And if the text, purpose, and framers' intent pull in different directions, which should a delegate actually follow when casting the admission vote? I'm less interested in how the vote should come out than in what each approach contributes: weigh the textual, purposive, and original-intent readings seperately, and say which should control and why.
