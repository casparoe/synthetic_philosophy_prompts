Something I keep going back and forth on from a lot our discussions in value theory. We treat truth-seeking as though it's valuable in itself, but tradition and purity as though they're only valuable if they do something for someone. Concrete version: if a physicist spends decades on a question everyone knows has no practical pay-off and won't change any decision, we call it admirable, even noble. But if a person keeps a purity practice — say, she won't eat from a pan that has ever touched a 'forbidden' food, while fully believing there's no health risk and no god who cares — we reach for words like compulsion, superstition, irrational. Both are following a rule that costs them something and produces nothing useful, and both say giving it up would feel like betraying themselves. Is there an actual principle that generates both judgments, or only a story about which people we like? Give me the strongest justification you can for the asymmetry, then stress-test it against a tradition case (someone keeping up a folk ritual that no one around them remembers the point of) and tell me whether the asymmetry should be kept, flattened, or reversed.
