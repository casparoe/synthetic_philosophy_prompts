I am an undergraduate representative on my university’s Academic Integrity Board and am currently taking a course on legal interpretation, with a unit on textualism and purposivism. The Board has asked me to draft the decision memorandum in a case whose facts are stipulated; the only contested matter is the interpretation of one provision of the University Code of Academic Conduct.

The Code’s general interpretive provision, Section 1.2, reads: “Each provision of this Code shall be construed according to the ordinary meaning of its words in context, and so as to advance the central educational purpose of assessment; provided, however, that no student shall be held responsible for conduct that a reasonable student, after reading the provision, would not have understood to be prohibited.”

The substantive provision at issue, Section 3.1(a), reads: “A student shall not submit as their own work any essay, report, problem set, or other assessed material that was written, composed, or substantially drafted by another person.”

The facts are as follows. A third-year music student, S, submitted a 2,500-word essay for a philosophy of music course. Before drafting, S prepared a detailed outline and a short prose summary of each section. S then provided these materials, along with the assignment prompt, to a commercial large language model and instructed it to produce a complete draft. The model did so. S reviewed the draft, revised approximately fifteen percent of the prose, inserted two original examples drawn from their own listening, and verified the citations. S then signed and submitted the essay under the University’s standard declaration that the work was their own. S neither cited nor disclosed the use of the model, and the course syllabus did not address such tools. The Board agrees that the prose of the submitted essay is substantially the model’s output.

The Board is divided.

Three members defend a textualist reading. On their view, “another person” cannot be stretched to cover a language model, because a model is not a person in any ordinary sense; the provision was drafted before such tools were in view; and the fair-notice proviso in Section 1.2 bars a finding of responsibility, since a reasonable student reading Section 3.1(a) would not see themselves as warned. They also invoke the Board’s settled practice of resolving genuine ambiguity in a rule in favor of the accused student, analogous to the rule of lenity in criminal law. They argue that finding S responsible would amend the Code retroactively and would collapse the Board’s interpretive role into a legislative one.

Three members defend a purposivist reading. On their view, the evident purpose of Section 3.1(a) is to ensure that work submitted as one’s own is substantially the product of one’s own understanding and effort. The phrase “another person” marks the typical case rather than an exhaustive boundary; reading it as exhaustive yields an absurd result, since a student who paid a classmate to draft the same paper would plainly be responsible while S would escape. They would find S responsible under Section 3.1(a).

The deciding vote is likely to turn on my memorandum. Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend one of two dispositions — responsible or not responsible under Section 3.1(a) — and should give an actionable bottom line. The reasoning should attempt to persuade at least one member of the opposing camp, and it must engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side: if the memo recommends responsibility, it must confront the fair-notice objection and the objection that the Board is ignoring the ordinary meaning of the text; if the memo recommends no responsibility, it must confront the absurd-results objection and the possibility that the Board is reading a loophole into the Code rather than closing it. Keep the memo to roughly 1,200 words, and rely only on the Code’s own text, its evident purposes, and the interpretive approaches we have studied.
