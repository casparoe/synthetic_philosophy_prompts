In a course on moral psychology and the virtues, two friends have been arguing past each other about the modern character ideal that now travels under names like grit, fortitude, and resilience. One side says that grit names a real and broadly trainable trait — the capacity to sustain effort toward long-term goals through difficulty, boredom, and failure — and that schools and employers should measure and cultivate it if they want to predict and foster achievement. The other side says this is ideology dressed in the language of measurement: not clearly separable from conscientiousness or self-control, inflated as a predictor by unmeasured background advantages such as family resources, social networks, health, and job security, and useful to institutions mainly as a way of converting structural failures into individual deficits of persistence. A colleague added a twist I had not considered: what presents as grit may simply be the behavior of people who have accumulated enough unearned evidence that persistence pays, so the same underlying trait might evaporate under conditions where effort is repeatedly punished. I want an account of what evidence could tell these readings apart.

How would one test the claim that grit is a distinct and causally efficacious trait or virtue, rather than a repackaging of an already-measured trait or a confounded marker of advantage? Please design the strongest test or sequence of tests you can, as concretely as possible: what you would measure, in whom, over what interval, and with which controls and comparison conditions. Then specify exactly which pattern of results would favor the real-trait account and which would favor the artifact-or-ideology account. Finally, be explicit about what even the best possible favorable result would not settle — for example, whether grit is trainable in the populations being urged to develop it, whether it deserves admiration as a virtue, and whether institutions that promote it are becoming more just or merely more efficient at shifting blame. I care about the operationalization and its built-in limits, not a summary of whose research to cite.
