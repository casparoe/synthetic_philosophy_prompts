I just spent a few weeks writing a long analysis for work — 40 or so pages of specific claims about our data, and I checked each one carefull. On any individual claim I'd say I believe it. But I'd also bet a lot that the document contains at least one error; I don't think I've ever produced anything that long without a goof somewhere. So it looks like I believe P1, P2, ..., Pn, and I also believe not-(P1 and P2 and ... and Pn). That's an inconsistent set of beliefs, and the principles I've always taken for granted are that rational beliefs must be consistent, and that if you're rational in believing each premise you're rational in believing their conjunction. Both of those feel obviously right in isolation. But the state I'm in also feels like exactly what a rational person should be in — the guy who claims there is zero chance of any error in 40 pages seems less rational, not more. I know this is basically the preface paradox, and I don't want a summary of it.

Act as an epistemologist who works on full belief versus degrees of confidence, think carefully, and before committing to a position, brainstorm the actual principles doing the work here — consistency, closure, the idea that enough probability licenses outright belief, maybe others. Then give me the strongest version of both sides and walk me through the negotiated verdict: what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what would it take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and is there a reading — maybe about different attitudes, or about how the two beliefs get formed by different kinds of reasoning — on which my state and the consistency requirement can both survive? Don't just tell me which side wins; I want to know what each move costs and what would justify making it.
