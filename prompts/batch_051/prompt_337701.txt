I'm taking an evening course on the history of evolutionary thought, and a friend in the class—who is religious—has been pressing me on whether Darwin actually does away with any non-natural ground for ethics. We reread the contents section from Chapter IV of *The Descent of Man*, where Darwin advertises the shape of his account:

"The moral sense—Fundamental proposition—The qualities of social animals—Origin of sociability—Struggle between opposed instincts—Man a social animal—The more enduring social instincts conquer other less persistent instincts—The social virtues alone regarded by savages—The self-regarding virtues acquired at a later stage of development—The importance of the judgment of the members of the same community on conduct—Transmission of moral tendencies—Summary."

My friend walked me through the following argument, and I don't know how to get off it. (1) On Darwin's view, the moral sense is an evolved complex of social instincts, acquired because groups whose members had such instincts did better than groups without them. (2) A social instinct, as Darwin describes it, is a behavioral disposition that wins out over other, less persistent impulses because of its effects on the community's survival. (3) So the content of conscience is explained by its survival value for the group, not by any independent moral correctness. (4) Therefore, if a community's members came to have a persistent social impulse to practice infanticide, honor slavery, or exterminate outsiders, Darwin's account would explain that impulse in exactly the same way it explains the impulse to rescue a drowning child. (5) So a Darwinian must say that both are equally moral for that community, and has no basis on which to call the first wrong and the second right. (6) But that is absurd: we can recognize that a persistent, survival-enhancing social impulse can be morally evil. (7) Therefore Darwin's account of the moral sense makes normative ethics impossible and thereby undermines itself. My instinct is that something slips in (3) or (5), but I can't yet name it without either smuggling in a non-evolved standard or quietly abandoning the explanatory story that made the account attractive. Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Identify which step to reject or, if the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to give up; say why that step looked plausible; and state what rejecting it costs in terms of the broader Darwinian picture.
