I write a column at the intersection of science and ideas, and next month's edition is an interview with someone who's spent the last few years arguing that the only sane moment to strike bargains about what happens after AGI is now, before any actor actually has it. Her pitch, quickly: after a system crosses the relevant capability threshold, whoever is behind it has no incentive to negotiate rather than dictate, so all the leverage lives in the period before that, when uncertainty about who'll be first is still symmetric. Her preferred instrument is a windfall clause — a pre-commitment that the first lab or state across the line shares the gains and forswears using the lead to lock everything down — backed not by promises but by technical tripwires: escrowed weights, staged disclosure, mutual monitoring, a neutral-ish trigger. There's also a veil-of-ignorance move in there, because ex ante no one knows whether they'll be the winner, so everyone should rationally prefer terms that protect the losers.

Give me eight to ten interview questions for her, and I want them to be the kind that make a careful guest work. Skip the general stuff about whether AGI is risky or whether this is just sci-fi. Press on the machinery. If enforcement only has to bite at the exact moment temptation is strongest, what's left to enforce the deal once a defector has overwhelming capability and has seen every tripwire you built? When she replies that the tripwires make defection self-punishing before that point, hit her with the follow-up about whether a contract drafted by pre-AGI minds can bind a post-AGI agent that will think circles around the drafters — isn't that a contract with an adversary you haven't met yet?

Build the rest of the set so it covers, in whatever order feels right: who the signatories are supposed to be (a lab can't bind it's own future system, states won't bind rival states, and shareholders don't sign treaties); why a lab that privately thinks it's ahead would sign away its lead rather than hold out for the whole gain; how you verify a trigger when capabilities are gradual or when the metrics themselves get gamed by the people being measured — if she invokes a neutral technical panel, the follow-up should be about Goodhart's law and the track record of benchmarks; whether this just recapitulates the failure mode of climate and nuclear arms-control regimes once monitoring is noisy and cheating is tempting; and whether locking in a deal now is actually premature because later we'll know more about what the technology even looks like, even if the window is closing. End with a question about what sort of development between now and the interview would most lower her confidence that pre-AGI bargains can work at all. The questions should sound like I've actually read the proposal, not like I'm meeting it in the green room.
