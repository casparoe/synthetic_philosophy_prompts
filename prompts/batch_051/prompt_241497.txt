I have been writing a short piece about whether the rhetoric of "lock-in" in historical explanation smuggles in a picture of history as contingent in a sense that determinism would rule out. A colleague pressed the following argument on me, and I find each step individually plausible enough that I am unsure where exactly to object. I would like a committed verdict on the argument, not a survey of possible responses: please say whether it goes through, and if it does not, identify the precise step at which it fails, explain the reading of that step on which it fails, and say how it would need to be reformulated. If it does go through, say so and spell out what I would then have to accept. Please write your answer in British English.

(1) Suppose determinism is true: the state of the world at any time, together with the laws of nature, entails the state of the world at every later time.
(2) Then, for any historical outcome — the survival of one technical standard rather than another, for instance — that outcome was entailed by actual prior conditions long before it occurred.
(3) If an outcome was entailed by prior conditions and the laws, then it could not have failed to occur; it would have occurred anyway.
(4) Path dependence, by contrast, claims that if some small, earlier contingency had been different, the outcome would have been different.
(5) Therefore path dependence requires that the outcome was not already fixed by prior conditions; it demands an indeterministic fork in history.
(6) So if determinism is true, what we call "lock-in" is really only our ignorance of which outcome was inevitable all along.

I suspect that step (3) slides from "determined given the actual past" to "determined no matter what the past was like," but I would like to know whether that suspicion is right, and whether the real error is at (3), at (5), or somewhere else in the chain.
