In debates about constitutionalism and entrenched rights, it is common to describe a constitution as a community binding itself to the mast. The idea is that the people, anticipating that future majorities will be tempted to pass panic-driven laws or erode basic rights, tie their own hands in advance, just as Ulysses had himself lashed to the mast so that he could hear the Sirens without steering the ship onto the rocks. The analogy is made to do real work: it is offered to show that constitutional constraints on majority rule are not anti-democratic but are a rational act of democratic self-government, and that precommitment is legitimate in general because the bound agent consented to it.

Audit this analogy. Provide an explicit table of correspondences — what plays the role of Ulysses, the mast, the ropes, the Sirens, the crew, the standing orders, and the later plea to be untied — and then identify the places where the mapping breaks down. In particular, ask whether the people who are bound are the same agent as the people who did the binding, whether a future majority is best understood as acting on a transient passion rather than a considered change of values, and whether constitutional provisions function like precise standing orders or like open-textured norms whose meaning is settled later by courts. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the intended use of the analogy from those that are merely incidental, and conclude by stating what the analogy can and cannot actually establish about the democratic legitimacy of constitutional entrenchment.
