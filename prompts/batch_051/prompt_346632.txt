I run a small weekly meetup that's half casual poker players and half people who like logic puzzles and spreadsheets. Lately I've gotten kind of obsessive about the idea that if you're willing to bet on something at one price and against that same thing at a different price, a clever bookie can bundle bets so you lose money no matter what happens. I tried explaining it on a whiteboard and everyone just nodded and said yes but in real life no one would take the stupid half of the book, which I think misses the point and maybe also kind of has a point. I'd like to design a 75-minute activity for 10 to 14 people where they feel the trap close on their own posted prices before I ever name the argument. My rough idea: give everybody a few simple events involving cards or dice — a red card gets drawn, a die lands on 5 or 6, that sort of thing — and make them write down what they'd pay for a $1 ticket on each event and what they'd sell that same ticket for. Then a facilitator (probably me) acts as the bookie and quietly combines their own prices against them.

But I need actual rules, a scoring or small-stakes setup (quarters are fine), a timeline, and a debrief that maps moments in the game onto the probability rules they broke — for instance, that your prices on an event and its opposite have to add up to a full ticket, or that your price on an either-or event should be the sum of the prices on the pieces when the pieces can't both happen. I also want to know what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: I suspect once someone realizes their on both sides of the same event they'll refuse to honor a price or start naming huge spreads, and I don't know how to handle that without turning the whole thing into a lecture. And be straight with me about which part of the idea the game can't make them feel — I have a hunch its the leap from your betting prices being incoherent to your actual degree of belief being irrational, but you tell me where the real soft spot is. The name Dutch book shouldn't come up until the debrief, after they've already been caught in it.
