Act as a philosopher of mathematics mentoring a PhD student. I want you to really think it through rather than reaching for a slogan. Here are the three criteria I was given for a mathematical choice to count as canonical: (i) it adds no data beyond what is already in the problem; (ii) any two competent people following the rule end up at the same object; and (iii) the chosen object is fixed by every symmetry, that is, every automorphism, of the relevant structure. Now consider the two square roots of −1. The automorphism of C that sends a+bi to a−bi fixes the reals but swaps the roots, so by (iii) neither is singled out; writing i for one of them looks arbitrary. Now the case I actually want your judgement on. We say √2 is the positive square root of 2. But that uses the ordering of the reals to break the tie. In the field Q(√2) (the numbers a+b√2 with a,b rational), the map sending √2 to −√2 is an automorphism as long as we forget the ordering, and the abstract field has two equally natural orderings, one making √2 positive and one making −√2 positive. So is the choice of the positive root canonical under my three criteria, or does it just smuggle an arbitrary choice inside the phrase "positive"? Does the √2 case show that criterion (iii) is incomplete, and that canonicity is always relative to a decision about what structure counts as given? Please start by brainstorming the borderline features of the two cases, then give a final judgement, and write your answer in British English.
