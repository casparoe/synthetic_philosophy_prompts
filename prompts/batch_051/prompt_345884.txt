I was arguing with my roommate about a case last night and realized my checklist for complicity might have a hole in it. The checklist I've been using runs: (1) somebody else is doing the primary wrong; (2) the person knows that wrong is happening; (3) the person does something that doesn't cause the wrong but helps it along — tools, information, legitimacy, encouragement, cover, that sort of thing; and (4) refusing wouldn't cost them so much that we'd excuse almost anything.

Now the case. A friend of mine does database optimization at a company that sends bulk email. The company is mostly a legitimate marketing platform, but one of its clients runs phishing campaigns aimed at elderly people. My friend has never touched that client's account, has no direct contact with it, and couldn't name a single victim. But the work he does makes the whole platform faster and cheaper, and the phishing operation benefits from that exactly the way every other client does. He knows the client is on the platform, has raised it internally a couple times, and still goes to work. The phishing would happen anyway, just at a slightly higher cost without his contribution.

Is he complicit? Go through the checklist criterion by criterion. Where the answer is no, tell me whether that means he's off the hook or whether the checklist itself is leaving something out. And say whether the fact that the wrong would happen anyway should matter for complicity, or whether it only matters for causation. Please write the answer in American English.
