I've been stewing on the whole-brain emulation debate, and I keep noticing a structural parallel with the way Unix fork() treats process identity. When one process forks, the child gets what is logically a complete copy of the parent's memory, register state, and code (copy-on-write asside), and yet no one is tempted to say the child process is the parent; the two just share a snapshot and then diverge, and identity lives in the process ID, not in the shared memory image. Is this a genuinely useful model for whether an emulated mind is the same mind as the original, or is it only a loose analogy? I want you to work out the mapping precisely: what plays the role of the parent process, the child, the snapshot, and the PID in a whole-brain emulation case? Where does the analogy break down? In particular, what does the fork model predict about the destructive-upload case (where the scan destroys the original brain), and is that a prediction someone who thinks upload is a form of survival should accept or push back on? I'm less interested in a settled yes or no than in seeing where the parallel earns its keep and where it quietly begs the question. And please say how confident you are in the various claims, and single out the point you are least sure of.
