I've been mulling over an argument that the whole value-drift worry is less serious than people in my circle think, but I can't tell whether its sound or just clever. It runs:

(1) If we have a reason to prevent our descendants' values from drifting, then we must be able to judge that their drifted values would be worse than ours.

(2) Any such judgment has to be made from the standpoint of some set of values or other.

(3) From the standpoint of our current values, the drifted values are worse; but from the standpoint of the drifted values, ours are worse. And there is no value-neutral standpoint from which to say one side is right.

(4) If the only available standpoints disagree, and none is neutral, then the judgment that drift is worse has no more claim on us than the opposite judgment.

(5) So we have no more reason to prevent value drift than to promote it.

(6) So the fear of bad values in the far future gives us no reason to, say, lock in our values or bias future institutions against drift.

I keep wanting to say (4) is doing something sneaky, but I can't make it stick. Tell me straight: is the argument valid? If not, which step fails and why exactly? And if it is valid, say which premise you'd reject and on what grounds. Don't give me a menu of options — I want your actual verdict and your reasons.
