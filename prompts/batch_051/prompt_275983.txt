I'm taking a seminar this term on collective responsibility, and we have just spent two sessions on whether organizations can be proper targets of moral blame, as opposed to merely legal liability. For a paper, I put together the following line of reasoning, and I now find it suspicious because I cannot accept where it lands, but I also cannot see where to break the chain.

(1) Moral blame can properly attach only to a moral agent — an entity that itself can be the subject of a negative moral judgment for what it did.
(2) To be a moral agent, an entity must be able to form intentions of its own and act on them; blame presupposes a subject that can intend, not merely a label under which various acts are coordinated.
(3) A corporation that knowingly markets an addictive and lethal product while suppressing evidence of its harms is a proper target of moral blame, not merely a legal defendant. If the company itself cannot be blamed, the blame must be spread across employees, executives, and shareholders, none of whom individually intended the full harm.
(4) But a corporation has no intentions of its own. What we call a corporate decision is the output of internal procedures and negotiations among individuals whose aims conflict, and saying that the company intended something is at best a useful fiction.
(5) So, from (2) and (4), a corporation is not a moral agent.
(6) So, from (1) and (5), a corporation cannot be morally blamed.
(7) But (6) contradicts (3).

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I would most like to keep (3), because giving it up seems to let organized wrongdoing hide behind the distributed character of corporate decision-making. But I want to know what I am paying for keeping it: which numbered step should I reject or qualify, and what does rejecting it cost the rest of my view?
