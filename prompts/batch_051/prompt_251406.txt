In John Rawls's *A Theory of Justice*, in his discussion of classical utilitarianism, Rawls develops the separateness of persons objection. The passage contrasts the way a single person may rationally accept a present loss for a future gain with the way a society might weigh one person's loss against another person's gain. It charges utilitarianism with extending the principle of individual choice to society as a whole, and thereby treating many persons' satisfactions as if they were the satisfactions of one person. I am trying to determine how much work this argument actually does against a sophisticated act-utilitarian who agrees that only individuals have mental states and that social entities are not agents, but who still holds that right action maximizes total well-being.

Please distinguish four precise claims the separateness-of-persons objection could be making:

- R1: a metaphysical claim that there is no social entity whose good could be maximized;
- R2: a normative claim that the boundaries between persons have intrinsic moral significance that forbids certain interpersonal trade-offs;
- R3: a rational-prudence claim that intrapersonal and interpersonal trade-offs are structurally disanalogous, because one person's future self is compensated by the same subject's later good while another person is not so compensated;
- R4: a deliberative-standpoint claim that the utilitarian impartial spectator is not a possible practical standpoint for real agents.

For each reading, give a faithfulness score from 1 to 10 measuring how well it captures Rawls's argument, and a damage score from 1 to 10 measuring how serious a problem it poses for the sophisticated act-utilitarian described above. Then rank the four readings by their combined seriousness, with reasoning for each placement and for the trade-offs between faithfulness and damage.

At the very end, output only the line \answer{R1 > R2 > R3 > R4} with your actual ranking substituted, and no text after it.
