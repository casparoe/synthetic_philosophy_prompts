I'm reading Tolstoy's *What Is Art?* (the Maude translation, so the text is over a century old) and I'm stuck on a passage in the early chapters where he first states his definition of art. Since I'm on a bus and don't have the book with me, I'll paraphrase it: he says art is a human activity in which one person deliberately transmits a feeling he has lived through to others by means of certain external signs, and those others are infected by that feeling and experience it too. He adds that the test of art is infectiousness, and that infectiousness depends on three things: the individuality of the feeling, the clarity of the expression, and the sincerity of the artist. Then, in a slightly later passage I keep worrying at, he claims this infection is not a metaphor but something that actually transfers a feeling, and he says art is not a means of pleasure but a means of union among people, a condition of human life.

The thing I can't make sense of is what "transfers" does here. Suppose I see an actor on stage clutch his chest and look terrified, and I feel a kind of fear in response. Is that my fear, caused by recognizing the signs, or is it the actor's fear, transferred to me? Tolstoy wants the second, but I don't see how the account supports it. Why isn't it enough that the external signs cause a similar feeling in me? Is there a reading of Tolstoy on which "infection" is not just causation plus resemblance, but something tighter? And if there isn't, does his definition collapse into just "art causes emotion in observers," which would make a horror movie or a roller-coaster count as art?
