In the mathematics study group I attend, an argument that began as a question about the continuum has now run for over a year, and no one has moved. One side holds that the real numbers are discovered. On this view, Dedekind cuts and Cauchy sequences are just two ways of building models of something that was already there: a completed continuum that would be what it is even if no mathematician had ever defined it, and in which every bounded set has a least upper bound independently of our axioms. The other side holds that the real numbers are defined, or invented. On this view, there is no continuum apart from our constructions and axioms; for something to be a real number is just for it to play the right role in one of those constructions, and saying that the real numbers exist is shorthand for saying that a structure of the appropriate kind exists up to isomorphism.

I am not asking which side is right. I want a diagnosis of the impasse itself. Is this a substantive disagreement about what exists, a verbal disagreement about the word exists, or are the two sides answering different questions: perhaps one about the metaphysics of mathematical objects and the other about how the term the real numbers gets its reference? Are there hidden factual or formal questions quietly doing the work, for example whether every complete ordered field is unique up to isomorphism, or whether the two standard constructions give us the same object rather than merely equivalent objects? And given that diagnosis, would anything count as progress: a distinction that lets both positions be stated without collision, a formal result, a change in the question, or should we expect no progress because the original question was ill-posed?
