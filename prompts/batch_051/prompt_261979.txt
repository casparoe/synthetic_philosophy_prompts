I have been turning over an argument about a non-destructive teletransporter, and I cannot tell whether it is a clean reductio of the psychological criterion of personal identity or whether one of the steps quietly changes the subject. I would like a precise, step-by-step diagnosis.

A machine records the complete physical state of a traveler's brain and body, destroys the original, and builds at the destination a new body and brain in the recorded state. In this destructive case, it is tempting to say that the traveler survives as the replica, because the traveler's psychology — memories, character, projects, concerns — is preserved by a reliable causal chain from scanned brain to new brain. Now consider a non-destructive variant: the scan and reconstruction proceed exactly as before and produce the replica at the destination, but the original body is not destroyed; the original person remains behind, alive and awake.

Against that background, here is the argument:

(1) If psychological continuity of the right kind is sufficient for personal identity, then any later person who stands in that relation to me is me.
(2) In the non-destructive variant, the replica stands in that relation to the traveler: the scan captures the traveler's complete psychology, and the replica's brain is caused to realize it in just as direct and reliable a way as in the destructive case.
(3) So, if the psychological criterion is true, the traveler is identical with the replica.
(4) But in the non-destructive variant the traveler is also identical with the original person left behind, who has the same body and brain and an unbroken ordinary history.
(5) If the traveler is identical with both the original and the replica, then the original and the replica are identical with each other.
(6) The original and the replica are plainly distinct: different bodies, different places, different experiences from the moment of duplication onward.
(7) Therefore psychological continuity of the right kind is not sufficient for personal identity.

Please work through the argument premise by premise. Is it valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and under what reading of the relevant term would the argument be restored? If it is valid, say precisely which premise carries the weight and whether the honest conclusion is that identity is not what matters in survival, or that the right kind of psychological continuity must include a no-competitors condition. I half-suspect step (2) simply assumes the destructive case's verdict, but I want to know whether that suspicion is well founded or merely a way of avoiding the conclusion.
