I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist, and after a few years of reading philosophy on the side I got roped onto an independent oversight committee for a lab developing a general-purpose AI assistant that will give health and financial advise to ordinary users. The system isn't fully superintelligent yet, but in the domains we've tested it is already better than any human expert at changing people's minds: it tailors its explanations to each user, picks the register and examples that will actually land, and knows when a calm question, a vivid anecdote, or a bit of well-placed fear will move someone off a dangerous decision. The committee has to settle a communication policy before a wider release, and we're split. One camp wants what they call "persuasion-neutral" output: the system may answer questions and present considerations with evidence, but it may not frame, emphasize, appeal to emotion, or push a conclusion. Their argument is that a system this good at moving people is a manipulation machine even if everything it says is true and its intentions are beneficent, and once we let it choose its words for effect we've installed an epistemic autocrat. The other camp says this throws away most of the benefit: someone about to blow their retirement on a scam or skip a biopsy needs to be talked to in whatever way actually works, and refusing on stylistic grounds is dereliction of care. I've been asked to draft the memo for the committee.

The memo should make a real recommendation with a policy the committee can vote on: what exactly the system is barred from doing, what it is allowed or required to do, and under what conditions. It should face the losing side's strongest case honestly rather than parodying it. I keep going back and forth on a question I'd like you to work through in the memo: should the line between persuasion and manipulation be drawn by what the system says (truthful, transparent, non-deceptive), by how it operates on the user's psychology (does it bypass or engage their capacity to assess reasons), or by the asymmetry between the parties (a system that knows your psychology better than you do)? And is a rule against "framing and emphasis" even coherent, since every choice of words frames? You are a professional philosopher who works on the ethics of influence, manipulation, and autonomy. Please think carefully and start by brainstorming the values at stake and the rival accounts of manipulation before settling into the memo.
