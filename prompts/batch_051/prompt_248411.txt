I am a novelist rather than a lawyer, and next month I have been asked to give a talk to a law-and-humanities workshop on a thesis I have been turning over while researching a novel about a shipping company that has, for a century, been a legal person. The thesis is this: legal fictions are neither false statements the law tolerates for convenience nor shorthand for longer statements about real people and real acts; they are constitutive stipulations of the same general kind as the stipulations by which a novelist brings a fictional world into being, and the law's invented entities — the corporation, the trust, the estate — are best understood on the model of fictional entities rather than on the model of facts or lies. My argument, in brief, runs as follows. A legal fiction is an instruction to treat something as so for the purposes of legal reasoning; once that instruction is accepted, new legal sentences become true that were not true before; the fiction therefore does not misdescribe an independent reality but constitutes part of legal reality; and the closest familiar practice of making sentences true by treating something as so is the construction of a fictional world. The audience worries me because it is not one audience. The workshop draws three kinds of people: doctrinal legal scholars who think legal fictions are simply devices for extending old rules to new cases and will find my talk suspiciously metaphysical; philosophers of law, several of them positivists, who will want to know whether I am collapsing law into fiction, since they take law to be a social fact and fiction to be precisely not a fact at all; and one or two specialists in the philosophy of fiction who will object that fictional entities are incomplete, created by a single author, and causally inert, none of which seems true of the corporation. Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as those people would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, a short reply for each calibrated to a two-minute answer, the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss, and the objection I should be most worried about even if it is not the first one asked.
