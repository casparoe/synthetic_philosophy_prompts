I have no formal background in philosophy, but I have been trying to think through the idea of dialetheism on my own, and I wanted to see how the older case for the opposite view goes. I read Book IV of Aristotle's Metaphysics and found this passage at 1005b:

"It is impossible for the same attribute at once to belong and not to belong to the same thing and in the same relation"; and we must add any further qualifications that may be necessary to meet logical objections. This is the most certain of all principles, since it possesses the required definition; for it is impossible for anyone to suppose that the same thing is and is not, as some imagine that Heraclitus says—for what a man says does not necessarily represent what he believes. And if it is impossible for contrary attributes to belong at the same time to the same subject (the usual qualifications must be added to this premiss also), and an opinion which contradicts another is contrary to it, then clearly it is impossible for the same man to suppose at the same time that the same thing is and is not; for the man who made this error would entertain two contrary opinions at the same time. Hence all men who are demonstrating anything refer back to this as an ultimate belief; for it is by nature the starting-point of all the other axioms as well.

My difficulty is that I cannot tell what kind of point Aristotle is making. A dialetheist does not claim every contradiction is true, only that some rare ones are. So when Aristotle says it is impossible for the same attribute at once to belong and not to belong to the same thing in the same relation, and then says it is impossible even to suppose that the same thing is and is not, is he offering a reason a dialetheist would have to accept, or is he just restating the very principle he set out to defend? I especially do not see why the fact that someone cannot believe two contrary opinions at once would show that no contradiction can be true, unless our ability to believe something decides what is true. Could you walk me through what the passage actually establishes, where it relies on assumptions, and where a dialetheist would most naturally push back?
