I have been trying, on my own, to think through what some writers call moral bioenhancement — the idea that biomedical means could be used to make people morally better — and one concrete proposal keeps leading me in circles. Suppose a jurisdiction introduces the following program for people convicted of violent offenses. At sentencing, a defendant may choose between serving the standard prison term and serving a substantially shorter term on the condition that, after release, they take a daily dose of a medication for the full supervision period. The medication's only notable effect is to make other people's suffering feel as immediate and vivid to the user as their own discomfort, for roughly twelve hours per dose. It does not change anyone's goals, values, or preferences directly; it only makes other people's pain as salient as a person's own pain already is. Compliance is verified by blood tests, and a missed dose means a return to prison for the remainder of the original sentence.

I am not asking whether this program is morally right or wrong. I am asking how it would actually behave if it were put in place. Who would take the deal and who would refuse, and would the people who take it be the ones the program is meant to reach? How would participants try to work around it — by timing doses, by blunting the effect on the days that matter, by gaming the blood tests, or in some other way? What would the program incentivize for judges, probation officers, the company that manufactures the medication, and the families and employers of participants? What second-order effects would appear — for instance, in how other people treat someone known to be on the medication, or in how prosecutors and juries behave once the option exists? And which of the predictable failure modes could be fixed without quietly turning the program into a different program altogether?
