A recent essay summarizes the debate over the norm of assertion roughly as follows. Assertion, like promising or betting, is governed by a standard one must meet to be entitled to speak. The three main candidates are truth, belief, and knowledge; the dominant view, associated with Timothy Williamson, is the knowledge norm — assert that p only if you know that p. But since knowledge entails truth, the knowledge norm is really just the old rule against saying false things, so the dispute between the knowledge and truth norms is largely terminological. The decisive argument for the knowledge norm is Moore's paradox: someone who says "It is raining, but I do not know that it is raining" has contradicted himself, because the first part of his statement can be in order only if he knows it. The main objection comes from lottery cases: before the draw, it would be perfectly proper to tell a ticketholder that their ticket is a loser, since it is almost certainly true, even though you do not know it; so the essay concludes that the knowledge norm is too strict for ordinary speech and must be weakened to a justification or high-probability norm. I suspect parts of this are unfair — the step from knowledge entails truth to equivalence with the truth norm seems too quick, and Moore's paradox is not supposed to be an outright contradiction — but I cannot pin the errors down. Could you audit the summary: what is right, what is subtly off, and how would a defender of the knowledge norm answer the lottery case?
