I spent my working life in the biomedical sciences, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science with more care than I ever could during my career. A habit I never questioned while I was active now strikes me as philosophically strange. Everyone in the laboratory knew, at least half-seriously, Stigler's law of eponymy: the generalization that no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer. The law is usually credited to the statistician Stephen Stigler, who is said to have credited it in turn to the sociologist Robert K. Merton, so the law would be an instance of itself. Yet the guild continues to speak of Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and Hodgkin's lymphoma, and it continues to mint new eponyms, even when later historical or bibliometric work shows that the name attaches to the person who published accessibly, marshalled the evidence, or outlived a rival rather than to the person who first saw the phenomenon.

Nor is this merely a survival from the nineteenth century. Contemporary nomenclature committees still deliberate over eponyms, textbooks reproduce them, and priority disputes are settled in the historical literature while the names themselves are left standing; the correction gets recorded as an irony rather than as a change in usage. I am trying to extract the philosophy that sits inside this practice. What view of discovery must one hold for it to make sense to name an object of inquiry after an individual? Presumably discovery has to be the kind of event that can be dated and assigned to a person, credit has to be the kind of thing one can hold as one holds property, and the growth of knowledge has to be decomposable into discrete contributions with identifiable owners. But if that were sincerely believed, would the names not be corrected when the attribution demonstrably fails? Or does the resilience of eponyms show that the practice is not really in the business of recording who discovered what?

What does the practice of eponymous naming in science strictly presuppose, as opposed to merely fitting naturally with? Which rival pictures of discovery — for example, discovery as a communal accretion, or as a construction that retrospective credit imposes on a messier past — does the practice quietly rule out? And what would have to change, culturally and institutionally, if we adopted instead a view on which discovery is distributed, gradual, and not the sort of thing a single proper name can pick out? Would eponyms have to be abandoned, or could they be kept honestly as something other than a historical report?
