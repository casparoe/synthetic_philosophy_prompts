In a class on the ethics of war, we've been going around in circles about a principle I first thought was some medieval holdover: the moral equality of combatants. The idea, as I understand it, is that two soldiers shooting at each other across a field have the same moral permission to fight, regardless of which side has a just cause, as long as they follow the laws of war. I get the practical point—if everyone gets treated as a criminal just for fighting, wars get nastier, prisoners get shot, surrender stops happening. But I don't see how it can be a real moral principle, because it sounds like saying the passenger and the getaway driver are equally OK as long as they both obey traffic laws.

So I'm trying to compare that position with the opposing one: that a soldier on an unjust side acts wrongly even when he only attacks lawful military targets, because he is contributing to an unjust war. My question is whether these two views are actually answering different questions. Is the moral equality view maybe just a convention for how soldiers should treat each other in the field, not a claim about whether the unjust soldier's acts are objectively permissible? And if you take the opposing view seriously, what should you say about a conscript who fights for an aggressor state under threat of death, aiming only at enemy soldiers, never at civilians? I'd like a comparison that spells out what each view says about blameworthiness, the wrongness of the act itself, and what rules it implies for how we should treat soldiers after the war.
