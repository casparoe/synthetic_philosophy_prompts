I write about science and ideas, and I've been asked to lead a ninety-minute session on the paradox of fiction for a book-festival audience of serious readers with no background in philosophy. Don't explain the paradox to me; plan how I should teach it. The session should be built around a single carrying example: I'm inclined to use a reader moved to tears by the death of a talking animal in a children's novel, precisely because no one could claim they were even momentarily taken in. Tell me whether that example can carry the whole argument or whether a more realistic case must come first, and why. What has to be in place before the question of how we can be moved by characters we know never existed even gets its grip, and in what order should the components be introduced so the inconsistency feels forced rather than theatrical? Which misconceptions should I expect — that it is all just suspension of disbelief, that the tears are really about the reader's own life, that fictional emotions are merely weaker versions of real ones — and at exactly which point in the sequence should each be headed off? State what I should openly refuse to settle at this level, and give me one question to ask at the end that tells me whether they have the paradox rather than a premature solution to it.
