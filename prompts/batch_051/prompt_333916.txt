I am writing the setup for a philosophy-of-psychiatry paper on monothematic delusions, and my working suspicion is that much of the intractability of the debate over whether delusions are beliefs comes from the two sides silently weighting different theoretical requirements. Before I commit to any account of how monothematic delusions are formed and whether they should be classified as beliefs, I want the full specification of what such an account would have to deliver.

Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of monothematic delusions, taking Capgras delusion (the patient claims that a close relative has been replaced by an impostor) and Cotard delusion (the patient claims to be dead, not to exist, or to have lost some bodily part) as the two cases every theory must handle. For each desideratum: state it precisely, give the clinical observation, experimental result, or puzzle that makes it a requirement, and classify it as non-negotiable for any account, specific to one camp (one-factor versus two-factor, doxastic versus anti-doxastic), or a preference that depends on one's further purposes. Make sure the list addresses at least the following phenomena, and if you think any should not constrain the debate, say so explicitly and justify the exclusion: the circumscribed character of monothematic delusions; the apparent dissociation in Capgras between intact recognition of a familiar face and a missing affective or autonomic familiarity response; the patient's resistance to counterevidence; the double-bookkeeping pattern in which patients verbally avow the delusion while acting in practice as though they did not hold it; the difference between monothematic and polythematic delusions; and the difference between explaining why a delusion has the content it does and explaining why that content is accepted and maintained as true.

Then, with the list complete: (a) identify which pairs of desiderata are in tension, including any combinations that no single theory can plausibly satisfy at once; (b) say which single requirement does the most work in separating or eliminating the currently popular accounts; and (c) give a brief scorecard of four positions — a one-factor neuropsychological account (a single deficit explains both content and maintenance), a two-factor account (an anomalous-experience factor plus a belief-evaluation factor), a doxastic account on which delusions are beliefs, and an anti-doxastic account on which they are imaginings, avowals, or otherwise fail to be beliefs — against the list. Keep the scorecard brief; the desiderata themselves are what I most want to see.
