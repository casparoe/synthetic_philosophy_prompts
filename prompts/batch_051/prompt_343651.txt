I am currently taking a course on the foundations of rational choice, and we have been working through Quinn's self-torturer. The case, as our instructor presented it, runs roughly as follows. A person has a device with settings numbered from 0 to 1,000. Setting 0 is painless, setting 1,000 is excruciating, and each one-step increase adds an amount of pain too small for him to feel in isolation. Each increment also pays an additional $10,000. The person knows how the device works, so he knows the small increases in pain are real and cumulative, even though adjacent settings feel exactly alike to him. The striking feature of the case is that he can be moved, one apparently easy step at a time, into a state he would refuse to enter in a single leap.

I tried to turn this into a precise argument for the conclusion that rational preference is not transitive, but I suspect something is being smuggled into the setup. Here is my reconstruction:

(1) For every n, the felt quality of the state at setting n is indistinguishable from the felt quality of the state at setting n+1.

(2) If an agent faces a pairwise choice between two states whose felt qualities are indistinguishable, and one of the states contains an additional $10,000, then the agent is rationally required to prefer that state.

(3) Therefore, for every n, the agent is rationally required to prefer setting n+1 to setting n.

(4) If rational preference is transitive, then an agent who is rationally required to prefer setting n+1 to setting n for every n is rationally required to prefer setting 1,000 to setting 0.

(5) But the agent is not rationally required to prefer setting 1,000 to setting 0. Refusing a single direct switch from setting 0 to setting 1,000 for the cumulative sum of $10,000,000 is rationally permissible, since the resulting pain is severe and the initial state is painless.

(6) Therefore, transitivity of rational preference is false.

I would like to know whether this argument is valid as written. If it is not valid, tell me exactly which step breaks and how the offending premise or inference should be revised. If it is valid, tell me which premise you would reject and why. I keep going back and forth about whether the real difficulty lies in step (2) or in the idea that pairwise felt comparisons should be allowed to override the agent's standing knowledge of the global structure of the choice.
