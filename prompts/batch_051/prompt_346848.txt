Take the usual statue-and-clay case. On Sunday there is a lump of clay on a bench. On Monday a sculptor forms it into a statue, Goliath. On Tuesday Goliath gets squashed, and the lump survives. The two-object theorist says that on Monday there are two material objects, Goliath and the lump, coinciding in one region. The one-object theorist says there is just one object, which is a statue for a while.

Now change the case. The clay is poured into a mould and cures directly as a statue, so the first moment the clay exists as a connected lump is also the first moment Goliath exists. And at the end, the whole thing is vapourised rather than squashed, so neither the lump nor Goliath outlives the other. Every actual asymmetry of pre-existence and post-existence is thereby removed: there is no actual time at which the lump exists and the statue does not.

The question: once those actual temporal asymmetries are gone, what remains of the two-object view's case? Does the two-object theorist now have to lean entirely on counterfactual or sortal claims, e.g. that the lump could have been squashed and survived but the statue could not? And how should a one-object theorist respond to that move? Walk through the altered case for each view, state what each view is now committed to, and say which of the two original answers survives the change and which has to be revised. Then identify the feature of the original case that was really doing the work. Be honest about which side now carries the heavier burden, and whether this variation makes the one-object view the default unless the modal arguments are defended.
