I run a ninety-minute workshop at a public library for about twenty adults who have no special background in philosophy, and I want them to leave having felt, rather than merely been told, the force of Benjamin Franklin's view that a systematic attempt at moral perfection through keeping a running ledger of one's own faults tends to undermine itself. The idea I want the exercise to carry is this: once a person is scoring his own conduct against a chart of virtues, he begins to manage the score rather than the character; virtues pursued all at once begin to trade off against one another; and Franklin's mature conclusion was that an imperfect but useful character is the wiser aim than a perfection the method cannot deliver. Please design a runnable activity in which participants choose a small set of personal virtues, define in advance what would count as a fault for each, and then spend roughly forty-five minutes doing ordinary small-group tasks while silently marking their own faults on a simple card. The rules, roles, and any small stakes should be arranged so that the temptation to make the card look good, and the experience of one virtue crowding out another, arise from the mechanics of the exercise itself rather than from the facilitator's explanation. I would also like a minute-by-minute timeline and a debrief that maps concrete moments in the exercise onto Franklin's view, predicts what will predictably go wrong when the session is run and how to handle it, and states honestly which features of Franklin's project, such as long-term habit formation or the religious frame he brought to it, this compressed exercise cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply in closing.
