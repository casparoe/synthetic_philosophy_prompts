A colleague who teaches a graduate seminar on Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka has asked me to write a short statement of where I stand, to circulate as a target for discussion. I sent back the following seven commitments. I consider each of them true, but I suspect they do not all sit easily together, and I cannot tell which of them is doing the damage.

1. The catuṣkoṭi, the four alternatives, exhausts the positions worth taking on any question Nāgārjuna examines. For a given claim the possible stances are: affirming it, negating it, affirming both, and affirming neither. There is no fifth stance, and the negation generating these alternatives is uniform throughout.

2. When Nāgārjuna runs the four alternatives on arising, he rejects each one outright — not from self, not from another, not from both, not without a cause. I read these as assertions that each of the four theses is false, not merely as a refusal to take one up.

3. Those rejections are ordinary classical reductios. They require standard negation and excluded middle, and nothing in the text forces us toward paraconsistent logic, dialetheism, or extra truth-values.

4. Conventional truth is not a defective copy of the real. Tables can break toes, debts can bind, and the difference between a cruel and a kind action is a real difference within the conventional frame. Emptiness is what makes that dependent functioning possible, not what cancels it.

5. Emptiness is not a thesis about the ultimate nature of things. To assert that reality is ultimately empty, as a metaphysical description, would be to reify emptiness and treat it as a foundation; the tradition insists that this is the one view from which one cannot recover.

6. Even so, the claim that all things are empty is true, and it is a conclusion worth arguing for. It has teeth: it rules out any account of arising, identity, or time that posits a self-sufficient own-nature, a svabhāva, standing under a thing.

7. Madhyamaka is a cognitive project, not merely a therapeutic one. Liberation involves seeing things as they actually are, and that seeing has propositional content. If it had none, the whole enterprise would collapse into a quietist refusal to answer questions.

I would like an audit rather than a defense. Which of these commitments pull against one another, and how severe is each conflict? Which conflicts are merely apparent and dissolve once a distinction is drawn? For each genuine conflict, tell me what would have to give and what the cheapest repair costs. Finally, tell me which single revision to the list would relieve the most strain, and what I would lose by making it.
