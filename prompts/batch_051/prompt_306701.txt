I am working through a dispute and want to use it to think through this passage from Walter Benjamin's *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* (translated by Harry Zohn):

"In principle a work of art has always been reproducible. Man-made artifacts could always be imitated by men. Replicas were made by pupils in practice of their craft, by masters for diffusing their works, and, finally, by third parties in the pursuit of gain. Mechanical reproduction of a work of art, however, represents something new."

A painter with a highly distinctive style objects that a company has trained an image-generation model on photographs of her entire catalogue and now sells generated canvases as 'new works in her style.' The company never claims she made them, and no output reproduces any single painting closely enough to be called a copy. The company leans on Benjamin's distinction: technical reproduction is new because it reproduces the work, but here no work is reproduced — a style is. The painter replies that the machine does to her authority something sufficiently like what Benjamin describes, and that the difference between a work and a manner does not matter in just the way the company needs it to.

Apply the passage to the case. Do Benjamin's three sources of replicas — pupils imitating in practice, masters diffusing their works, third parties pursuing gain — force us to place the model inside his schema, or does the schema draw its line at the work and leave this case outside it? If the passage can be read both ways, which reading better fits the essay's concern with how a new technique of reproduction changes the status of the original, and what follows for the painter under each reading?
