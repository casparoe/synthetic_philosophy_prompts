I've been going down a rabbit hole on reserve powers because my girlfriend is Swedish and we kept arguing about whether the king could actually do anything if a government went full authoritarian. Then at dinner my flatmate, who thinks the monarchy is just ceremonial, proposed this amendment to the UK constitution mostly as a provocation, and I can't get it out of my head:

The monarch may, within ten days of a bill being presented for royal assent, refuse assent in writing on the ground that the bill is a threat to democracy. The refusal is valid only if the monarch abdicates at the moment of refusal. A binding referrendum is then held within 30 days on the question: Should the monarch's refusal be sustained? If yes wins, the bill is void, the government must resign, and fresh elections must be held within 60 days. If no wins, the bill becomes law and the monarchy is abolished.

I don't want a verdict on whether it's a good idea. I want you to treat it as a mechanism and work out how it would actually play in normal times and in a genuine crisis.

Please structure your answer as:

The rule in plain terms (about 100 words)
What it does in normal times (about 150 words)
What it does in a genuine authoritarian crisis (about 200 words)
How it gets gamed (about 200 words)
Second-order effects on parliament, courts, and public opinion (about 150 words)
Are the failure modes fixable or inherent? (about 150 words)

Make the analysis specific to the actual incentives the rule creates, not just the stated intention. Also, don't assume constitutional actors are saints: the interesting part is what a desperate PM, a partisan palace, and a polarized electorate would actually do with it.
