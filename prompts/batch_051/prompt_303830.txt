One thing's been bugging me in our moral epistemology seminar this term: the reflective equilibrium debate seems to have completely stalled, yet the defenders and critics each keep saying the other side hasnt actually understood the method. The defender's picture, roughly, is Goodman's move for inference rules — you justify a rule by its fit with the particular inferences you accept — applied to ethics: you go back and forth between considered judgements about cases and general principles, revising both until they hang together, and in the wide version you also bring in background beliefs about persons, society, and so on. Rawls treated the starting judgements as fixed only provisionally: revisable in principle, but not all thrown away at once. The critic says this is a machine for laundering whatever you already believe — coherence among contaminated inputs is worth no more than the inputs, and widening the equilibrium just gives the same bias more chances to veto an unpleasant conclusion. My hunch is that the two sides are not really disagreeing about one thing. I suspect the impasse hides three disputes with different structures: a verbal dispute about the word 'justification', a substantive dispute about whether intuitions carry any evidential weight at all, and a disagreement about what a method in ethics is even supposed to do — track a mind-independent moral truth, systematise a point of view, deliver agreement, something else. Is my hunch right, or is there a single empirical or conceptual question underneath that I'm missing? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than a history of who said what, and rather than a verdict on who wins. Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a psychological finding, a formal result, a change of question — or make the case that we should expect the stalemate to persist and why. And please write the answer in British English.
