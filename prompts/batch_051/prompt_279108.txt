Act as a professional philosopher working at the intersection of moral psychology and ancient Stoic thought. Think carefully before you commit, begin by brainstorming several candidate principles, and test each candidate against the cases below rather than settling on the first account that sounds right.

I am a novelist. In my current book, a character is trying to order her life around the Stoic separation of what is up to her from what is not up to her, and I want her discipline to be psychologically exact rather than slogan-sized. To work out what I actually take this separation to be, I have written down my own verdicts on a series of cases, along with a rough principle I no longer fully trust. What I want from you is not recycled textbook doctrine but the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best captures my verdicts.

My rough principle is this: a thing is up to me only if I can bring it about directly by deciding to, and whatever depends on my body's performance, on other people, or on the world is not up to me, or at most only indirectly so.

Here are the cases.

1. A storm washes out the road on the way to the reading. Whether the road is closed: not up to me.
2. Whether my hands shake as I walk on stage: not up to me. Whether I walk on stage: up to me.
3. A horn blasts behind me and I startle before I know what the sound is. Whether I startle: not up to me.
4. Someone interrupts me and my first impulse is contempt. Whether the impulse arrives: not up to me. Whether I feed it or set it aside: up to me.
5. A reviewer calls the novel provincial. Whether I spend the first few seconds brooding on that sentence: not up to me. Whether I am still picking at it three days later: partly up to me, but only through habits of attention I built earlier, not through a single act of will in the moment.
6. Whether I believe, right now, that this new chapter deserves to stay in the book: not up to me. I cannot acquire the belief simply by deciding to have it. Whether I send the chapter to my editor despite that uncertainty: up to me.
7. After a month without drinking, whether I want a drink when I pass the bar: not up to me. Whether I pour one: up to me.
8. A false rumor sinks my standing with the local paper. Whether my reputation survives it: not up to me, though I could have cultivated goodwill earlier; I keep going back and forth on whether that earlier cultivation means the reputation was partly up to me after all.
9. Whether, over a decade, I become the kind of person who finishes novels: up to me only indirectly, through many separate small choices, no one of which seems to make the difference.

Is there a single principle that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts, or does the best principle force some of them to go? Work through each case under whichever principle you propose, and treat the cases it cannot accommodate honestly. I especially want to know whether my rough principle survives contact with cases 5, 6, and 9, and what has to give if it does not.

Then apply the account you defend to the case I have not been able to settle: whether the first movement of wanting—the tug, before I have endorsed or reviewed it, of wanting to check my sales ranking on a bookseller's site—is up to me, and what your answer implies about what a discipline of attention can and cannot do for my character.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the principle you end up defending: which further case, argument, or empirical finding would lead you to abandon or substantially revise it, and where you think its reasoning is most likely to fail.
