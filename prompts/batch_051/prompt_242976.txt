After a long argument with a friend about whether satisficing is genuinely distinct from optimizing, he left me with the following challenge. Herbert Simon’s 1956 paper, Rational Choice and the Structure of the Environment, contains the scissors metaphor: one blade is the structure of the task environment, the other is the computational capacities of the agent, and rational behavior is cut by the two together. That image persuaded me that a theory of rational choice should not ignore either side. But my friend reconstructed Simon’s own position into an argument that, he says, shows the two blades collapse into one:

(1) A bounded agent cannot maximize expected utility, so it sets an aspiration level and chooses the first option that meets it.
(2) The aspiration level itself must be set somehow. Either the agent sets it by estimating the expected costs and benefits of further search, or it is fixed by a process that does not represent the agent’s reasons.
(3) If the aspiration level is set by estimating the expected costs and benefits of further search, then satisficing is just expected utility maximization under computational constraints.
(4) If it is fixed by a process that does not represent the agent’s reasons, then satisficing is not a normative account of rationality; it is at best a psychological description of how agents happen to stop searching.
(5) Therefore satisficing either collapses into optimizing or forfeits its claim to be a theory of rational choice.

He says this shows Simon’s scissors is really one blade: the environment matters only insofar as it is represented inside the agent’s computational blade. The conclusion strikes me as too strong, but I cannot locate the false step. I would prefer to keep the scissors metaphor and the idea that satisficing is a distinct form of rational assessment rather than a disguised optimization. Which premise should I reject, and what exactly do I pay for rejecting it? Could the force of the scissors metaphor be preserved without conceding the collapse?
