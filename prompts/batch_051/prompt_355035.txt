A popular-science book I am reading gives the following summary of Noether's theorem:

"Noether's theorem says that every symmetry of the laws of nature carries with it a corresponding conservation law, and conversely every conservation law reflects an underlying symmetry. For example, the fact that the laws of physics do not change over time — time-translation symmetry — yields the conservation of energy; invariance under spatial translations yields conservation of momentum; and invariance under rotations yields conservation of angular momentum. The conservation of energy is therefore not a brute fact about the universe but a logical consequence of the laws being the same yesterday, today and tomorrow. The theorem thus gives physicists a general recipe: find a symmetry of a system, and a conserved quantity comes out for free."

I have no physics background and suspect this is mostly right but compressed in ways I cannot identify. Could you assess how fair this summary is: which parts are accurate, which are overstatements or conflations, and how a physicist or philosopher of physics would amend it? In particular, I would like to know whether it is right to say that energy conservation is simply a logical consequence of the laws not changing over time, whether any symmetry really does yield a conserved quantity "for free", and whether one is in fact entitled to say that the symmetry explains the conservation law rather than merely restating it in mathematical terms.
