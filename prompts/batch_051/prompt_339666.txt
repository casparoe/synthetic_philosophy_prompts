My friend has this argument he says proves that generics don't actually license the obvious inferences people think they license, and I cant find the flaw.

(1) "Mosquitoes carry malaria" is true. Flatly denying it would be bizarre.
(2) A true generic of the form "Ks are F" licenses the default inference from "x is a K" to "x is F".
(3) So the inference from "there's a mosquito in the room" to "it carries malaria" is a licensed default inference, and a licensed default inference is a good inference.
(4) But that inference is not a good inference: the vast majority of mosquitoes don't carry malaria, so given no other information it's much likelier that the mosquito in question doesn't.
(5) So the same inference is both good and not good.
(6) Since (1) is obviously true, (2) must be false.

He says that means generics only talk about kinds and never give you a reason to infer anything about an individual mosquito or duck, which would make them weirdly useless. That conclusion feels too strong, and I suspect something moves in the middle steps, but I can't say exactly what. Is the argument valid? If not, tell me exactly which step fails and why. If I should instead give up (2), give me the strongest true replacement and rerun it to see what it gets me. Take a definite position and defend it; don't just lay out readings.
