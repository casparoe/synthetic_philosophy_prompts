The country I live in has an 1819 constitution with a clause I can't stop thinking about, because it's a Ulysses contract aimed at dead hands: 'A sitting Parliament may not bind its successors. Any law may be amended or repealed by the Legislature at will, and any term of any law, treaty, or agreement that purports to restrict this power, or to require an extraordinary majority for its exercise, is void.' You can see the drafters plainly: they had watched one generation lock in its preferences and wanted each Parliment to stay sovereign.

Here's the case that's got me stuck. Thirty years ago a government sold off the water system under a ninety-nine-year concession. The contract contains a clause saying that if Parliament ever terminates it early, the state must pay the operator the entire present value of the profits it would have earned for the remaining years, assesed by an international arbitration tribunal whose awards are enforceable abroad. A new Parliament has just been elected on a promise to bring the water system back into public hands. The treasury now tells them that terminating the concession would cost about a fifth of GDP, so the repeal the new majority wants is legal on paper but financially impossible in fact. No special majority is required; the expected payout does the binding.

Run the four readings for me properly, not the lazy versions. Plain text: is a compensation clause a 'term that purports to restrict' Parliament's power, or just a price attached to using the power? Purpose: if the drafters were trying to keep every Parliament sovereign, does a clause that makes repeal technically possible but ruinously expensive count as binding the successor? Drafters' expectations: they'd never heard of investment arbitration or regulatory expropriation; what would they have said if someone had explained a fifth-of-GDP exit fee to them? Reasonable reader: the state plainly has to be able to sign enforceable long-term contracts or it can't govern, so where is the line between ordinary contracting and entrenchment by price tag? Then tell me which reading should win, and whether the voidness clause takes down the whole concession or only the compensation term.
