I teach a course on the philosophy of love and twentieth-century American fiction, and I want to build the Gatsby week around a distinction the philosophers call appraisal versus bestowal. The relevant debate is associated with Harry Frankfurt, David Velleman, and Niko Kolodny, who disagree about whether love is answerable to reasons and where such reasons could come from, but I want to use a simplified version that students can work with without reading the papers. On one side, an appraisal view: loving someone is in part a response to the beloved's properties, and the lover can be asked what there is about the beloved that elicits that response; the value is, so to speak, discovered. On the other side, a bestowal view: love does not merely track a value that was already there before the love arrived; the lover confers value on the beloved, or makes a volitional commitment to the beloved's interests that is not licensed by a prior inventory of their merits. The standing worry about appraisal is that it makes the beloved replaceable by anyone with a slightly better version of the relevant properties; the standing worry about bestowal is that it makes love arbitrary or self-deluding.

Fitzgerald's The Great Gatsby seems to me to dramatize exactly this fault line and to keep moving across it. Gatsby is drawn to Daisy partly by properties that are not purely personal but social: she is the first girl of secure upper-class background he had ever been close to, with the beautiful house and the voice the narrator associates with money. That looks like appraisal, though of a rather suspect kind. But the novel also tells us from its earliest pages that Gatsby had an unusual hopefulness, and that he had built Daisy up into something the flesh-and-blood woman could not plausibly justify; the image outruns its object, which sounds like bestowal tipping over into illusion. I would like you to work out a reading of the novel organized around this question: Is Gatsby's love for Daisy best understood as an appraisal of her properties, as a bestowal of value upon her, or as something the novel shows collapsing between the two? Please go through the scenes that seem to pull each way — I am thinking of the green light, of Daisy weeping over Gatsby's shirts, and of the final chapters — and say what the novel as a whole shows about the relation between a lover's reasons and the beloved's worth. I want the reading to include an honest strain: where does the novel resist the appraisal/bestowal vocabulary, where does the distinction have to be stretched to fit, and what does Gatsby add to the philosophical debate that the standard arguments miss? Finally, because I intend to teach this, please tell me which consideration or piece of evidence would most likely change your verdict about the novel's position, and where your reading is most likely to fail.
