You are a professional philosopher working in Kantian ethics and contemporary bioethics. Please think carefully and slowly, and before you commit to a verdict I would like you to begin by brainstorming the different ways one might fix the meaning of dignity and where each leads.

I have been trying to make sense of a debate between two positions on human dignity. On one side is a broadly Kantian view: dignity is the ground of a special moral status, and it attaches to beings with a rational nature. On this view, the relevant criteria for being a bearer of dignity are roughly these: (1) the being has the capacity to set ends, to deliberate about means, and to adopt and revise maxims; (2) the being has an unconditional, non-negotiable worth, a worth that has no price and cannot be traded off against aggregate welfare or social usefulness; (3) the being is an end in itself, so others owe it duties of respect that do not depend on their inclinations, their relationship to it, or its current psychological state; and (4) this worth grounds at least some claim-rights, such as a right not to be used merely as a means and a right to have one's bodily integrity respected.

On the other side is a deflationary challenge of the sort associated with Ruth Macklin. The challenge says that, in actual bioethical practice, dignity does no independent work: when the person can exercise autonomy, dignity-talk just is respect for autonomy, and when autonomy is gone, dignity-talk is a rhetorical placeholder for other values — the sanctity of human life, the feelings of the family, professional integrity, or a social taboo around treating a human body as mere matter.

Now consider this borderline case. A woman is in a permanent vegetative state after a cardiac arrest and prolonged lack of oxygen. Her brainstem keeps her heart and lungs working, and she has sleep-wake cycles, yawns, and reflex movements, but there is no awareness, no thought, no capacity to set or revise ends, and no prospect of recovery. She was once fully rational. Her family and her clinical team continue to speak of treating her with dignity: they cover her body, lower their voices, address her by name, and insist that the decision to withdraw life support is not like deciding to dispose of a broken object.

Does this woman still have dignity in the sense defined by the four criteria? Go through the criteria one at a time, and say honestly where each is satisfied, where it fails, and where it underdetermines the answer. Then tell me what the case shows: does it show that the rational-nature criterion is too narrow and needs to be revised, that the deflationary challenge is right and the dignity-talk here is really about something else — and if so, what exactly — or that dignity is a genuine value that persists through the loss of the very capacities that supposedly ground it? I am less interested in a yes or no than in where the concept bends, and whether the criteria or the concept should give way.
