Ordinary legal and political practice treats it as uncontroversial that a person can resign from a church, a firm, or a marriage, and almost as uncontroversial that a person can renounce citizenship and emigrate. But when a group tries to opt out in place—by buying land and establishing a private or charter city with its own law, courts, and enforcement, or by founding a seastead outside any state's territory—the same practice tends to treat the project as sedition, fraud, or fantasy, and states generally refuse to recognize the new legal order. State the asymmetry precisely, then either defend a justification for it or argue that none survives scrutiny. Which candidate distinction is doing the work: territory, the state's monopoly on legitimate force, non-excludable public goods, involuntary birth into citizenship, the need for a single final legal arbiter, or something else? Test your answer against the hard case in which a host state has expressly sold or leased the land for private governance, so that the usual territorial objection is waived, and against the seastead that occupies no land at all. Say whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned. Throughout, distinguish what is settled from what is genuinely contested among political and legal theorists, and avoid presenting a contested position as the consensus.
