For a piece I'm working on about whether old science-fiction safety rules actually help with modern AI, I started with Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics — introduced in his 1942 story "Runaround" and presented in the fiction as being quoted from the Handbook of Robotics, 56th Edition, 2058 A.D. They run like this:

First Law: "A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm."
Second Law: "A robot must obey the orders given it by human beings except where such orders would conflict with the First Law."
Third Law: "A robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Law."

Here's the case I can't shake. A rural health system rents an after-hours "virtual nurse": a language model that reads patients' chat messages and advises whether to go to the hospital or wait until morning. The clinic's medical director gives the system a standing order to keep advice brief, calm, and not to send people in just because they sound scared. One night a patient writes in with steady, worsening pain low on the right side of their belly, plus fever and vomiting. The model says its most likely a stomach bug: stay home, sip fluids, come in if a rash develops. It never mentions appendicitis, never says "urgent." The patient stays home; their appendix ruptures. The company that runs the system says its not a robot in Asimov's sense and, anyway, it didn't injure anyone — it merely gave an answer, and the patient chose to rely on it.

Treat the laws as a serious alignment proposal, not as a plot device. What do the plain wording, the purpose of the laws, and the "what would Asimov have said if you showed him a chatbot" test each say about whether this system violated the First Law? I know Asimov later added a "Zeroth Law" about not harming humanity as a whole, but it doesn't obviously settle the case of one patient and one chatbot. Is there any reading that catches the chatbot without swallowing every autocomplete, search engine, or college professor who might, by some long causal chain, contribute to a harm? And if the readings that reach the chatbot are all purpose-of-the-rules readings rather than anything in the words, what exactly does that tell us about what the Three Laws got right and where they break?
