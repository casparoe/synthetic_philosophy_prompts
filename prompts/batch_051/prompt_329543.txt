I've been toying with a term in my graduate seminar and I want someone to kick it's tires before I try turning it into a paper. I call it stigmergic punishment. The picture is this. We talk about cancel culture as if a mob decides to punish someone, but an actual online pile-on rarely works like a court, or even like a lynch mob. What happens is that thousands of people independently make tiny, low-cost public gestures — an unfollow, a quote-tweet, a one-star review, a 'why is this person still employed here' comment, a request that a venue drop them — and each gesture leaves a trace, and that trace then becomes the main input for the next person's decision. The accumulated trace is what does the damage, not any person's actual assessment of the original offense. The eventual deplatforming is often just the last and most legible trace of an environment that had already made the person radioactive. It's ants: no ant plans the nest, each one responds to the pheromones the last ones left. The sentence is written by nobody.

My actual view, the part I want checked, is that most of what gets called cancel culture is not coordinated punishment at all. It's stigmergic: an emergent sanction no single participant intends at full strength. And I think this changes the moral analysis, because the whole debate assumes a punisher. Defenders treat the crowd as a legitimate check on people otherwise insulated from consequences; critics treat it as an unaccountable tribunal. On my account there is no crowd to be legitimate or unaccountable, only a self-amplifying environment of cheap signals. It would also explain why targets so often describe the experience as being buried by nothing in particular — no one decisive act, just paper cuts — and why severity so often overshoots the offense: the size of the sanction tracks how screenshotable or quotable the trace is, not how bad the act was.

So the honest assessment I want is this. Is this a real distinction or am I just renaming informational cascades, reputational cascades, or moral panics with a better metaphor? Does the no-one-intended-it structure actually change the moral analysis, or does it let individual participants off the hook too easily? And where does it break — try to hit me with the clearest counterexample, either a case that is obviously coordinated collective punishment but my account would wave off, or an obvious case of stigmergic sanction that we shouldn't want to call punishment at all.

Please write it as plain continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables — like you're writing me an email.
