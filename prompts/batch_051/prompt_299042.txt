I'm a mathematics graduate student, and a course on formal verification has unexpectedly unsettled how I think about proof. This term I've been using an interactive proof assistant to check the steps of derivations, alongside reading Poincaré's methodological essays about the place of intuition in mathematics. As I understand him, Poincaré held that the life of mathematics is in intuition: logic's role is to check, to justify, to make an argument rigorous, but it does not itself supply the idea, and a logically correct chain gives us a certificate of correctness rather than understanding. The proof assistant has made this concrete for me: I have finished verification sessions knowing that every step went through mechanically, yet without any real sense of why the lemma was true.

Poincaré died before the era of computer-assisted mathematics, so he never had to face this question, but I keep wondering how he would have responded to it. Imagine he could be shown the proof of the four-color theorem, whose central case analysis was carried out by a computer and is too large to check by hand in practice, or a modern theorem whose correctness rests on a machine-checked formalization. Would he have called these proofs in the same sense as a human-surveyable proof, or would he have said that they establish the truth of a theorem without yet giving mathematical understanding?
