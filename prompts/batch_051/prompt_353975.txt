I cover science and ideas, and a philosopher I'd been interviewing about how people make hard calls walked me through an argument he said was a real problem for any "indirect normativity" view — the idea that what I have reason to do isn't read straight off the world but off what a suitably improved version of me would choose. It's been rattling around my head ever since, especially now that I'm deciding whether to publish part of a leaked hospital dataset that could identify some patients.

Here's the argument as I understand it.

(1) A natural view: I have a reason to do something just in case a fully informed, unbiased, flawlessly deliberating version of me would do it. You know the move — "think it through with all the facts, and whatever you still endorse is what you really have reason to do."
(2) "Fully informed, unbiased, flawlessly deliberating" has to mean: all and only the distorting influences on my deliberation are removed.
(3) But we can't say which influences are distorting without already knowing what the correct output of deliberation would be. Is my fear of exposing an innocent third party a bias or a legitimate concern? To classify it is already to take a normative stand.
(4) So the view is circular: it needs the very standard of correctness it was supposed to deliver.
(5) The obvious escape — specify the idealization neutrally, say full non-normative information plus valid inference — doesn't help. That's just one list of psychological features among many, and nothing about the list gives it authority over my actual deliberation except a further normative choice about which features matter.
(6) So the view is either circular or arbitrary.
(7) But then, by the same standard, "what I'd think if I were better informed" has no special status either. Improving my deliberation — gathering more facts, slowing down, checking my framing — is just a psychological change, not progress toward how I should act.
(8) That can't be right. When I consider that publishing the dataset might get a named patient extorted, the thought that I'd reject publication if I knew that outcome is not just another mood; it's exactly the kind of consideration that makes the decision better or worse.

Which step, or the move between which two steps, should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep (1), because otherwise deliberation becomes pure therpay. But I can't see how to keep it and still block (6). I need the diagnosis spelled out step by step. Also, when you answer, give me a read on your confidence: how sure are you of the diagnosis, and which point in it would you least want to defend?
