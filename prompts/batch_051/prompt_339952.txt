I keep circling back to a passage in Locke’s An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter XXVII (“Of Identity and Diversity”), where he writes: “And as far as this consciousness can be extended backwards to any past Action or Thought, so far reaches the Identity of that Person; it is the same self now it was then; and ‘tis by the same self with this present one that now reflects on it, that that Action was done.” I find this account compelling because it gives a clean criterion for mind uploading and gradual replacement: if the silicon successor’s consciousness can be extended backwards over my present thoughts—if it remembers them as its own, in the right first-personal way—then the successor is me, even if no single biological cell or original atom carries over. That view has real systematic virtues: it avoids positing a mysterious immaterial substratum, it explains why I care specially about my future self, and it makes the technology of gradual replacement into a question of preserving consciousness rather than preserving some particular biological stuff.

But I keep hitting a firm intuition that resists this. Imagine a procedure in which one neuron is replaced each day by a functionally identical silicon device; over time the whole brain is replaced while behavior, memory, and self-reports remain continuous. Lockean consciousness-based views say the person who wakes up after the final surgery is straightforwardly me. Yet I have a stubborn sense that there is a further fact here: that the original biological brain was not just a vessel for consciousness but partly constitutive of the person I am, and that by replacing it we have destroyed one entity and created a successor, however psychologically continuous. I don’t want to be the person who insists on a biological substrate merely because I grew up in one, but the intuition doesn’t feel like a bias I can just talk myself out of.

Your job: as a professional philosopher working on personal identity and philosophy of mind, think carefully and, before committing to a conclusion, brainstorm the strongest arguments on both sides. Then walk me through what each option costs. Should I bite the bullet and accept that the digital successor is me, treating my biological-substrate intuition as something to be debunked or explained away? Should I revise or abandon the Lockean psychological-continuity view, perhaps by adding a no-rival-substance condition or an original-substrate condition? Or is there a reading of Locke’s passage on which my intuition and the theory are compatible? I’m asking for a negotiated verdict, not just a pick: tell me what would justify each move, and where you think the final pressure should land.
