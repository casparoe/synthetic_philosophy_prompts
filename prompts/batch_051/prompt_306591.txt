My sister died eight months ago, and I have spent much of the time since watching my own grief closely enough to develop a small theory about it, which I would like someone to assess before I commit to it.

I have been calling it the retraction account of grief. The core claim is that grief is not primarily an emotion about the lost person, and not primarily about the relationship as it actually was; it is the felt price of retracting one's own future-tense thoughts. Each plan, routine expectation, and unspoken assumption in which the person figured gets deleted, and the sadness is the accumulated error signal of those deletions. On this account, the intensity of grief tracks how many of one's simulations of one's own future contained the person, and how specific those simulations were, rather than how much one loved them in some abstract sense.

I think this explains several things I have observed in myself. It explains why I have mourned cancelled routines (the calls, the holiday logistics) at least as much as the person. It explains the waves: each wave is a distinct future-tense prediction being retired on its own schedule, not one continuous mood. It explains why the sadness feels located inside my expectations rather than in a world that mostly looks unchanged. And it yields a practical prediction: grief should be helped more by explicitly contemplating the cancelled futures, writing them out or saying the unsaid sentences, than by generalized comfort or sheer time.

I suspect this is adjacent to something already worked out, and I suspect it may be false in an instructive way. The points should, in whatever order you think best, reconstruct the account in its strongest form, say what it genuinely gets right, name the nearest existing idea or framework, develop the strongest objection, and say where the account breaks or stops being distinctive.
