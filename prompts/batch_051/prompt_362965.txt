I find myself caught between a theory that seems right in its broad outlines and an intuition the theory tells me to distrust, and I want help deciding which should yield.

The theory is the stupid-optimizer view of evolution. Natural selection, on this account, is best modeled as something close to stochastic gradient descent over a fitness landscape: variants that currently reproduce more successfully increase in frequency, supplying a noisy local gradient, while mutation, recombination, and drift supply stochasticity. Because selection can act only on current differential reproduction, it has no access to future payoffs; a valley cannot be crossed because of the paradise on the other side, only by drift, luck, or a move that is already neutral or beneficial in the present. The process has no representation of the landscape beyond the distribution it is currently sampling. This theory is compelling because it unifies a lot at once: the prevalence of kludges and engineering mistakes, path dependence, long sojourns on merely local peaks, the fact that traits are good enough rather than optimal, extinction, and the systematic failure of organisms to anticipate novel environments. It also gives me a disciplined translation procedure: wherever I am tempted to say evolution found a solution, I say instead that a greedy, massively parallel, local search happened to climb to that point, and I refuse to smuggle in foresight.

But I have an intuition that survives all of this. Evolution has produced features that look less like points on a fixed landscape than like upgrades to the search process itself. Canalization buffers the phenotypic effects of mutations, so that many genetic changes are neutral and large-effect changes are relatively rare, while stress can expose hidden variation precisely when novel phenotypes might help. Recombination and sexual reproduction can move a lineage through genotype space in ways that are not local from the point of view of any single allele. Regulatory architecture lets a single mutation switch an entire developmental module rather than jitter one scalar trait. These look like the sorts of things one would expect if selection could act, even weakly, on evolvability: a lineage's disposition to generate useful variation in the future. And yet the theory says selection never favors a step because of what that step will make available later. How does a process that never optimizes for future searchability surround itself with machinery that appears designed to make future search more productive?

I can already formulate the standard moves, but I want a verdict on whether they are adequate or merely confidence-preserving. The debunking move says my intuition is hindsight plus survivorship: I see only the surviving lineages, so I over-credit features that happened to make those lineages more evolvable, and I never observe the vastly many lineages in which such machinery never arose or arose without the right background. The amendment move says the theory is misspecified: selection operates at multiple levels, including on lineages and on the genotype-phenotype map itself, so evolvability can be an object of selection even when no individual organism ever benefits from future variation. The compatibility move says both sides capture something: the machinery had immediate payoffs at every step, and the landscape is not fixed anyway, because organisms modify their environments and their own developmental constraints, so the right formalization is not gradient descent on a fixed landscape even though the no-lookahead core of the theory is untouched.

Walk me through the strongest version of each option, what would justify it, and what would count against it, and then tell me which you would pursue and what consideration should make me switch. In particular, is the hindsight-and-selection-bias debunking of my intuition a real argument here, or is it a way of protecting the theory from recalcitrant data?
