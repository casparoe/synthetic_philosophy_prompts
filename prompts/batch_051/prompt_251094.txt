I have spent most of this year sitting with someone whose pain system has turned on them — chronic neuropathic pain that no drug quite touches — and I have come out of it unable to dismiss a view I would once have filed under millenarian science fiction: David Pearce's hedonistic imperative. As I understand it, the view holds that suffering is not a necessary feature of sentient life but a product of blind Darwinian selection; that biotechnology will eventually let us phase out the biological substrates of suffering and replace them with gradients of well-being; and that we are morally required to do so. The passage I keep returning to is from the section "Saving Vehicles With Bad Drivers" of Pearce's The Hedonistic Imperative:

"Blind selective pressures have acted on living organisms over hundreds of millions of years. Darwinian evolution has powerfully favoured the growth of ever more diverse, excruciating, but also more adaptive varieties of psychophysical pain. Its sheer nastiness effectively spurs and punishes the living vehicles of genetic replicators. Sadness, anxiety and discontent are frequently good for our genes; they're just psychologically bad for us. In absolute terms, global suffering is probably still increasing as the population explosion continues."

I want you to test that passage for me. I know that living with someone else's pain makes a view feel true without making it true, so I am asking for the strongest possible map of what would count against the position the passage supports. Treat the passage as a bundle of separable claims: empirical claims about selection and the adaptiveness of negative affect; a conceptual claim about the distinction between pain as a damage signal and pain as felt suffering; and a normative claim about what follows from the empirical story. For each, tell me what would have to be true for that claim to fail, and whether its failure would be fatal to the hedonistic imperative or merely awkward. Say what the position predicts that a rival such as ordinary palliative medicine does not. I also want defeaters of the philosophical kind: commitments the view turns out to have that would be unacceptable, cases it cannot describe well, or results that would show it collapsing into a rival. Be explicit about which defeaters Pearce's own text already concedes or anticipates, and which ones a committed abolitionist would dismiss and why. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which single finding or argument should move me most. Finally, assess whether the position has been qualified — by its "gradients of well-being" rather than flat bliss, by its "over the next thousand years or so", by its hedged "probably" — into unfalsifiability, and whether that insulation is a problem for a manifesto of this kind or an appropriate feature of a long-run technological forecast.

Please organize the response into these sections, in this order, with these approximate lengths:

1. The position and its moving parts (200 words). Restate Pearce's view as the passage commits him to it, separating the empirical, conceptual, and normative claims.
2. Empirical defeaters (350 words). What observations, results, or theoretical developments would count against the selection story, the hedonic treadmill, the separability of nociception from felt suffering, or the claim that global suffering is increasing; rank them from decisive to awkward.
3. Philosophical defeaters (350 words). What unacceptable consequences, lost goods, consent problems, or value-theoretic objections would count against the imperative even if the biotechnology worked; include at least one that a committed abolitionist should concede as counting against the view.
4. Decisive to awkward: a ranked summary (250 words; a table is acceptable). At least six specific defeaters, each with a one-line verdict.
5. Is the view insulated? (200 words). Whether the position has been stated so flexibly that no possible finding could touch it, and whether that is a defect or a feature here.
6. My verdict (150 words). What should most move someone in my position, and how I should hold the view going forward.
