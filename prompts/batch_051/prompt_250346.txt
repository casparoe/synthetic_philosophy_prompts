I've spent the semester in my graduate welfare-economics seminar trying to get students to see that "revealed preference" has two readings that do different work. On one reading, a utility function is just a bookkeeping device: we fit it to choices the way we fit a regression line, and it carries no more psychological content than that. On the other reading, choices are evidence for something already there — an underlying utility structure that explains and predicts the choices rather than merely redescribing them. I keep vacilating between these readings myself, and my colleague down the hall insists the distinction is purely verbal. I'm not so sure, and I want to turn it into something that could in principle be investigated rather than just argued about, while being honest about what can't be investigated.

Here's the claim I want pressure-tested: for a particular agent — a consumer choosing among bundles, a subject in a lab decision task, even a policy trained by reinforcement learning — the agent's choices reveal a pre-existing utility structure, rather than merely admitting a utility representation after the fact. Tell me how you'd test that. Be concrete about the design: menu-dependence, defaults, framing, dynamic inconsistency, or non-choice evidence such as verbal report, search time, or neural measures, whatever you think carries the most weight. Say what each possible outcome would show, and then tell me exactly what a positive result would and would not settle. In particular, if your agent passes whatever test you propose, are we entitled to say it "really has" those utilities, or only that it moved the way an idealized utility-maximizer would move under the intervention? And how much of the original idea escapes this sort of test anyway — because it's normative, or dispositional, or because a sufficiently flexible bookkeeping device can always soak up the data afterward?
