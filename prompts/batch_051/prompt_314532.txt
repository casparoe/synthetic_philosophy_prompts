You are a political philosopher who specializes in the paradox of tolerance and has advised universities, libraries, and membership organizations on speech and assembly policy. I chair a Speaker and Events Committee at a public university that reviews roughly forty requests per term: student groups seeking rooms or funding, outside speakers renting campus space, and tabling requests on the central quad. The university is formally committed to free inquiry and viewpoint neutrality, and our decisions are routinely appealed to the provost and occasionally become local news, so the procedure must be defensible in writing. But we keep confronting the classic problem: some requests come from groups whose stated program is to use every available platform to undermine the openness of inquiry itself, and we hear, on one side, that our commitment requires tolerating even views we abhor, and, on the other, that tolerance rightly ends where its enemies begin. I want you to help me turn a carefully interpreted version of the paradox of tolerance into a reusable decision procedure — a gated checklist or ordered set of tests, not a one-off opinion — for deciding when the university should approve a request, deny it, or approve it with conditions.

Before you write the procedure, take a paragraph to reconstruct the principle you are operationalizing and to say explicitly what tolerance is for in your account — for instance, whether it is an unconditional stance toward speakers or a norm that exists to protect the continuing possibility of mutual criticism, exposure to error, and repair across disagreement — because the downstream tests will turn on that choice. The procedure must be usable by committee members with no philosophy background, in about twenty-five minutes per request, using only information available at the time of the request, and it must separate factual findings (for example, whether a group has any plausible capacity to act on its stated aims) from judgment calls (for example, how to weigh a threat to targeted students against the precedent set by viewpoint-based restriction). It should explicitly handle at least these hard cases: a small but genuinely intolerant student group with no appreciable power; an outside speaker whose views are repellent to much of campus but who has no organizational tie to violence; a coalition group in which one faction has an intolerant program that the others disavow; and a group that books rooms repeatedly under innocuous descriptions in order to occupy scarce time slots. Apply the finished procedure to three worked cases, including at least one in which the procedure returns an answer I will not like. End by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do, where it is likely to misfire, and which decisions remain irreducibly political or moral rather than procedural.
