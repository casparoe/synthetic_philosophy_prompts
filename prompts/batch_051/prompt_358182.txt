I've been going around in circles with my roommate about Hofstadter's superrationality. The classic setup is this: two perfect copies of the same reasoning program play a one-shot prisoner's dilemma. Payoffs: both cooperate gives each 3, both defect gives each 1, and if one defects while the other cooperates the defector gets 5 and the cooperator gets 0. Since the copies are identical, the argument goes, the only real possibilities are both cooperate or both defect, and cooperating is better; that's the superrational move.

Now we tried to make it messier. Suppose the two copies are still identical, but at run time each has an independent probability p of a glitch: with probability p the copy doesn't execute the algorithm at all and instead flips a fair coin between cooperating and defecting. Both copies know p, but neither can tell whether it's currently glitched. My roommate says superrationality still says cooperate, because barring glitches the two copies will do the same thing, and defecting just trades 3 for 1 in the symmetric branch. I think the glitches break the 'my action is evidence about yours' correlation enough that defection starts to look better once p is high enough. Work through this variant carefully. What does the superrational analysis actually recommend for arbitrary p, is there a threshold where the recommendation flips, and does the idea of superrationality even stay coherent once symmetry is noise-forged like this? I'd like to see the reasoning detailed, not just an appeal to ordinary expected utility.
