I sit on a prize committee that still runs under rules drafted in 1962, before any of this was imagined. The governing clause says: "A proof shall be admitted to the competition only if it is set down in writing in such a form that a single mathematician of ordinary competence may, by his own unaided inspection, verify each step of the reasoning." This year there are two submissions. The first is a proof of the four-colour theorem that reduces the whole claim to roughly two thousand finite configurations, and then reports that a computer programme has checked each of them exhaustive; the written part includes the algorithm, the source code, and a description of how the search works, but not the several thousand pages of case-checks themselves. The second submission does the same sort of thing but adds that the entire proof has been encoded in a proof assistant, so a seperate, independently written programme has verified that every inference follows from the axioms. So the question is whether either one satisfies the clause. I'm less interested in how the committee should actually vote than in what "by his own unaided inspection" can mean now. Does reading and testing the code count as inspecting the proof? Does trusting one machine to check another satisfy the clause's purpose even if it violates its words? What would the people who wrote that clause in 1962 have said if they'd been asked about these cases? And if the readings diverge, which one should govern and why?
