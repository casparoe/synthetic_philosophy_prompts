I direct a clinical prediction unit in an NHS trust. My analysts turn routinely collected data into individual risk estimates that clinicians act on — whether to start anticoagulation, refer for surgery, or send a patient home. Several times a week an analyst is asked to produce a risk figure for a particular patient, and the software can return materially different figures depending on which group of previous patients the patient is compared with. The same patient sits in many reference classes at once: all patients with the same diagnosis, patients matched on age and sex, patients with the same test results, patients managed in the same trust. The estimates differ, and nothing in the data by itself picks one class as the correct one. This is the reference class problem, and it has become the main source of unresolved disputes in our unit.

Rather than continue deciding these matters ad hoc, I want a reusable procedure my analysts can follow when they have to choose the reference class for an individual risk estimate. The procedure must:

- be laid out as questions to ask in order, with a clear statement of what counts as an adequate answer to each;
- include explicit stop rules where the correct output is "do not issue an estimate" rather than a forced number;
- say what the analyst should do when two or more reasonably chosen classes give different answers and there is no statistical reason to prefer one;
- mark every step at which a value or policy judgement is being made, and state who in the trust should own that judgement;
- be usable by analysts who know statistics but have no philosophical training; and
- leave an audit trail that a clinical governance committee could review.

Assume we can only use the registry data we already hold; we cannot commission a new study or import an external model. The conceptual constraint you must respect is that the reference class problem is not dissolved by a rule such as "always use the narrowest class with at least 40 cases", because that merely relocates the choice into the sample size and the similarity metric. Please keep the distinction between a class that is too broad — stable but only partly informative — and one that is too narrow — closely matched but highly unstable — and be explicit about how a procedure should trade those off without pretending the trade-off is purely statistical.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical cases, showing exactly which questions it asks and what it outputs:

1. A 61-year-old woman with atrial fibrillation, well-controlled hypertension, and no previous stroke. The registry offers a large class of all atrial fibrillation patients, giving an estimated annual stroke risk of 4.3%, and a smaller class matched on age, sex, blood pressure control, and stroke history, giving 6.9%. The two classes overlap heavily but give materially different estimates. What does the procedure instruct?

2. A 74-year-old man whose particular combination of kidney function, frailty score, and medication burden is matched by only 14 previous patients, nine of whom had a serious adverse event within a year. The narrow class suggests a risk around 64%, with very wide confidence limits; the nearest wider class suggests around 18%. Does the procedure issue an estimate, issue it with a caveat, or refuse?

3. A patient whose risk differs substantially depending on whether the reference class is defined to include a characteristic the trust is legally and ethically wary of using in individual decisions — for example, ethnicity or an area-level deprivation index — even though including it materially improves calibration for that patient. The analyst would strongly prefer to include it because it makes the number more accurate. State what the procedure says, including whether it hands the decision to a named person or committee.

In at least one of the three cases the procedure should return a conclusion the trust will not welcome; do not soften it. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do — the cases on which it will systematically misfire — and say what the analyst should record when the procedure terminates at a stop rule.

I would like the answer written in British English.
