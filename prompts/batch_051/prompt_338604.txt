Here's a counterfactual I've been using in my aesthetics seminar, and I want you to actually work through the mechanisms rather than hand me the usual line about authenticity.

Suppose that in 1875 a Glasgow chemist, messing around with varnishes, discovers a cheap process that takes any oil painting, leaves the original untouched, and produces a second canvas indiscernible from the first by every physical or chemical test available before 1950. It reproduces the pigment, the varnish, even the pattern of age cracks and the dust. It will not copy frames, sculpture, paper, or anything else. He publishes the reciepe freely, and within a decade these duplicates are everywhere in the European and American art trade, usually without the current owner knowing wich canvas is the one they originally bought.

Now give me your best reasoned prediction of how the next seventy-five years play out. I want the chain of reasoning explicit: which effects flow from dealers' incentives to authenticate, from collectors' willingness to pay, from the law's need for a stable object of title, and from museums' need to show something? Does value migrate almost entirely into documented provenance, so that a well-documented old canvas fetches a fortune and its perfect twin fetches scrap? Or does provenance become too easy to forge too, and the pricing scheme comes unglued? Does the category of an original shrink into a bare historical marker, or collapse outright? For each major effect, tell me whether it is robust, plausible, or a shot in the dark, and say what would have to be true about people or institutions for that effect to reverse. Then give me a one-sentence summary of your overall verdict on whether the category of an original survives, changes, or collapses, as your very last line beginning 'Verdict:'.
