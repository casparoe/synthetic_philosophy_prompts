I'm a graduate student preparing a seminar presentation on Sen's liberal paradox, and I've realized I don't trust my ability to separate the theorem itself from the commentary around it. Everyone says the result shows that individual rights and Pareto efficiency clash, but I suspect the real action is in how a right gets formalized, and I want to locate it precisely. Below is my own reconstruction of the paradox as a numbered argument. I've made the steps explicit so that, if one of them fails, it can be pinned down exactly; if none of them fails, I want to know which premise is the philosophically vulnerable one.

Set up a two-person society, P and L, and three alternatives: B_P (P reads a certain book and L does not), B_L (L reads it and P does not), and B_0 (neither reads it). P, who dislikes the book, prefers B_0 to B_P to B_L: he most wants no one to read it, but if it must be read, he would rather read it himself than have L read it. L prefers B_P to B_L to B_0: he most wants to see P read it, but he would rather read it himself than see it go unread. Now consider the following argument:

(1) A social preference relation must be transitive: if it ranks one alternative above a second and the second above a third, it must rank the first above the third.

(2) Pareto: if both individuals strictly prefer one alternative to another, the social preference relation must rank the first above the second.

(3) Minimal liberalism: each individual has at least one protected pair of alternatives that differ only in what that individual himself does, and the social preference relation must follow that individual's preference over that pair, regardless of how anyone else ranks the alternatives.

(4) P's preference over the pair {B_P, B_0} lies in P's protected sphere, so by (3) the social preference relation must rank B_0 above B_P. L's preference over the pair {B_L, B_0} lies in L's protected sphere, so by (3) the social preference relation must rank B_L above B_0.

(5) Both prefer B_P to B_L — P because, if the book is to be read at all, he would rather be the reader; L because he most wants P to read it and only secondarily wants to read it himself — so by (2) the social preference relation must rank B_P above B_L.

(6) Together, (4) and (5) force the cycle B_0 above B_P above B_L above B_0, which transitivity forbids. Therefore no social preference relation can satisfy all three conditions at once.

My question is this. Is the argument valid as stated, and if not, which step fails and under what exact reading? My suspicion is that step (3) is where the trouble enters, because it encodes a right as society's obligation to adopt the right-holder's preference over the protected pair, rather than as something weaker — say, a constraint on the kinds of grounds that may be used to override the right-holder's choice, or an exclusion of certain other people's preferences from that decision. But I cannot tell whether that suspicion dissolves the paradox or merely relocates it. So: either identify the precise failure in one of the steps and give the correct formalization of the relevant premise, or, if the argument is valid as a piece of reasoning, say which premise should be rejected or weakened, and defend that choice. I want the diagnosis at the level of the steps, not a survey of responses.
