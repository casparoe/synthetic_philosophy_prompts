Im a retired fisheries biologist — I spent most of my career on farmed salmon and then shrimp hatcheries — and since retiring ive been reading philosophy of animal minds and animal ethics. It's made me realise the aquaculture welfare debate is a bit of a mess, because people slide between several different questions without noticing: whether the animal matters at all, whether what matters is the death or the life, whether good paperwork about water quality is the same as good welfare, and whether improvements should be a legal floor or just a premium label for rich consumers. I'd like you to build me a sorting instrument for a standards working group I now advise informally.

The group has fish-health vets, salmon and shrimp farm managers, one person from an animal NGO, and a seafood buyer. They don't know the philosophical labels and don't want to learn them, so the questions all have to be concrete and about farms. I want a decision tree — or a short quiz with a scoring key, whichever comes out cleaner — that sorts a person into a position on how much farmed fish and crustaceans matter and what practices should be required. Build it from the logical space rather than from names: write the diagnostic questions first, lay out the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the real positions in that space. Where a combination is empty, mark it and say in a sentence whether it is incoherent, too unattractive for anyone to hold, or just unexplored. Flag any positions the tree cannot seperate and say why.

Keep it to five to seven questions, and make at least three of them use these cases: (1) a shrimp hatchery that removes eyestalks from broodstock but keeps excellent water quality and uses a fast kill at harvest; (2) a salmon farm with low stocking density but no preslaughter stunning, where fish are just air-exposed until they stop moving; (3) the choice between judging a farm by what it provides (space, oxygen, feed) and judging it by what the fish actually experience (fin damage, wounds, disease, abnormal behavior). The questions should force a choice without giving away which answer lands you in which camp.

The leaves should cover at least these positions, if the space allows them: only a reasonably painless death matters and the rest is just added cost; the whole life matters, which means animal-based outcome measures rather than input box-ticking; crustaceans are morally different in kind from fish and should not be in the same standard; welfare improvements should be sold as a certified premium rather than required from everyone; and if you can't guarantee the life, you shouldn't be farming fish at all. If any of those ends up unoccupied by the combinations you allowed, don't force it — mark it empty and say why. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and make the scoring key transparent enough that a person who landed somewhere they don't like can see exactly which answer put them there.
