I cover emerging technology and the ideas behind it, and I keep returning to a puzzle in aesthetics that seems newly urgent. For the past quarter century, philosophers have studied imaginative resistance: readers will happily accept that a story's world contains dragons, time travel, or people who photosynthesize, but they balk when a story asks them to accept that a morally repugnant act is right, or that a character who performs such an act is praiseworthy. They understand the words; what they seem unable or unwilling to do is take on the story's moral evaluation as part of the imagined world. The standard view treats this as revealing something deep about evaluative concepts, about the nature of imagination, or both.

Now suppose that a growing share of the narrative text people actually read is drafted by large language models and selected by engagement-optimizing recommendation systems. These systems can generate morally inverted fictions—stories in which, for example, torturing a captive is presented as virtuous, and the reader is invited to share the evaluation—with complete fluency, no authorial unease, and none of the hedging or instability that human writers typically display when they attempt the same thing. If the producer of the text shows no resistance whatsoever, does the classic imaginative-resistance argument survive, or does it need repair? I can see at least three positions. The first: the argument is untouched, because a language model does not imagine in the relevant sense, so its fluency is beside the point. The second: the argument survives but its target moves, because the interesting resistance now belongs to the human reader or the human trainer, not the generator. The third: the ease with which the machinery exchanges one moral valence for another shows that the original asymmetry was a contingent fact about human motivational psychology—an unwillingness rather than an inability—and therefore a phenomenon to be explained, not a constraint on what fictions can be built. Which position is closest to right, and why? I am asking what happens to the argument, not what policy should follow.
