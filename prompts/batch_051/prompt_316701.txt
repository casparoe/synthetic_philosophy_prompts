I follow an explicit policy in my scientific work for living with underdetermination — the fact that the available data never force a single theory. The policy has three parts: (1) a rival model earns my consideration only if it makes at least one new, discriminating prediction, meaning some feasible observation that would come out one way on my model and another way on the rival; (2) until such a prediction is produced, I do not lower my confidence in the model I already hold at all — I treat the rival as if it had never been proposed, rather than distributing my credence across all the models that fit the data; and (3) I never count mere consistency with the existing data as evidence for a new model, because after-the-fact fit is cheap and only novel predictions count.

The situation that made me test this policy: a collaborator recently fitted the same dataset with a model that has one extra free parameter and reproduces the published numbers about as well as mine. It treats a pattern in the data as signal where my model treats it as noise, and I could not think of any feasible experiment that would separate the two. Because the collaborator could not name a discriminating observation either, I kept my model at effectively full confidence and did not mention the rival in the write-up. I know that in principle the data underdetermine the choice among indefinitely many curves through any finite set of points, and that if I gave every such curve a hearing I could never believe anything at all. But I also worry I am using that fact to protect my own model from alternatives that merely have not generated a distinctive test yet — alternatives that might be more elegant, use fewer parameters, or unify anomalies my model currently handles with added assumptions.

Is this policy a defensible response to underdetermination, or is it a bias dressed up as a method? In particular, does the sharp distinction between predicting something new and merely fitting what we already have carry too much weight, given that a model can earn the right to be taken seriously for other reasons? Does clause (2) require an account of why the incumbent model deserves its status, beyond having been there first? And how should I tell, from the inside, whether I am applying the rule because it makes inquiry possible or because it makes my own view safer than it should be?
