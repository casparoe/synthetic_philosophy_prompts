Two members of my philosophy of biology reading group became caught up in a running argument over whether the propensity interpretation of fitness genuinely escapes the old tautology objection to natural selection. Neither was aware that I was recording the exchange; afterward both asked me to referee, and I could not. Here is how the argument unfolded.

1. The first opened with the classic worry: if fitness is measured by actual reproductive success, then the claim that the fitter type spreads says little more than that the type that spreads is the type that spreads. Natural selection, so understood, cannot explain why one lineage increased and another did not.

2. The second replied that this attack only lands on a crude, outcome-based definition. Fitness is usually understood as a propensity: an expected reproductive output fixed by an organism's traits in a specified environment, like the bias of a die, which exists before any particular throw. A genuinely fit organism can die in a flood; fitness concerns what tends to happen, not what actually happens.

3. The first answered that the die analogy buries the problem rather than solving it. A die's bias is only learned from a long series of throws, so if the evidence for a fitness propensity is the very survival data it was meant to explain, the theory has exchanged tautology for unfalsifiability. It would become impossible for a type that spread to count as unfit, and nothing would have been explained.

4. The second said this is exactly where the analogy is more useful than the first allows: a die can be weighed, X-rayed, or machined to a known asymmetry before it is thrown. Likewise, fitness can be estimated from forward-looking features—foraging efficiency, disease resistance, clutch size, developmental stability—none of which is identical to final reproductive success. The claim that the type with the higher estimated propensity will, in expectation, out-reproduce the other is then a substantive statistical hypothesis, and it can fail.

5. The first replied that those forward-looking features earn their place only because they have correlated with reproductive success in past populations, so the criterion remains outcome-relative at one remove. And because fitness is relative to an environment and a timescale, any poor performer can be redescribed as fit with respect to some more carefully specified setting. Whatever empirical content exists is supplied by the operationalization, not guaranteed by the concept.

6. The second conceded the environment-relative point but denied that it shows circularity: solubility is relative to a solvent, toxicity to a dose, fragility to a stress, and none of these dispositions is thereby empty. There is a causal ordering from trait differences to fitness differences to differences in expected offspring, and that ordering has a direction of dependence that a tautology lacks; it can turn out to be false. The residual difficulties—drift, noisy estimates, choosing the right timescale—are real, but they are epistemic problems about measurement, not a problem of conceptual emptiness.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, defensible but overstated, or clearly mistaken, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who was more right overall, and identify the most important consideration that both participants failed to raise.
