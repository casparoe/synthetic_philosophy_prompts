I keep noticing a structural parallel between textualism in statutory interpretation and the intentional fallacy in literary criticism, and I cannot tell whether it is deep or merely verbal. Textualists hold that a statute means what the ordinary public meaning of its words was at the time of enactment, and that the legislature's private purposes, committee reports, and floor statements are not authoritative guides to that meaning. Wimsatt and Beardsley, writing about poems, hold that a work's meaning is discovered in the work itself, not reconstructed from the author's plans, notes, or stated intentions. Both positions treat the text as something whose meaning is fixed independently of its author's mental states; both refuse to settle meaning by asking what the author was trying to do.

But the justifications diverge. Textualism is typically defended by rule-of-law values: fair notice, predictability, the constraint that only what was actually enacted binds citizens, and the separation of lawmaking from judging. The intentional fallacy, by contrast, was defended as a point about criticism and evaluation: the work succeeds or fails as a public artifact, and private intention is neither reliably available to the reader nor a criterion of success. So the two positions might merely overlap on the slogan that the text, not the author, controls.

Is there a shared anti-intentionalist principle here — something like the thesis that all publicly binding linguistic objects have their meaning fixed by convention rather than by individual design — in which case the two positions genuinely reinforce one another? Or would a textualist be mistaken to treat the New Critics as allies, because the intentional fallacy was a doctrine about aesthetic judgment, not about the legitimate exercise of authority over future conduct? And does the parallel cut the other way: is a purposivist who appeals to legislative history making precisely the move Wimsatt and Beardsley call fallacious, or does the fact that a statute is an authoritative command addressed to future action make the author's intention relevant in a way that it is not for a poem?
