I teach ethics, and every so often a former student asks me to turn some bit of theory into something their colleagues will actually use. Right now the student runs responsible-sourcing at a midsized apparel company — the kind that doesn't own factories but places very large orders with suppliers in Bangladesh, Vietnam, Cambodia, and occasionally discovers a subcontractor nobody knew about. She told me her team basically has one question they answer over and over: should we keep doing business with this factory? The problem is that the only test they've been using is a mutual benefit test — as long as the workers are better off than they'd be if the factory went away, the relationship is counted as fine. I've been trying to convince her that this misses the whole phenomenon of mutually beneficial exploitation: cases where both sides gain relative to their baseline, but the buyer or the factory owner still takes advantage of workers' lack of decent alternatives, or grabs nearly all the surplus, or profits from a background injustice it didn't create but now sits on top of. She's now convinced enough to ask for an actual procedure, not an essay.

So here is what I want you to produce: a decision procedure for a non-philosopher sourcing/compliance person to apply factory by factory, in under an hour per factory, and that can be defended to their legal department and to senior management. It should at minimum distinguish "lousy but not exploitative by this company," "exploitative and we should either stay and fix the terms or exit," and "not exploitative in the transaction itself but still supported by some other wrong in the supply chain that we shouldn't be party to." It should say what to do when you cant get reliable information about a worker's next-best alternative — which is most of the time. It should also guard against the two lazy moves the company keeps making: waving away every finding with "the workers chose this," or with "they'd be worse off without us." To make it concrete, work through two or three hypotheticals — including one where the procedure requires the company to pay meaningfully more even though current wages are already above the local alternatives and workers would line up around the block for the jobs. Be explicit about where the procedure stops being philosophy and becomes a manager's judgment call, and where it is most likely to misfire in the real world.
