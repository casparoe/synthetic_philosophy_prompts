I have a standing policy for decisions in which probabilities are only partly known, and I want to know whether it is defensible or is a bias dressed up as a rule. The clearest case runs like this. I can draw a ticket from lottery A, which says on the side that it contains 50 winning and 50 losing tickets, or from lottery B, whose 100 tickets are divided into winning and losing tickets in some fixed proportion I am not told; I know only that the number of winning tickets in B lies somewhere between 0 and 100. My policy is to evaluate each option by the worst chance of success compatible with what I have been told, and then to choose by that number. If I am hoping for a winning ticket, A scores 50 percent and B scores zero, because a composition with no winning tickets is compatible with my information. If I am hoping for a losing ticket, A still scores 50 percent and B again scores zero, because a composition with no losing tickets is compatible with my information. So I choose A both times. A colleague says this is the Ellsberg pattern and that it cannot be rational: for my two choices to come from any single estimate of how many winning tickets B contains, that estimate would have to be below 50 to explain the first choice and above 50 to explain the second. My policy, he says, manages to avoid assigning B any probability only by assigning the relevant event whichever probability is most convenient for refusal. To me, though, this is caution rather than contradiction. I refuse to pretend I have a probability I do not have; I treat missing information as missing and protect myself against the worst composition my information still permits; and I would rather be provably safe against every composition compatible with my information than be spuriously precise in a way that could be badly wrong. What I want to know is whether the policy is defensible as a policy for someone who faces many such decisions, not just this one. Is my colleague right that it is incoherent, or is his demand for a single probability the real mistake? If my policy is defensible, what exactly is it a rule about: the world, my information, or the cost of being wrong? If it is indefensible, is there a nearby policy that keeps the caution toward unknown compositions without whatever failing mine has, and where precisely does my version go wrong? Lead with your bottom line in a sentence or two, then argue for it, all in continuous prose as if writing a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
