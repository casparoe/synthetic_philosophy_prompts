Here's an asymmetry in how people use the "what if everyone did that?" move, and I can't tell whether it's tracking something real or is just a rhetorical tic. You hop a turnstile or drop a wrapper on the trail, and the question is treated as near-decisive: if everyone did that, the system or the place would be ruined, so don't. But nobody says "what if everyone became a nurse?" as a reason not to become a nurse, even though if literally everyone became a nurse the world would be in at least as much trouble as if everyone dodged fares. Same for moving to the same city, buying the same index fund, learning the same trade, having a second kid — the test isn't even entertained. The difference isn't just legality, and it isn't just that the universalized outcome is less vividly bad. Is there a principle that explains why the question works as an objection in the first kind of case and is a non-starter in the second — something about self-defeatingness rather than bad outcomes, or about relying on others not doing the thing while you make an exception of yourself? Or are we just aiming the question at things we already disapprove of and then pretending it did independant work? Give me your best account of what, if anything, justifies the asymmetry, and say whether the universalizability test — Kant's maxim version, Hare's idea that it's a logical property of moral language, or the act-based generalization-argument version — survives it.
