I'm chairing the ethics subcommittee of our state's crisis-standards-of-care advisory group, and we've been asked to send the full group a decision memo on one provision we can't agree on. Under our draft pandemic triage protocol, when ventilators run short, everyone is scored on a validated physiology index that predicts survival to about six months; the ones with almost no chance even with ventilation get comfort care, and everyone above that threshold is put in the queue. The fight is over the tie-breaker. Right now the text says that if two patients score similarly on predicted survival, the vent goes by lottery. A bloc of intensivists wants to replace that with a life-years rule: among patients with similar short-term odds, give the vent to whoever is expected to live longer after discharge, adjusted for quality of life — in effect maximizing QALYs from the machine. Their case is that this lets a 30-year-old with excellent recovery prospects beat a 75-year-old with the same odds of surviving the illness, which is exactly what a scarce-resource allocation should do, and that refusing to compare future life-years is just squeemishness. The other side says the life-years rule smuggles age and disability discrimination back into a protocol that otherwise avoids them, and that a lottery is the only tie-breaker that visibly doesn't rank some lives above others. I need a memo for the full task force that takes a definite position: adopt the QALY tie-breaker, keep the lottery, or adopt some modified version (a life-cycle rule that gives priority by years already lived, say, or a lottery weighted by life-years but bounded so no one gets zero weight), with the reasoning stated so a hospital lawyer or administrator could act on it. Pick one and defend it, and make sure the strongest argument for whichever side loses is stated fairly before you explain why it doesn't carry the day.
