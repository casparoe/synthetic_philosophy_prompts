I have been trying to pin down an inconsistency I half-see in the debate over whose law governs the internet, and I have come to suspect that there may be no principled basis for it. Consider how differently two structurally identical assertions of jurisdiction are received. On one side, the European Union's data-protection rules are applied to platforms incorporated elsewhere, simply because those platforms process the personal data of EU residents, and almost no one in the liberal democracies where these debates take place treats that as an intolerable extraterritorial power grab. On the other side, when a state with restrictive speech or public-order laws orders a foreign platform to remove speech that is lawful where the platform operates — or to hand over data about the state's own citizens — many of the same people reject the assertion of authority as censorship, even though the affected people are inside the state and the content reaches them there. In both cases the underlying theory of jurisdiction is the same: the law of the place where information is received, or where the affected person lives, should govern that information, wherever it happens to be stored. Yet one assertion gets described as the ordinary application of law to the internet, and the other as extraterritorial censorship.

I want to know whether any defensible principle can generate that asymmetry, or whether it is just the familiar habit of treating the laws we approve of as universal and the laws we dislike as territorial. The candidate principles I can think of are: privacy regulation is content-neutral while speech regulation is content-based; liberal and rights-respecting polities are entitled to an extraterritoriality that repressive ones are not; privacy harms are more concrete than harms to reputation or public order; and intermediaries should simply follow the law of every audience they knowingly serve. Each of those seems to fail under hard cases. Your task is to do better than my candidates. Give the strongest justification you can for drawing a line somewhere, stress-test it against nearby cases — a global delisting order for defamation, a right-to-be-forgotten request against foreign media, a democracy's false-information law enforced against a foreign platform, a state's demand for user data to prosecute a local crime — and then say plainly whether you would keep the asymmetry or abandon it.
