I lead a small evening reading group for adults with no formal philosophy background. We have just worked through the early part of Section IV of Hume's Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, and we stopped at his billiard-ball example: someone who has seen one ball strike another and the second move has no basis, from the mere sensible qualities of the balls before impact, to infer that the same effect will follow the next time. The group found this unsettling but basically persuasive, and I want to use that momentum to move into the deeper problem — I won't name it yet, but it is the one that gets its modern sharpening from Goodman's "grue." I don't want to just explain it to them; I want a plan for how I should run the next ninety-minute session. What has to be secured from the Hume discussion before the grue problem can land, and what can I safely set aside? I need an order of ideas with reasons for that order, a single carrying example that will do the real work (and what it must have and lack), and a map of the misconceptions I should expect: the likely moves like "but we know the sun will rise because of physics," or "the past is all we could mean by evidence," or "this is just a verbal trick." For each one, where in the session should I head it off rather than wait for it to harden? I also want a closing check for understanding that would show me they have seen the shape of the problem rather than just felt confused, and a short statement of what I should openly refuse to resolve at this level.
