I'm leading a reading group on Anscombe's writings about the ethics of war, and we have assembled a short record of her verdicts on particular cases, all reconstructed and paraphrased rather than quoted. She calls Truman's decision to use the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki murder, and she denies that the aim of ending the war or the number of lives it may have saved changes that verdict. She says the same about deliberate area bombing of cities, where the point of the raids is to destroy civilian housing and break the population's will to continue. She treats as permissible in principle a precision attack on a munitions plant inside a city, where the plant is the object of the attack, the civilians killed in adjoining buildings are foreseen but not wanted, and no less destructive alternative is available, provided the expected civilian deaths are proportionate and are not themselves part of the plan. And she calls the shooting of enemy prisoners of war in reprisal for the enemy's killing of one's own prisoners murder, on the ground that prisoners are no longer taking part in the war and may not be killed in order to influence the enemy.

We now want to extend this record to a case of a different kind. A commander proposes a total blockade of a coastal region: no food, fuel, or medicine may enter. The stated and intended object is to starve the enemy army and break its capacity to fight, not to kill civilians. The enemy government is certain to distribute the remaining food to its soldiers before its civilians, so most of the foreseeable deaths will be civilian deaths from hunger and disease. The commander does not want those deaths and would avoid them if he could, and he has no other way to achieve the military object before the next campaign season. The expected civilian deaths are very large, but the blockade is expected to shorten the war substantially.

What would Anscombe have said about this blockade, or, if that is too strong, what does consistency with her recorded verdicts commit her to? We are less interested in the bare answer than in the reasoning. In particular: first, state the rule or rules that would make her four recorded verdicts hang together, and say which candidate rules the record already excludes. Second, apply each remaining candidate rule to the blockade and say exactly where they agree or divide. Third, give your best prediction of her verdict, identify the recorded case that is the closest analog, and state which principle she would be adopting either way. Fourth, describe one further case, structurally different from the blockade, that would best separate any rules that still divide on it, and say what her answer in that further case would establish.
