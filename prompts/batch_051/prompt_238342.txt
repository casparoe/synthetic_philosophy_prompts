I keep noticing that a lot of places seem to run several different theories of what a vote is at the same time, and I'd like someone to reconstruct the implicit theory of the franchise, if there is one. Australia will fine you for not showing up, so there voting looks like a civic duty, like jury service or taxes. Then Austria and Scotland (for its own elections) have lowered the voting age to 16, which sounds like they think the competence bar is low or maybe that competence isn't the point at all. And New Zealand lets permanent residents who aren't citizens vote in national elections, while a scattering of cities let noncitizens vote in local ones, which looks like the franchise is about being affected by the government rather than being a member of the polity. These three moves seem to pull against each other: duty versus right, citizen versus resident, competent adult versus older teenager. Reconstruct the theory of what a vote is for and what qualifies someone to cast it that could make the whole package coherent — or show me that there is no single theory doing the work and the package is just a bunch of different rationales stuck together. Then tell me what a country would have to change if it actually ran on the view that voting is a duty owed to everyone whose interests the state affects, rather than a right of competent adult citizens. And no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables — continuous prose only, like an email.
