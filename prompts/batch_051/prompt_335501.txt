I am a teaching assistant for a history-methods course, and I am grading a batch of final exam answers. I would like your committed judgment on one particular answer rather than a survey of possible readings of it. Here is the examination question:

"Historians of epidemic disease, climate, and geological catastrophe often write as though non-human processes are themselves historical actors, with phrasings such as 'smallpox conquered the Aztecs,' 'the Little Ice Age destabilized early modern states,' and 'the volcano ended the Minoan civilization.' Drawing on the distinction between causes, background conditions, and agents, critically assess how far this idiom is warranted. Do such statements assert anything more than that non-human factors were causally important? Discuss at least one way in which the idiom could misfire."

Here is the student's answer, reproduced as written:

"I think the idiom is warranted, but only as a metaphor for causal importance, not as a literal attribution of agency. When historians say smallpox 'conquered' the Aztecs, they do not believe the variola virus had intentions, plans, or a point of view. They are foregrounding a factor whose effects no human party fully anticipated or controlled, and which would be understated if it were treated as a fixed background condition rather than a dynamic cause. This is no more animistic than saying 'the recession destroyed the government,' which everyone understands as a causal claim about economic processes, not a claim that recessions have minds.

Where the idiom does become dangerous is when it is used to imply inevitability, because inevitability and agency are the real opposites. To say smallpox conquered Mexico is to suggest the decline of the Aztec state was already settled by biology, when in fact the Spanish introduced and spread the disease; they also enslaved, killed, and disrupted the population in ways that made the epidemic far more lethal. Agency, properly speaking, belongs only to entities that can form intentions and select among alternative courses of action, so a virus is a cause but not an agent.

The standard objection is that this criterion of agency is too restrictive, because it excludes institutions, markets, and other collective entities to which historians routinely attribute agency. But that objection fails: an institution just is an aggregate of intentional human agents, and any statement about what 'the Crown' or 'the market' did can be translated, without loss, into statements about what individual human beings intended and did. So the intentional criterion is not too restrictive; it simply forces us to be precise.

In sum: I would permit the idiom as a shorthand for causal salience, reject it as a literal claim about non-human agency, and add that its main rhetorical risk is that it quietly shifts responsibility away from the human choices that shaped how destructive these non-human events were."
