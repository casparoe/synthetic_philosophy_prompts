Me and my friend got into a long argument about two charities. One funds a surgery that stops one child from being in severe, unremitting pain for years. The other one is a dirt-cheap public health program that would prevent forty million people from having a mild but genuine irritation—think a bad eyelash in the eye for an afternoon—over the course of a week. We both knew the standard utilitarian move: add it all up and the second one probably wins on total suffering. But my friend still said the first one was the obviously right choice, and I had the exact same gut reaction even while I was making the aggregation argument. Set aside the usual practical worries about overhead, evidence quality, and whether the program really reaches everyone; assume the second intervention works exactly as advertised.

Once I noticed it, I started seeing the asymmetry everywhere: people are horrified by one person being tortured but not by a product that gives millions of people a mild stomachache; someone would break the law to save one trapped child but wouldn't pay a dollar to spare a stadium full of people a papercut. I want to know whether there's a defensible principle that puts concentrated harms in a different category from huge numbers of tiny harms—something that says no number of small pains adds up to outweigh one horrible one—or whether that's just scope insensitivity and a bias toward vivid images. Give me the strongest principle you can find that would generate the asymmetry, the case that most pressures it, and an honest verdict about whether we should keep the asymmetry or give it up.
