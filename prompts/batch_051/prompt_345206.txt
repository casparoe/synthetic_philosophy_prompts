A colleague in my philosophy of mathematics reading group presented the following argument, and now several of us worry that it proves too much, though each step seemed acceptable as he said it.

(1) Let f be the polynomial x² + 1. In the complex numbers it has two roots, the numbers customarily written i and −i.
(2) Complex conjugation is an automorphism of the complex field: it preserves addition and multiplication, fixes every rational number, and interchanges the two roots of f. Consequently, no statement expressible in the language of fields with the rationals available as constants can be true of one root and false of the other; the algebraic structure itself is unable to tell the two roots apart.
(3) If two objects are indiscernible in the relevant structure, then the structure provides no basis for singling out one rather than the other; any label attached to one of them is an arbitrary convention rather than a genuinely structural description.
(4) The expression "the root of f with positive imaginary part" looks like it singles out one root by a genuine property, not by a bare label.
(5) But having positive imaginary part is not definable in the language of fields. It presupposes a choice of orientation on the complex plane—a decision about which half-plane is to count as upper—and complex conjugation does not preserve it, since conjugation sends the root with positive imaginary part to the root with negative imaginary part. The apparent property is therefore only a label in disguise.
(6) It follows that there is no mathematical fact of the matter which root is which: "i" and "−i" are, at best, two arbitrary names for things the mathematical structure itself does not distinguish.
(7) But if there is no fact which root is which, then the claim that f has two distinct roots cannot be meaningful either. To assert that there are two roots, one would have to distinguish one root from the other, and step (6) says exactly that no such distinction exists.
(8) Yet f does have two distinct complex roots; over the complex numbers it splits into two distinct linear factors. The argument has therefore made a trivial fact of elementary algebra meaningless, which cannot be right.

Where does this argument go wrong? I would most like to keep (2), since symmetries seem to be precisely how we separate structural facts from artifacts of notation; but I also cannot give up ordinary algebra. Which step should be rejected, and what does giving it up cost?
