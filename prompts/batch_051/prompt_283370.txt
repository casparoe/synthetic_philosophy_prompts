Write a deadpan venture-capital pitch memo, roughly 700 words and broken into short section headings, from the founder of a moral-trade clearinghouse. The product lets two parties with incompatible moral frameworks trade concrete concessions — one commits to a year of veganism, the other commits to a matching donation to a global-health charity — so that each advances the other's values without either updating toward the other's view. The audience is an effective-altruism-adjacent seed fund whose partners already accept moral trade in principle but worry about implementation. The satire should target what moral trade actually assumes rather than a strawman: how a concession is verified and priced, what escrow means for a moral commitment, whether the clearinghouse is selling indulgences under another name, what happens when a party's values drift before the contract matures, and where profit fits in a market meant to make disagreement productive. The founder must remain deadpan and internally consistent; the indulgence objection should leak through the risk section and the fine print rather than through any wink. A reader who already knows the moral-trade literature should find the satire fair, and one who doesn't should finish understanding the promise of moral trade and roughly why it might be too clean.
