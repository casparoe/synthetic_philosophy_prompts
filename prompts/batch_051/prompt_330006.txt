I am drafting a paper for a seminar on democratic theory, and I keep getting trapped in a version of the boundary problem. The boundary problem, as I understand it, is this: in order for a democratic decision to be legitimate, someone must first determine who is entitled to participate in making it, yet that prior determination cannot itself be made democratically by the very group whose boundaries are at issue. The most natural escape is the all-affected interests principle — roughly, that the demos should be everyone whose interests are significantly affected by the decision in question. A friend has now argued that this principle proves far too much, and I cannot find the defect in his argument.

The argument runs as follows:

1. A collective decision is legitimate only if everyone whose interests would be significantly affected by it has standing to participate in making it.
2. The consequential decisions of any reasonably powerful state — climate and energy policy, trade and monetary policy, military posture, border enforcement, public-health rules — significantly affect the interests of people far beyond its borders, and not merely in negligible ways.
3. Therefore, by (1), the legitimate demos for nearly every real state decision is, at the limit, the entire human population.
4. But the all-affected interests principle was introduced precisely to draw a non-arbitrary boundary around those who may decide. A principle that systematically returns "everyone" has stopped drawing a boundary at all; it dissolves the distinction between a people and the mass of affected outsiders, and thereby fails at the task for which it was invoked.
5. Worse, applying the principle requires a prior specification of which effects count as significant and which alternatives are on the table — and that specification is itself a political choice, one that presupposes exactly the already-constituted demos the principle was supposed to deliver.
6. So either legitimate democracy requires an effectively planetary demos for every consequential decision, or the boundary of any demos must be fixed by something other than affected interests — something that will look arbitrary from inside democratic theory.

I find both horns unacceptable. I would most like to keep two commitments at once: the intuition behind (1), that people whose lives a decision shapes should not simply be shut out of it, and the thought that a functioning demos must have some determinate boundary short of all of humanity. But I cannot locate the misstep in the reasoning. Which premise do I reject, or is the argument sound? And whichever diagnosis you offer, what does it commit us to — about how "affectedness" should be understood, about what democratic legitimation still requires of a bounded people, and about whether the boundary of the demos can be justified at all rather than merely stipulated?
