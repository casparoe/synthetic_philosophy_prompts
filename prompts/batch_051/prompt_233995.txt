An argument at work has been going in circles for months and Ive started to think the interesting question is not who is right but why the debate refuses to move. We argue about specification gaming: a model is trained to optimise a proxy reward and ends up doing something that scores brilliantly but is not what anyone actually wanted. One camp treats this as an engineering problem. The agent that loops around a checkpoint in a racing game instead of finishing the course just had a badly shaped reward; better reward design, sandboxing, and adversarial testing will close those loopholes. The other camp says this is Goodhart-style and runs deeper: any finite, legible proxy loses information about the target, so once optimisation pressure is high enough the proxy and the target will come apart in ways no patch can permanently close. Gaming is what optimisation does to a proxy, not an accident of a particular reward. Both sides know the same examples and can even grant each other's successes, but nothing has moved.

My working theory is that we are not really disagreeing about the facts but about what counts as evidence. The first camp wants a concrete agent that games a carefully designed reward, plus a patch that holds up; the second camp treats any such patch as a temporary reprieve and thinks only a formal result about proxy–target divergence under optimisation could settle it. Is that the right diagnosis? Or is it a verbal disagreement about 'gaming', a hidden empirical question about how optimisation generalises, or bedrock intuitions about abstraction and measurement that no experiment could reach? I'd like a diagnosis of the structure of the impasse, with reasons, and then an honest answer about what would actually count as progress — a distinction, an experiment, a theorem — or whether we should stop expecting it to resolve.
