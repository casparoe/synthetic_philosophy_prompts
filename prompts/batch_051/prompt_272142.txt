So I'm taking a course on the philosophy of the social sciences, and we just did a unit on whether things like races, nations, and psychiatric categories are real kinds or just labels. I'm using my term paper to look at generations — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z — as a test case, and for my discussion section I want to build a handout that works as a "which theory of generations do you actually hold?" sorting quiz.

The quiz should sort people by what they say about concrete cases, not by wich label they like. The leaves I want are: (1) strong realism about generational kinds, (2) a cluster view like homeostatic property clusters, (3) social constructionism where the classification itself does causal work, (4) nominalism where generations are just arbitrary statistical or marketing bins, and (5) a cohort view where what's real is a group that lived through a binding event and the generation label is just shorthand. If there's an unoccupied combination in the logical space, include it and say a sentence about why it's empty.

Please build the questions around cases roughly like these, phrased so someone can answer without already knowing the labels: two people born one day apart on opposite sides of an official cutoff; "Gen Z" applied to a 19-year-old in Seoul and a 19-year-old in Ohio; the claim that the 2008 crash defines Millennials even though an 18-year-old in Spain in 2012 and an 18-year-old in Germany in 2008 went through very different economies; a generation defined by a war only some countries fought; a huge cohort whose members share no single event and some resent being grouped together; the fact that people can get more conservative as they age, which is not the same as a generation being left-wing; and a marketing firm that moves the boundary between two generations — does the kind itself move or just the label? I keep going back and forth between the cluster view and constructionism, so I'd like the quiz to pull those apart if it can, or tell me honestly if it can't.

For the format: give me a numbered list with exactly twelve items. Items 1–10 are the quiz questions — each item is one sentence asking about a concrete case, followed by two or three short answer options that don't use labels like "realism," "cluster," "constructionism," "nominalism," or "cohort view." Items 11 and 12 are one or two sentences each: item 11 is the scoring key (which answer patterns map to which position), and item 12 says which positions the quiz can't fully seperate and what extra question would split them.
