I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I keep running into an asymmetry in how Chesterton's fence gets applied around me. The principle, as I understand it, is that before you remove an existing practice—a piece of legacy code, an old process, a rule whose original author is long gone—you first have to understand why it was put there, and if you can't reconstruct the reason, the default is to leave it alone. My colleagues invoke this constantly when someone proposes a deletion, and they often pair it with a Burkean-sounding point that a long-standing arrangement carries accumulated experience no single person can fully see.

But they do not apply anything like it in the other direction. When someone proposes adding a new abstraction, a new safeguard, or a new process, no one asks why it has been absent all this time, and an inability to explain its absence is never treated as a reason to pause. Yet an addition is also a change to a complex system whose hidden dependencies we only partly understand. If a practice can exist for a reason nobody remembers, a practice can also be absent for a reason nobody remembers—and a new addition may quietly break whatever arrangement that absence is doing.

So the asymmetry is this: we treat the existing state as a default that requires a historical justification to change in one direction, but not in the other. A removal must be licensed by an understood past; an addition merely needs to survive a list of foreseeable objections.

I am not asking whether caution about change is sometimes wise. I am asking whether this specific asymmetry is defensible. The strongest justifications I can produce are that existing things have survived some kind of selection while proposals have not, and that removal destroys information while addition only creates it. Take the strongest version of whichever principle you find—or a better one—and test it against nearby cases where an addition is just as irreversible or just as likely to destroy tacit information as a removal. Then say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or dropped. If it is really just status quo bias dressed up as wisdom, I want to hear that directly.
