I've been defending a package of views about how to update on uncertain evidence in a formal epistemology reading group, and I want to know whether it actually hangs together before I write it up. The package is roughly this. (1) I think when evidence is less than fully certain — say I glimpse a shape at dusk and shift to 0.7 that it's a fox without becoming certain of any exact proposition — the rational update is Jeffrey conditionalization (probability kinematics): keep the probabilities conditional on each cell of the relevant partition fixed, and just move the partition cells to their new values. (2) I think the order in which pieces of evidence arrive shouldn't matter; if the total body of evidence is the same, my final credences should be the same regardless of sequence. (3) I think rational updating should be invariant under how the evidence gets carved up: it should depend only on the probabilistic content of the evidence, not on which partition or redescription I happen to use to state it. (4) I also think there is nothing deeply special about learning a proposition for certain — in an idealized model, any rational update should be representable as conditioning on some proposition or other, so certainty isn't a magic threshold.

At first this felt like a tidy package: (1) generalizes standard conditioning, and the rest are just invariance-ish niceness conditions. But I've started to worry the package papers over some real tension, and I can't tell which bits are load-bearing and which are just my taste. Walk me through it: are these four jointly tenable? Which commitments actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they conflict, and what is the cheapest repair — what would I have to give up or modify, and what does that cost? Please end with a single line beginning "Bottom line:" giving your one-sentence summary of where the package comes apart first.
