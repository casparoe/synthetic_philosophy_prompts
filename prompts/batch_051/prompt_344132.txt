I'm putting together a small group curriculum on the gifts of the Spirit, and I've realized the labels "cessationist" and "continuationist" are doing basically no work in the room—people sort themselves into them by what church they grew up in, then hold a mix of views the labels don't actually predict. So I want a "which position do you actually hold?" sorting quiz, not another lecture comparing the labels. The audience is bright but non-specialist Christians; they can handle nuance, but the questions need to be concrete cases or claims they can answer without already knowing theology jargon.

Build it as a decision tree or a short scored questionnaire, around eight to ten questions max, with at least six leaves. The leaves have to cover the whole spread: the person who says miracles and gifts simply stopped when the apostolic age ended; the person who says the gifts were scaffolding for the apostolic deposit of revelation and the closing of the canon shut them down, while ordinary providence continues; the person who denies that anyone today holds a standing gift of healing, tongues, or prophecy but insists God still answers prayer with genuinely miraculous healings; the "open but cautious" sort who says all the gifts remain available but not promised or normative, and prophecy is fallible and testable; the Pentecostal-charismatic sort who says the gifts should be expected in normal Christian life, with tongues as a normal sign of Spirit-baptism; and the restorationist sort who says modern apostles and prophets are a real office the church should recognize. If some of these split further—for instance a hard "no more miracles at all" view versus a "no more sign gifts, but providence can be surprising" view—split them and say which cut you made.

The diagnostic work is what I'm actually after. Make sure the instrument seperates at least these four cuts: (a) "God does something episodically and specially" versus "a person has a stable, repeatable gift"; (b) "possible but not promised" versus "expected as part of normal Christian life"; (c) "ended when the last apostle died" versus "ended when Scripture was complete"; and (d) "a modern prophecy carries something like old-covenant prophetic authority" versus "all modern prophecy is fallible and always subject to correction." Phrase each node as a concrete scenario or claim someone could respond to without knowing which camp would claim them—say, a parishioner who gives a specific, testable word of knowledge about a stranger's illness, or a missionary who suddenly preaches in a language she has never learned. Also flag any pairs of positions the instrument can't separate; I already suspect the "soft cessationist who believes God heals today" and the "open but cautious person who is skeptical of every actual report" may end up with identical answers. And wherever a combination looks logically available but nobody actually lands there—like, does anyone hold that tongues ceased but prophecy continued in full force?—say whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.
