Two friends of mine, Dana and Marcus, got into it after a seminar on risk. The seminar had used the estimate that the Riverton Bridge has a 3 percent chance of collapsing in the next twenty years, based on the class of truss bridges of its age, design, and maintenance grade. Dana said that number is reference-class gerrymandering. You could also put the bridge in the class of all truss bridges, all bridges in its state, all bridges inspected by the firm that did its last review, or all bridges whose name starts with R, and get different numbers. So 3 percent isn't really a fact about this bridge; it's a fact about whichever group you stuffed it into. Marcus said some groupings are clearly better than others. Age, design, and maintenance are causally relevant to collapse, initials are not, and the fact that a bad analyst can gerrymander doesn't show that no grouping is defensible. Dana came back: calling a factor causally relevant is just giving the same move a respectability upgrade, because you still have to decide which causal factors matter, how fine-grained to make them, and when a class is too small to trust, and those decisions are not read off the bridge. Marcus said if you follow that line you end up saying there's no single objective probability that this bridge collapses, which would make cost-benefit analysis and insurance pricing arbitrary. Dana shrugged and said maybe that's the right conclusion: single-case probabilities are relative to the reference class you care about, and the bridge just has various frequencies in various classes. They asked me to settle it, but I honestly couldn't. Can you referee the exchange, move by move? For each move, say how strong it is and what it establishes, then say who is right about what overall, and whether there's a principled solution to the reference class problem or whether Dana's final point is basicly correct.
