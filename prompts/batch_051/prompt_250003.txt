After a reading-group argument about the norm of assertion, I was asked to stop gesturing at positions and actually write down my commitments. I have done that below, and I would like someone to stress-test the set before I have to defend it. My confidence in the individual items is moderately high; my confidence in their joint consistency is not. Here is the position, written out as plainly as I can.

1. Assertion is governed by a genuinely epistemic norm. There is a standing requirement of the form: assert that p only if you stand in the right epistemic relation to p. Violating this norm is not merely being unhelpful, dishonest, or practically unwise; it is a distinctive kind of defect in the assertion itself.

2. The correct relation is knowledge, so the rule is: assert that p only if you know that p. My main evidence is the pattern of challenge and retraction. A flat-out assertion is answerable to the challenge how do you know, and if the speaker cannot meet that challenge, the assertion is retracted. This pushes me away from truth-based, justified-belief-based, and reasonable-belief-based norms.

3. Because knowledge entails truth, I accept normative factivity: if p is false, then flat-out asserting p violates the norm, even when the speaker was careful, rational, and fully evidence-responsive. Such a speaker may be blameless, but blameless violation is still violation.

4. However, I am convinced that some selfless assertions are proper. My favored case is a creationist biology teacher in a community that overwhelmingly rejects evolution, who privately disbelieves the relevant claims but follows the standard curriculum and flat-out tells the class that humans and other primates share a common ancestor. I want to say the assertion itself satisfies the norm, not merely that the teacher is excused by his job or is blameless. That seems to show that the norm cannot simply be a sincerity or belief requirement.

5. I also have the standard lottery intuition: you may not flat-out assert that your ticket lost before the drawing merely on the strength of the odds, even when the odds are overwhelming. I take this to support the knowledge norm, because the speaker is maximally justified in believing the ticket will lose without knowing it.

6. That said, I do not want the norm to be so demanding that ordinary conversation becomes a minefield. I think people often flat-out assert unremarkable things on ordinary fallible evidence — that the flight leaves at six from a remembered schedule, that someone's keys are on the table from a quick glance, that a restaurant will be open based on its usual hours — and I want to say those assertions are often proper, not violations that we charitably overlook.

I am asking for an audit rather than a lecture. Tell me which pairs of commitments are actually in tension and which are only apparently in tension, and order the real tensions by severity. For each real tension, say what the cheapest fix is and what it costs — which of my commitments has to give or be reinterpreted. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to clean up the whole set. Please write the answer in American English.
