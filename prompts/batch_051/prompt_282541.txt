I used to play a tabletop game in which changing the rules was itself a legal move: on your turn you could propose a rule change, and after a vote it became part of the game, even when it changed the rule about how rule changes happen. Our group eventually got into the deep trouble the game seems designed for — someone amended the amendment rule so it no longer covered itself, someone proposed a rule that forbade its own repeal, and we spent an evening arguing about whether a rule that had deleted itself could still be cited to justify the deletion. I've kept thinking about that evening, and about the way a rule can turn on itself: a rule that cancels itself, a shield for itself, a rule that revises the procedure that let it be made.

I'd like a poem that captures this, and I want the poem's form — not just its subject matter — to enact the idea. Please write a villanelle in which the two refrains are rules of such a game. Follow the villanelle's architecture of returning refrains alternating and then meeting in the final two lines, but deliberately break its rule of verbatim repetition: each time a refrain returns, amend its wording in a way that changes what it permits, forbids, or applies to. Each amendment should read as a plausible rule change, not just a synonym swap. The poem should end with the two amended refrains side by side in the closing couplet, now contradicting or undercutting each other so that no consistent set of rules remains — and it should be a paradox that a reader can't resolve by going back to the earlier stanzas, because those versions no longer govern.

Someone who knows games of self-amendment should recognize the structure for what it is, and someone who has never heard of them should still come away with a good poem rather than a puzzle with rhymes. After the poem, add a single final line beginning "Verdict:" that states in one sentence what paradox the poem's form enacts.
