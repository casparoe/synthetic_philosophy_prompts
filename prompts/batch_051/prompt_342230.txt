I have no academic background in philosophy or law, but I have been thinking on my own about capital punishment and recently read John Stuart Mill's 1868 speech in Parliament in which he argued against abolishing the death penalty. The part that stayed with me is near the end, where he turns to the possibility of executing an innocent person. As I understand him, he argues that the risk is real but much smaller than abolitionists suggest, because juries demand a very high degree of certainty before convicting in a capital case, because the Home Secretary's power to commute acts as an additional safeguard, and because the same style of objection, if followed consistently, would condemn many other practices that carry a small risk of death and that those making the objection do not reject. He then asks whether the deterrence gained is worth that small risk, and answers that it is.

I want to know whether that move actually works, and I think the best way to test it is to force two positions to produce a joint document. Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a deterrence-first retentionist and an innocence-risk abolitionist. The retentionist accepts the forward-looking aim of punishment, believes that capital punishment deters some murders, and holds that the risk of executing the innocent is low enough that abolishing it would lose more lives than it would save, including future victims who are themselves innocent. The abolitionist does not deny that a deterrent effect may exist, but holds that the state may not accept the possibility of irrevocable wrongful execution as a cost, because the person executed by mistake is used as a means of deterrence without having committed the capital crime, and because the error cannot be corrected.

The document should be in British English and should have two parts. First, a numbered set of agreed claims, precisely worded so that both camps can sign them, about how capital cases are actually tried, what the death penalty is supposed to achieve, what a mistaken execution does to the deterrent justification, and which parts of Mill's argument are merely factual claims. Second, a disagreements table. Each row should state one exact proposition in dispute, phrased in neutral terms; give the retentionist's formulation of their own position and the abolitionist's formulation of theirs, each in wording the respective side would accept; identify the crux question that would have to be settled for one side to move; and propose a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on that crux. For each crux, record in advance what each side predicts the evidence would show.

The statement should also engage directly with Mill's passage. I want to know whether his comparison between the risk of wrongful execution and other socially accepted risks of death is something both sides can include in the agreed claims, or whether the abolitionist must reject the comparison itself. If their disagreement reduces to a difference about how to weigh small probabilities of irreversible harm against uncertain future lives saved, say so exactly, and say whether that part is a genuine disagreement or a verbal one. Do not just report each side's position; produce the document they could both sign, with the residual disagreement made exact.
