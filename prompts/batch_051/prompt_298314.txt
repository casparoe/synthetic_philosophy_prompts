I have been working through the problem of infinite regresses, having come at it from two directions at once: the cosmological argument, where the regress of movers or causes is standardly treated as an objection, and the theory of justification, where the question is whether a chain of reasons may run forever or must bottom out. I find that I hold a roughly unified view. Call a regress vicious when the original task remains permanently unperformed because each step can be carried out only once the next step has been completed; the series is a queue of preconditions, and no member of it is ever actually reached. Call a regress benign when it is generated from something already secured, by a rule already in hand, so that the infinity is an indefinitely long tail rather than an indefinitely postponed starting point. On this test, the regress that takes us from a proposition p to the truth of p to the truth of that, and so on, is benign, while the justificatory regress and the regress of movers are vicious in the interesting sense.

I want you to tell me what would count against this test. Treat it as a claim about what viciousness consists in, and give me a map of its exposure rather than a survey of the literature. Which cases would a defender of the view concede are awkward, and which would be fatal? In particular, press on the distinction between a precondition in a queue and a consequence generated from a base: can it be drawn sharply enough to classify a regress that is equally well described in each way? Are there benign-looking regresses that this test wrongly lets through, or vicious-looking ones it wrongly condemns — for example if a defensible infinitism about justification is correct, so that a non-terminating chain of reasons can still succeed? If the test turns out to be correct only after one has already decided how to frame the original problem, does that show it is not a test of viciousness at all, or does it show that viciousness properly belongs to the pair consisting of a question and a series? Please start by brainstorming candidate defeaters before you rank them, then sort them from fatal to merely awkward. I would like a definite, committed verdict in the end: does the test survive in a hedged form, and does the hedged form still explain why some regresses are rejected and others accepted? Answer as a professional philosopher working on explanation and the metaphysics of dependence. Please write in British English.
