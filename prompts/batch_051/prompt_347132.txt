In ordinary moral judgment, drunkenness does double duty. When someone says something cruel, bigoted, or unexpectedly candid while intoxicated, we tend to treat the utterance as especially revealing: drink is supposed to have stripped away inhibition and exposed what the person really thinks. Yet when the same person does something cruel or reckless while intoxicated, we often treat the drink as a mitigating or excusing condition: his judgment was impaired, he was not himself. The very state that is taken to be a window into the true self when it produces words is treated as a veil over the true self when it produces deeds. Is there anything that justifies this asymmetry?

Take seriously a contrasting view suggested in Plato's Laws: that intoxication, in a controlled setting, is valuable precisely because it is a test of character—drink exaggerates and exposes the underlying conditions and desires that sobriety hides, so that they can be trained. If that picture is right, it is unclear why only drunken speech should be diagnostic and drunken action excused. Supply the strongest defense of the ordinary asymmetry, test it against nearby cases such as anger, fatigue, or hypnosis (where we likewise divide between what a state reveals and what it excuses), and say whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned.
