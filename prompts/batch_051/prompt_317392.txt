I'm a novelist, and one of my characters is an evolutionary biologist who wont stop saying that "the fittest" is doing two different jobs—and that the two can point in opposite directions. I want her to have a clean one-paragraph example, something she could sketch on a napkin while talking to a non-biologist. Build me a thought experiment where a gene spreads through a single, freely interbreeding population even though it lowers the survival or reproduction of the organisms that carry it. Constraints: realistic (a mouse or a wildflower, nothing sci-fi), no kin selection, no group selection, no heterozygote advantage, and the gene can't be doing anything useful for the body. The gene's edge should come from how it behaves in replication or meiosis, not from helping anybody. After the scenario, spell out what gene-level and organism-level selection each predict, why they dissagree, and which feature of the case is doing the work. Then tell me how a defender of the organism-level view would try to dismiss the case.
