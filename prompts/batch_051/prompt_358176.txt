I'm presenting a paper at a constitutional-theory workshop next month. I defend the thesis that describing an emergency measure—a border closure or a lockdown—as illegal-but-legitimate is conceptually unstable: what gets called legitimacy is either a prediction that the measure will win retrospective legal or political ratification, or an unavowed moral judgment about necessity and proportionality wearing institutional language, and neither reading gives the label independent justificatory force. My argument runs: (1) the canonical examples split into those two readings; (2) everything the legitimacy label is asked to do can be done by prediction-talk or moral-talk; (3) retaining the label obscures the real trade-off between rule-of-law values and outcome-based justification. The audience is mostly legal positivists who treat legal validity and moral legitimacy as distinct questions, administrative lawyers who work on emergency powers and retrospective ratification, two public-health ethicists who defended lockdown orders, and one political theorist who holds that emergencies expose the way every legal order rests on a political decision that legality itself cannot ground. Give me a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense: the objections in roughly the order this audience will raise them, phrased as each camp would put them, which objections have real force, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a short reply for each with a note on where the reply is soft, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
