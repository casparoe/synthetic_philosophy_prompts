I run a small sovereign-risk and long-range forecasting desk in a finance ministry. Every quarter we must give ministers a forecast about at least one durable institution — a state, empire, federation, military alliance, or dominant firm — estimating, for 30-year and 100-year horizons, the probability that it will survive in recognisable form and its most likely mode of failure. The analysts are generalists with about a week per forecast; the note must be one page and must give a number with a range, because the minister distrusts hedged essay answers. The desk repeatedly falls into the inside view: it builds a plausible story from the case's details and ends up confirming the minister's priors. I want a reusable outside-view procedure instead, in the sense of reference-class forecasting: begin from the distribution of outcomes in a class of sufficiently similar past cases, then adjust sparingly and only for documented respects in which the present case differs.

Please write me that procedure as a sequence of steps an analyst can follow. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer and what the analyst should do if no sufficient answer can be given. Include rules for: (i) constructing the reference class before looking at the case's individual features; (ii) combining more than one defensible class; (iii) handling survivorship bias and gaps in the historical record; and (iv) placing a ceiling on how far an inside-view argument may move the base rate. Mark explicitly the steps where judgement enters and cannot be eliminated, and the point at which the procedure should hand the question back as political rather than predictive.

Then run the procedure on two cases. Case A: a 150-year-old imperial federation, dominated by a core nation, with a restive periphery and slow demographic decline; the minister says its founding treaty makes it uniquely durable. Case B: a technology platform that has dominated its market for two decades and whose executives claim it is a new kind of empire to which no historical base rate applies. Make sure at least one application produces a conclusion the client will dislike, and explain which step of the procedure forces that conclusion. Close with a paragraph on what the procedure cannot do. Write your answer in British English.
