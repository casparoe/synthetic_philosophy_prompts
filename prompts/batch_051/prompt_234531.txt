I am a novelist writing a short essay on Kant's On a Supposed Right to Lie from Benevolent Motives. I want to test whether my reading of one specific paragraph is defensible before I develop it further. The paragraph I have in mind is the one in which Kant describes the murderer at the door. I will paraphrase its argument rather than reproduce its wording. Kant imagines that the speaker answers truthfully that the friend is not in the house, but the friend has in fact left without the speaker knowing, and the murderer meets him outside; or the speaker lies, and the friend leaves by another path the speaker did not foresee, and the lie brings the murderer to him. Kant's point seems to be that the truth-teller is not responsible for these unforeseeable outcomes, while the liar is responsible for the direct consequences of his lie, so the duty of truthfulness remains intact even in this case.

My essay argues that this paragraph is more radical than that summary suggests. The usual reading is that Kant is making a point about moral luck: the liar becomes answerable for whatever follows from his lie. But I think the deeper point is about authorship, and it is especially sharp for a novelist. Kant is refusing to let the speaker write himself into the event as the one who controls outcomes. The liar, unlike the truth-teller, has made the situation his own in a way that makes him answerable for the plot. That is why the murderer example unsettles people who construct fictions: we make stories in which characters lie and we arrange consequences, but Kant seems to say that a real lie, unlike a represented one, makes the liar the author of consequences he cannot revise.

Please give me comments on this essay-in-progress. Specifically: (1) is my paraphrase of the paragraph faithful enough that the argument would not strike someone who knows the essay as an obvious misreading? (2) where would a careful Kantian object to the analogy between lying and authorship? (3) is the literary framing doing any philosophical work, or merely decorating the thesis? Be specific, and flag any place where I seem to be importing assumptions from the practice of fiction rather than from Kant's actual argument.
