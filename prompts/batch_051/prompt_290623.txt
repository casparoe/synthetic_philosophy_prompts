I'm presenting a paper next month at a small works-in-progress workshop in criminal law theory, and I'd like to walk in with a Q&A prep sheet rather than improvise. The paper's thesis is that statutes of limitations do something more interesting than the standard story says. The standard story treats them as responses to the decay of evidence over time and to a defendant's interest in eventual freedom from indefinite legal exposure. I argue that these rationales are real but incomplete: they cannot explain why a limitation period should bar prosecution when the evidence has survived unusually well (a reliable confession, DNA, or video), nor why a merely forgetful community would not regain the right to prosecute if the evidence happened to survive. I defend instead what I call an identity-attenuation rationale. Drawing on psychological-continuity accounts of personal identity, I claim that the desert-relevant features of the person who would be punished decades later — their evaluative commitments, their quality of will, the deliberative standpoint from which the act issued — will typically have been revised or replaced, so that the person before the court is only a partial successor of the agent who acted. On my view, a limitation period is the state's acknowledgment that, beyond a certain stretch of time, the presumption of relevant discontinuity becomes strong enough to defeat the state's claim to punish. The paper is deliberately non-empirical: it defends the moral structure of this rationale and leaves the choice of any particular period as a separate legislative question.

The audience is not naturally on my side. It consists mostly of criminal law scholars and moral philosophers who work on responsibility, blame, and desert and who have strong retributivist leanings; there are also two or three civil-procedure specialists who think limitation periods are purely administrative rules about stale evidence and finality; and the workshop organizer works in a Strawsonian tradition about blame and holds that quality of will is assessed at the time of the act, not at the time of sentencing. I expect them to be unsympathetic to anything that sounds like a claim that the later self is not the earlier self unless I make the mechanism of discontinuity precise, and they will press hard on the consequences for unrepentant offenders, for fugitives who benefit from their own concealment, and for victims.

Prepare the prep sheet. I want the eight to ten objections I am most likely to receive, each assigned to its rough source (for example, the retributivist, the proceduralist, the Strawsonian), phrased in the vocabulary this specific audience would use, and ordered by how likely and how damaging they are. For each objection, give me a roughly two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is softest or likely to collapse under follow-up. Tell me up front which points I should concede before the questioning starts, and close with the question I should most hope nobody in the room asks.
