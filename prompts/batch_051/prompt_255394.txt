I teach ethics and political philosophy, but formal social choice theory is a bit outside my home turf, and a colleague in PPE recently cornered me with an example I can't stop picking at. Imagine one copy of a scandalous novel and two people, A and B. A has control over whether A reads it or nobody does; B has control over whether B reads it or nobody does. A is a bit prudish: her top preference is that nobody reads it, but if someone has to, she'd rather read it herself than have B read it. B is not prudish: his top preference is that A reads it, then that B reads it, then that nobody reads it. If A follows her ranking and B follows his, A declines to read and B reads, so B ends up with the book—even tho both of them strictly prefer that A read it instead. And if you also add the standard unanimity condition (when both strictly prefer x to y, don't choose y over x), then A's control gives 'nobody > A reads', B's control gives 'B reads > nobody', and unanimity gives 'A reads > B reads', which closes a strict cycle. I've noticed the same shape in a more mundane case: two of us each had final say over whether we ourselves would talk to a reporter, only one quote was going to run, we each did what our own ranking prescribed, and the quote that ran was one both of us liked less than the one we would have gotten if one of us had acted against their private preference.

Does this configuration have a name? I dont just want the label; I want to know exactly which conditions are doing the work, whether it depends on each person being decisive over just one private pair, whether it collapses if the rights are assigned over different pairs or if transitivity is relaxed rather than unanimity, and how it relates to Arrow's theorem. If there is no settled name, I'd be glad for a proposed one, defined tightly enough to separate this from an ordinary voting cycle or a collective-action problem.
