I'm not a historian, but I read a good deal of history and biography, and I have settled into a firm rule. When a writer excuses a historical figure's cruelty, exploitation, or bigotry by saying that such attitudes and practices were simply normal for the period, I set that defense aside almost entirely and judge the figure by the standards I would apply to a person living today. I extend the same rule to the writers themselves: when a scholar insists that the past must be understood on its own terms, I tend to hear that as a way of laundering the era's blind spots. Lately, though, I have begun to worry that this rule makes me worse at explaining why people actually acted as they did, and I cannot answer the objection that my own standards will look indefensible to people two centuries from now. Is my rule defensible as it stands, or is it moral arrogance dressed up as a principle? If it is not fully defensible, is there a version of it that keeps the refusal to make excuses without pretending that I hold a permanent and impartial vantage point?
