I have been working through the literature on pain asymbolia and the structure of pain's badness, and I have reached an interpretative question I would like you to argue through. As I understand the condition, patients with pain asymbolia can still identify a noxious stimulus as painful: they locate it accurately, rate its intensity, and describe it as sharp, burning, or cramping. But they insist that it does not bother them, and they show none of the usual protective behavior — no flinching, no withdrawal, no guarding of the affected part. The standard philosophical lesson drawn from this is that the badness of pain is a separable component, distinct from its sensory-discriminative core, and that in asymbolia the sensory core remains while the affective component that carries the badness is lost.

I would like you to make an argument for a stronger and, I think, more accurate conclusion. Please argue that pain asymbolia should lead us to split the badness of pain itself into two strands rather than treating badness as a single detachable component. The first strand is evaluative-representational: a pain presents the body as being in a state that is bad for the organism or that calls for some response. The second strand is affective-motivational: that presented badness is felt as suffering, with its characteristic urgency and its pull on attention and action. On the view I want you to defend, the asymbolic patient retains the first strand but loses the second. This would explain why the patient still has something recognizably painful, still classifies the sensation as pain rather than as a neutral tickle or itch, and still has a thin sense that the body is not as it should be, while lacking the felt awfulness and the imperative to act. In making the argument, please address the obvious objection that this two-stranded account multiplies badness without need, and that the standard single-component view already handles the data more parsimoniously. Be explicit about which reports and behaviors of asymbolic patients the two-stranded account captures better than the standard view.
