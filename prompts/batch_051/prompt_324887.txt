Our reading group has been stuck on causation by omission for nearly a year, and I'm starting to think the two camps are just running different projects under one heading. One camp says of course omissions cause: if Sam's not watering the plant is the counterfactual difference that explains why it died, or if the lifeguard's not jumping in is why the kid drowned, then the omission caused the outcome—refusing to say so is just word policing, and it would make a mess of legal and moral talk about culpability. The other camp says that's loose speech: causation is a relation between events or physical goings-on, and "no one watered the plant" is not an event, it's the absence of one. Absences can explain, but they can't cause. Both sides agree on the counterfactuals and on blame; what they disagree on is what a cause is. My hypothesis is that one side is doing metaphysics of the causal relation while the other is doing semantics-plus-moral-psychology of causal attribution, and the so-called debate is an equivocation. But it might also be more substantive than that—a real question about whether absences are part of the world at all. Diagnose this: is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or two questions under one name? And what would actually count as progress—a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, or an honest conclusion that nobody should expect it to end?
