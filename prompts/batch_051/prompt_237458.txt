I run a small podcast and next week's guest defends legibility as a training objective. Her position, roughly, is that the field has it backwards when it treats correctness and interpretability as separate problems, with post-hoc explanation as the usual patch. She wants models trained so that the reason a model gives for an answer is the actual reason, in a form a competent reader could audit, and she says she's willing to pay a capibility cost for that. She has a couple of standard deflections: that capability and legibility only trade off if you assume human-legible reasoning is a ceiling, and that unfaithful chain-of-thought is a measurement problem rather than an objection to the whole project.

Draft me eight to ten questions that actually press the view instead of letting her restate it. I'd like at least one each on:

- What the training signal for legibility even is. If you score chains of reasoning for audibility, aren't you mostly scoring style and self-presentation? What stops the model from producing legible-looking but unverifiable rationalizations, and how could you tell the difference?
- The capability cost. Where does "legible to a competent reader" stop being a feature and start being a cap on what the model can think? Does requiring process explanations rule out kinds of reasoning humans can't follow even in principle?
- Behavioral vs internal legibility. If a model gives faithful, checkable derivations behaviorally but its internals are still opaque, does her view count that as success? What if it's behaviorally legible but probing shows the actual computation is doing something else entirely?
- Domains with no objective answer. If correctness means "well-founded" rather than "true," isn't legibility just a socially preferred surrogate for correctness? In an area where no one can check the answer, could a legible wrong answer be worse than an opaque wrong answer?

For each of those, write the follow-up to the reply she's most likely to give. End with a question about what would count as evidence against the position, phrased so she can't just say "that's an engineering problem." No generic podcast filler.
