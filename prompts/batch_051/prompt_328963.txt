In a seminar on philosophical arguments for theism, a classmate dispatched Anselm's ontological argument in a single move. Gaunilo's island, he said: if Anselm's reasoning were sound, we could run the same argument for an island than which no greater island can be conceived. Such an island would be greater if it existed than if it did not, since existence is being treated as a greatness-making feature; so the greatest conceivable island must exist. But no line of reasoning that obliges us to accept a necessarily existing perfect island can be trusted, so Anselm's argument for God must share whatever defect the island argument has. In my classmate's hands the island is not merely a joke but an exact structural analogue: every step in the theistic argument has a counterpart in the island argument, and the absurdity of the conclusion is held to be a refutation of the form of reasoning, not just of the island instance.

I doubt the two cases are exact analogues, but I want a systematic audit rather than a defense of one side. Please set out the correspondence in full: what maps onto what between the divine case and the island case at each stage of Anselm's reasoning. Then identify the disanalogies, and separate the ones that matter for the parody's argumentative purpose from the ones that do not. Candidate pressure points I keep circling: whether an island is the kind of thing that has an intrinsic maximum of greatness at all, whereas God is standardly taken to be the ceiling of an unbounded scale; whether existence can be treated as a perfection in the same way for a contingent kind as for a necessary being; and whether Anselm's argument depends on special features of the concept of God, such as that God cannot be conceived not to exist, that have no island counterpart. I want you to rule on whether these break the analogy in a way that matters, and whether other disanalogies are more fundamental. Finally: does the island objection need the two cases to be exact analogues to do its work, or does it only need to show that Anselm's logical form is too permissive? And if the analogy fails as a refutation, is there a better analogue that does the same job more honestly, or should the island be retired?
