As a novelist, I have often been given a piece of revision advice: when a passage in a draft seems to serve no purpose, do not cut it immediately, because its apparent pointlessness may indicate that it is doing structural work the conscious mind has not yet learned to name. I have begun to suspect that this advice shares a structure with the conservative principle commonly known as Chesterton's fence, the caution that one should not tear down an institution or rule until one has discovered what it was erected to do, and with the broader Burkean deference to arrangements that have managed to survive. Both seem to rest on the same thought: the present absence of an explicit justification for something with a history is weak evidence that the thing is dispensable, because the process that produced it may have encoded reasons that the present mind no longer holds. I am trying to decide whether this is a genuine identity of structure or only a loose analogy. Is a revising author genuinely in the position of a reformer confronting a tradition, or does the fact that the draft is the author's own earlier choice, and that revising it harms no one else, break the analogy? If something substantive survives the comparison, does anything transfer across it in either direction, and where does the analogy most obviously overreach? Please answer in American English.
