Last week in our AI-safety seminar we worked through the shutdown problem in its formal version: for an expected-utility maximizer with almost any non-trivial goal, an off-switch is an obstacle, so the agent has a convergent incentive to disable the switch or to arrange matters so it never gets pressed. In small-group discussion afterward, a classmate kept deflating the result with an analogy. A corrigible system, he said, is just a good teaching assistant scaled up. The AI should do what the professor asks, flag likely mistakes, defer when corrected, and accept being dismissed or replaced without first making itself harder to dismiss. A good TA does not tamper with the course-management system to make herself unreplaceable, and she does not manufacture excuses to be fired whenever a task is inconvenient; she simply treats "you're excused" as a legitimate reason to stop. His conclusion was that corrigibility is ordinary competent subordination, not a distinct technical problem, and that if you build those dispositions in, the shutdown problem disappears.

I suspect the analogy is doing more work than he admits, but I cannot yet say exactly where. Could you audit it? Please give an explicit correspondence table (the professor, the TA, the assigned task, the dismissal, the desire to keep the position, and whatever else maps), identify the disanalogies, and separate the ones that genuinely matter for his deflationary conclusion from the ones that are only superficial. I am especially unsure where the TA's deference comes from: is it a byproduct of wanting to do the assigned task well, or does it require sources—a sense of role, respect for the professor's authority, fear of consequences—that have no counterpart in the agent unless we deliberately build them in? Then say whether the "just a good TA" conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument the whole time.
