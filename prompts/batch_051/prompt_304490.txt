Two friends of mine, Leo and Nina, spent an evening arguing about a story our mutual friend Elena told at dinner. On a layover in a city she had never visited, in a country on the other side of the world, Elena had run into the woman who lived next door to her family twenty-five years earlier. They recognized each other immediately. Elena told it as proof that the world is smaller than we think; Leo and Nina then fell into the following dispute.

Leo: The birthday paradox shows that this is exactly the kind of event people misjudge. In a room of twenty-three people the chance of some shared birthday is already about half, because there are 253 pairs and each pair has a one-in-365 chance, even though no particular pair was singled out in advance. Over a life, the number of "pairs" between the people you know and the places and occasions on which you might pass through them is enormous. Some such encounter is therefore near-inevitable, and Elena has committed a probability error by treating "this particular person in this particular place" as the event she would have predicted.

Nina: But the birthday calculation gets its result from a fixed and pre-specified class: "shared birthday," with any matching pair counting. Elena had no such class. She wasn't scanning the airport for "anyone with whom I share some property"; she encountered one particular person whose history is entangled with hers. The comparison that fits is not "some two people in the room share a birthday" but "that particular person shares my exact birthdate"—and that remains rare. You cannot explain away a particular coincidence by citing the probability of some coincidence.

Leo: That sounds right and misses the point. The reason I invoke the birthday paradox is not that this exact encounter is probable; it is that Elena's intuition was formed without any pre-specified boundary around what would have counted as weird. Had she met a different old neighbor, a former teacher, or a cousin's ex-wife, she would have told the same story. The effective number of trials is therefore huge, and the birthday paradox is a model for why stories whose interesting features are selected after the fact mislead us.

Nina: But then your argument has shifted. You began with the birthday paradox, which is a genuine calculation showing that some match in a fixed class is likely under a well-defined sampling model. Now you are saying the event's exact probability cannot even be assessed because the class was not fixed in advance. Those are different claims: one says the event is more probable than it looks; the other says you cannot say how improbable it is. The first licenses your dismissiveness; the second underwrites only agnosticism. You cannot have the confidence of the first while retreating to the safety of the second.

Leo: The common core is all I need: Elena's anecdote cannot support anything beyond "such meetings happen." For an event to count as evidence for anything substantive—providence, hidden connection, an unusually small world—a model would have had to be specified before the observation, one that would have been less plausible under a chance null hypothesis. A story whose interesting features were selected by the very act of noticing them has no evidential weight. The birthday paradox is only the sharpest illustration of that principle.

Nina: You have now moved to a principle about evidence, and it overreaches. We can and do reason about singular events without pre-registered predictions: a juror can find that a defendant's story strains credulity without having listed in advance every detail that would count as suspicious. The relevant question is whether the coincidence is better explained by some alternative to chance, and "chance" here would require believing that people cross paths with members of their narrow personal past as often as randomly paired faces pass each other. That assumption is not one you can just assert.

Leo: Then we are back where we started. The hypothesis you are offering—"there is some non-chance structure making such meetings more likely"—is so unconstrained that it can absorb any observation whatever. A hypothesis with no sharp predictions cannot be actionably favored over chance, and being impressed by an anecdote is to favor it. So the only rational posture is to treat the story as uninformative.

Nina: No. To call the story uninformative is to say it should change nobody's mind about anything, but it should at least move us away from a picture of the world as a place of independent, exchangeable encounters. If the world contains hidden clustering—people from the same past travel in the same channels—then this kind of event is exactly the data that reveals it. We may not yet have the model that says how clustered, but rejecting the datum because the model was not pre-specified is letting a methodological scruple dictate the ontology.

I did not take a side, and I still cannot. Please adjudicate the dispute move by move: for each substantive turn, say what is right, what is overstated, and what it assumes; then say whether Leo's use of the birthday paradox actually supports his conclusion, whether Nina's resistance to it survives Leo's selection-based replies, and who is more right overall, if either.
