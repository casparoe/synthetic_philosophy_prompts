I follow disputes about how courts should interpret statutes as an interested layperson, and a thought experiment has been bothering me about one side of that debate. The case for textualism, as I understand it, has at least two moving parts. One is a point of principle: what the legislature enacts is the text, and the unenacted hopes of individual legislators are not law. The other is a practical point: a legislature is not a single mind; the surrounding record of hearings, committee reports, and floor speeches is enormous and contradictory; and a judge who goes looking for legislative intent will almost inevitably find, or manufacture, a version that supports his or her own reading. Purposivists answer that this is overstated, and that ignoring a statute's evident purpose leaves a court reading words in the dark.

Now imagine that document-analysis software had become good enough and cheap enough for a court to read and weigh an entire legislative record in one pass: every draft, amendment, report, and speech, including the parts that conflict. Suppose the software could honestly report which purposes were expressed most consistently, which were held by only a few members, and how closely the recorded purposes line up with the enacted wording — knowing, of course, only what was said on the record, not what anyone privately wanted. My instinct is that this would dispose of the practical objection: intent would no longer be undiscoverable, and consulting it would not require cherry-picking. But I have the feeling a thoughtful textualist would not surrender, because the point of principle would still be standing. What I cannot work out is what that surviving argument actually rules out. Does the textualist argument survive intact? Does it survive only in a repaired form — something like, legislative purpose is now discoverable, but even a complete and honest reconstruction of it should not control the meaning of the words actually enacted? Or does the repaired version quietly give away what textualists cared about all along? And if the argument survives, what use of the tool's output, if any, would it permit a court to make?
