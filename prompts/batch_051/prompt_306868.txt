Last night a friend and I had a long argument about the problem of dirty hands in politics, and we ended up disagreeing not about the moral principle but about the consequences of a specific institutional fix, which neither of us could think through completely. She maintained that the gravest danger is that politicians claim necessity too easily, so any institution that officially recognizes wrongful-but-necessary conduct will be captured and turned into a laundering device. I maintained that the alternative is worse, because driving necessary wrongs entirely into the dark selects for officials who are comfortable committing unrecorded wrongs and lets each episode be narrated later in whatever self-serving form survives. We converged on a proposal, and I want to understand how it would actually behave.

Suppose a democratic polity adopts the following rule. Every senior executive or security official who decides that a compelling public interest requires violating an ordinary moral or legal constraint must file a dirty hands declaration with an independent registry. The declaration must: name the wrong as precisely as the officer can; state the foreseen harms and who bears them; state the intended public good and the date by which the officer expects it to become visible; list the less wrongful alternatives considered and why each was rejected; and, if the conduct is ongoing, set a date for mandatory reconsideration. Filing does not grant immunity. It triggers review by a standing panel with security clearance and no operational role, which must publish a redacted summary within six months and the full declaration after ten years. An honest filing and cooperation with review count as substantial mitigation in any later criminal, civil, or political proceeding, while a knowing failure to file is itself an offense and an aggravating factor in any later proceeding.

Treat this as a mechanism to be analyzed, not as a moral position to defend or attack. Walk through its likely behavior in the first year, the fifth year, and the twentieth year. Who files honestly, who finds ways not to file, and who files strategically or falsely? How does the registry change what wrongs get authorized, which wrongs get hidden, how blame is later distributed, and how the public reads each new scandal? What do officials, subordinates, courts, legislators, and journalists learn to do with these declarations that the designers did not intend? Identify the first three places the scheme gets gamed or breaks, and explain whether each failure can be patched without turning the registry into a new way of performing public virtue. End with your best judgment about its steady-state behavior: does it make dark choices rarer, more honest, or merely better documented, and for whom?
