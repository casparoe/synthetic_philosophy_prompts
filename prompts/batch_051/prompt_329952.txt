My sister is thinking about following a bunch of her friends into a small line of work, and at a barbecue two friends got into an argument about whether our friend Dara was rational to open a bakery on a block that already has three new bakeries. I couldn't referee, but the argument felt like it was about exactly the thing my sister is deciding, so I want to understand it.

Rina said Dara was basically rational. Other people's choices are evidence. Each of those bakers presumably looked at the block and saw something—foot traffic, rent, the new apartment complex—and Dara shouldn't have to redo all that research from scratch. If several people independently reached the same conclusion, that's real Bayesian evidence that the block can support another bakery, even if any one of them could be wrong. It's just the division of epistemic labor.

Sam said the independence assumption is doing all the work. The second bakery probaly opened because the first one had lines out the door; the third copied the second; by the time Dara shows up, she's looking at one visible success plus a chain of imitators, not three independent readouts on the neighborhood. That's an information cascade, and in a cascade people rationally ignore their own private information and copy the crowd, so the crowd can be wrong even while everyone is being individually sensible. The number of bakeries is mostly one signal, repeated.

Rina shot back that Sam was proving her point. If Dara knows later entrants might be imitators, she can discount them rather than discarding them. And Dara had private information of her own: she lives near the block and has seen how busy the places are. So this isn't a pure cascade where she ignores her own signal. Sam is asking her to rederive every signal the market already paid to collect, which would make it unreasonable to ever learn from other people's actions.

Sam said that moves the goalposts. The defense was that enough other people doing it is evidence. Now it's that Dara did her own scouting. If her own observation was the real reason, the cluster of bakeries was redundant and the original argument collapses. In an actual cascade you can't infer that a block can support four bakeries from the fact that three others opened, because the fourth entrant changes the market. Dara is committing exactly the cascade mistake: treating the number of predecessors as stronger evidence than it is.

Rina answered that Sam's cannibalization point applies to every entry decision, so it can't be an objection to herding specifically. The rational response to possibly dependent evidence isn't to ignore it; it's to discount it. Dara can notice that the earlier places are all chasing the same brunch crowd and choose a different niche, in which case the cluster is still evidence about fixed local demand. She wasn't irrational to treat the cluster as evidence; she just had to weight it properly.
