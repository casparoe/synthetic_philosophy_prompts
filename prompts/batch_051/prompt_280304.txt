I'm a novelist, and I'm writing a book where a character is extremely gentle with animals but cold or even cruel to people. I've been trying to understand Kant's view on cruelty to animals because it seems at first like the perfect material for the character's self-justification. In the notes from his ethics lectures, if I have it right, Kant says something like: we have no direct duties to animals, but cruelty toward them is wrong because it weakens a disposition in us that is needed for our duties to human beings. His example is a man who has an old dog shot when it can no longer be useful to him; the wrong isn't against the dog, but against the gentleness the man owes to humanity and perhaps to himself. I think there's also a passage in the Doctrine of Virtue about avoiding violent and cruel treatment of animals because the shared feeling of their suffering gets dulled.

I want to write a short essay for a philosophy reading group arguing that this indirect-duty view is actually more perceptive about moral psychology than modern readers give it credit for: it treats kindness to animals as a kind of training ground or diagnostic test for how we are capable of treating beings who cannot retaliate. But I'm worried I'm just projecting my novelist's interest in self-deception onto Kant, and I'm also not sure whether Kant's view can be separated from his broader teleology or his claim that animals aren't self-conscious. What I'd like comments on is: first, whether my summary of the indirect-duty position is accurate enough for a non-specialist essay (and where its most likely to go wrong); second, whether the thesis itself is interesting or just a softer version of the same old objection to Kant; and third, whether there's a version of the essay that could make the tension between "no duty to the dog" and "cruelty is still wrong" genuinely unsettling rather than just a historical artifact. Ive been going in circles on whether to include the self-consciousness point at all, since it seems to change the stakes.
