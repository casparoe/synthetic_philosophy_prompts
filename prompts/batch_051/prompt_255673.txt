I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a piece on why some fields of inquiry advance much faster than others. Before I publish, I want a consistency check on the position I've arrived at. I hold the following:

(a) Fields advance fastest when they have short, unambiguous feedback loops: you can be wrong quickly and cheaply, and the field finds out.
(b) In fields that lack such loops, the right remedy is methodological tightening — pre-registration, replication, stricter statistical standards — which restores a field's ability to correct itself without changing its subject matter.
(c) The main driver of scientific progress has been better instrumentation and measurement, not theoretical or conceptual innovation.
(d) Peer review is, on the whole, a reliable gatekeeper for what gets published, and fields with strong peer-review norms are healthier as a result.
(e) Heavily mathematized fields such as parts of physics and economics progress faster than otherwise similar empirical fields, because formalization supplies its own rapid check: internal inconsistency is caught before the world has to punish you.

Which of these are actually in tension, which pairs only look like they are, and what is the least costly revision — and what does it cost?
