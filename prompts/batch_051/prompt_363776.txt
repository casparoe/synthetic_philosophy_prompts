At a committee meeting about whether our university should add a new racial category to its application forms, a colleague dismissed the worry that the categories ought to answer to some independent fact about applicants. Their argument was an analogy: racial categories are constructed the way money is constructed. Just as there is no real dollar over and above the shared practices that let it circulate, there is no real race over and above the practices of classification that constitute it. From this they concluded that racial categories are no more discovered than currency denominations are, and that revising them is a matter of institutional decision rather than of accurately describing the world.

I would like that analogy audited rather than accepted or rejected wholesale. Build the correspondence table: what is the analogue of the central bank, the unit of account, counterfeiting, demonetization, and the debts denominated in the currency? Then identify where the mapping breaks. I suspect the two cases are constructed by very different mechanisms — money through explicit legal fiat and centralized issuance, race through diffuse and often involuntary patterns of perception, kinship, institutional sorting, and stigma — and that this difference matters more than the shared adjective socially constructed. Separate the disanalogies that undermine the colleague's conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic. Does the money analogy quietly import the assumption that a constructed kind is voluntary, easy to exit, revisable at will, and costless to those classified under the old scheme? Does it collapse if racial categories function more like interactive kinds that reshape the people sorted into them? Finally, does the conclusion about redesigning categories survive the audit, and what would a better analogy be if this one cannot carry that weight?
