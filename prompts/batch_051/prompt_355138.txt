I'm in a seminar on memory politics, and there's an analogy that keeps getting pulled out as if it just settles things: a nation dealing with its past is like a patient in psychoanalysis working through a trauma. On this picture, Germany's Vergangenheitsbewältigung is the model case—the patient who actually went to therapy, talked through the Nazi past, put memorials in the middle of the capital, taught the Holocaust in schools, and came out healthy. Japan, Turkey, and the American South, so the story goes, are the patients still in denial: Japan with its textbook controversies and Yasukuni visits, Turkey with its refusal to call what happened to the Armenians a genocide, the American South with it's Lost Cause monuments and its claim that they're about heritage rather than hate. The analogy is being used to license a strong conclusion: that public memory work—apologies, reparations, truth commissions, memorials, school curricula—is literally therapeutic for a society, and that countries that skip this stay stuck or neurotic.

I can see the pull, but I keep tripping on the mapping. The trauma is presumably the crime itself—the Holocaust, slavery and Jim Crow, the Armenian genocide, imperial war. The defense mechanisms would be official silence, heroic self-images, or monuments that prettify the past. Working through would be public debate and institutional acknowledgment of guilt. But who exactly is the patient? The state? The public? The descendants of victims and perpetrators born generations later? What corresponds to the unconscious, the analyst, the symptom, the cure? And individual therapy assumes a single mind with something like memories, defenses, and a telos of health; a country is a tangle of institutions, generations, interest groups, and narratives that are doing present-day political work.
