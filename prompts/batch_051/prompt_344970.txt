Ok so I've now seen, like, three different rationalist-adjacent posts dunking on "expected value maximization" by appealing to that Ole Peters coin-flip thing, and one of them summarized it in a way I'm maybe 70% sure isn't quite right. The summary goes something like this:

"Peters showed that if you repeatedly bet your whole bankroll on a fair coin flip that pays +50% on heads and −40% on tails, the expected value of your wealth grows 5% per flip, so naive expected-value reasoning says you should take the bet — yet almost everyone who actually plays it ends up poorer. The reason is that expected value averages over an ensemble of parallel copies, while a real person lives along a single path through time. So expected utility theory is just wrong for individual decision-making, and the correct rule is to maximize the time-average growth rate, regardless of your utility function or risk preferences."

Now, I know the arithmetic itself tracks — geometric returns, variance drag, all that — and I actually find the time-average stuff kind of compelling. But the leap from "this bet has positive EV and still ruins most players" to "therefore EUT is just wrong" feels like it's sneaking past something, and the "regardless of risk preferences" bit especially seems to overclaim. I want someone to go through it claim by claim and tell me: which parts would a careful economist (or a Peters defender?) happily sign off on, which parts are compressed into being misleading, and do the big normative conclusions actually follow from the true parts? Also, is the time-average growth rate really just log utility wearing a fake mustache, or is that an unfair way to read Peters?
