I spent my working life in the natural sciences and have come to philosophy only in retirement, so I have been working through ethical theory slowly and testing general claims the way I used to test models: against cases. The golden rule has become my current difficulty. The familiar positive form — act toward others as you would have them act toward you — and the negative form — do not do to others what you would not want done to yourself — looked equivalent to me at first, and the standard repair, act toward others as they would want to be treated, seemed to solve the obvious problem of divergent preferences. My considered verdicts now pull in several directions at once, and I would like you to help me find the principle, if there is one, that captures them.

Here are the verdicts I find myself with. (1) At a conference, I told a younger colleague, in front of two or three others, that the central argument in his talk was unsound; I would want to be stopped before embarrassing myself further, and I regard what I did as fine. (2) I took the car keys from a drunken friend who insisted he was fit to drive; I would want someone to stop me in his condition, and I regard that as fine as well. (3) I once weeded a neighbor's garden unasked, knowing he preferred to do his own gardening, because I would have been grateful had a neighbor done that for me; he was annoyed, and I now think the act was not fine. Cases 2 and 3 look structurally alike to me — I did to someone what I would want done to me, and they did not want it — yet I come out opposite ways, which is exactly what unsettles me. (4) A dying friend, a believer, asked me to pray with him, though I am not religious; I said the words. I would not want anyone to pretend to share my convictions, but I think what I did was fine. (5) A former student who had plagiarized in my course later asked for a letter of recommendation; I refused, though I would have wanted the letter in his place and knew he wanted it, and I think the refusal was fine. (6) I did not tell my brother that his wife was having an affair, though I would want to be told and suspect he would too; I think staying silent was right, but with less confidence than the others.

My tentative hypothesis is something like: do what the other person would endorse after reflection, unless a role-obligation or a fact about desert overrides it. But case 6 seems to require a third exception about standing, and I was trained to distrust principles with too many free parameters. The move to Kantian universalization also seems to me to change the subject from reciprocity to consistency, so I would like the repaired principle to stay close to the reciprocity idea if it can. The unresolved case is this: my adult daughter has entered a business partnership with someone I consider manipulative; she has not asked for my view, and I do not know whether she would want a warning. Please state the principle or small set of candidate principles that best captures these verdicts, say which case motivates each clause, tell me what your principle says about my daughter, and be explicit about which of my verdicts no simple principle can accommodate. I would also like to know whether the positive and negative forms of the rule genuinely diverge in practice or only in contrived examples. Please end with a one-sentence summary of the principle you recommend, as the final line of your reply.
