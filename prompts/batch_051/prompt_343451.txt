I've been going down a rabbit hole about Hofstadter's idea that your self isn't a soul or a thing but a pattern — a weird loop of thoughts pointing at itself — and that other people who know you really well run a blurry copy of that pattern in their heads. I turned it into an argument for a club handout and now I think it proves way too much. Where does it break? I cant pin it down, so I want a committed answer: tell me the exact premise or step to reject and what rejecting it costs, not just a menu of possibilities. And write it in American English, please.

Here's my argument:

(1) What makes me me is the pattern of my memories, habits, ways of thinking, etc., not the particular atoms in my skull.
(2) A pattern can be instantiated in more than one place at the same time with no single original: the same song is playing on two different radios, and neither one is the "real" one.
(3) So if someone who knows me very well is running a detailed enough version of my pattern in their brain, the thing that is me is in there too, just at lower resolution — not a model of me, but literally a bit of me.
(4) And there's no sharp cutoff for "detailed enough": it runs from my best friend, to the kid who sits next to me in bio and knows my jokes, to the TikTok algoritm that predicts what I'll tap, all the way down to someone who only remembers my name.
(5) So right now I'm spread across dozens of brains and servers, and whenever one of those copies of my pattern gets deleted — someone forgets a story about me, an account stops predicting for me — a little piece of me dies.
(6) By the same reasoning, I can never fully die as long as any close-enough version of my pattern keeps running, which makes the difference between me being alive and me being remembered just a matter of degree.
(7) But that's absurd. The me that is reading this sentence is the one whose lights go out when my brain stops, even if everyone still tells stories about me afterward.
(8) So premise (1) is false, and being me can't just be a pattern.

I'd rather keep (7) than (1), but I want to know what I'm actually paying for that. If the argument is valid and (7) isn't really absurd, say so, but then explain why it felt so obvious.
