I'm drafting a short essay on whether generational categories such as Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z pick out real kinds, and I'd like your comments on the argument before I write the full version. My working thesis is that the real-kinds framing is mostly a distraction. Generational categories are not natural kinds, and they are not institutional kinds of the sort exemplified by marriage or money; but they are also not mere arbitrary labels. The interesting phenomenon is that they function as what I provisionally call self-ratifying cohort classifications: once a category circulates widely, it changes how people describe themselves, how institutions sort them, and which behaviors get noticed and remembered, so that the category acquires a loose causal profile even though it was not discovered by tracing an underlying shared mechanism.

The projected structure is as follows. Section 1 distinguishes three questions that generational commentary habitually runs together: whether the categories have sharp boundaries; whether they support reliable generalizations; and whether they track a common causal mechanism. I argue that most public discourse moves illicitly from the second to the third. Section 2 argues that generational categories fail as natural kinds: the boundaries are conventional bins of roughly fifteen to twenty years, and the attributed traits are aggregate statistical differences with wide within-cohort variation and no shared generating process. Section 3 argues that they also fail as institutional kinds: unlike marriage or currency, there is no single standing arrangement or procedure whose application makes someone a Millennial, and the category's continued use is dispersed and persistently disputed. Section 4 introduces the self-ratifying mechanism: the label enters self-understanding and third-party sorting in marketing, hiring, and political polling, which feeds back into behavior and produces the very patterns later cited as evidence for the kind. Section 5 concludes that the productive question is not whether generations are real, but through which mechanisms a generational label becomes causally efficacious and under what conditions that efficacy should make us count the category as real in a deflationary sense.

Please comment specifically on three things. First, does the distinction among the three Section 1 questions do real work, or does it collapse once the questions are pressed? Second, is Section 3's contrast with institutional kinds fair, or are generational categories close enough to informal statuses such as youth or elder, which many people already treat as real without any centralized rule, that my contrast proves too much? Third, does the self-ratifying classification idea earn the deflationary conclusion, or would the essay be stronger if I abandoned the realism question and defended only the claim that generational categories are pragmatic groupings with feedback effects? Be concrete about which sections or moves you would cut or rewrite, and flag any place where the argument would strike a philosopher of social ontology as naïve or under-argued.
