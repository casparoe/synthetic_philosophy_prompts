I have been trying to extract a consistent meta-principle from my own philosophical practice: a rule for deciding when a case-based intuition should count as evidence against a principle, and when a principle should count as evidence against a case-based intuition, with transitivity and simplicity as two especially sticky test cases. My judgments resist every simple story I try, and I would like either a precise account or an honest diagnosis of which judgments I should revise.

Here are the cases and how I actually find myself judging them. (1) A well-specified forced-choice procedure, administered across many sessions with order and framing randomized, yields a cycle: I prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, and for each pair I can supply a plausible rationale. I nonetheless conclude that at least one pairwise judgment is wrong even though I cannot feel which one; I refuse to let "better than" be cyclic. Transitivity wins over the case judgments. (2) Two theories fit the same data; one posits three basic kinds, the other seven. The seven-kind theory matches my pre-theoretic classifications of borderline cases slightly better, but I find myself calling the residual classifications boundary noise and going with the three-kind theory. Simplicity wins over fine-grained fit. (3) A simple maximizing principle implies that it is permissible to inflict a lifetime of severe pain on one innocent person to prevent a very large number of barely-noticeable annoyances. My judgment against this conclusion is robust across reframings, and I would bet on it; I overturn the principle rather than the case. (4) A person's disgust-driven conviction that some harmless but unnatural act is wrong is vivid, stable, and widely shared, yet I do not count it as strong evidence against a harm-based principle, because I accept a debunking story about disgust. Not every vivid case intuition wins. (5) In the familiar Allais pattern, I choose a sure $3,000 over a 0.8 chance at $4,000, but I choose a 0.2 chance at $4,000 over a 0.25 chance at $3,000, and I retain this pattern after reflection and after hearing the argument that it is an error. I conclude that the independence axiom of expected utility theory (that mixing each option with a common gamble in the same proportions should not flip a choice) is what needs revision, not the pair of choices. Here case judgments beat a formal axiom. (6) A derivation appears to show that a self-referential sentence is both true and not true, and a paraconsistent logician offers this as evidence for dialetheism. I treat the derivation as evidence that something in the framing or the derivation is wrong, not as evidence against non-contradiction, even before I can locate the flaw. Non-contradiction beats the case.

The tensions are visible: (1) and (5) seem to draw a line through the axiom set of decision theory, but I cannot state it cleanly; (5) and (6) are structurally similar, a formal principle against a robust pattern of case judgments, yet I keep the case pattern in one and the principle in the other. I have tried three candidate meta-principles, each of which crashes on at least one verdict: (i) structural principles are never tested by cases, while substantive ones are; (ii) case intuitions count as evidence only when they are vivid, stable, shared, and not debunked; (iii) principles are priors and case intuitions are evidence, so outcomes depend on prior strength and likelihood. Please state the best candidate principle or small set of candidates precisely, test it against my judgments, tell me what it predicts for a case I have not listed, and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and what the cleanest revision of my pattern would be. In particular, I want to know why a cycle should discredit its pairwise judgments while an Allais-style violation should be treated as a legitimate finding about preference or value.
