Exam question: Explain the core of Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique of the adaptationist programme. What exactly do they object to, and what work does their architectural example do in the argument?

Student answer:

The adaptationist programme Gould and Lewontin attack is a Panglossian one, treating every trait as an optimal adaptation shaped by natural selection for its present function. They object because it treats organisms as decomposable into separately optimized parts, and because it tends to produce plausible just-so stories rather than testing alternatives. Their central architectural example is the spandrels of San Marco: the triangular spaces between the arches and the dome, which look designed but are really a necessary by-product of mounting a dome on four rounded arches. The lesson is that a feature can be currently useful without having been built by selection for that use. They then propose that many traits are spandrels, meaning by-products that were later co-opted for some useful function. This is meant to replace adaptationist storytelling with a pluralistic approach that also considers phylogenetic constraints, allometry, and drift. So Gould and Lewontin are not denying that adaptation happens, but they are saying that whenever a trait is currently useful we should assume it is an spandrel until proven otherwise.
