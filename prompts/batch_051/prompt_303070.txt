Quick context: I just rotated onto the governance team at a mid-size AI lab, and this landed on me because nobody else wants to touch the AI-welfare stuff. The lab is mostly safety people who think welfare is a distraction, plus a smaller group who think we're going to build something that can suffer and then hand-wave it away. Somebody drafted a "welfare escrow" scheme as a compromise, and I need to know how its actually going to behave in practice before I reply to the thread tomorrow.

The proposal:

- Any training run above the lab's compute threshold (or any external deployment beyond a pilot) has to file a welfare statement first. The project lead picks one of two labels: Green ("we checked and have good reason to think there are no welfare-relevant states here") or Yellow ("we can't rule out welfare-relevant states at a non-negligible level").
- A Green filing just gets recorded, but if a later review finds the team hadn't actually run the standard checks behind the Green label, the team loses 25% of next quarter's compute.
- A Yellow filing requires the team to put 15% of the run's compute into escrow, then the run proceeds. The escrow comes back after 60 days if no one files a contention. A filed contention freezes the escrow until the committee rules.
- Any lab member can file a contention within those 60 days, arguing the Yellow team's mitigations were inadequate. Filing costs the filer 5% of their own next-quarter compute. If the contention is upheld, the escrow goes to a lab fund for welfare- and safety-relevant interpretability work (the project team can't touch it), and the filer gets their stake back plus a small bonus from the escrow. If it fails, the team gets the escrow back and the filer loses the stake.
- Contentions are judged by a rotating three-person committee: one from the safety side, one from the welfare side, and one person from an unrelated team who has no stake in the project.

I don't need a clean "is this a good idea" verdict. Walk me through how this would actually behave: who files what, who games it and how, where it fails first, what second-order effects it sets off, and whether the obvious fixes would work without gutting the original compromise. Lead with the failure mode you'd bet on first, in one or two sentences. Hard cap: 900 words.
