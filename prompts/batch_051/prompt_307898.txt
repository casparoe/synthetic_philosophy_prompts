The interpretation of Parmenides has long been stalled on two connected disputes. The first concerns the status of the cosmology in the poem's second half: one established reading treats it as a serious account of the best possible explanation of the changing world in light of the preceding arguments; another treats it as an account of mortal error that Parmenides presents only in order to abandon it; still another treats it as a dialectical or therapeutic exercise aimed at a particular audience. The second dispute concerns what the Eleatic denial of plurality and change actually denies. Some readers take the arguments to establish a metaphysical thesis about reality — that being is unitary, ungenerated, imperishable, and changeless. Others take them to establish a constraint on thought and speech: what is not cannot be thought or said, with the result that change and plurality turn out to be unintelligible rather than simply false. Still others take them to register a formal point about the use of the verb to be, with no direct ontological import. My working hypothesis is that the impasse persists because these are not competing answers to one question but answers to three different questions — what Parmenides believed, what his arguments commit him to, and what remains usable for modern metaphysics or philosophy of language — and the three questions have been run together under one heading. Diagnose this impasse. Engage the positions as I have characterized them, say whether the disagreement is best understood as verbal, substantive, textual, or something else, and explain what would constitute progress: a new distinction, a sharper reconstruction of the argument, a clarified question, or a reason to conclude that no progress should be expected.
