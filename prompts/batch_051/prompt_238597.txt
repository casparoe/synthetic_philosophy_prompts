The founding statement of the Besom Hill Association of Churches, adopted in 1847, contains this article:

"In the world as first made by God, no living creature suffered pain, sickness, or death; pain and death entered creation only after and because of the first human sin."

A member of one congregation, who teaches paleontology, has asked the Association's doctrine committee whether she may remain in good standing while teaching the following. She has shown her students casts of a fossilized skeleton from late Cretaceous deposits dated to about sixty-six million years ago: a plant-eating dinosaur with a healed fracture in its hindlimb and the broken-off tooth of a predatory dinosaur embedded in the bone, indicating that it was attacked, injured, and survived for some time. She argues that injury and hunting occurred for many millions of years before any human being existed, so the article's claim about the origin of pain and death cannot be literally true.

The article was adopted before these finds were known and says nothing about deep time or pre-human creatures. Interpret the article as applied to her case. Set out four approaches in turn: the plain wording of the text; the apparent purpose of the doctrine; what its original drafters would most plausibly have said if presented with this specimen; and what a reasonable member of the Association today would take the article to mean. For each approach, say whether her teaching would be compatible with the article or would put her at odds with it, and conclude by saying which approach should govern and why.
