Since I left the religious community in which I was raised, two of my closest friends have spent a year arguing about whether the position one of them now holds — that there are no objective moral facts, and that a human life has no purpose except one it invents for itself — can genuinely be lived. One camp, which I will call the Livable-Nihilism position, says it can: valuing, caring, and deliberating do not require the metaphysical backing that the nihilist denies, so a sincere nihilist can keep promises, care for children, and plan for the future without contradiction or bad faith. The other, which I will call the Unlivable-Nihilism position, says it cannot: to deliberate is already to take some considerations to count in favor of an act independently of one's say-so, so someone who really believed the nihilist's denial would find agency itself corroded, and any appearance of ordinary life would be possible only because an unacknowledged commitment to objective reasons was doing the work.

A third claim has increasingly come to occupy me in the middle of their argument: mereological nihilism, the thesis that composite objects do not exist and that talk of tables, mountains, and persons is only a convenient way of speaking about simples arranged tablewise or personwise. Both camps treat this as a philosophical curiosity rather than an existential threat, even though it appears to deny the existence of the very persons who are supposed to live with it. I suspect that asymmetry is doing more work in the dispute than either camp has admitted.

Please draft the adversarial-collaboration document these two camps should have produced after a good-faith effort. Do not write a verdict; write the document both could sign. It should contain:

(a) a numbered agreed-claims section with precise formulations of moral nihilism, existential nihilism, and mereological nihilism, and of what a person must believe, reject, or suspend to count as holding each, including whether the moral view is best stated as the claim that all positive moral judgments are false, that such judgments are neither true nor false, or that they are true only relative to an invented standard;

(b) a disagreements table that states the remaining disagreements in wording each side would accept for its own view, and that explicitly separates genuine disagreements from merely verbal ones; and

(c) a list of cruxes, understood as sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side, with, for each crux, the case, argument, or test both would accept as bearing on it and each side's prediction recorded in advance.

The central crux should be what livable means here: whether living as a nihilist is a matter of what a person asserts, feels, does, or of how they deliberate, and what kind of evidence could show that a view is too costly or too unstable for a human life. The document should also say where mereological nihilism belongs in the dispute — as part of the same package, as harmlessly irrelevant, or as a pressure point on one or both camps.
