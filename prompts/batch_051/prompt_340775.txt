I've spent a fair amount of time in rationalist and EA circles, and for a while now I've been trying to make coherent extrapolated volition (CEV) bear on ordinary, present-day decisions rather than only on the far-future alignment problem. The standard gloss — roughly, the preferences a person would have after learning the relevant facts, reasoning more clearly, and becoming more the person they would endorse being — is much too coarse when I apply it to cases where actual and idealized preferences pull apart. I've been writing down my own verdicts on such cases, and I want to know whether a single principle captures them, or whether some of my verdicts should be abandoned.

Here are the cases with the verdicts I actually find myself reaching.

1. A person in opioid withdrawal demands to leave the emergency department to use again. During a prior sober period they wrote an advance instruction saying they want to be prevented from leaving under exactly these conditions, and their own sober account says the demand is not what they want once the craving is removed. Verdict: hold them against their current stated wish. The idealized preference wins.

2. A severely depressed patient with a good prognosis refuses life-sustaining treatment. Between episodes they have consistently said they want to live, and clinicians expect that once treated they will again want to live. Verdict: treat them despite the current refusal, on the ground that the refusal is a symptom. Again the idealized preference wins.

3. A competent adult refuses a safe, effective vaccine because they hold a false factual belief picked up from unreliable sources; they are reasoning badly, not delusional. If they accepted the evidence they would consent, but they do not. Verdict: do not vaccinate against their refusal; persuade, inform, and wait. The actual preference wins even though it rests on a false belief and would not survive better information.

4. A woman raised in a conservative religious community that assigns women a subordinate role says she freely accepts that role, values the community, and wants to raise her daughters within it. Suppose that, had she been raised elsewhere, she would likely neither accept nor want that role. Verdict: treat her present acceptance as authoritative; we should not override or rescue her, though her daughters should have genuine exit options. The preference her extrapolated self might have had does not beat the one she actually has.

5. A seven-year-old insists on an all-sweets diet and no school. They are bright but have no stable conception of their later interests. Verdict: the parents should overrule them. I do not find this hard; I include it only to flag an edge.

6. A friend stays with a partner who isolates her and gradually undermines her confidence. She says she wants to stay, and I predict that a year after leaving she would describe the staying-want as something she was pressured into. Verdict: I would not forcibly intervene or treat her stated wish as invalid; I would stay close, say what I see, and make leaving easy, but the actual preference still controls. This feels different from case 1, and I cannot state the difference cleanly.

7. A majority of voters oppose a policy that the best available evidence says would materially help them, where their opposition is factual confusion rather than a difference in values. Suppose I administer the relevant program and the governing law is genuinely ambiguous. Verdict: do not implement the policy until there has been more public deliberation; the present, uninformed preference outweighs the extrapolated one when I am deciding for others. This sits awkwardly next to cases 1 and 2.

The case I keep oscillating on: I have been offered a job I currently want mostly for status and money, but I can predict from experience that the preferences I acquire in that job will be preferences I now regard as worse than the ones I would acquire in a lower-paid, less prestigious job. My present self wants the job; my best estimate of my extrapolated self wants me not to take it. I do not know which self's verdict should be authoritative when the decision is itself partly about which self I will become.

Is there a principle — a restricted version of CEV, or something else — that yields all or nearly all of these verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts should go first, and why those? What does the principle say about the job case?
