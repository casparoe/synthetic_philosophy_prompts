I facilitate a political theory reading group that has spent the better part of a year on the boundary problem: the question of who ought to make up the demos, understood as the set of persons with standing in the process by which a political community takes binding collective decisions. Two members have settled into opposing positions and have been arguing past one another for months. The first defends the all-affected principle: whoever's interests are importantly affected by a given decision should have a say in making it. The second defends the all-subjected principle: standing belongs to those who are systematically subject to the coercive legal order of the state taking the decision, irrespective of how much any particular measure affects them. The all-subjected member objects that the all-affected view expands the demos to everyone connected by the endless chains of global interdependence and thereby makes coherent self-government impossible; the all-affected member objects that the all-subjected view is radically under-inclusive because it leaves out outsiders who bear the externalized costs of a state's decisions through climate policy, trade, border enforcement, or military action, while counting temporary visitors and others merely because they fall under a jurisdiction while present.

I want you to write the adversarial-collaboration statement these two should be able to co-sign after a good-faith effort to stop talking past each other. Begin with a numbered list of claims both sides can affirm without hedging: claims about what a boundary principle must minimally do, about which standard cases both principles get right, such as long-settled noncitizens, and about which cases pressure both principles, such as future persons or a state's policy whose main harms fall outside its borders. Then provide a disagreements table in which each side's central thesis is stated in wording that side itself would accept as its own; the table should distinguish the question of who belongs to the demos from the separate question of how a demos, once constituted, ought to weigh affected interests. Next, identify the genuine cruxes: the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify one or two concrete test cases both would agree bear on it, with each side's required verdict or prediction recorded in advance. I suspect two of the cruxes are whether affectedness unaccompanied by subjection to the deciding state's coercion can be sufficient for standing, and whether subjection unaccompanied by demonstrable current affectedness can be sufficient; you may add others and should say which are load-bearing. Finally, say which portions of their dispute, if any, are merely verbal, and whether there is a stable hybrid principle both could accept or whether any apparent convergence is an artifact of under-described cases.
