I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and I've been in a seminar on social conventions all term where my professor never states her principle—she just rules on cases. I've been writing them down, and they're starting to look like a pattern. So far she's said: tipping a waiter at a sit-down restaurant in the US is a genuine moral obligation, not just etiquette; there's no obligation to tip at a counter-service place with a tip jar by the register; a tourist who cuts into a London bus queue does something wrong; crossing against a red light on a completely empty street at 3 a.m. is not wrong; and in Tokyo a visitor who insists on leaving a tip after a meal isn't being generous—she's making a mistake, because there's a soft obligation not to tip there.

Next week's case is one of ours. At a city's night market, the local convention is that everyone joins one long single-file line that feeds all the food stalls, even though the stalls are independent. A visitor quietly starts a shorter second line at the emptiest stall, reasoning that separate lines for separate stalls, like a food court back home, are plainly more efficient. Nobody is actually delayed—there's spare capacity—but a few locals mutter and wave him back into the big line, more because he's not doing it the local way than because they lose out. He asks whether he actually did anything wrong.

I want to predict her verdict. What do you think she'd say, which of her earlier cases is the closest analog, and what principle would either verdict commit her to? If her old verdicts are actually consistent with two principles that disagree on this case, tell me both and say what further case would settle which priniciple she's running. And be honest about whether the closest analog is the London queue or the Tokyo tip.
