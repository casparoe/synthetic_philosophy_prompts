I have been reading a view attributed to Wittgenstein about religious belief, and a friend has pressed an argument against it that seems to me to prove too much. The view, as I understand it, runs like this. Religious belief is not a defective empirical theory about the world. It is a different sort of activity, governed by its own grammar and embedded in a form of life; it would be a category mistake to demand that belief in a final judgment be supported in the way a claim in cosmology or ancient history is supported, because religious language is not in that line of work.

I find the view attractive because it spares believers from having to defend a resurrection as though it were a borderline archaeological claim. But my friend offers the following argument.

(1) If religious belief is not answerable to ordinary empirical standards and is intelligible only from within its own form of life, then no one outside the practice is in a position to criticize it, since there is no shared standard across the boundary.
(2) Flat-earth belief, astrology, and ancient-aliens speculation each have the same defensive structure: their own vocabulary, their own rules for what counts as evidence, and their own ways of absorbing anomalies without giving up the core claim.
(3) Therefore, if religion earns immunity from outside criticism by having its own grammar, flat-earth belief and astrology earn the same immunity.
(4) But flat-earth belief is false, and we rightly reject it by standards its practitioners do not share, such as orbital photography, geodesy, and the behavior of ships on the horizon.
(5) So the view cannot tell a serious form of life apart from a crackpot cosmology.
(6) If the view cannot do that, it should be abandoned in favor of treating all such discourses as answerable to shared empirical standards.
(7) Therefore religious utterances are ordinary claims about how the world is, and they must meet the same burden of evidence as anything else.

I have tried two ways out, and both collapsed. First, I said flat-earth belief is still an empirical hypothesis, so it cannot have its own grammar in the required sense; my friend replied that the religious form of life includes historical-sounding claims, such as that a man rose from the dead, so it, too, makes claims about the world. Second, I said a religious tradition is a long-lived shared practice while flat-earth belief is a fringe aberration; he pointed out that astrology is ancient and resilient too, so age cannot be the criterion.

I find (4) undeniable and (7) too high a price to pay, but I cannot locate the step at which the argument overreaches. It may be sound, and I should simply accept (7); still, I would rather keep the original view if it can be held without licensing flat-earth belief. Please tell me where the argument goes wrong, if it does; if I must reject a premise, tell me which rejection is cheapest and what it costs.
