For an advanced undergraduate philosophy of language seminar, write a decision tree that sorts a respondent into one of the main positions on the semantics of generic sentences. The target phenomenon is exception-tolerance: how a sentence like "Ducks lay eggs" is accepted even though only mature female ducks lay eggs, while closely related sentences are rejected despite being true of a larger share of the kind. The instrument should classify people by their pre-theoretic judgments, not by which theory they can name: every internal node should be a concrete yes/no question that a student could answer without knowing any theory of generics, and each leaf should carry the position's descriptive name and a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment.

Use the following real-language cases as the fixed test battery, and say what judgment each is meant to elicit (a respondent may deviate; that deviation is itself data, not an error):
- "Ducks lay eggs" — accepted, although only mature female ducks lay eggs.
- "Lions have manes" — accepted, although only adult male lions have manes.
- "Mosquitoes carry malaria" — accepted, although only a tiny minority of mosquitoes ever carry the parasite.
- "Sharks attack bathers" — accepted, although a minuscule minority ever attack a bather.
- "Lions are male" — rejected, although roughly half of all lions are male.
- "Dogs are brown" — rejected, although many dogs are brown.

Add one stipulated case to separate views on which actual prevalence is not what matters: a mosquito species whose females are physiologically competent to transmit malaria, but in which, by environmental accident, no individual has ever actually done so. The tree should ask whether "These mosquitoes carry malaria" would be true of that species.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of labels. First fix no more than six diagnostic questions, each phrased as a concrete case-judgment a student can answer without theoretical vocabulary, and say which answers force a respondent away from which positions. Then map answer-patterns to leaves; for unoccupied patterns, note briefly whether the cell is empty because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The leaves should reach, under descriptive names, these positions: the strict majority view; the domain-restricted statistical view; the salience or low-threshold view; the prototype (characteristic-property) view; the normalcy (normic) view; the dispositional or causal view; the kind-based view; and a contextualist or pluralist view. If two of these cannot be separated by the six questions, flag them explicitly rather than inventing a spurious distinction, and state what further case or question would separate them.

Write the whole thing as an instrument a teaching assistant could hand out unchanged, and make it answerable without sources: no references, bibliographies, or author citations.
