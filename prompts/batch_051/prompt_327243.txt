Consider two legal regimes side by side. The United States leaves even openly racist or antisemitic public speech unpunished, on the rationale that the cure for bad speech is better speech, yet its courts routinely uphold punishment for credible threats of violence, targeted harassment, and incitement to imminent lawless action. Most European legal systems, by contrast, criminalize public incitement to hatred against groups defined by race, religion, ethnicity, or national origin, while continuing to protect sharply critical political and artistic expression. The two regimes therefore draw opposite lines between speech and punishable harm, and each draws a puzzling internal line of its own: American law treats group-directed abuse as protected but person-directed intimidation as regulable, while European law treats group-directed abuse as the core offense but must separate it from harsh political criticism of a group's views or conduct. What theory of the relation between speech, harm, and public order does each practice presuppose? Is the American distinction between protected hate speech and punishable threats best explained by an implicit commitment to a causal-harm principle, by a picture of citizens as autonomous arguers in a self-correcting marketplace of speech, or by something else? Does the European practice strictly presuppose that expression can cumulatively damage a group's standing over time, or is it equally compatible with a narrower view on which the law regulates only speech that makes discrete acts of discrimination or violence more likely? Reconstruct the implicit theory behind each regime, distinguish what each strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify where the legal lines are philosophically underdetermined.
