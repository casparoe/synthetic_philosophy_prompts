A friend and I spent most of a long evening arguing about a tension in the way legal systems treat statistical evidence, and neither of us managed to state the underlying principle cleanly. The law is famously unwilling to let naked statistical evidence settle a finding of fact. In the standard blue bus case, a plaintiff is run off the road by a bus and can show only that 80 percent of the buses on that route belong to the Blue Bus Company; courts are reluctant to treat that as enough to find the company liable, even on the civil standard. In the standard gatecrasher hypothetical, 1,000 people attend a rodeo but only 499 have paid admission, so it is more likely than not that any given attendee entered without paying; yet nobody thinks the ticket-taker can simply point at someone in the stands and collect, and no criminal court would treat the numbers alone as proof beyond reasonable doubt. But the very same legal system increasingly relies on actuarial risk scores. At pretrial detention hearings, at sentencing, and at parole, a judge may receive a numerical risk assessment built by aggregating data about how often people with a defendant's characteristics and record have reoffended, and that score can determine whether the defendant is jailed before trial or for how long. My friend calls this a flat contradiction: if a base rate cannot establish that this defendant did this act, a risk score should not be allowed to decide what happens to him. I suspect the practice presupposes a defensible distinction — perhaps between finding that a person committed a specific past act and predicting how a person will behave under uncertainty, or between the epistemology of a verdict and the epistemology of a risk-management decision — but I could not get it into focus.
