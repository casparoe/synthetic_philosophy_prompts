I'm stuck on a line of thought about Schopenhauer's pessimism, and it's started to look like it proves too much. In The World as Will and Idea (Haldane-Kemp translation, vol. 1, §57), he argues that every satisfaction is only the removal of a want—that what we call happiness is, at bottom, negative rather than positive. He puts it as a general point about the will: it only strives because something is lacking, and when the lack is filled, the striving stops, which is not the same as enjoying a positive good. A friend pushed me into an argument from that passage and I can’t find the exit.

(1) If all satisfaction is only the elimination of a felt deficiency, then the will has only two possible states: lack, or the temporary cessation of lack.
(2) For Schopenhauer the inner essence of a person is will, so our life at its core just oscillates between these two states.
(3) Therefore, by his own account, life contains no positive good—only temporary relief from pain.
(4) But Schopenhauer also treats aesthetic contemplation and the ascetic denial of the will as having real value, indeed as the only escapes from the treadmill.
(5) If (1) is right, then either aesthetic contemplation and denial are also just ways of removing a deficiency, or they aren't genuine states of will at all.
(6) So either these escapes have no positive value, or they contradict his metaphysics of will.
(7) Since Schopenhauer plainly thinks they do have positive value, (1) must be false.

My friend says the argument shows Schopenhauer's pessimism undercuts his own aesthetics and ethics, because it makes even the so-called escapes negative and instrumental. I feel like the slide happens around (5)—maybe a conflation of the will as thing-in-itself with the will as it appears in phenomenal motives—but I can’t make that diagnosis precise without weakening the original passage. Which step should I reject, and what exactly would rejecting it cost for the rest of the system?
