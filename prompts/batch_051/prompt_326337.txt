I'm giving a research seminar next month defending a disjunctivist account of perception. Roughly, the thesis is that a veridical perception of a table and a subjectively matching hallucination of one are not two species of a common mental kind, and that the argument from illusion therefore cannot force us to posit sense-data or representational contents. My talk turns on a particular move in Bertrand Russell's *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912, Chapter I, "Appearance and Reality"), where he writes:

"It is evident from what we have found, that there is no colour which pre-eminently appears to be the colour of the table, or even of any one particular part of the table—it appears to be of different colours from different points of view, and there is no reason for regarding some of these as more really its colour than others."

My line is that Russell slides from this perfectly true observation about changing appearances to the conclusion that what we are directly aware of must be something other than the table itself, and that the slide is the real engine of the argument from illusion.

The audience is a mixed philosophy department: mostly people working on representation and consciousness who are broadly sympathetic to sense-data or at least to representationalism, plus two vision scientists who work on colour constancy and illusions. Nobody there is a disjunctivist, and I suspect half of them think disjunctivism is just a way of refusing to take the science of perception seriously.

Write me a Q&A prep sheet for this lot: the six or eight objections I'll actually get, phrased roughly the way they'd put them in their own vocabulary, ordered by how damaging they are. For each, give me a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. Then tell me what I should concede up front, the one reply that has to land for the talk to survive, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Please write it in British English.
