I'm a program officer at a mid-sized family foundation. I recommend about eight grants each quarter, mostly to small arts and youth groups, and our board requires a written rationale with an explicit ranking. The problem is that the best proposals are often good in ways that don't seem to sit on a common scale: one is artistically ambitious but reaches fewer people, another is unadventurous but has wide uptake, a third keeps a fragile organization solvent. Every rubric I try feels like it is doing the deciding instead of the reasons.

From what I've gathered, there's a serious philosophical view that some values are incommensurable, and that certain choices are hard choices where the options are on a par — neither better, nor worse, nor exactly equal — and the honest move is to stop pretending you're maximizing and instead commit.

Please turn that view into a decision procedure I can actually run each quarter. I want the questions in order, what counts as an adequate answer at each step, where the procedure stops with something decisive like one proposal strictly dominating another, where it concludes the choice is genuinely on a par and hands the decision back to me as an irreducible values call, and where it says the problem is a confusion in the framing rather than real incommensurability. Keep it usable by someone without a philosophy background and under time pressure, and mark the places where the procedure cannot remove judgment.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a clear dominance case, (2) a hard choice case, and (3) a case where a common metric like cost-per-participant looks comparable but would actually distort the decision. Make one of these a case where the procedure gives an answer my board won't like. Also say what the procedure can't do. And tell me how confident you are in the philosophical claims you're leaning on, and which point you're least sure about.
