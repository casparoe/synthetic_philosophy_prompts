I’m in an undergrad legal interpretation seminar, and my study group has been trying to nail down what counts as textualism. We came up with a rough checklist: (1) the text’s ordinary public meaning at enactment is the law; (2) committee reports, floor statements, and behind-the-scenes drafting intent are not authoritative, though they may be used as linguistic evidence; (3) a judge can’t let an unstated purpose override clear text, and even when text is vague, the gap should be filled by reference to how a reasonable reader at the time would understand the term. Then someone brought in Scalia’s opinion in Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Services, Inc., where he says: “We have held that this not only covers ‘terms’ and ‘conditions’ in the narrow contractual sense, but ‘evinces a congressional intent to strike at the entire spectrum of disparate treatment of men and women in employment.’” That sentence sounds like it is doing exactly what our third criterion says textualists shouldn’t do: appealing to a broad congressional intent rather than to what the words because of sex meant in 1964. But Scalia then uses that intent to hold that same-sex harassment can be discrimination because of sex, which is a textually grounded result. So is this a borderline case for the checklist? Go through the criteria honestly; where the passage fits uneasily, say whether that is a problem for our definition of textualism or for calling Scalia a textualist here. Also say whether a sensible textualism needs to make room for something like “evinces a congressional intent” as shorthand for the text’s objective legal content.
