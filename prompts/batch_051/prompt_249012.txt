In my school's philosophy elective we've been working through a unit on decision theory, and one puzzle has taken over far more of the conversation than our teacher intended: the two-envelope paradox. Two sealed envelopes, one containing exactly twice the money of the other; you choose one at random and, before opening it, you reason that the other envelope is equally likely to contain double or half of what yours contains, so its expected value is one-and-a-quarter times yours, and you resolve to switch. Then someone points out that if you had picked the other envelope first, the very same calculation would instruct you to switch back, and that after each switch the argument simply restarts, never settling down. Our last meeting of the unit is in two weeks, and I want to end it with a poem that gives the paradox its proper shape—something that keeps the loop running the way the puzzle actually does, rather than stopping it with a false resolution.

Please work on this as someone who knows decision theory carefully and also knows how to write poems, and draft a villanelle: the nineteen-line form in which two refrain lines alternate through five tercets and finally meet in the closing quatrain. I chose that form deliberately, because a villanelle is itself a machine that keeps returning, and I want the poem's returns to be the paradox's returns. Assign the two refrain slots to the two mirror-image halves of the reasoning—perhaps the promise of doubling and the dread of halving, though you may choose a better pairing if you can defend it—and make each tercet a fresh iteration of the argument, advancing the reasoning stanza by stanza rather than restaging the same scene. The closing quatrain, where the two refrain lines finally stand side by side, should not smuggle in a resolution; it should make the reader see the symmetry that was there all along, that each envelope's argument points at the other and did so before anyone picked. Someone who knows the paradox should recognize that the actual inference and the actual reason it cannot be right are being rendered, not merely a name-drop of the puzzle; someone who has never heard of it should still find a good poem.

Before writing, take a few sentences to plan: say exactly which two claims will occupy the refrain lines, what each tercet will add, and what the final quatrain will reveal. Then give the poem. After it, add a short note outside the poem naming which standard diagnosis the poem leans on—for instance, the fact that no single probability distribution makes it true that the other envelope is equally likely to be double or half for every possible amount, or the swap-the-envelopes symmetry argument—and flag any place where the poem compresses or takes liberties with the idea. And please do not simply comply if the brief is confused: if you think the villanelle's repetition would end up endorsing the endless switching rather than exposing it, or that a different form would serve the symmetry better, say so plainly and explain, rather than producing a polished poem that betrays the puzzle.
