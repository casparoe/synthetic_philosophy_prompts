A friend in a philosophy of logic seminar fed me this argument and I can't shake it. Every line looks right but the conclusion is obviously too strong.

Take any sentence S.

(1) Either S or not-S. (Excluded middle — this is just logic, right?)
(2) The T-schema holds: 'S' is true iff S, and 'not-S' is true iff not-S. ('The mug is on the table' is true iff the mug is on the table.)
(3) So either 'S' is true or 'not-S' is true.
(4) Saying that 'not-S' is true is just saying S is false.
(5) Therefore every sentence is either true or false — bivalence, no truth-value gaps anywhere.
(6) But that can't be right: 'there will be a sea battle tomorrow' (before anything settles it) looks like it's neither true nor false, and so does 'Todd is bald' when Todd is right in the middle of a sorites series.
(7) So one of (1) through (4) has to go.

I really want to keep (1) and (2) — ditching excluded middle or the T-schema both feel like throwing logic away to save a hunch. But I dont see a less drastic exit. Where does this argument go wrong, and if it doesn't, which step do you reject? Explain what the main gap-friendly positions (something like three-valued logics and supervaluationism) each have to pay to block the conclusion. Also, answer in American English please.
