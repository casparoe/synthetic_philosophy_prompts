I retired a few years back after a career in population ecology, and I've been spending my afternoons on decision theory, so be patient if I've tied myself in knots here. I keep coming across the money pump argument, and it's left me genuinely puzzled.

As I understand it, an agent has a cycle of strict preferences: she prefers A to B, B to C, but C to A. You start her off holding A, and offer to swap A for C for a small fee. Since she prefers C to A, she accepts. Then you offer to swap the C for B for another small fee; since she prefers B to C, she accepts. Then you offer to swap the B for A for a third small fee; since she prefers A to B, she accepts. She's now back holding A, exactly where she began, only a few pounds poorer. Each trade, taken by itself, was one she wanted.

Now, the argument is nearly always put as if it proves that the preference cycle itself — the intransative preferences — are irrational, full stop. And I find that compelling. If every link in the chain is something she genuinely prefers, and yet the chain lands her in a state she had no reason to end up in, surely the cycle is the culprit. But then I read people who seem to think some intransitive preferences might be defensible, and I can't make sense of that. Are they just denying that you should choose what you prefer? Or is there a step in the money pump I've missed? Because if the argument is sound, shouldn't we be trying to root intransitive preferences out of ourselves whenever we spot them, rather than defending them?
