I've been trying to formalize the procreation asymmetry for a reading group on population ethics, and I've become convinced that the position, as usually stated, is internally inconsistent. Here is the problem. It seems clearly wrong to create a child whose life, if created, would contain net suffering; we explain this by saying the suffering would be bad for that child. By exactly parallel reasoning, it should be permissible, perhaps even required, to create a child whose life would contain net happiness, because that happiness would be good for that child. The procreation asymmetry accepts the first claim and denies the second, or at least denies that the second claim has the same force. But the two claims appear to invoke the very same normative object — a merely possible person's well-being — counted on one side and ignored on the other.

The only way I can think of to avoid calling this a contradiction is to say that happiness generates reasons only once someone already exists, whereas suffering generates reasons even before anyone exists. But that way of putting it seems to imply that a world full of happy people is in no respect better than an empty world, which I find hard to accept. Where does my derivation go wrong, and what is the least revisionary way to hold onto the intuitive asymmetry without accepting the implication I just mentioned?
