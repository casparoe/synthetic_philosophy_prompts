I have spent some time thinking on my own, outside formal study, about the charge that consequentialist moral theories demand too much. By that I mean this family of objections: the view that one is always required to perform the act with the best consequences, whatever the cost to one's own projects and attachments, asks more of a person than morality reasonably can. I have collected what I take to be the standard objections, and I do not need them repeated. They are:

1. The loss of the supererogatory. If the optimal act is always obligatory, no act can go beyond duty, and the distinction between what is required and what is saintly or heroic disappears.

2. The integrity objection. A person can always be required to abandon the ground projects and commitments around which her life is organized, and under that standing threat she cannot regard her life as her own.

3. Negative responsibility. Because the consequences of an act include how other people will respond, the agent becomes responsible for bad acts she merely fails to prevent, with no principled stopping point.

4. The partiality objection. The theory treats one's children, parents, partners, and friends as entries in a calculation rather than as reasons for special treatment, which cannot be squared with actually living inside those relationships.

5. The ought-implies-can objection. No human being can live as an impartial maximizer, so the theory demands what cannot be done, and no one can be required to do the impossible.

6. The unattractive ideal objection. A life that actually satisfied the demand would be so occupied by moral calculation and sacrifice that no room would remain for the nonmoral goods — play, taste, love pursued for its own sake, idle curiosity — that make a life worth choosing.

7. The missing prerogative objection. A plausible morality lets each person weight her own interests somewhat more heavily than an impartial calculation would dictate; consequentialism grants no such permission.

I believe that list covers the standard material. I would like one more objection of the same general kind — a reason for thinking that consequentialism's demands are excessive — that is not on the list. It must not be a rewording of any item already there, a special case of one (such as naming a particular relationship or a particular project), or a combination of items already on the list. I am not asking for a defense of consequentialism or a solution to these objections. After giving the new objection, tell me honestly whether it is any good: does it identify a real defect in the theory that the seven above miss, or does it only sound like one?
