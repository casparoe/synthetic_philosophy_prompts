I am a novelist writing a book whose protagonist—a retired magistrate who now chairs a village assembly—keeps trying to settle every proposed local rule by one question: can the person proposing it accept a world in which everyone, in the relevantly similar circumstances, does the same? I want his test to be intellectually serious: it should earn real authority in the early village debates and then fail for identifiable reasons in later, harder cases. I do not want him to be a mouthpiece for an undergraduate caricature of the categorical imperative.

So I am not asking for a reading list, for secondary literature, or for exegesis of particular texts. I am asking for the specification of the testing procedure itself. Act as a moral philosopher specializing in the universalizability tradition after Kant—above all R. M. Hare's prescriptivist account and Peter Singer's use of reversible, equal-consideration reasoning. Think carefully before you write; start by brainstorming the hardest cases for any "what if everyone did that?" test and the requirements those cases force on it, then refine that into a clean list.

The deliverable is a list of desiderata that any adequate universalizability test must meet: the cases it must classify correctly, the puzzles it must dissolve or honestly mark as unsolved, and the distinctions it must respect. For each desideratum, give the concrete trouble case behind it—ideally drawn from village life, such as shared commons, levies, marriage customs, fire-watch duties, occupational roles—so I can see how the test behaves in the kind of small community the novel depicts. Mark which desiderata are negotiable and which are not.

In particular, the list should handle at least these traps, which are where I suspect cheap versions of the test break:

1. Description gerrymandering: an agent can make almost any rule pass by loading "the relevant circumstances" with self-serving particularity—people whose name begins with the same letter, people from the north lane, people who were owed a favor. An adequate test must constrain the level and kind of description without already assuming which features matter.

2. Preference pathology: an agent may accept the universalized practice applied to himself, sincerely and after vivid imagination, because his preferences are not what an onlooker would call decent. The test needs an answer to whose acceptance counts—the agent's actual preferences, those he would have after full information and acquaintance with every affected position, or something else again.

3. Role scarcity: some rules cannot be universalized not because they are wrong but because they are positional—if everyone became the village healer, or everyone stood exempt from the fire-watch, the practice behind the role would collapse. The test must say whether that is a moral defect in the rule, a defect in applying the test to roles, or both.

4. Partial compliance: in a village of three hundred, a rule about the common grazing land may work when most people comply and fail when literally every person does—yet literal universality is exactly the comparison the test invites. The test must say how the universalization clause should be read when widespread-but-incomplete compliance is the live possibility.

After the list, flag any pair of desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, or can be jointly satisfied only by giving the test an authority no single villager could plausibly hold when addressing equals. Identify the one desideratum that does the most work in ruling out a naïve version of the test. Then, briefly, score the three main versions against the list: Kant's formula of universal law, Hare's prescriptivist universalization, and Singer's equal-consideration universalization—saying where each does well and where it fails the desiderata. Keep the scoring short; the weight belongs on the desiderata and their cases.

Do not quote from the literature and do not cite texts; if some view is relevant, name it and state it in your own voice. The test, as specified, should be defensible enough for one character to argue from in earnest, yet precise enough about its own limits that the novel's later cases can break it for reasons we understand.
