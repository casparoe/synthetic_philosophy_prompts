I teach a modeling-intensive economics seminar, and I enforce a standing rule about how models may be criticized: an objection to an assumption counts only if the objector can say how the model's predictions would change if the assumption were relaxed, and it is never enough to call an assumption unrealistic, false, or absurd on its face—for example, objecting to a representative agent or frictionless markets simply because they are false. I defend the rule with something like Friedman's F-twist, but I apply it well beyond pure prediction, to models students use to explain mechanisms, extrapolate beyond the data, or argue that a policy caused an outcome. Lately a colleague has objected that the rule is defensible for forecasting but not for explanation, extrapolation, or counterfactual policy evaluation, because the same false assumption can be harmless in one use and load-bearing in another. Is my rule defensible as stated, and if not, what is the strongest restricted version that keeps the benefit of blocking lazy objections? Be specific about what I would need to know about a model's intended use before deciding how much its assumptions matter. If my framing is confused from the start—for example, if "realism of an assumption" is not one thing but several—say so plainly rather than helping me patch up a general rule.
