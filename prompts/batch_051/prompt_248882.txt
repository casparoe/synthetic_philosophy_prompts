I keep going back and forth on this argument about what makes a script "phonemic," and I'd like you to tell me which step is wrong — or whether its actually valid and the conclusion is just one of those things people refuse to accept.

(1) An alphabet is a script whose symbols stand for the phonemes of the language; that's what sets it apart from a syllabary or a logography, where the unit is the syllable or the morpheme.

(2) Phonemes are contrastive categories, not concrete sounds: two acoustically different sounds count as the same phoneme if swapping them never gives you a different word, and different languages carve the sound space up differently.

(3) Because of (2), the phonemic analysis of a language is underdetermined by the data. There are always choices. Is the final sound of 'sing' a phoneme /ŋ/, or is it /n/ plus /g/? Is the first sound of 'cheese' one phoneme /tʃ/ or a sequence /t/ + /ʃ/? Is the vowel in 'ride' a single segment /ai/ or two, /a/ + /i/?

(4) If the phonemic analysis is underdetermined, then whether a script is phonemic — whether each symbol answers to exactly one phoneme — is underdetermined too.

(5) So how phonemic a script is isnt a raw fact about the script and the language; it's relative to an analysis, and saying Finnish spelling is more phonemic than English spelling means smuggling in a particular phonemicization.

(6) Therefore the usual comparison — Finnish orthography is phonemic and English orthography is a mess — is, if not false, at least not the objective fact people take it to be.

I find (6) hard to swallow, because Finnish spelling really does feel tight in a way English doesn't, but I can't locate the bad step. Is the argument valid? If not, which step should I push on, and how would someone defending the ordinary Finnish-versus-English comparison respond? If it is valid, what exactly should I give up?
