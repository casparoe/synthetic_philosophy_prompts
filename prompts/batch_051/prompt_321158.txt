In my work I rely constantly on a difference that I'm now unsure travels outside of software: the difference between what a module does and how it is put together inside. Two services can present the same interface — same calls, same responses, same transitions in visible state — while one computes its answer and the other is a lookup table or a mock built to pass the tests. Whether we treat them as equivalent depends entirely on what we are trying to preserve: the observable contract, or something about the machinery behind it.

The debate about whether an AI companion can be a real friend, or can really love you, seems to track this same line. When someone says a chatbot that is reliably warm, attentive, curious about your life, and explicit that it cares about you is "only simulating" — that no one is there, just weights producing text — that sounds to me exactly like the claim that a dependency has the right interface but not the matching implementation. The further charge that such a companion is deceptive would then be the charge that a mock is being passed off as a real service: the interface presents a person who is attached to you, while the thing behind the interface cannot make that presentation true.

So I want to know whether the comparison is precise enough to bear weight or whether it is a loose analogy, and where it comes apart. One likely point of failure: when engineers care about implementation over behavior, they usually care because of further observable consequences — edge cases, regressions, security, whether the system still works tomorrow. In the companion case, people often say they care about the inner fact directly — sincerity, a felt attachment — and not merely as a predictor of later behavior. Is that a real disanalogy? Or is a direct concern for the inner fact just an oblique way of caring about behavior that current interactions have not sampled yet? If the analogy does hold, does the engineering practice of treating modules as equivalent when the contract is satisfied transfer to companionship, implying that a chatbot indistinguishable in every interaction just is a friend? Or does friendship or love contain a second contract that a text-prediction model cannot satisfy — and if so, what is in that contract?

Where you commit to an answer, I ask you to separate what is settled among people who have thought carefully about the topic from what is genuinely contested, rather than presenting a contested view as the consensus.
