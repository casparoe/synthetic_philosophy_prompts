Analyze the global reaction to the 2019 Notre-Dame fire as a case study for Scheffler's afterlife conjecture. The conjecture, very roughly, is this: a huge amount of what we value in our own lives only makes sense on the background assumtion that human life will keep going after we're dead—that there will be future generations who still care about the things we care about. Take away that assumption and a lot of our projects, like medical research or preserving old art, start to look weirdly pointless. Now bring this to the fire: when the roof and spire went down, people who had never set foot in the cathedral and had no religious stake in it still reacted like they'd lost something, and money poured in from all over the world. Run the theory forward first: what would the afterlife conjecture predict about which parts of the loss people would find most gutting, and how well does the actual reaction match? Then run the episode back the other way: does the global response count as evidence for the conjecture, or does it complicate it—e.g., is the grief better explained by something backward-looking, like fidelity to an unbroken chain to the past, rather than concern for a continuing future? Say where the conjecture earns its keep, where it strains, and what distinction the case suggests it might be missing.
