Something i've never understood about the fight over reading the Bible literally versus allegorically is why it's been running for nearly two thousand years without settling into a stable compromise. Origen, back in the third century, already thought some passages are impossible or unworthy of God if read at face value, and that the Spirit planted those stumbling blocks to force readers past the letter. Augustine had his own layered way of reading, and the medievals systematized the fourfold sense (literal, allegorical, moral, anagogical), where the senses mostly didn't have to compete. Then the Reformers came along and said the literal sense is the one that matters and allegory is just a machine for making the text mean whatever the clergy wants. And modern fundamentalism turns "literal" into an identity badge: treating Genesis as history or the miracles as flat fact is close to a test of orthodoxy, not merely a hermeneutic. The debate hasn't moved: the same verses get fought over, the same arguments get recycled, and almost nobody changes sides.

My working theory is that this is not mainly a disagreement about what the text means. It's a disagreement about authority (who gets to overrule the plain sense: tradition, church, individual conscience?), about what the text is for (a record of God's acts, a rule of life, a ladder for spiritual ascent?), and about what counts as evidence — the fundamentalist sees the weirdness of a six-day creation as a test of faith, while the Origenist sees exactly that weirdness as the signal to switch registers. But i'm not at all sure; it might still be a genuine disagreement about meaning and authorial intent, with the authority stuff as a symptom rather than the disease.

Give me a diagnosis, with reasons. What kind of disagreement is this really — verbal, factual, methodological, about which cases count as data, or just starting intuitions neither side can argue for? And once you've diagnosed it, tell me what would actually count as progress: is there a distinction, an argument, or a historical discovery that could move the thing, or is the stalemate permanent because the two sides aren't answering the same question?
