I am an undergraduate taking a course on practical rationality and the philosophy of risk, and we have spent the last few weeks on expected-value reasoning, Pascal's-wager-style arguments, and the question of whether expected utility remains a reliable guide when probabilities become very small and payoffs very large. In our reading group, a classmate who is much more involved in the rationalist side of these debates than I am gave what he presented as the standard case for signing up for cryonics. As I understood him, the argument runs as follows. Cryonics is best understood as a bet: if revival works, the payoff is enormous, plausibly centuries of additional life; if it fails, you are no worse off than if you had never signed up, because the alternative is being dead either way. The only real cost is the life-insurance premium, which is small relative to the size of the payoff. Since a tiny probability of an enormous gain can still have a large expected value, even a very small chance of revival makes signing up rational, and those who refuse are mostly exhibiting status quo bias or failing to think in expected values. Moreover, because the revived person would share your memories and personality, it is obvious that it would still be you, so there is no serious philosophical problem about identity. The only genuine objection, he concluded, is whether the technology will actually work, and that is an empirical question about future science rather than a conceptual difficulty with the practice.

I find the broad shape of this convincing, but a few moves strike me as too quick, especially the jump from a small probability of a huge payoff to the conclusion that signing up is rationally required, and the way the identity question is dismissed in a single sentence. I am not sure whether these are genuine problems or just confusions on my part. Could you give me an accuracy audit of my classmate's summary? In particular: which parts would a careful defender of cryonics accept, which are common simplifications, and which are overstatements or misrepresentations? Does the conclusion, that signing up is clearly rational once we set aside empirical doubts about the technology, survive the corrections? And please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, so that I do not come away treating one side of a live debate as the consensus.
