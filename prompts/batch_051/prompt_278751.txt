Analyze the 1964 U.S. Surgeon General's report on smoking and health as a case study for the metaphysics of causation. The committee concluded that cigarette smoking causes lung cancer, but it did so by advancing explicit criteria — consistency, strength, specificity, temporality, and coherence — for treating nonexperimental evidence as proof of causation, at a time when no mechanism from smoke to tumor had been worked out. Run a two-way test against Russell's eliminativism about causation, the view that mature science has no need of the notion of cause and that it survives only as an outdated holdover. Say what an eliminativist would claim the report was really doing when it attributed causation, whether the report's criterion-based practice refutes eliminativism, or whether it instead shows that causal talk functions as a pragmatic shorthand for stable regularities plus a decision to intervene. End by saying which position the episode forces into the larger concessions.
