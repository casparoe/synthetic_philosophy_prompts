I keep noticing a structural parallel between two ways of refusing to apply later standards to earlier conduct, and I cannot tell whether it is deep or merely verbal. Criminal law rejects ex post facto punishment: an act is judged by the law as it stood when the act was done, with the standard rationales being fair warning to the agent and a limit on the state's power to rewrite the past after the fact. Historical moral judgment sometimes runs the same way: we withhold or soften blame for Jefferson's slaveholding or Columbus's treatment of the indigenous peoples he encountered, on the ground that those acts were broadly accepted in their own time and the agent was a man of his time. Both principles seem to say: assess conduct by the standards or rules available at the moment of acting, not by later ones. Is this a genuine identity of structure, so that arguments for one practice transfer to the other? Or does it break down because legal non-retroactivity is about notice, coordination, and the legitimate use of coercion, whereas historical blame is about whether the agent could have been expected to know better? Say precisely where the mapping holds and where it comes apart, and name something the parallel would predict that turns out false.
