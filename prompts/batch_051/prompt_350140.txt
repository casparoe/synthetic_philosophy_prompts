I cover planetary science and ideas for a living, and the 2006 Pluto demotion is a story I keep coming back to. Lately I've been reading Quine's Two Dogmas of Empiricism, and I can't shake the feeling that its the same story told twice. So I want you to run the case through the theory, in both directions.

Here's the episode as I'd report it: through the 1990s and early 2000s astronomers kept finding large trans-Neptunian objects — Eris especially, a body that seemed at least Pluto-sized and maybe bigger — and it got untenable to just add every new round one to the list. In 2006 the IAU voted on a definition at Prague. A planet had to orbit the Sun, be massive enough to pull itself round, and have cleared its orbital neighborhood. Pluto flunked the third test and got reclassified as a dwarf planet. The fight was strange because it looked partly semantic (what should the word planet mean?) and partly empirical (what's actually out there, and is Pluto more like the eight planets or more like its neighbors in the Kuiper belt?). The neighborhood-clearing criterion came to do a lot of physical work, but it arrived through a floor vote, which is about as conventional as it gets.

Now put Quine on it. On his picture there's no clean line between truths of meaning and truths of fact; we hold the web of belief together and revise it in pieces under pressure, even the bits that look definitional. I want a disciplined mapping: what does that picture say was going on in Prague, and what does it get right — the way planet, dwarf planet, and Kuiper belt all got adjusted together, say, or the fact that the definition was neither pure stipulation nor a clean empirical discovery? Then tell me where it strains. Does the fact that the winning criterion latched onto something physically real, dynamical dominance, show that the category was doing more than Quine's holism admits, or is that just more evidence for the holism? Where exactly does the analytic–synthetic distinction, or its absence, do explanatory work here, and where does politics inside a professional body do work that Quine has nothing to say about? And finally run it in reverse: does the episode land as a confirmation of Two Dogmas, an anomaly for it, or a case it wasn't built to handle? I'm less interested in whether Pluto should've kept the title than in what the whole affair shows about whether empirical discovery and semantic decision can be pried apart at all — or whether there just never was a seperate line to begin with.
