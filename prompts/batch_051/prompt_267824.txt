I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long piece on what neuroscience has actually established about free will. The move I keep running into goes like this: in Benjamin Libet's well-known experiments, the readiness potential, a slow electrical buildup in the brain, begins about half a second before a self-initiated movement, while subjects report the conscious decision or urge to move only about two hundred milliseconds before the act. The standard inference is that the brain had already set the movement in motion before the person consciously decided to move, so the felt decision is a late-arriving story rather than the cause of the action. The argument seems to have real force, but I keep feeling that it proves far too much, and I need help making that objection precise.

Here is the argument, as tightly as I can reconstruct it. (1) If a conscious decision causes a voluntary action, then the decision must occur before the neural process that initiates the action. (2) Libet-style data show that the neural process that initiates a voluntary movement begins before the subject reports the conscious decision. (3) So the conscious decision does not cause the movement. (4) What holds for a simple wrist flexion holds for decisions generally: if the conscious decision does not cause the simple action, then the same temporal structure would show that conscious deliberation never causes complex actions either, such as writing an article or reaching a verdict about evidence. (5) Therefore conscious deliberation has no effect on what anyone does, including what scientists conclude when they examine their own data. (6) But if conscious deliberation never causes scientific conclusions, then no result has ever been accepted because the evidence supported it; everyone would merely have been caused to think it was supported. That conclusion is self-undermining in a way no researcher, including the experimenters themselves, can coherently accept as a result of weighing the evidence.

Where does the argument go wrong, and what exactly does it cost to reject that step? I want a committed diagnosis rather than a survey of positions: name the premise or inference you would reject, explain why it initially looked sound, and say what remains of the Libet challenge once the offending step is removed.
