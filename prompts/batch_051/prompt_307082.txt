I teach an upper-level undergraduate course in philosophy and I am writing a final-exam essay question on the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the person who dies. The question should be one a prepared student can answer in about an hour, and it must be impossible to answer well by reciting the familiar contrast between Epicureanism and the deprivation account; it should force students to work through the temporal structure of harm. Build the exam question around the following reconstruction, which you may number and use but should not distort:

(1) Something can be bad for a person only if there is some time at which it is bad for that person. (2) For something to be bad for a person at a time, that person must exist at that time and be in a bad condition at that time. (3) At every time before death, the person has not yet died; at every time after death, the person no longer exists. Therefore, death is never bad for the one who dies.

The exam question should direct students to do three things: (i) identify which premise the deprivation account rejects and explain why rejecting it is a substantive thesis about the nature of harm rather than a mere dodge; (ii) present the strongest Epicurean objection to that rejection — the demand that the deprivation theorist locate the harm in time — and give the best reply available to the deprivation theorist; (iii) reach and defend a verdict on whether the argument's central insight, that nothing can be bad for you unless there is a time at which you are around to have it, survives intact, survives only in a weakened form, or is refuted.

Then write (a) a model answer of no more than 500 words that would receive full marks, and (b) a marking scheme for 20 marks that names the specific claims an A answer must make under each of (i)–(iii), the marks each is worth, and the identifiable mistakes that separate a B answer from an A answer. The marking scheme should be no more than 250 words.
