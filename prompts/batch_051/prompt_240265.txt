I am currently taking a course on statutory interpretation, and I have been trying to work out where the absurdity doctrine actually begins and ends. The course states the doctrine as: a court may decline to give a provision its plain meaning where doing so would yield a result so absurd, manifestly unjust, or plainly unreasonable that the legislature cannot rationally have intended it. I find that formulation easy to accept in the abstract and hard to apply at the margins, so I want to test it against a case rather than the stock examples.

Consider section 7 of the (fictional) District Courts (Decorum) Act 1988:

Section 7(1): "No person shall bring into any part of a courthouse any device capable of emitting an audible signal, including but not limited to a pager, portable telephone, or alarm."
Section 7(2): "Subsection (1) shall not apply to a hearing aid worn by a person with a hearing impairment."

Now take a juror who wears a continuous glucose monitor. The device consists of a sensor and a small dedicated receiver; the receiver has no telephone or paging function, and it emits a loud alarm when the juror's blood glucose falls to a level that could quickly cause unconsciousness. The audible alarm is her only reliable warning, and she cannot safely remove or silence the device. On arrival she is refused entry by a security officer under section 7(1). On the wording, the officer is correct: the receiver is a device, it is capable of emitting an audible signal, and it does not fall within the section 7(2) exception.

I am not asking whether a literal reading reaches the device; I am asking what a court ought to do once it sees that it does. In particular, I would like a disciplined account of what each of the main interpretive approaches would treat as the legally operative reason here. What does the plain-meaning reasoner do with the fact that the legislature drafted a single express exception — does the maxim that the specification of one exception excludes others settle the case, or is that too wooden when the listed exception targets the same underlying interest of access for disabled persons? What would a purposivist do with the statute's evident aim of keeping disruptive audible interruptions out of proceedings, given that a hypoglycemia alarm is itself a possible interruption? What would an interpreter who asks what the drafters would have said if the case had been put to them in 1988 conclude about a wearable medical monitoring device of a kind not then in ordinary use, and how much weight should that counterfactual be given? What would a reasonable reader of section 7, standing in the juror's position, take the provision to require or permit?

The heart of my question is the absurdity doctrine's threshold. Is barring this juror absurd, manifestly unjust, merely harsh, or merely the kind of obsolete result that a plain-meaning approach should tolerate until the legislature amends the statute? On one hand, the text is clear and the exception is narrow; on the other, the consequence is exclusion of a medically vulnerable person from a public obligation, for conduct that would otherwise be irrelevant to the order and safety of the courtroom. If a court concludes that the application is contrary to what the drafters would have wanted but not "absurd" in the doctrinal sense, what should it do — enforce the text anyway, construe the word "device" or "capable" narrowly, imply a medical-necessity exception, or something else? I am less interested in a preferred outcome than in a clear statement of what each approach licenses, and an honest assessment of what the absurdity doctrine can and cannot justify in this kind of case.
