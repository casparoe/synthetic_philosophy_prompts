I am a retired physicist and a recent arrival to philosophy of language, and I have been turning over a particular argument until I can no longer tell whether it proves too much or too little. I want a committed verdict on it: tell me whether the argument is valid; if it is not, identify the exact step that fails and under what reading; if it is valid, identify which premise you reject and defend that choice. Do not lay out possible interpretations and leave the decision to me.

The claim I want to test is that the geometry of a word-embedding space — its distances, offsets, and analogy directions — can be treated as the structure of semantic space itself. The contrary argument runs as follows.

(1) A word embedding is a learned representation. For the usual stochastic training methods, training the same objective on the same corpus from different random initializations yields different coordinates and not exactly the same pairwise distances, while meeting the objective about equally well.
(2) If a quantity differs across two representations that are equally adequate to the same underlying data, then that quantity is a feature of the representation rather than of the underlying reality.
(3) The pairwise distances and offsets that define an embedding's geometry — for example, the difference between the vectors for king and man, or the offset by which king − man + woman approaches queen — differ across such equally adequate runs.
(4) Therefore, pairwise distances and offsets in embedding spaces are representational artifacts.
(5) Since the geometry of an embedding space is constituted by pairwise distances and offsets of just this kind, that geometry is not the geometry of meaning.

My uneasiness is with the move from (2) and (3) to (4). In my former field, we discount quantities that are not invariant under the symmetries of the representation, but we do not automatically treat every difference between two numerically distinct solutions as proof of non-reality; some variation is the analogue of gauge freedom or of incomplete convergence, and some quantities can be real yet represented differently in different solutions. So I cannot decide whether the argument is valid but rests on an overly strong premise at (2), or whether the inference to (4) misapplies a sound principle by equivocating on what it is to be equally adequate. Give me a definite answer: which step, if any, fails, and if none fails, which premise is false and why.
