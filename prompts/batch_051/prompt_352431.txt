I am a PhD candidate in philosophy, working on the epistemology of philanthropic priority-setting. A draft chapter of mine defends a deflationary treatment of the measurability-bias objection to effective altruism. I argue that the objection is best decomposed into two claims that critics tend to run together. The first is cognitive: when an intervention cannot be assigned a tractable expected-value estimate, evaluators treat the absence of a number as a mild signal against the intervention, rather than treating the estimate as merely missing. The second is institutional: a funding ecosystem in which quantified outcomes are easier to publish, audit, and advertise to donors will select for quantified work, so the observed preference for measurable interventions could arise even if no individual evaluator ever made the cognitive error. A colleague in my working group, an empirically minded scholar of nonprofit evaluation, wants me to stop presenting both claims as conceptual theses that data cannot disturb. I think she is probably right about the institutional claim but not obviously about the cognitive one. I would like you to take her side as seriously as possible and do what my paper currently does not: specify what evidence could actually bear on these claims. For each claim, or for their conjunction, set out the strongest feasible test or natural experiment, even if it has to be idealized; say what pattern of results would count as evidence of a genuine measurability bias rather than a rational adaptation to scarce evidence about less-measured interventions; and state what a positive result would and would not settle. I am especially interested in the boundary question: can any observation distinguish a cognitive bias from an institutional selection effect, or is that distinction in part imposed by us rather than found in the data?
