Consider the following claim: in the standard endowment-effect experiments, in which participants randomly receive a mug or similar object and then state selling and buying prices, the usual gap between willingness to accept and willingness to pay is better explained by a genuine change in the good's value to its new owner than by a bias that distorts a stable, framing-independent preference. Give a number between 0 and 1 as your credence in that claim, and then decompose it.

Assign rough probabilities to at least the following sub-questions: (a) How likely is it that the measured gap is in significant part an artifact of transaction costs, strategic misreporting, or participants' confusion about the elicitation task? (b) Conditional on the gap's being real, how likely is it that the core mechanism is a change in valuation produced by ownership itself, rather than loss aversion around a reference point? (c) If the mechanism is loss aversion, how likely is it that loss aversion is a defensible sensitivity to reference-dependent value rather than a framing effect to be corrected? (d) How likely is it that the standard random-allocation demonstrations generalize to the real-world endowments people actually care about, such as houses, jobs, or relationships? (e) How likely is it that a sharp distinction can be drawn between an underlying true preference and an expressed preference warped by framing, so that the original claim has a determinate answer? Show how these sub-credences combine into your overall number, and state which sub-question the overall credence is most sensitive to.

Then identify the single argument or experimental result that would move your credence most, and say where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
