A writer in a popular book on economics and rationality gives the following account of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem:

"According to Harsanyi, if each person's preferences satisfy the standard axioms of decision theory, and society's preferences satisfy those same axioms plus the minimal Pareto condition — that society prefer what everyone prefers — then society must rank alternatives by a weighted sum of individuals' utilities. Normalizing the weights turns this into the principle of maximizing average utility. Utilitarianism is therefore not one moral doctrine among others but a theorem: any rational evaluator who respects unanimous preference is logically forced to be a utilitarian. The same result also makes interpersonal comparisons of utility respectable, since it grounds them in nothing more than each person's own risk preferences. The only remaining debate between utilitarians and their critics is a practical one, about measurement, not a principled one."

I find this suspicious: the conclusion looks far too strong for the premises, and the phrase about grounding interpersonal comparisons in risk preferences seems to be doing hidden work, but I don't know enough about the theorem's assumptions to pinpoint the errors. Go through the account claim by claim. Separate what is accurate from what is subtly distorted, flag any conflation of claims that a careful statement of the theorem keeps apart, and say whether the final claim — that only measurement disputes remain — survives the corrections.
