I keep running into an asymmetry in how people judge protections against a weak will, and I can't find the principle behind it. If I delete a distracting app from my phone, move part of each paycheck into an account I can't touch until the end of the year, or ask a friend to change my password on a time-wasting site until my exams are over, people call it self-mastery. If my employer blocks the same site on the devices it owns, if a bank moves part of my income into the same locked account by default, or if a friend changes my password without being asked because he can see I need to study, many of the same people call it paternalism, treating an adult like a child. The natural reply is that I chose the first set of constraints and not the second. But the person who wants to browse the site at two in the morning never chose any of them; he simply wants to browse. Both kinds of rule handcuff a later self who was never asked, on the authority of an earlier, cooler self. Is the consent of that earlier self really enough to mark the boundary between self-respect and overreach? If it isn't, what does the boundary rest on — and is it a difference in kind or just a difference in whose fingerprints are on the handcuffs?
