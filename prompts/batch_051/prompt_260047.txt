I have been trying to work out, from my own reactions to some concrete cases, when it is fair to punish someone for harm or wrongdoing they did not intend and did not know about, and my instincts keep pulling in opposite directions. A delivery driver, obeying the speed limit, sober, and not distracted, hits a child who darts out between parked cars; I do not think he should go to prison or be left with a criminal record. A factory owner whose plant meets every environmental rule and is inspected on schedule has a valve fail and poison a creek; I think the company should pay for the cleanup and compensate those harmed, but criminal charges against the owner would be excessive. A bar owner whose bouncer checks identification carefully serves a twenty-year-old with a sophisticated fake ID; here I feel that a fine is appropriate, and I have little desire to let the bar off. A pharmacist dispenses a sealed bottle exactly as prescribed, but the manufacturer had sealed the wrong pills inside and a patient is injured; the pharmacist, it seems to me, should not be punished. A woman is caught at the airport with drugs her boyfriend hid in the lining of her suitcase without her knowing; I would not punish her for trafficking, though I understand holding her while the story is checked. And then there is the case I keep circling back to: a woman marries a man who shows her a final divorce decree that later turns out to be a forgery, and she genuinely and reasonably believed he was free to marry. My gut says she has still committed bigamy and should not simply walk away, but when I compare her to the pharmacist I cannot state the difference.

The rough principle I keep testing is that there should be no punishment without genuine fault: if someone takes the precautions a reasonable person would take and has no reason to suspect they are doing anything unlawful, the law ought not to treat them as a wrongdoer. That principle fits the driver, the factory owner, the pharmacist, and the woman at the airport. But it would also excuse the bar owner and the woman who married on the strength of a forged decree, and I am unwilling to excuse either of them, or at least not fully. I notice that the two I want to punish both involve relying on a document that looks real but is not, which makes me suspect I am not being consistent about how much documents excuse mistakes. I also notice that I am more willing to punish mistakes when the activity is something a person has chosen to enter under special rules, such as serving alcohol or marrying, and less willing when the activity is ordinary life, such as driving or flying with luggage someone else packed.

Is there one principle that captures all of these verdicts, or would I have to sacrifice some of them to get it? If so, which verdicts does the principle force me to surrender, and what would I lose by surrendering them? I also do not know what to say about a landlord who follows every screening rule and rents to a tenant who turns out to have run a fraud from the apartment; it feels closer to the bar owner in kind but closer to the pharmacist in my sympathies.
