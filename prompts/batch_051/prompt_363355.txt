I've been going back and forth on the whole "what is a biological function" debate and I can't tell whether it's a genuine fight or just two ways of talking that never quite make contact. On one side there's the historical, etiological view: the function of a trait is, roughly, what it was selected for—the reason it's there at all. So the heart's function is to pump blood, not because it does that now in any especially impressive way, but because ancestors with pumping hearts left more descendants. On the other side there's the causal-role or systems view: the function of a part is just whatever it does that contributes to some capacity of the larger system you decide to analyze, with no selection-history requirement at all. Relative to the circulatory system, the heart's function is pumping; if you shift to treating the whole body as a transducer of mechanical forces, the function of that same heart might come out quite different, and on this view that's not a bug, it's the view working as intended.

Where they come apart, obviously, is exaptations and byproducts: a trait originally selected for one job that now reliably does another (say, an enlarged wrist bone co-opted into a bamboo-stripping digit), or a byproduct that was never selected but happens to be useful now. The historical camp will say the function is the old selected-for job, or that there is no function at all. The causal-role camp will say the function is whatever contribution the thing makes to the system you're analyzing right now. My working theory is that this isn't one substantive debate but two different explanatory projects—"why does this trait exist?" versus "how does this system work now?"—that share a word, and the real argument is just about which project is allowed to keep the word "function." But I worry that's to deflationary, and that underneath it there's a real disagreement about whether history can literally be part of what a thing is, or about what counts as a trait or a system in the first place.
