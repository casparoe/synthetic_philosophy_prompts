Suppose a small city in a country where vote-buying and patronage are common tries the following reform. Instead of banning gifts to officials and politicians — most gifts just look like ordinary hospitality and the ban is unenforceable — anyone may give anything, but every gift over a token amount has to be accompanied by a signed card naming the giver, the recipient, the occasion, and what the giver values the gift at. The cards are posted online for anyone to see. Within thirty days the recipient has to either refuse the gift in writing or post a return card saying what, if anything, the giver has asked for in return (including "nothing"). And if the gift is above a slightly higher threshold, then for the next six months any official act that benefits the giver is automatically flagged and has to be justified in writing by a different official in the same department before it can go through.

Walk me through how this would actually play out. What does it incentivize for givers, recipients, and the officials who have to write the justifications? Where does it get gamed first — under-valuing gifts, splitting gifts, routing them through spouses or businesses, doing the favor after the window closes, capturing the justifying official, or something else? What does the public wall do to ordinary non-corrupt gift-giving, in a context where refusing a gift is a serious insult? And how much of the design rests on a specifically Western assumption that a gift is a discrete thing that can be cleanly separated from an ongoing relationship and from official conduct? Say whether the failure modes are fixable without gutting the scheme, and where you'd expect the scheme to change behavior for the worse rather than the better. Toward the end, seperate what's fairly well established about disclosure and cooling-off rules from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
