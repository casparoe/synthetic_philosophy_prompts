I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've spent the past few weeks down a rabbit hole about "if" sentences, so apologies if this is badly put. I keep using a distinction of my own and I want somebody to tell me honestly whether it's defensible.

I call it the split between settled conditionals and live conditionals. A settled conditional is one where the facts are already fixed and the sentence just reports how things stand: "if the butler did it, he did it in the study." That one's simply true or false. A live conditional is one where the antecedent is still open and the sentence works as a policy for updating your confidence: "if the butler did it, he used the candlestick." I can't settle that one now; what I have is a degree of expectation I'd move to if I learned the butler did it.

I know the standard background: the usual idea is that the probability of an indicative conditional "if A then C" is the conditional probability, P(C|A). Lewis's triviality results then showed that if the conditional is an ordinary proposition, and its probability equals P(C|A) across enough probability functions, the whole thing collapses into triviality. So my thought is that Lewis's results are really a proof that we need my split. Settled conditionals are propositions with ordinary truth conditions, but we don't measure them by P(C|A). Live conditionals aren't propositions at all; they're inferential commitments, and P(C|A) is the right number for them, so there's no proposition there to be trivialised.

Am I onto something, or have I just re-labelled a distinction that's already well known? If the idea is coherent, where does it break? And if its already got a proper name in the literature I'd rather use that. Please give me a candid assessment, and answer in British English if you wouldnt mind.
