Exam question: Explain Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism. Use the George and Jim examples to indicate what the objection is meant to show, and discuss what Williams means by the idea of one thought too many.

Student answer: Williams's integrity objection starts from the idea that a person's life is held together by ground projects, the long-term commitments and ideals she cares about most. Utilitarianism asks the agent to treat those projects as just one more set of consequences to weigh. So George, who is opposed to chemical weapons, is told to take the job in the weapons lab because he would do the research less zealously than the person who would otherwise take the job, and Jim is told to shoot one of the captured Indians because that leads to fewer deaths overall. Williams's point is that in these cases utilitarianism forces the agent to step back from the commitments that give his life its point, and in doing so it attacks his integrity: it makes him, in his own eyes, the instrument of a state of affairs he rejects. This connects to negative responsibility, since on the utilitarian view Jim becomes responsible for the nineteen deaths if he refuses, simply because his refusal is one of the causes of how things turn out. Williams thinks the utilitarian verdicts in these cases are wrong, and that showing they conflict with the agent's deepest commitments is enough to establish the integrity objection. The phrase one thought too many is his name for the extra calculating thought utilitarianism imposes on Jim before he can shoot: the thought that this killing is the optimific thing to do, and Williams thinks that extra thought is what corrupts the action.
