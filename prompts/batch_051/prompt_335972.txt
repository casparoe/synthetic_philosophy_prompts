A friend in the econ department walked me through this and now I can't find the exit. He uses it to argue that the common prior assumption is self-refuting, but the conclusion feels way to strong. I'll write it out.

(1) Two ideal Bayesian reasoners with the same prior and the same total evidence must assign the same posterior probability to any proposition. That's just conditionalization.

(2) Harsanyi's common prior assumption: any difference in priors between two ideal reasoners is itself the trace of a difference in evidence at some earlier point. There are no brute, unexplained differences in priors; in the limit, ideal reasoners share one prior.

(3) Aumann's agreement theorem: if two reasoners share a common prior and their posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge between them, then those posterior probabilities are equal.

(4) Take two climate economists who have read the same papers and still disagree by a factor of five about the social cost of carbon. Their evidence is public, and each side's posterior is common knowledge in the relevant sense: they publish their numbers, each has read the other's work, each knows the other has read theirs, and so on.

(5) So, by (2), (3), and (4), their disagreement means at least one of them has private evidence they haven't shared or is falling short of ideal rationality.

(6) But that's a crazy thing to say about two well-informed, careful economists. Persistent reasonable disagreement among experts is a real thing.

(7) So the common prior assumption, plus Aumann, proves that reasonable expert disagreement is basically impossible. My friend says that's a reductio of the common prior assumption. I can't accept the "basically impossible" part, but I also don't want to just assert that the common prior assumption is false and leave it there.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I'd rather keep (6) than (2), but I want to know what I'm paying.
