I keep going back and forth on a problem in the philosophy of perception, and I would like a professional philosopher of perception to work through it with me as carefully as possible. On one side, I am drawn to disjunctivism in its metaphysical form: when I see the coffee cup on my desk, the experience I am having is in some important sense a relation to the cup itself, not an inner state that a perfect hallucination could share. Disjunctivism seems to honor the manifest fact that perception puts me directly in touch with the world, and it blocks the slide from the fact that perception and hallucination can be subjectively indistinguishable to the skeptical conclusion that we only ever experience our own mental states. On the other side, the argument from illusion keeps pulling at me. Consider a hallucination produced by a drug or a laboratory stimulation that is, from the inside, exactly like my veridical experience of the cup. It feels almost forced to say that there is one kind of conscious state present in both cases, a seeming to see the cup, and that the only difference is whether the world cooperates in producing it. That common-kind assumption strikes me as a firm intuition rather than a theory-driven prejudice, and disjunctivism's denial of it can sound like special pleading: how could two experiences be indistinguishable and yet differ fundamentally in kind?

What I want is a negotiated verdict, not a one-sided brief. Present both positions at their strongest, including the disjunctivist's best reply to the argument from illusion, and start by steelmanning each side before you argue. Then tell me which move you would make and why: should the common-kind intuition be debunked and dropped (and if so, what debunking explanation actually works — does it confuse indistinguishability with sameness of kind?), should disjunctivism be amended or abandoned in favor of a common-kind view, or is there a reading on which they are compatible after all (for example, by distinguishing epistemic indistinguishability from metaphysical commonality)? Say explicitly what would justify each of those moves. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which consideration, argument, or counterexample would most likely lead you to reverse your verdict, and where is your own reasoning most likely to fail?
