I have been collecting the rulings of a judge whose treatment of legal fictions I admire, and I want both a prediction and an analysis of the rule she is implicitly following. Her published rulings include the following. She upheld the treatment of a trading corporation as a legal person that can hold property, contract, and be sued in its own name, while noting that no one believes the corporation has a mind. She refused to aggregate fragments of knowledge held by different employees into a single corporate knowledge for a criminal fraud charge, on the ground that the law should not pretend an institution knows what no individual person knows. She affirmed treating an adopted child as if born to the adoptive parents for the purpose of intestate succession, cutting off the biological father's line. She rejected a constructive-possession charge against a defendant who held the only key to a safe but had never exercised control over its contents, holding that criminal possession requires actual control rather than a pretence of control. She allowed an amended pleading that corrected a misnamed defendant to relate back as if filed on the original date, because the defendant had notice within the limitation period and a time bar would be a windfall. She declined to pierce the corporate veil between a parent and its fully owned subsidiary where there was no actual domination, holding that two entities are not one merely because treating them as one would help a plaintiff reach the parent's assets.

The new matter before her is this. An investment firm proposes to charter an autonomous fund: a pool of assets managed entirely by an AI, with no human shareholders, partners, or employees, recognized as a legal person for the limited purposes of holding title, entering contracts, and being sued in its own name. No natural person would stand behind the fund as a member or owner, and the proposal is explicit that the personhood is a fiction: the fund is not claimed to be conscious, to know anything, or to be capable of blame. I can see at least two rules that fit all six of her rulings and that appear to point in opposite directions here, one turning on whether a fiction is dispensable shorthand rather than a device that does real normative work, the other turning on whether a fiction stays within property, status, succession, and procedure rather than ascribing mental states or expanding liability.

What should I expect her to say, and what would consistency with her own record require? Please lay out the candidate rules that fit the six rulings, say what each predicts about the autonomous fund, give your best prediction with the reason for preferring it, identify which of the six precedents is the closest analog and why, and name the further case that would most cleanly separate the rules.

I want the answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. In one of the items, tell me what would most change your mind: which of the six rulings, had it come out the other way, or which fact about how the fund actually operates would most shift your prediction, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
