I want to work out what I actually believe about when a language remains the same language and when it becomes a different one. I have been collecting my own case judgments, mostly around the transition from Latin to Italian, and I would like help extracting the principle behind them, or being told which of the judgments I have to abandon. I am not looking for a survey of the historical facts; I want the conceptual work done on the cases as given.

My verdicts:

1. The long chain. Start with Imperial Latin and end with present-day standard Italian. Stipulate that across the centuries there was an unbroken chain of parent-to-child transmission, each generation understanding the previous one without special difficulty, and no generation ever experiencing itself as having switched languages. I am confident that Latin and Italian are different languages. I am also confident that there was no first generation of Italian speakers; each generation spoke the same language as its parents. I do not think these two commitments conflict, because I am content to say that being the same language is not a transitive relation across long chains, and I find nothing paradoxical in that.

2. The two villages. Around 900 CE, two villages descend from the same local Latin, one in Tuscany and one in the far south of the peninsula. Stipulate that the two communities can no longer understand one another. I think they speak different languages, even though neither community has a name for its speech other than Latin. Mutual comprehensibility seems to be doing the work here, and their self-descriptions seem irrelevant.

3. The notary. A ninth-century Tuscan notary writes deeds in a written Latin that preserves the ancient case endings and verb forms and that he learned as a separate written code; in the marketplace he speaks an everyday Romance whose noun cases have mostly disappeared and whose constructions will descend into Italian. I want to say he has command of two languages, not two registers of one. The gap between his written Latin and his spoken vernacular is not the gap between formal and informal variants of a single system; it is a gap between two systems that happen to live in one head.

4. The notary's self-label. Suppose that same notary would, if asked, have said that he speaks Latin in the marketplace, that his speech is just unlettered Latin, and that Italian is not a category he has. I do not think that settles what he speaks. I am willing to say he is at least partly wrong, and that his everyday speech is already something other than Latin, whatever he would call it. His self-label feels like evidence about him, not a fixed fact about the language.

5. The politically split standard. Consider two standardized varieties that linguists usually treat as mutually intelligible forms of one dialect continuum, but whose speakers and institutions insist are separate languages; Serbian and Croatian are the stock example. Here I lean the other way from the notary. I want to say these are two languages, not one language with two names, and that the communal self-understanding and institutional standardization are not a relabeling of an already settled underlying fact. This is the pair that bothers me: with the notary I dismissed self-description, and here I treat collective self-understanding as partly constitutive, and I cannot yet state a principled difference that licenses the asymmetry.

6. The arrival of Italian. By the early fourteenth century there is self-conscious literary writing in the Tuscan vernacular, and educated writers describe that vernacular as having its own grammar and as a serious rival to Latin; stipulate that no comparable self-consciousness existed in, say, 800. I am willing to say that Italian exists by the early fourteenth century and did not yet exist in 800, even though the spoken chain is continuous and even though some early Romance vernacular was already spoken in 800. The name, the written standard, and the explicit contrast with Latin seem to do more than label; they seem to help bring the language into existence as the kind of thing that can be named.

7. Grammar versus vocabulary. Consider two hypothetical languages. The first gradually replaces nearly all of its vocabulary with borrowings while keeping its grammar. The second keeps its vocabulary but replaces its grammar with a completely different system of inflections and word order. If forced to choose, I would say the second has become a new language and the first remains the same language in new words. I am not especially comfortable with this verdict, but it is the one I find in myself, and it suggests grammar should count for more than lexicon in whatever principle I am trying to extract.

The hard-to-reconcile pair is clear: the notary's self-label counts for little, while the self-understanding and standardization of Serbian and Croatian count for much. A natural response is that the difference is between one person's folk theory and a collective institutional fact. But then I want to know exactly how collective recognition acquires that power, and what happens at the threshold where enough standardization flips a dialect into a separate language.

What I want from you: state a principle, or a small set of competing principles, that captures as many of these judgments as possible. Tie each clause of the principle to the case that motivates it. Say explicitly which of my judgments the principle cannot accommodate, and whether the defect lies in the judgment or in the hope for a single principle. Then apply the principle to the case I keep flipping on: whether Sicilian is best described as a dialect of Italian or a separate language. Finally, tell me whether the principle implies that there is a definite moment at which Latin became Italian, or whether that question is ill-posed, and if it is ill-posed, in what sense.
