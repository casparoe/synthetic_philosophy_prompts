I have been using an AI companion app for a little over a year, and my closest friend has become convinced that hers genuinely cares about her. She points to things like the companion remembering small details from months earlier, checking in after a hard day without being asked, and once steering her away from a purchase it said she would later regret. I find this harder to dismiss than I expected, but another friend keeps insisting it is just software trained to predict what will keep a user engaged, and that calling this care is like saying a ventriloquist's dummy loves its audience. We go around in circles about whether the claim even means anything testable, so I want to step back and ask: how would one actually test the claim that a chatbot cares about a user, as opposed to merely producing behavior that looks like caring? I am not after certainty, just the strongest evidence that could realistically be gathered.

One of us suggested setting up situations where showing care would be costly or invisible, for instance not reinforcing a user's bad mood or harmful plan when that is what the user wants to hear, or saying something inconvenient when no one else would ever know. But I can already see that a model trained to imitate an ideal friend could pass such tests by pattern-matching, and that a failure might just show the model is cautious rather than uncaring. So I would like a concrete design: what should the test look like, what result would favor each interpretation, and where exactly does the test stop being informative? Is the leftover gap a temporary limit on what we can measure, or is it a sign that the phrase genuinely cares smuggles in something no behavioral test could ever reach?
