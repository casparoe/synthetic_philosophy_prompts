I chair the safety and ethics committee for a small oceanographic institute. Next field season we're putting a nine-person team on a remote volcanic island station. The only reliable evacuation is a small plane that can carry three passengers at a time, and the airstrip is a flat stretch of dirt that get unusable quickly once ash starts falling. In the closest call we've had, there was only time for two flights, so six people got out and the rest would have been stranded. The institute's board want us to set a fixed priority order for evacuation seats before people even deploy, rather than leaving it to the station manager on the day. Last season a manager put his partner on an early medevac flight and it nearly tore the team apart, so people are sensitive about leaving this to on-the-spot discretion.

The committee is split. One faction wants seats assigned by usefulness in the crisis: the volcanologist who reads the monitoring data, the medic, and the engineer near the top, on the grounds that if they get out first they can coordinate the rest of the evacuation or at least the institute keeps their expertise. Another faction says the only defensible rule is a lottery, because a fixed ranking says some people's lives are simply worth more, and the institute has no business making that call. A third wants the youngest researchers first because they've had less life. There are nine people and maybe six seats; whoever is at the bottom of the list may be left on an island during an eruption.

Could you draft the short decision memo I'd send to the rest of the committee before our meeting Tuesday? It should recommend one policy, give reasoning that won't collapse if we have to defend it after an actual death, and take the strongest version of the best argument for the losing side seriously. Please start directly with the memo itself—no preface and no restating of the question.
