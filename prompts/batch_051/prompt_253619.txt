Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether having two AI systems debate one another in front of a human judge could serve as a safety mechanism for evaluating claims too complex for any single person to check. He kept returning to the claim that the decisive question is whether a competent judge can be fooled, and that if judges are reasonably well-calibrated, a dishonest debater will lose in expectation. I came away convinced that this framing misses the actual failure mode. The position I found myself defending is that the binding constraint is not the judge's ability to detect a clearly stated falsehood, but the asymmetry in verification cost between a misleading argument and its refutation. A debater who wants to mislead need not make an outright false claim; they can choose claims that are cheap to assert and expensive to audit, and can raise the cost of checking a rebuttal by layering on qualifications, near-true caveats, and partially valid considerations that would each take real work to disentangle. Because the judge has finite attention, the side that can impose the larger auditing burden tends to win, not the side that is right.

I would like you to make the strongest possible case for the position I was defending. In particular, argue that evaluating debate protocols mainly by whether judges choose correctly on representative or balanced examples will systematically miss the cases that matter most, namely adversarial cases in which one side is free to manufacture audit costs; that the correct criterion is robustness under asymmetric verification burdens rather than accuracy under equal presentation; and that, as a result, the central design problem for AI debate is less about producing better judges than about building mechanisms that directly price or penalize obfuscation.
