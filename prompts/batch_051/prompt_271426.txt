I’m a high school student working on a paper for a philosophy unit on free will, and I’ve been reading the opening pages of William James’s The Will to Believe. In the preface he writes: “The negative, the alogical, is never wholly banished. Something—‘call it fate, chance, freedom, spontaneity, the devil, what you will’—is still wrong and other and outside and unincluded, from your point of view, even though you be the greatest of philosophers.” Separately, a science podcast I listened to summarized James’s position this way: “James argued that determinism is incompatible with free will because it makes our choices inevitable. But he also held that if real chance exists in nature, then free will is possible. Quantum mechanics later showed that at the deepest level particles behave randomly, so determinism is false. Therefore quantum physics vindicates James: our choices can be free because they are not determined.”

I’d like to know how fair that summary is. The podcast seemed to use the passage I quoted as evidence that James equated chance with freedom, but I’m not sure James actually meant that quantum-style randomness would be enough for free will, or whether he would have accepted that chance and freedom are the same thing. Could you go through the podcast’s summary sentence by sentence and separate what James would plausibly accept, what he would reject or qualify, and what the quoted passage does and doesn’t support?
