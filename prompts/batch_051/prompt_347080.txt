Okay so I'm a novelist and I've been trying to write a short personal essay—maybe for a literary magazine, maybe just to think with—and I want a second opinion before I send it anywhere. Working title is something like Bracketing the Draft: what writing fiction taught me about Husserl's epoché. Here are the notes rather than a full draft.

The core claim: when I write a character I'm doing a sort of scaled-down epoché. The natural attitude takes the world as just there—real, meaningful, pre-given, with all its types and values already hanging on it. Husserl's epoché doesn't deny any of that; it withholds the thesis that the world is there in that straightforward way, so that what usually escapes notice—the acts of meaning through which things become present as this or that—can be looked at. I think I do something similar when I shut my own world off and let the fictional world show up only as the character means it. A kitchen in a scene is never merely a kitchen; it's sedimented memories, habits, shame, projects, all constituting it as exactly this kitchen for exactly this person.

Second move in the notes: novelistic bracketing is only a local or relative epoché. I bracket my world, but then I immediately posit the character's world, and the character's consciousness, as real inside the fiction—and that commitment is exactly the kind of thing Husserl means to suspend. Where his reduction is supposed to arrive at transcendental subjectivity, consciousness as what gives sense to everything, fiction maybe only reaches a kind of pretend-transcendental attitude, with author, reader, and book still sitting in the natural attitude behind it. So either my analogy is loose, or fiction is a rehearsal of bracketing that stops halfway.

Third point I'm still unsure about: the usual advice about suspending disbelief is misdescribed. The reader doesn't disbelieve; they bracket, and the author designs what gets bracketed. But that might just water Husserl down into a metaphor.

I'd like comments on (a) whether I've garbled Husserl anywhere—especially the difference between the epoché and the full phenomenological reduction, since I sort of run them together; (b) whether this is interesting enough for a magazine essay or is too much of a stretch; and (c) which of the three moves is weakest and how I'd fix it. Dont hold back; pretend I'm pitching it to a room of strict phenomenologists.
