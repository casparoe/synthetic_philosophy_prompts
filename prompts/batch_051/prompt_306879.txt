Not long ago I read Simone Weil's essay arguing that political parties as such ought to be abolished. Her line, if I've got it right: a party is a device for turning its members into one collective passion; once you join, your judgment on the party's positions is constantly squeezed by loyalty and group pressure; and whatever ideals a party starts from, keeping the party alive and growing becomes the real goal. So her claim is that parties, as an organizational form, are incompatible with members actually thinking for themselves, that reform can't fix this, and that the fix is to replace parties with looser, short-lived associations formed around a specific issue that dissolve once the issue is settled.

I've gone back and forth on it. I'm at maybe 0.55 that she's substantively right, and a friend who volunteers for a party is at about 0.1, though they might just be defensive. I don't want a bare number. Give me your actual credence that Weil is substantively right, decomposed into the sub-questions it actually turns on: how much of the claim is conceptual (what counts as a party) versus an empirical claim about how party incentives actually work; the probability that the psychological mechanism — membership crowding out independent judgment — is real and strong enough to matter; the probability that some stable institutional arrangement (weak party discipline, open primaries, proportional representation, internal secret ballots, whetever) would break the mechanism she's pointing at; and the probability that the looser associations she wants instead would just recreate the same pathology, making the real problem organized collective action generally rather than parties in particular. Give a number and a line of justification for each term, show how they combine, then tell me which term most plausibly separates someone at 0.55 from someone at 0.1.
