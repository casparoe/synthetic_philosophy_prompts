I've been trying to pin down which animals I actually take to be conscious, working from gut verdicts on cases rather than from any theory. For what it's worth, my judgments go like this. Dog: obviously yes. Crow: yes, no hesitation. Octopus: yes, even if the architecture is alien—there's somebody home. Honeybee: a reluctant yes. Jumping spider, even one that seems to plan a route around an obstacle: no, I can't get past the feeling that it's elegant circuitry. Sea slug that shows learning: no, it reads to me as a reflex with extra steps. Five-day-old zebrafish larva: no, with a twinge of guilt. Jellyfish: no. C. elegans: no—a beautiful little machine.

The pair that nags at me is the honeybee and the jumping spider. By nearly any behavioral measure the spider looks more sophisticated, yet I say yes to the bee and no to the spider. And I'm clearly not tracking nervous-system similiarity, since I say yes to the octopus and no to a vertebrate larva. Is there a principled account of the distribution of consciousness that captures these verdicts, or nearly all of them? What would it say about the cases I keep wavering on—a rat, a newborn human, a bivalve, a wasp—and which of my verdicts would a clean principle force me to drop?
