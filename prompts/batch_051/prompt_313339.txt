Okay, so I've noticed an actual policy I've slid into over the last year, and I want it audited by someone who won't just tell me it's fine. I keep a running predictions spreadsheet — markets, some sports, the occasional political call — and I deliberately never log my reasoning, only the call and what ended up happening. The hit rate is decent and it's paid for itself. The policy I caught myself following is this: when a call turns out right, I don't try to work out *why* it was right, and I don't try to turn it into anything I could defend. If it printed, it printed; if I was wrong, I shrug and adjust a little. My friend doing a philosophy PhD keeps insisting I'm basically living inside the Meno problem. He reminds me that even in Plato, the guy with mere true belief about the road to Larissa gets you there exactly as well as the one who knows it, so the question is why anyone should want knowledge over true belief. I tell him my spreadsheet is my Larissa and I'm already there most of the time. But he also brings up a swamping-style worry: if a true belief already does all the practical work, whatever justification adds can look like it adds nothing — which would make my stance exactly right, not lazy. The thing is, I can't tell whether I actually believe that or whether I've built a nice warm home for a lucky streak. If the only thing I value is landing on the truth, then I shouldn't care whether I got there by judgment or by a vibe, and my confidence should be flat across defended and undefended wins. It isn't, and that bugs me. So: is my policy a defensible take on why knowledge beats true belief, or is it a bias dressed up as a principle? How would I tell the difference from the inside?
