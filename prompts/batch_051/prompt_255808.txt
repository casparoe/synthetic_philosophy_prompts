I am an undergraduate in a philosophy of science course, and we have been discussing simultaneous or near-simultaneous discoveries, with the usual examples being the independent development of the calculus by Newton and Leibniz and the near-simultaneous arrival at natural selection by Darwin and Wallace. In the course these cases get treated as evidence for a broad inevitability thesis: in a sufficiently developed field, once the relevant concepts, instruments, and observations are in circulation, the next major result will be reached by someone within a fairly narrow window of time, and individual brilliance determines mostly the name attached to the discovery rather than whether or when it occurs. I want to know whether this thesis is a testable claim about how science actually works or a retrospective narrative that cannot fail.

My main worry is a selection effect. We cite cases in which a second or near-second discoverer existed, but we never observe the counterfactual field: the one that looked ripe and yet produced nothing for decades. So I would like you to treat the inevitability thesis as a hypothesis and design a test that could distinguish it from the rival claim that discoveries are substantially dependent on particular individuals and their unpredictable paths.

Please be concrete. Propose at least one study (experimental, quasi-experimental, or archival) that could produce evidence one way or the other. Describe the units being observed, the measurements you would take, and the pattern of results expected under the inevitability thesis and under the individual-contingency alternative. Then say what a positive result would and would not settle. In particular, address whether a positive result would license the word inevitable, or whether we should retreat to something weaker such as overdetermined under some conditions or more likely than it looks. Finally, add a sentence or two on what the strongest test would imply for the practice of crediting individual discoverers, if the discovery was coming anyway.

Please organize the response into these five sections, in this order, with the approximate lengths indicated: (1) The thesis restated as a testable hypothesis (150 words); (2) A concrete test, with measurements and expected outcomes (300 words); (3) What a positive result would and would not show (250 words); (4) Whether the word inevitable survives, and what remains of scientific credit (150 words); (5) Confidence audit: how confident you are in the main claims, and the point you are least sure of (100 words).
