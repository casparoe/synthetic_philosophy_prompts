I'm a translator, and I've been going back and forth with a collegue about a job I just turned in. My association's code of ethics has a clause I've read a hundred times: "The translator shall render the meaning of the source text faithfully and accurately, and shall not deliberately omit from, add to, or otherwise alter the source text, except as required by the ordinary conventions of the target language." The clause is old, written when the main worry was translators quietly cutting difficult passages or adding their own commentary.

The job is a short story in Spanish whose last line turns on the verb esperar, which covers waiting, hoping, and expecting without making you choose. The narrator has spent the whole story telling herself she is just waiting for a man who disappeared, not hoping, not expecting — and the final verb lets the reader see she has been doing all three all along. Straight into English you have to pick one, and whichever single verb you pick drops two-thirds of the point. I ended up rendering the last line as "All my life I waited for him — hoped, expected." A collegue of mine says I've breached the clause by adding words the author didn't write. I say the real breach would have been picking one verb and silently deleting the other two, because the meaning of the line is the layering, not any one of the senses on its own.

So, does my rendering violate the clause? I'd like it argued from the plain words, from the purpose of the clause, and from what the people who drafted it would probably have said if someone had shown them this case, and then a view on which of those approaches should decide it. If the clause does reach me, what does that mean for translating anything built around a pun or a word that won't come apart? And if it doesn't, I want to know what its drafters thought the clause was ever protecting.
