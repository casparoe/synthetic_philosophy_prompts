I was debugging a query at work the other day and got stuck on something weirdly philosophical. In SQL, if you compare a NULL to a value, like `WHERE middle_name = 'Ann'` when middle_name is NULL, the condition doesn't come out FALSE, it comes out UNKNOWN. And UNKNOWN combines with AND and OR in a very specific way: `(middle_name = 'Ann') OR (salary > 100000)` is TRUE when the salary side is TRUE, but `(middle_name = 'Ann') AND (salary > 100000)` is FALSE when the salary side is FALSE. So the database isn't just throwing up its hands; it's running a three-valued logic. That looks almost exactly like the truth-value gaps philosophers talk about: a sentence like the present king of France is bald (when France is a republic) or a borderline case of a vague predicate (that patch is red) is often said to be neither true nor false, and then you have to decide what NOT, AND, and OR do with the gap. Some of the standard three-valued tables philosophers discuss make the same choices as my query.

But I can't tell whether this is a deep structural parrallel or just a surface resemblance around the word unknown. In the database case the third value means missing information: the person probably has some determinate middle name, it just wasn't entered, and a more complete table would make the condition plain true or false. In the philosophy case the gap is supposed to be in the sentence or the world itself — there is nothing to be ignorant of, not just an unrecorded fact. (Russellians go further and say presupposition-failure sentences aren't gappy at all, just false.) So: if I treated a database with nullable fields as a formal model of a non-bivalent semantics, which position on truth-value gaps does SQL actually correspond to — a gap, a genuine third truth value, supervaluationism, something else — and where does the analogy break?

One concrete test I'd like you to run. If a condition C evaluates to UNKNOWN, the database rules make `C OR NOT C` come out UNKNOWN too (since UNKNOWN OR UNKNOWN is UNKNOWN). But I half-remember that some philosophical treatments of vagueness — supervaluationism in particular — still make the sentence that patch is red or that patch is not red come out true, because every admissible sharpening makes it true. If that's right, the parallel predicts the wrong thing there. I'd like to know whether that specific divergence is exactly the difference between missing information and no fact of the matter, or whether it reveals something else.
