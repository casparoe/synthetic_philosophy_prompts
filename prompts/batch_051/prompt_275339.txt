I've been turning over an old argument in the units of selection debate, and I'd like you to actually go somewhere with it rather than summarise the textbook version. The gene's-eye view, associated with Williams and Dawkins, says roughly this: selection needs heritable variation, but organisms are temporary and get scrambled by sex each generation, so the persisting unit that selection really tracks is the gene—a discrete, high-fidelity replicator. Organisms are vehicles; apparent group or individual selection is ultimately about changes in gene frequencies.

Now suppose a new line of work convinces you that in some lineages a decent chunk of adaptive heritable variation is carried by something like DNA methylation marks or small RNAs passed through the germline. These aren't discrete alleles and they don't have the copying fidelity Dawkins was trading on: the offspring inherits a continuous amount of the mark, the amount is noisy, and it can be erased or re-induced by the environment within a few generations. It can still be adaptive and inherited long enough for selection to act on it, but there is no countable unit being copied.

Does the gene's-eye argument survive this development, or does it quietly depend on particulate Mendelian inheritance in a way this breaks? Say which premise is under pressure, and whether the argument can be repaired into something weaker that still does the work people wanted it to do. If you think it survives more or less in tact, show exactly why the new channel doesn't matter; if you think it dies, say what should replace it. Could you reply in British English, please.
