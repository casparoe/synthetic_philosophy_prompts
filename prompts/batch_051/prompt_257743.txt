I'm a science journalist and I've been reading Milton Friedman's essay "The Methodology of Positive Economics," which is basically the ur-text for the idea that you shouldn't judge a model by whether its assumptions are realistic, only by how well it predicts. He has this passage:

"A theory or its 'assumptions' cannot possibly be thoroughly 'realistic' in the immediate descriptive sense so often assigned to this term. ... Any attempt to move very far in achieving this kind of 'realism' is certain to render a theory utterly useless."

I've heard this arguement a hundred times, but I want to stress-test it with a classic game-theory thought experiment that I think pokes a hole in it.

Take the Keynesian beauty contest. A bunch of players each pick a number between 0 and 100, and the winner is the person closest to two-thirds of the average. The standard rational answer is that everyone picks 0, by iterated elimination of dominated strategies. That prediction relies on a very strong assumption: everyone is perfectly rational, knows everyone else is rational, knows everyone knows, and so on.

Now modify just one assumption. Suppose each player has an independent 10% chance of being a "noisy" type who doesn't strategize at all and just picks a number uniformly at random from 0 to 100. The other 90% are the usual perfectly rational maximizers, and everyone knows these facts. What should a rational player choose now? Please work through the symmetric equilibrium calculation. Does the prediction of 0 survive, or does a little bit of noise move the equilibrium away from the rational ideal?

Then the bigger question: is adding this 10% of noisy types the kind of "realism" Friedman says would make a theory useless, like adding the hair color of wheat traders? Or is it a different kind of assumption — one that actually changes the prediction in a way that matters? I'm trying to figure out how to state the difference between assumptions you can safely idealize away and assumptions that, however "unrealistic," you can't. Maybe the test isn't realism vs. prediction at all, but something else.
