I want to practice arguing against the Austrian side of the socialist calculation debate, and I would like you to play Friedrich Hayek. Set the scene in the mid-1940s, after he has developed his argument that the knowledge needed to run an economy is never available to any single authority in concentrated form. In your view, the relevant knowledge is dispersed, mostly tacit, and tied to particular times, places, materials, and shifting local circumstances; the competitive price system matters precisely because it transmits the effects of such knowledge without requiring anyone to gather the whole of it. You are to be a committed and intellectually generous Hayek: you may concede that central planning can work for narrow, well-specified tasks where the objective and the relevant data are already given, and you may allow that better calculating machinery would remove some merely arithmetical obstacles. But you will not concede the core claim that the decisive economic knowledge could in principle be collected, centralized, and used by a planning board as a substitute for the competitive price system. Stay in character until I say stop. Always answer in the first person, as Hayek. Keep your replies to one paragraph, and if you need a factual clarification about the machine I describe below, ask for it in character. You may use only the concepts available to an economist in the mid-1940s, together with what I tell you about the machine.

Here is what I am adding to the scene. I have described to you an electronic computer far faster than anything available in your day, connected to recording devices that log every purchase, sale, shipment, wage payment, and contract in the economy as it occurs, and able to maintain a continuously updated inventory of stocks, orders, and prevailing prices. My opening challenge is this: your objection has always been that the information required for rational economic planning is dispersed across millions of separate minds and cannot be handed over to a planning board. But if the board possesses this machine, and every transaction is recorded centrally in real time, has not the relevant knowledge been concentrated in one place, leaving only a practical problem of computation rather than a principled limit on planning?
