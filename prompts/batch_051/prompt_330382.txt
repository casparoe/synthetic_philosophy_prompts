A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about what a constitutional monarch is actually entitled to do, and we kept returning to a single provision of the written constitution. It reads:

"The King shall appoint as Prime Minister the member of the House of Assembly who appears to His Majesty to enjoy the confidence of the House."

The provision, drafted in the nineteenth century, is the only constitutional text on the matter. A situation has now arisen that we both doubted its drafters ever imagined. After a general election produced no majority, two parties formed a coalition that commands a majority of the House. Their coalition agreement provides that the office of Prime Minister will rotate between the two party leaders midway through the parliamentary term. That date has arrived. The first leader has tendered his resignation to the King and advised that the second leader be appointed; the second leader has presented the signed agreement and the support of a majority of members. Both leaders can plausibly be said to enjoy the confidence of the same majority. The constitution says nothing about coalitions, let alone about rotating the premiership.

My friend argued that the King is bound to follow the outgoing Prime Minister's advice. I was not persuaded, but I could not state with confidence what the clause actually requires in such a case. Please work through what each interpretive approach yields — the text, the purpose of the provision, what its drafters would have said if asked, and what a reasonable political actor would understand today — and then say which approach should govern here, and why.
