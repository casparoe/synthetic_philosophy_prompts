I am taking an upper-year philosophy of language course on vagueness, and the seminar has been organized around the contrast between epistemicist and supervaluationist treatments of vague predicates. One informal one-liner has been doing far more argumentative work than it seems able to bear. Whenever a student argues that there is no fact of the matter about a borderline case, our instructor cuts the discussion off by saying, as though no further argument were needed, that vagueness is just ignorance. Last week I objected that if it is borderline whether Pat is bald, then the reason we cannot answer is not that we are insufficiently informed; it is that the facts about Pat’s hair, the meaning of ‘bald’, and the context together fail to decide the question either way. He replied that I was confusing our epistemic situation with the underlying semantics: one of ‘Pat is bald’ and ‘Pat is not bald’ is already true and the other false, but we are simply not in a position to know which. When another student then floated the supervaluationist thought that a borderline sentence is neither true nor false, or true only relative to some permissible sharpenings and false relative to others, he repeated the same one-liner and moved on, as though that view had been ruled out rather than merely restated.

I want to come to office hours with a disambiguation of this one-liner. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims it could be expressing, from the almost trivial to the strongly revisionary. In particular, distinguish readings along these dimensions: (a) whether it commits one to a sharp, perhaps context-invariant boundary for every vague predicate, and whether that boundary exists independently of our classificatory practices; (b) what kind of ignorance is alleged—current lack of information, in-principle unknowability for human knowers, or unknowability for any possible investigator; (c) whose ignorance it is—individual speakers, the whole linguistic community, or some more idealized epistemic subject; and (d) what it entails about truth-value gaps, bivalence, and the coherence of our semantic competence in the face of unknowable boundaries. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or implausible. Then identify which readings would actually support our instructor’s dismissal of the supervaluationist view and which would not, and say whether there is any reading the supervaluationist should grant and which reading the epistemicist actually needs. Please be explicit about how the readings differ in their claims about truth, knowledge, and competence. Throughout, indicate how confident you are in each proposed reading and each verdict, and at the end single out the point in your answer that you are least sure of.
