My four cousins and I jointly inherited our grandfather's parcel at the edge of town, a house and some adjoining lots. A developer has offered us a fixed price for the whole property, and the offer expires next month. If a proposed freight-rail extension is approved, the land could be worth several times the offer; if the river commission instead classifies the low ground as protected floodplain, we may be stuck with land we cannot sell for years. We cannot assign probabilities to either outcome, because both depend on political decisions that have not been made and on people who are not saying what they will do. We have to decide at a family meeting next week. One cousin says we should take the guaranteed money and not gamble with what we have. Another says we would never forgive ourselves if we sold and the rail came through, and that the right choice must be the one we would regret least whichever future arrives. A third says that when you truly do not know the odds, you should treat the possible futures as equally likely, multiply each option by each future, and choose the option with the best average. There is also a separate fight. We intend to split whatever we receive into five equal shares, but one cousin has a severely disabled child and depends on this money, while another is a surgeon who does not need it. I have been arguing that we should choose with an eye to protecting whichever cousin is most exposed, even if that lowers the average for the rest of us; one cousin calls that sentimental and says we should maximize the family's overall position. I never studied any of this, but I keep feeling that these quarrels are about something more than temperament. Are any of these rules actually defensible, and which one should I defend at the meeting? Please answer as continuous prose, like an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
