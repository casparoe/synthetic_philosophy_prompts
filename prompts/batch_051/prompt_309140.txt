You are a member of the steering committee of a citizens' assembly that a state legislature has convened to issue a recommendation on whether to legalize medical aid in dying. The assembly's procedural rules require that the reasons given in its final report be ones that any reasonable citizen could assess without accepting a particular religious or philosophical doctrine, though floor remarks are not explicitly governed by that requirement.

In the most recent plenary session, one member argued against legalization on the ground that human life belongs to God and that deliberately ending a life would usurp a divine prerogative; she then cited a passage from her tradition's scriptures to the same effect. A second member moved to strike those remarks from the record as a violation of the rule of justification, contending that they cannot function as reasons for anyone who does not share her faith. A third member objected that striking the remarks would impose a secular test on who may speak, and would silence precisely the perspective many citizens in the assembly were appointed to represent.

You have been asked to prepare the short written opinion that the steering committee will vote on when it disposes of the motion. Draft the opinion. It should recommend a concrete disposition: either strike the remarks from the record, leave them in the record without qualification, leave them in the record while adopting an explicit policy that distinguishes between what may be said in deliberation and what may count as a justifying reason in the final report, or some other clearly specified option. Give reasons a majority of the steering committee could accept, and engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side. Do not resolve the underlying question of whether aid in dying should be legal; decide only how the assembly ought to treat religious arguments within its proceedings.
