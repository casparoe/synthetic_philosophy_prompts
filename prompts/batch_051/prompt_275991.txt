I'm a law student prepping for a seminar on environmental liability, and I keep going in circles on a clause in the cleanup statute we're reading. Section 7(b) of the state Hazardous Substance Compensation Act says:

"No person shall recover compensation under this Act unless the tribunal is satisfied that the act or omission complained of made that person materially worse off than they would otherwise have been."

The case I've invented to stress-test it: a small town in 2025 has to choose between two ways of managing a dump that will stay toxic for centuries. Option A, containment, costs a lot now and leaves the far-future air clean. Option B, on-site venting, is cheap now, but the models are solid that in roughly 300 years people living downwind will be born with a chronic but endurable respiratory impairment; their lives are still clearly worth living, but they're worse than the lives the A-population would have. The demographers also agree that the choice between A and B will predictably change who exists 300 years from now, because different timing of migration, marriages and conceptions shakes out. So the specific people who get the impairment under B would never have been born at all under A; a completely different population, same size, would live there instead.

So under B, nobody can point to themselves and say the town made them worse off then they would otherwise have been, because in the only alternative the town actually had, those people don't exist. Yet everyone agrees the town picked B because it was cheap and pushed a foreseeable impairment onto whichever people ended up downwind.

My question isn't really about this town, it's about the clause. What does Section 7(b) actually require here: are the B-people "worse off" in the relevant sense, or does the plain wording lock them out? And what do the usual interpretive moves each give on this: the words, the evident purpose of the Act, what the drafters would have said if someone had shown them this case, and what a reasonable reader takes the clause to cover? I'd also like a view on whether any reading reaches the B-people without quietly rewriting "worse off than they would otherwise have been" into "worse off than some other possible person" — and if none does, what that tells us about what the clause is actually able to protect when the act changes who exists.
