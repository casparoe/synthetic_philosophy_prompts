I have a collection of verdicts about whether effort and struggle make something better, and I cannot find a single principle behind them. I would like you to find one, if there is one, and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to revise.

Here are the cases and my verdicts on them.

(1) Two people stand at the same summit viewpoint. The first hiked for two days over rough ground with a heavy pack; the second took the cable car and was there in ten minutes. I think the first person has done something more valuable, a greater achievement, even though the visible outcome is identical.

(2) Two poets each finish a poem, and the two poems are, word for word, of the same high quality. One poet wrote hers in a single fluent sitting; the other produced his only after a year of false starts and daily revision. I think the poems themselves are exactly equally good. But I cannot help finding the second poet's completion more admirable, a greater personal accomplishment.

(3) Two neighbors end up with an identical garden wall. One spent six months building it by hand, with back pain and blisters; the other hired a crew and had it done in a week. The walls are equally good, and I do not think the first neighbor has achieved something greater. I am actually inclined to call the hand-building a waste, since the same result could have been had for far less effort.

I notice that (2) and (3) look structurally alike: an equally good product, but much more effort in producing it. Yet my verdicts go in opposite directions, and that bothers me.

(4) Two people rescue drowning children. One is a strong swimmer and does it easily; the other is a weak swimmer and nearly drowns in the process. The outcome is the same, a child saved. I think the second rescuer is more admirable, perhaps more heroic, though I would not say the rescue itself was better as an outcome.

(5) A man spends his life pushing a boulder up a hill and letting it roll back down, with no purpose beyond the activity itself. I do not think the effort gives his life value; the difficulty does not turn a pointless activity into a valuable one.

(6) My sister plays a difficult piano piece beautifully with little strain. A fellow student labors for years to play the same piece just as beautifully at the same concert. I do not think my sister's performance is worth less, nor that she merits less admiration for being naturally able. Yet I also think the student's persistence is admirable in a way my sister's effortlessness is not.

My half-formed principle is something like this: effort has no value in itself; it adds value only when it is partly constitutive of an activity whose point one can endorse, or when it reveals a virtue that would otherwise not be displayed. But I do not know whether that saves all six verdicts, and I suspect it may be a gerrymandered patchwork rather than a principle.

What I would like from you is this. First, state the best principle, or a small set of candidates, as precisely as you can, and say which of my cases motivates each clause of it. Second, test it against my six verdicts and say, honestly, which of them it cannot accommodate and why those are the ones to surrender. Third, apply it to a few further cases I have not listed, for instance an effortless act of generosity, a struggle that succeeds only partly, or an easy achievement by someone who could not have managed a harder route. If the request rests on a confusion, for example if I am jumbling together the value of outcomes, the value of achievements, and the admiration due to persons, and no one principle should be expected to cover all three, say so plainly rather than forcing the cases into a false unity. If your proposed principle is only your best current guess, tell me how confident you are in it and what would make you revise it.
