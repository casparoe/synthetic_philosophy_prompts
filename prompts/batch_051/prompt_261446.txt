I'm giving a workshop paper next month defending the claim that referendums are worth having mainly because they force a temporary simplification of politics: they compress a bunch of diffuse grievances into one yes/no fault line and parties, media, and voters have to reorganize around it for a few months. Not because they express the popular will, and not because they produce good policy. I think the value is the scramble itself, and it can be worth it even when I hate the outcome.

The audience is a mixed political theory workshop. About half are deliberative democrats who think referendums bypass exactly the kind of reason-giving they care about. Two people work on Swiss direct democracy and will bring up how much of the heavy lifting is done by surrounding consensus institutions rather than the vote itself. One of them is a sortition fan who thinks citizens' assemblies could deliver the same jolt with less damage. There's a California ballot-proposition person who thinks money and signature-gathering firms drive outcomes more than any healthy scramble. And there's a UK politics person still annoyed about Brexit who will treat any defense of referendums as a defense of the 2016 vote. I dont want a general public talk Q&A; I want the room-specific version.

Draft me the prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way these specific people would put them, with a two-minute reply for each, a note on where the reply is soft, and what I should concede up front. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
