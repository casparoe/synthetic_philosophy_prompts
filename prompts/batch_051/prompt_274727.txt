You are a professional decision theorist and moral philosopher who specializes in choice under ignorance. I would like you to think carefully, begin with some explicit brainstorming about what is distinctive in the situation I describe, and only then settle on your recommendations.

The topic has recently become urgently personal for me. Within two months, my partner and I must decide whether I accept a tenure-track position in another state. She has a chronic illness with a highly uncertain trajectory: a few serious downside scenarios are clearly possible, but neither of us can honestly assign probabilities to them, and moving would put us far from her current specialists. The stakes are high, the decision is effectively one-shot, and most of the uncertainty is what decision theory calls "ignorance" rather than "risk." I want to reason through this rather than decide by anxiety or inertia.

Please give me two things.

First, rank the following decision rules from most to least suitable for our choice, and assign each a suitability score out of 10: Wald's maximin rule; Savage's minimax regret rule; the Laplace (principle of indifference) rule, which assigns equal probabilities to the scenarios; the Hurwicz optimism–pessimism criterion; and Harsanyi-style expected-utility reasoning with subjective probabilities. For each rule, add one or two sentences explaining its position: what it protects us from, and what it ignores or distorts in a one-shot, high-stakes choice under deep uncertainty.

Second, discuss how the Rawls–Harsanyi disagreement about choosing behind a veil of ignorance bears on our two-person decision. In particular, say whether the rule you ranked first would remain your recommendation if you modeled our decision as a small society of two choosing principles under a veil that deprives us of knowledge of who will bear which burden, and whether thinking of us as jointly maximizing something would change the ranking.

Write your answer in American English.
