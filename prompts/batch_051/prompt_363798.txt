A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether alignment work should hold systems to worst-case standards or settle for average-case evidence, and the argument ended exactly where it began, even though we agreed on a surprising amount. The dispute keeps reappearing in a cluster of questions: whether passing an evaluation suite should raise one's credence that a system is safe, or should only be treated as falsifying specific failure modes; whether an interpretability result has to survive adversarial red-teaming before it counts as evidence about a model's internals; whether a safety case needs something like a worst-case argument at its center, or whether a careful expected-value analysis over a specified distribution is the best anyone can honestly do. My friend's position, roughly, is that deployment is effectively adversarial: whatever distribution you test on will not be the distribution you meet, so average-case results are systematically misleading and the field should make its threat models explicit and demand guarantees relative to them. My position, roughly, is that worst-case standards are unreachable for learned, high-dimensional systems — and, more sharply, that results called "worst-case" usually rest on assumptions about stationarity, specification, or the boundedness of the adversary that quietly fail in deployment, so they borrow the authority of a proof they haven't earned. To which my friend replied that average-case evaluation is just worst-case analysis with an unstated, narrow adversary, so it is the average-case side that is hiding assumptions, not the worst-case side.

I've begun to suspect the impasse is not about facts, or even about which research methods to fund, but about something upstream: what would have to be true of deployment for each position to be right, what should count as evidence of safety at all, and maybe different attitudes toward ambiguity rather than risk. But I do not trust that hypothesis.

I would like a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than a verdict on it. Is this best understood as a hidden empirical disagreement about how stationary or adversarial deployment will be, a difference about standards of evidence, a difference in risk attitude under ambiguity, or a case in which the two sides mean different things by "guarantee" — one side pointing at a property of a mathematical object and the other at a property of an operational process? Is the persistence of the debate a sign that the question is ill-posed, or just that it is hard? Please engage the two positions concretely, including the charge I called guarantee-laundering and its mirror image. Then say what would count as progress under your diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves the dispute, a formal result, a different reporting norm, a change of question — or an argument that no resolution should be expected and why.

Finally, so that I know how much to lean on your answer, please tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis and single out the point you are least sure of.
