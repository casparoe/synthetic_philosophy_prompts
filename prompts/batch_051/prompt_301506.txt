A colleague and I have been arguing about a neurotechnology company's claim that its non-invasive headset "reads your thoughts." The device is trained on an individual user and then, from neural signals alone, renders text that the user was silently imagining saying — a form of decoded inner speech. My colleague thinks the phrase is at worst a harmless shorthand: if the system reliably reports what the person was thinking, then it read the thought. I think it may be carrying a heavier claim — that the device is in contact with the thought itself, rather than with motor imagery, subvocalization, or other measurable correlates that merely track it. I would like to know whether that distinction is empirically load-bearing or merely verbal.

Design the strongest experimental battery you can for the claim that a brain–computer interface reads a person's thought or deliberate inner speech rather than detecting a behavioral or physiological proxy. Be concrete about the contrasts to run — for example novel sentences versus those used in training, content the user tries to suppress, abstract propositions versus imagined movements, and conditions that isolate subvocal or ocular artifacts — and state what pattern of results would favor each reading. Then say exactly what a decisive result would and would not settle: whether some version of the question survives any outcome, and whether that residue reflects a gap in measurement or a boundary in the notion of "reading a thought" itself. Conclude by saying what your analysis implies for talk of mental privacy and cognitive liberty: if the stronger reading fails, does a system that decodes inner speech still engage privacy in a distinctive way, or is it closer to a very good inference from observable behavior? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
