I sit on the professional-standards committee of our state bar association, and I need a short written opinion to circulate before our Thursday meeting. We must decide a concrete disciplinary case, and I want something the committee could act on directly.

The case is this. A midsized law firm, Kessler & Roe, used an automated document-management system that deleted certain categories of client correspondence after ninety days. While litigation was pending, the system’s retention settings were left at the default despite a litigation hold requiring preservation, and the misconfiguration was flagged once in an internal IT ticket and then closed without action. Around the same period, firm associates followed official matter-efficiency guidance instructing them to construe document requests narrowly and not to volunteer borderline material. No single lawyer saw the document collection as a whole or knew what others had withheld. A court later found serious discovery violations and referred the matter to us. We have already found the underlying misconduct; the only question before us now is whether the firm itself, as distinct from any individual lawyer, should be publicly censured.

Our rules, in summary, permit the committee to censure a firm where its policies, systems, or supervision caused the harm, and they expressly allow a firm to be censured even when no individual lawyer is found culpable. The firm’s counsel argues that a law firm is not a person with a mind; it cannot know, intend, or feel shame, so censuring it cannot be genuine blame. On that view, what we would really be doing is imposing the reputational and financial costs of blame on partners and staff who did nothing wrong. The strongest reply is that this is organizational wrongdoing in its purest form: the harm was produced by the firm’s policies, incentives, and systems, not by any human being’s culpable state of mind. If the entity is not a fit object of blame, then no one is reached, and the misconduct disappears into the gaps between fifty people’s partial views. Underneath the rule is the question the committee keeps circling: whether an organization, as distinct from the people in it, can be the proper object of blame at all.

Please write the opinion so that it makes one definite recommendation — censure, decline to censure, or censure with stated conditions — and defends it with reasons rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to me or the committee. Give an actionable bottom line and a concrete time frame for any conditions you recommend. Whatever position you take, engage honestly with the strongest consideration on the losing side and explain why it does not prevail.
