A startup training a coding model is tired of pure outcome-based RL, where the reward only lands when the final code passes the tests, because credit assignment is so sparse. They propose a hybrid: they'll train a process reward model that scores each step in the model's generated solution, and the plan is to have human raters label stepts as good or bad after they've seen whether the final code passed the test suite. The raters are told to judge whether each step was a reasonable thing to do at that point, but they also get the benefit of knowing how it all ended. The startup argues this gets the best of both worlds: step-level labels, but informed by outcome ground truth. Walk through how this scheme actually behaves over time. What sort of labels do the raters converge on, and what does that do to the process reward model? Where does it break, what second-order effects show up in the coding model's behavior, and which failure modes can actually be fixed—say, by blinding raters to the final outcome, or by splitting the label into a looked-reasonable-at-the-time score and a turned-out-to-matter-to-the-result score—without losing whatever the outcome-informed labeling was supposed to buy in the first place?
