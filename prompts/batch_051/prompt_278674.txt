I'm auditing an upper-level seminar that was supposed to be on concepts and social ontology, but its basically turned into a class on conceptual engineering. My instructor has one move that keeps throwing me. Whenever somebody asks a "what is X, really?" question — last session it was whether 'refugee' genuinely covers people displaced by climate, and before that whether 'woman' gets gender and sex tangled together — she says some version of: the question isnt what the concept means, its what we want it to do. She says it like that obviously settles the semantic part of the argument and lets everyone move on to the political part.

But I can hear at least three different claims packed inside that line, and they don't all do that work. One reading is just the boring point that words are conventional. Another seems to be the much stronger claim that there is no difference between describing a concept and stipulating one. A third looks like it quietly changes the subject from meaning to policy. I want to bring a list to office hours. Take that one-liner and lay out the distinct precise theses it could be asserting, from the trivial to the radical, and for each say whether it's a platitude, a substantive-but-defensible claim, or false. Then tell me which reading or readings would actually license using the line to shut down a "but what does X really mean?" question in a debate about a contested social term, and which readings would just make it rhetoric. No names or citations needed; I just want the readings separated cleanly enough that I can ask her which one she means.
