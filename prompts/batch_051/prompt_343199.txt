I've been trying to work out a position about what it means to say that part of a trained neural network "represents" something, and I would like an honest assessment of the view I have settled on. I call it representational instrumentalism. The thesis is this: for a component of a network — a neuron, an attention head, a direction in activation space — to represent a feature such as "this token is a verb" or "this passage is in quotation marks" just is for that component to stand in the right kind of counterfactual relationship to the network's behavior. Specifically, when one intervenes on the component by turning it up, down, or off, the model's behavior should change in ways that track the changes one would expect if the model were genuinely using that feature; and there is no further fact of the matter about whether the representation is "real." A representation, on my view, is nothing more than a stable, well-tested handle for intervening on a model and anticipating what it will do.

The view appeals to me for two reasons. First, it makes interpretability an engineering problem rather than a metaphysical one: a representation claim is vindicated by successful intervention and prediction, not by correspondence to some internal code. Second, it explains why two different research teams can give incompatible but equally useful descriptions of the same network — they have found different intervention-stable patterns, and my view says there is no question of which description is the true one. But the view also makes me uneasy, and I want to know exactly how serious the problems are.

Three cases bother me in particular. (1) A thermostat stands in essentially this kind of counterfactual relationship to a room's temperature, and I am not sure I want to say the thermostat's bimetallic strip represents "the room is too cold" in the way a network component represents a grammatical category; so my criterion may over-generate. (2) In the networks I actually study, a single component often appears to carry several unrelated features at once, so an intervention that probes one feature drags the others along; my account seems unable to say which of the bundled features, if any, is "the" content of the component. (3) There are also networks in which no individual component is necessary for a capacity — remove any one and behavior barely changes — yet the capacity is plainly carried by the network as a whole; my component-level criterion seems to miss these distributed representations. My questions are: is something like representational instrumentalism already the standard view in the interpretability literature, or a known minority position? Does it survive cases like the three above, and if it must be weakened or supplemented, what is the least revision that saves it? And finally, is "representation" even the right word for what survives, or would some thinner notion — information, sensitivity, predictive usefulness — be more honest about what interpretability actually gives us?
