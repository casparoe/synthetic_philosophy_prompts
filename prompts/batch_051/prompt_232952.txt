I am a PhD student working on the epistemology of recorded evidence, and I am stuck between a view I find theoretically compelling and an intuition I cannot shed.

The view is as follows. For most of their history, film and video played the role of an epistemic backstop: reports could be fabricated, memories were fragile, and documents could be forged, but producing convincing moving images with sound was sufficiently costly and detectable that a recording was rightly treated as near-decisive evidence of what it depicted. Increasingly photorealistic synthetic media are eroding that asymmetry. On a broadly evidentialist picture, the rational response is to lower confidence in unauthenticated recordings across the board: absent an independent provenance chain, such as cryptographic signing, continuity of custody, or corroboration from trusted institutions, a video should now count as only weak evidence that the depicted event occurred. The honest consequence is a generalized deflation of video testimony. The view's systematic virtue is that it rests on a single, uniform base-rate consideration and does not gerrymander by topic, speaker, or stakes.

The intuition is as follows. I am shown a clear, recent phone video of a close friend making a cruel remark about me, or a neighbor's security-camera recording of my car being hit. In these ordinary contexts I treat the recording as overwhelming evidence, and I think I would be unreasonable, even paranoid, not to. If I actually assigned unauthenticated video the low weight the view demands, I would be epistemically disabled in everyday life, hesitating over recordings in situations where almost no one has any motive or means to forge one targeting me. The intuition says default trust in recordings remains rational absent a specific reason for suspicion, because the local probability that this particular recording was synthetically manufactured is tiny even if the general base rate has shifted.

I want a negotiated verdict rather than a side. Give the strongest formulation of each position, including the strongest debunking reply to the intuition, such as that the intuition is adaptive lag, a scope error, or a confusion of local and global base rates, and the strongest amendment reply to the theory, such as that it must distinguish adversarial or public epistemics from ordinary private epistemics. Then say which should yield, under what conditions, and whether there is a reading of the words evidence and trust on which both positions can be true. What would genuinely justify treating the intuition as the thing that has to go, and what would genuinely justify restricting the theory to adversarial or public contexts?
