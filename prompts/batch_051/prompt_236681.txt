For several years I have taught a first-year course on the epistemology of online life, and there is one distinction I have never managed to teach as cleanly as the idea deserves. The distinction is between an epistemic bubble and an echo chamber. In my own formulation, an epistemic bubble is a structural matter of missing exposure: a person's information environment simply does not contain the relevant contrary evidence or sources, so the natural remedy is to widen exposure. An echo chamber is different in kind: it is a structure of cultivated distrust, in which members have been taught to regard everyone outside the chamber as epistemically compromised. As a result, the standard remedy for a bubble fails badly in the case of an echo chamber, because the very voices one introduces are disqualified in advance; under the wrong conditions, more exposure can even reinforce the chamber rather than weaken it.

I would like you to design how I should teach this distinction in a single ninety-minute session to students whose only background is an introductory critical thinking course and who tend to assume that bad belief online is simply a problem of insufficient exposure to good information. Please give me the plan rather than the explanation itself: the order in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what; the one example or case that should carry the distinction, and what that example must contain and must avoid; the misconceptions students will arrive with or form along the way, in the order they will appear, and the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off; a check at the end that would show whether they genuinely possess the distinction rather than merely the vocabulary for it; and what I should deliberately leave out at this level and say so. My own suspicion is that the deepest misconception to anticipate is the assumption that both structures are, at bottom, the same kind of failing in the individual — a bias or a laziness — and I would want the session's arc to be built around showing why that is mistaken, though I am open to being corrected about which misconception should structure the plan.
