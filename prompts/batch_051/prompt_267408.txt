I've been reading the Zhuangzi for a class on ancient skepticism and I keep getting tripped up by what looks like a contradiction inside the second chapter. At the end of that chapter there's the butterfly passage: Zhuang Zhou dreams he's a butterfly and is completely delighted, then wakes up, and he can't tell whether he was Zhuang Zhou who had dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he is Zhuang Zhou; the passage closes by saying that between the two of them there must be some distinction, and that this is what the transformation of things means. Fine — that sounds like the familiar skeptical worry. The dream is so convincing that it leaves the question of who you really are genuinely undecided, and it even makes the boundary between dreaming and waking start to look shifty.

But earlier in the same chapter he says that while a person is dreaming they don't know it's a dream, and that only after a Great Awakening will we know that this all has been a great dream — and he adds that fools think they are already awake, as if they knew what was going on. That second passage seems to rely on exactly the kind of priviledged standpoint the butterfly passage seems to deny: some eventual waking from which the dream-status of ordinary life becomes obvious, and from which you could presumably settle the butterfly question too. So which is it? Is the dream/waking distinction something we're stuck inside and can't adjudicate, or is there an awakening from which it becomes clear? Walk me through the candidate ways of reconciling these two passages, and then tell me which reading is the most honest and which ones are special pleading. I want you to take both passages seriously, including the possibility that the Great Awakening isn't an epistemic state at all or that the second passage is aimed at the fools rather than offering a real criterion.
