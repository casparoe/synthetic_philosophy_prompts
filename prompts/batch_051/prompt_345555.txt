After a career in physics, I have been using retirement to work through the philosophy of language, and I find myself approaching semantic theories the way I used to approach physical models: I want to know, for each one, where it holds and where it breaks. The area I keep coming back to is the geometry of meaning — the representation of words as vectors in a high-dimensional space, with similarity among those vectors taken to encode semantic structure. That family of approaches descends from the distributional hypothesis, the idea that words are similar in meaning to the extent that they occur in similar linguistic environments. A rival strand of semantics treats the meaning of a predicate as at least partly a matter of its truth conditions: what it takes for the predicate to be true of a thing. I would like a thought experiment that forces these two pictures apart in the direction that is rarely constructed.

Construct a miniature artificial corpus — you may specify its syntactic frames and the distribution of lexical environments rather than listing thousands of sentences — and choose two ordinary English predicates such that (i) a standard static embedding built from co-occurrence counts, with a standard weighting and cosine similarity, places the two predicates far apart, well outside each other's nearest neighbours, while (ii) in the stipulated world described by the corpus they are truth-conditionally equivalent: whatever one is true of, the other is true of, and sentences remain true when one predicate is substituted for the other. Then, for contrast, specify a second pair of predicates in the same corpus that are truth-conditionally disjoint — they cannot both be true of the same object at the same time in the same respect — yet are near neighbours in the embedding space. The words must be ordinary English, and the verdicts must fall out of the corpus structure and a stated semantics; do not build the case on homonymy, non-English items, nonsense positions, or by simply stipulating the outcomes.

After the scenario, show the reasoning. Walk through the co-occurrence evidence or counts for each pair, state precisely what the distributional model and the truth-conditional account each predict, and identify the structural feature of the corpus that does the work in each divergence. Finish by saying what happens if that feature is removed, and by giving the most honest reply available to a defender of the distributional hypothesis. In particular, does the case show that embedding geometry does not encode meaning after all, or only that it encodes a different relation — and if the latter, what becomes of the claim that semantic structure can be recovered from distribution alone?
