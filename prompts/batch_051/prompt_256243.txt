Constitutional government is often described as an exercise in collective self-binding. Consider two specimens of that exercise. In the first, a majority ratifies a charter that requires a two-thirds vote before certain individual liberties or the rules of free elections may be changed. Most people call this legitimate constitutionalism: a democracy entrenching the framework that makes its later decisions democratic in the first place. In the second, the same majority writes into the charter that a specific tax rate, tariff, or land-use rule may be changed only by a two-thirds vote. Many of the same people now speak of a dead hand, of one generation hijacking future policy, or of abdicating self-rule. Yet from the perspective of a later majority the two arrangements function identically: the present has been bound by the past, and all that differs is what has been entrenched. Is there a difference in kind between entrenching a right or a framework and entrenching a policy, or is the asymmetry just a bias toward commitments that flatter our own convictions? Articulate the strongest defensible version of the distinction, test it against provisions that blur the line—entrenched property regimes, official-language clauses, established-religion clauses, balanced-budget rules—and then say whether the asymmetry should be preserved, narrowed, or abandoned.
