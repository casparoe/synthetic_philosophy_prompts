I sit on the digitisation committee of a university library. We have been offered a bequest with two parts. The first is a set of unpublished manuscripts, letters and photographs; for more than half of them the creators cannot be identified, or can be identified but appear to have died without traceable heirs, and our rights officer categorises them as orphan works in the ordinary sense. The second is a box of floppy disks and two hard drives containing software written in the 1980s and 1990s. Some of the programs were written by a sole developer who died in 2003 without a will and whose estate appears to have no surviving relatives; some were produced by a company dissolved in 1996, with no identifiable successor firm and no assignee of its intellectual property; and one database was maintained collaboratively by people known only by pseudonyms, none of whom has answered our attempts at contact.

Our committee must decide whether to digitise the collection for researchers and, in the case of the software, whether to permit non-commercial copying and emulation. Existing policy allows digitisation of orphan works only after a diligent, good-faith search for the rights holder that ends in failure. It is silent on the software, where the difficulty is not that the owner cannot be found but that the owner seems to be gone in a stronger sense: dead without heirs, or dissolved without a successor.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our meeting. It should recommend a concrete policy for both categories, with a timetable for implementation; it should state what 'abandoned' or 'ownerless' ought to mean in this context, and whether the manuscripts and the software differ in any morally relevant way; and it should engage seriously with the strongest argument for treating the software more permissively than the manuscripts. Conclude with a set of criteria the committee can apply to future items. We are not after a summary of copyright law; we want a defensible institutional position, and we are prepared to be challenged on the distinction we draw.
