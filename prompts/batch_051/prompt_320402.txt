I'm trying to turn my notes into a short essay with the working title "Karma Is Not a Currency," and before I flesh it out I want a gut-check from someone who actually knows this stuff.

Can you act as a philosopher who works on social epistemology, trust, reputation, and the economics of attention? I'd like comments on the notes below — not line edits, but substantive comments. My notes so far:

- Core claim: people say "karma economy," but karma isn't really an economy in any technical sense. A currency needs scarcity and exchange; karma has neither (there's no budget constraint, no double-spend problem). What karma actually is, I think, is a routing system for attention: votes are how a community decides whose posts/what claims get seen. So "karma economy" is sort of a metaphor stretched too far.

- But then there IS something economic going on, because attention is scarce and karma is one of the main levers for allocating it. So maybe the real scarce good being allocated is attention, and karma is just the ledger. Not sure this distinction matters or is just word games.

- Second claim: karma systems try to make one number do two jobs at once: (a) is this contribution good (true, useful, interesting), and (b) is this user a trustworthy/upstanding community member. Those are different judgments. A hilarious shitposter in a meme community is a "good contributor" in the quality sense, but that tells you nothing about whether you should trust their comment in a technical community. One scalar can't carry both signals, and the more it gets used for (b), the more it corrupts (a) and vice versa.

- Third: inflation/debasement. Karma from different communities isn't comparable, because upvotes are "cheaper" in big default communities (low-effort memes) than in small expert ones. But the product shows a single global number. This feels like a Gresham's-law situation: cheap karma drives out the informational value of expensive karma, because the two are indistinguishable in the UI. Maybe "drives out" isn't the right word, more like dilutes.

- Fourth: the metric-becomes-a-target problem. Once people optimize for karma, it stops measuring quality and starts measuring karma-farming skill (Goodhart's law, maybe; I might be using that wrong). I think this is a seperate issue from the inflation problem but I'm not sure.

- Fifth/possible fixes, all of which feel bad: make downvotes cost something to the downvoter (entrenches incumbents and chills dissent?); make karma decay over time (unfair to someone who wrote one great thing years ago that's still read); show karma per-community instead of globally (breaks the global leaderboard/gamification loop, but maybe that loop is the problem). I keep going in circles.

3. Which of the three fixes is least bad, and is there a standard philosophical distinction that would help me see why they all feel off?
4. What am I missing that a philosopher would see immediately — e.g., something about testimony, trust, how norms get enforced, or whether "reputation" is even a thing an anonymous system can have?
