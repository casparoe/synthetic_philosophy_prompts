In my ethics class we have been discussing compromises between people whose value systems differ sharply, and I have been trying to sort out my own intuitive verdicts. The general idea I keep returning to is a kind of moral gains from trade: two parties each give up something they care about less in order to gain something they care about more, and both end up preferring the agreement to no agreement at all. I have clear feelings about particular cases, but I cannot find the rule behind them.

Here are my verdicts. A vegetarian and a non-vegetarian friend who eat together regularly decide to alternate restaurant choices, so the vegetarian sometimes sits through a meal of side dishes at a steakhouse and the other sometimes eats vegetable dishes at a vegetarian place: fine, maybe even admirable. Two parties form a governing coalition in which an environmentalist party gets a carbon tax and a business-oriented party gets lower corporate taxes, with each openly voting for a package containing a plank it dislikes: fine, and a normal part of democratic government. A religious charity accepts public money to run a homeless shelter on the condition that it not require clients to participate in religious activities: fine, though it leaves me slightly uneasy. An activist group that has campaigned against a polluting factory settles with the company, which cuts emissions by thirty percent and accepts independent audits in exchange for the group issuing a statement calling the factory a responsible producer: not fine. That last case feels like a kind of moral corruption even though the agreement is voluntary and both sides prefer it to no deal.

The principle I keep reaching for is that a compromise is morally acceptable when it is voluntary, when each side by its own lights gains more than it gives up, and when outsiders are not made significantly worse off. But that principle would bless the activist endorsement case, which I reject. I want a principle that captures my verdicts, or an honest account of which verdicts I should abandon for the sake of a cleaner principle. I also have two further cases I cannot settle. A committed pacifist votes for a candidate who would launch a military intervention but would also fund healthcare and raise the minimum wage, reasoning that no viable candidate shares her pacifism. And a country lowers tariffs on goods produced under labor standards it considers inadequate, in exchange for its trading partner agreeing to independent inspections and a rising minimum wage. Is this second case fine because it moves things in the right direction, or compromised in the same way as the activist statement? What does your principle say about these two cases?
