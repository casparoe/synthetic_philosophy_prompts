I follow a specific policy in my reasoning about anthropic questions, and I want to know whether it is a defensible epistemic habit or a bias dressed up as a rule. The policy is this: I always reason as though I am randomly selected from the class of all actually existing observers relevantly similar to me — what is usually called the self-sampling assumption — and I refuse any inference that requires the self-indication assumption, the rule that my own existence is evidence, so that among hypotheses consistent with my observations, those that predict more observers become more likely simply because I exist.

The situation that makes this policy bite is the presumptuous philosopher thought experiment. Suppose two physical theories are empirically indistinguishable so far: H1 predicts that the universe will contain a trillion observers, and H2 predicts a billion. No ordinary evidence favors either theory. The self-indication assumption would have me move from even odds to near-certainty in H1, and I regard that as a reductio of the principle rather than a conclusion I should adopt: a vast claim about the universe drawn only from the fact that I showed up.

I apply the policy broadly, not just to carefully stated thought experiments. Last week a friend argued that I should sharply raise my credence that the universe contains far more observers than our current evidence suggests, on the grounds that such a universe would contain more people like me and so my existence is more likely in it. I rejected the move out of hand. I am willing to condition on an observer count once a theory supplies one, but I refuse to let my own existence count as evidence that many-observer hypotheses are true.

I know the self-indication assumption has serious defenders, and my question is not whether it is true. It is whether my standing policy — treat any anthropic principle whose characteristic conclusion looks presumptuous as refuted by that conclusion, and use self-sampling as the fallback — is defensible as a way of managing these questions. When is a blanket refusal to update on one's own existence rational, and when is it protecting a prior from being revised? Could the policy be restated so that it keeps the sanity check against presumptuous updates without the costs of a one-sided rule? Or am I, from the inside, mistaking a sound argument for a reductio because I do not want to accept where it leads?

Please write the answer in American English.
