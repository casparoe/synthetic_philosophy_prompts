You are a political philosopher with a specialty in the moral psychology of the sentiments, preparing an advanced undergraduate seminar on whether envy and resentment are vices or signals of injustice. Your task is to write a which-position-do-you-actually-hold sorting quiz of exactly eight questions, with a scoring key, that classifies respondents by their judgments about concrete cases rather than by label self-identification; no question should simply ask which philosophical view they accept. Before writing the questions, lay out the logical space as a set of diagnostic axes, and use those axes to derive the leaves. The axes must cover at least these four splits: (a) whether envy can ever be a reliable signal of injustice or is always a destructive vice; (b) whether resentment properly requires a wrong under a shared principle, or may also fittingly track harms that are not moral wrongs; (c) whether the appropriate response to excusable envy is political (altering distributions) or personal (altering one's desires); and (d) whether fitting resentment is admirable, merely permissible, or vicious. The leaves must include a Rawlsian position treating resentment as a moral sentiment while envy is a non-moral sentiment that can be excusable yet never a legitimate ground of a claim of justice; at least one egalitarian position treating much ordinary envy as misdescribed indignation at injustice; a virtue-ethical position treating both envy and resentment as passions to be disciplined rather than trusted; and at least one further position of your choice, named if it has a defender and unnamed otherwise. Include at least one case question that forces a choice between the Rawlsian rule that excusable envy should not be used as a reason for institutional choice and the egalitarian rule that widespread envy under durable inequality is defeasible evidence of injustice. Flag any pairs of leaves the instrument cannot cleanly separate, and identify at least one logically available answer combination that no plausible position occupies, with a one-sentence diagnosis of why it is empty. Begin by brainstorming axes and candidate cases, then present the final quiz and scoring key.
