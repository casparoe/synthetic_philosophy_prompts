Take the ventilator triage decisions hospitals in Lombardy had to make in the first weeks of March 2020 — sudden scarcity, more COVID patients than machines, doctors forced to decide who gets one. I want you to analyze that episode through Taurek's argument that the numbers shouldn't count when your choosing whom to save. Not the obvious version, though. The obvious version goes: a utilitarian triage rule maximizes lives saved or expected life-years; Taurek says that if you can save either one stranger or five strangers, and all six have an equal claim on you, the fact that five is more than one isn't by itself a reason to save the five, so equal concern pushes you toward a lottery that gives each an equal chance. What I actually want you to do is push on a place where triage doesn't fit his setup. In triage the candidates aren't equal: they have different survival odds, ages, comorbidities. So (1) work out exactly what Taurek's view would say about these unequal-prognosis cases. Does the lottery argument still have anything to say when the patients differ in their expected benefit, or does his position just go silent the moment the clean one-vs-five tie disappears? (3) Now flip the lens: what does Lombardy as a stress test show about Taurek's position?
