I'm a software engineer and my team is stuck in an argument about our legacy codebase. One group treats everything old as basically sacred—they don't want to touch the original comments even when they describe logic that no longer exists, and they get upset if someone replaces a module that "we've always had." Another group wants to delete anything that doesn't justify itself right now, including old design docs, because they think it's just noise. A third group keeps holding up the original architect's early code as the gold standard for what good engineering looks like, and they use that to block changes that don't look like what the founder wrote twenty years ago.

I'm not a philosophy person, but I picked up Nietzsche's essay On the Use and Abuse of History for Life. He claims we need history for the sake of life and action, not as an escape from life, but he also says that too much history turns the past into a weight that stops you from acting at all. He describes different ways of relating to the past—I don't remember the exact labels—but one is revering great moments, another is preserving things simply because they're old, and a third is dragging the past to court and condemning it. The mapping to my three groups at work is almost too neat.

So here's my actual question: is there a principled way to figure out when a dev team should remember its past, when it should deliberately forget it, and when holding up an old code artifact as a model is helping rather than crippling current work? I want something more practical than "it depends." What would a healthy relationship to a codebase's history even look like?
