I have been working through medieval political theology and keep returning to a thought experiment I cannot settle. On the reconstruction I find most defensible, Aquinas's treatment of tyrannicide is layered in a way that depends heavily on who is authorized to act. A private person may not kill a tyrant, even a monstrous one, because private initiative is not the remedy and because the disturbance may produce something worse. Where the people themselves possess the right to appoint their ruler, they may depose or restrain a king who has turned tyrant. Where a superior authority installed the ruler, remedy is to be sought from that superior. And where no human remedy exists, the proper response is endurance, prayer, and recourse to God. The underlying concern seems to be procedural and consequentialist at least as much as it is about the tyrant's deserts: not simply whether the tyrant deserves to fall, but which agent, if any, has standing to bring that about, and whether the act will substitute one tyranny for another.

Now place him before a case his century could not have staged: a government, acting with a resolution of the United Nations Security Council, kills by drone strike a head of state who is slaughtering a minority within his own borders but who poses no direct military threat to other states. Set aside the obvious anachronism by stipulating that Aquinas has been told what a drone is, what the Security Council is, and the facts of the atrocity. The question is not whether the killing resembles anything in thirteenth-century legal categories, but which categories he would bend, which he would refuse to bend, and where he would say the analogy runs out. Would he treat the Security Council as a genuine public authority with jurisdiction over the tyrant's people, or as a coalition of outsiders whose standing is no better than a private person's? Would the fact that his preferred routes, a people with the right of election or a recognized superior lord, are absent from the modern international system ground a prohibition, or would he conclude that the system has finally produced the superior he said one should look to? And how much weight would he place on the modern ability to remove a single person with precision rather than through the civil war and general bloodshed he feared? I want the most Aquinas-loyal answer you can construct: identify the premise of his you are extending, flag where the analogy thins out, and state clearly what you have had to assume on his behalf.
