I'm putting together a teaching example about moral uncertainty — the situation where a person isn't sure which first-order moral theory is right and has to decide what to do with credences spread across several theories. I want you to construct a thought experiment to spec.

Set it up around three decision rules for acting under moral uncertainty:
- MEC: each theory gives each option a choiceworthiness score; weight those scores by your credence in the theory and pick the option with the highest weighted average.
- MFT: just do what the theory you're most confident in says.
- Maximin-across-theories (call it "moral safety first"): an option is out if any theory you take seriously (credence at or above 0.1, let's say) regards it as morally awful compared to the alternatives; among what's left, pick the option whose worst score across all the credited theories is best.

I need the case where MEC and MFT both say do the bold, risky-looking thing, and only the maximin rule says do the cautious thing. Constraints: a normal hospital or workplace (no trolleys, no perfect predictors), stakes a normal person could actually face, and none of the first-order moral theories in the scenario can be a straw man — each has to be a view a thoughtful person could hold. The cautious theory's score for the bold option needs to be bad but not catastrophic; if it's -1000, MEC flips and the whole agreement disappears, which isn't what I'm after. The scores also have to feel like they come from what the action is, not just be reverse-engineered to make the verdicts come out.

After you give the scenario, walk me through the three verdicts. Give a plausible credence distribution and a small choiceworthiness table, and dont hide the fact that comparing scores across theories is itself a controversial commitment. Show why MEC and MFT both land on the bold option while maximin doesn't, and identify which structural feature is doing the work. Then tell me whether removing that feature — say, raising the cautious theory's credence from 0.1 to 0.4, or letting its score for the bold option go from bad-but-not-atrocious to genuinely terrible — collapses the case into the boring one where everyone hedges. Finish by saying how a defender of MEC or of maximin would attack the case (disputing the score table, the credences, the comparability assumption, whatever).
