I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, so my angle on Mill is probably unorthodox: the passage below makes me think of how something that is really just repeated configuration starts to feel like a built-in default of the system. I want a poem that treats that idea seriously.

Here is the passage from John Stuart Mill's The Subjection of Women (1869):

"But it would be a mistake to suppose that the difficulty of the case must lie in the insufficiency or obscurity of the grounds of reason on which my conviction rests. The difficulty is that which exists in all cases in which there is a mass of feeling to be contended against. So long as an opinion is strongly rooted in the feelings, it gains rather than loses in stability by having a preponderating weight of argument against it."

Write a villanelle that treats this passage as an observation about how custom gets mistaken for nature. The two refrains should do the philosophical work. Make one refrain a line from the point of view of someone defending women's subordinate position by calling it natural. Make the other refrain a Millian reply that identifies apparent naturalness as custom that has repeated itself into a feeling of inevitability. The villanelle form itself should enact the idea: the refrains should first seem like fixed, self-evident fixtures, but by the closing quatrain the reader should see that their apparent inevitability is something the poem chose to keep recursing. Commit to a definite reading of what Mill means by "feeling" in the passage, and make the poem argue for that reading rather than floating ambiguously between options.

After the poem, add a short note flagging where you compressed Mill's view or leaned on a contested reading of it.
