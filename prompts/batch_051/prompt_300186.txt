I've been going down a rabbit hole on algorithmic information theory, and I keep seeing Solomonoff induction presented as if it just settles the problem of induction. I wrote out what I take to be the standard argument as a clean numbered chain so I can see where the magic happens:

(1) Any regularity in a sequence of observations can be represented by some program that outputs that sequence, so hypotheses just are programs (or sets of programs).

(2) Solomonoff's prior assigns each program probability 2 to the power of minus its length, and we use a prefix-free universal machine so the probabilities sum to at most one.

(3) Since shorter programs get exponentially more prior probability, the prior automatically encodes Occam's razor: simpler hypotheses start out more plausible.

(4) Bayes' theorem then does the work: as data produced by the true program accumulates, the posterior probability of that program goes to 1, while longer lookalike programs get exponentially downweighted.

(5) Therefore Solomonoff induction solves the problem of induction: it's the unique rational prior, it converges on any computable regularity you throw at it, and the only real objection is that it's uncomputable.

Something in there feels like a shell game, but I can't tell whether it's step 4, step 5, or just the slide from 4 to 5. Is the argument valid?
