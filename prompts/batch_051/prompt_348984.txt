I've been trying to work out what follows for epistemology if something like the hinge idea in Wittgenstein's On Certainty is right, and I keep winding up with the following argument. I can't tell whether it exposes a genuine structural fact about rational support or whether it smuggles in an equivocation and then congratulates itself for the conclusion. The argument runs:

(1) Any attempt to justify a belief by citing evidence must take for granted various background certainties — for example, that one's senses are broadly reliable, that there has been a past, or that there is an external world.
(2) These background certainties cannot themselves be justified by appeal to evidence, because any evidence one could cite would already presuppose them; to doubt them would dissolve the very frame within which evidential support takes place.
(3) So these hinge commitments are held without evidential justification.
(4) Holding a proposition without evidential justification, where no other source of justification is available, is ordinarily just irrational.
(5) But the hinge commitments are not irrational — they are not epistemic defects — and indeed a functioning inquirer cannot proceed without them.
(6) Therefore there is a class of commitments that are neither justified nor unjustified but that are nonetheless epistemically proper.

My instinct is that the weak point must be somewhere around (3) and (4): the move from not being justified by evidential support to not being justified at all. But that move might be exactly what the hinge picture is entitled to, since its point is that evidential support has a limit that is not itself assessed by evidential support. Alternatively, one could say hinges are not held as propositions at all but are something more like rules of epistemic practice, in which case (3) commits a category mistake. I want to know which step actually fails, on what reading, or whether I should just accept a conclusion I find uncomfortable — that epistemology must allow a class of commitments that are proper without being justified.
