Mathematicians treat two computer-dependent results as theorems in good standing. The first is the four-colour theorem: its proof requires checking an enormous but finite set of configurations, a task done by computer, and no human has inspected every case. The second is the Kepler conjecture about the densest possible packing of equal spheres, accepted on the strength of a formal, computer-verified proof whose full derivation is far beyond what any single mathematician can survey. A referee of either result cannot sit down and follow the reasoning step by step; what gets checked is the algorithm, the specification, and sometimes an independent implementation. Yet the same community rejects as proof any argument that merely reports successful trials: a conjecture confirmed for a huge but proper subset of cases, however large the sample, is not considered established.

I want an account of what conception of mathematical proof the practice of accepting such unsurveyable proofs presupposes. In particular, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Does it presuppose that mathematical justification can remain a priori even when the justificatory chain is too long for any human to survey? Does it presuppose that the essential object is a mechanically checkable formal derivation rather than a humanly surveyable argument? Does it presuppose a specific attitude toward the reliability of computation, or toward the division of epistemic labor among mathematicians and software? And is it consistent with the profession's continuing suspicion of experimental mathematics?

To sharpen the question, suppose a fully automatic theorem prover were to output a formally verified proof of a major conjecture, with no human able to say much more than that the checker accepted it. Would accepting that result change the practice under analysis, or would it only make explicit something already conceded when the four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture were accepted?
