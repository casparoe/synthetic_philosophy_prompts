I am a novelist. For the past year I have been writing a novel about a woman who arranges her entire life around the memory of a friend who died young; the book turns on whether her devotion is love or something closer to idolatry. The work has forced on me a set of convictions about what it means to value something in the wrong way, and I should like them examined for consistency before they harden into the novel's unspoken metaphysics. They are these:

1. Idolatry is a defect in how one values, not in what one believes; one can idolise someone whilst holding no false beliefs about them.

2. It is not simply a matter of caring too much, or of valuing a thing beyond its worth. One can arrange one's life around something one sincerely regards as modest, and one can grossly overrate a thing without ever letting it become an idol.

3. What marks the idol is the structure of the devotion: the object is treated as unconditionally overriding, as final — whatever competes with it must yield, and other goods are kept only insofar as they can be made to serve it.

4. Yet some attachments with just that structure are plainly not idolatrous. A parent who refuses to bargain away a child, or a spouse who keeps a vow at ruinous cost, treats the child or the vow as final without making an idol of it.

5. The difference must therefore lie not in the shape of the devotion but in what it is answerable to. The parent's devotion answers to the child; the idolater's devotion is unanswerable, answering only to itself or to its own need for an object.

6. But answerability threatens to collapse back into proportion: to say that devotion must answer to its object and not outrun it is to allow that devotion can be too much or too little relative to the object, which is the very overvaluation picture I had set aside.

7. And answerability may be invisible to the one who loves. From within, an idolatrous devotion presents itself as a response to an object that demands and repays every sacrifice, never as the imposition of a shape upon an object that does not itself require it.

I should like to know which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each tension is. Rank them from the most damaging to the merely apparent, say what each pair would force me to give up if I wished to keep both commitments, and identify the single revision that would leave the whole position cleanest. I ask as a novelist: a distinction that could never surface in a life, or in a scene, would not be of much use to me.
