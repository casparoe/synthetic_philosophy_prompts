You are a theoretical linguist who works on writing systems and phonological theory. Think carefully before you settle on an answer, and begin by brainstorming candidate principles rather than committing to the first rule that fits. I want you to reverse-engineer the criterion a particular linguist has been using, from a track record of her judgments, and then predict her verdict on a new case.

My colleague, whom I will call R, has spent years giving offhand and published judgments about how phonemic various alphabets are. In my own paraphrase, her track record is as follows. For self-containment, by a phoneme I mean a contrastive sound unit, such that swapping one phoneme for another can produce a different word; by an allophone I mean a surface variant of a phoneme whose occurrence is predictable from context and never by itself distinguishes words.

Her judgments:

- Spanish. She calls it a phonemic alphabet. She mentions silent h, the b/v spellings, and regional differences among c, s, and z, but treats them as small exceptions to an otherwise close letter-to-sound relation.
- Italian. She treats it as a paradigm case of a phonemic alphabet.
- English. She refuses to call it phonemic. In her view it is a deep or morphophonemic orthography: it preserves morphological identities and etymological relationships at the cost of regular letter-to-sound mapping, as with words like sign and signature or the invariant spelling of the past-tense ending.
- Thai. She says it is not phonemic. Several distinct letters can spell the same consonant sound; tone is not read off from a single straightforward mark but reconstructed from consonant class, vowel, final consonant, and tone mark; and many values depend on syllable position or etymological class.
- Pinyin, used for Mandarin. She calls it phonemic: consonants and vowels are represented by letters or fixed digraphs, tone is marked by visible diacritics, and the remaining conventions are a small set of syllable-spelling rules rather than lexical irregularities.
- Hangul. She calls it alphabetic and phonemic, and adds that it is also featural, because the letter shapes themselves encode place and manner of articulation. She treats the featural quality as a further elegance, not as part of what makes a script phonemic.

Now the new case. A field linguist has designed a practical alphabet for a language called Tava, to be used in a village literacy program. In Tava, the sounds [t] and [tʰ] are in complementary distribution: [tʰ] occurs at the beginning of stressed syllables and [t] elsewhere. The same holds for [p] and [pʰ], and for [k] and [kʰ]. No two Tava words are distinguished solely by aspiration, so the standard analysis treats each pair as a single phoneme: /t/, /p/, /k/. The proposed alphabet is otherwise a one-letter-per-phoneme system and is mechanically regular, but it deliberately gives the aspirated and unaspirated stops separate letters, writing the aspirated variants with th, ph, and kh whenever they surface. Every letter therefore maps to exactly one surface sound, and every surface sound has a unique spelling; nothing depends on word-specific knowledge. The designer claims the script is maximally phonemic, more so than Spanish, because pronunciation is fully recoverable from spelling and spelling from pronunciation.

The question is what R would say, if she wants to stay consistent with her track record. Would she call the Tava script a phonemic alphabet? Would she rate it more phonemic than Spanish, less phonemic, or not phonemic at all? I am less interested in the bare label than in the implicit criterion behind it.
