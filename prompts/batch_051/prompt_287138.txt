I've been bothered by a pattern in ordinary conversation that I don't have a word for. Two coworkers have the same evidence and the same confidence, but one says "the build is broken" and the other says "I think the build is broken." If the build turns out fine, I blame the first in a way I don't blame the second. It isn't that he was more certain, and it isn't just that one sentence is literally true while the other is false — the hedged one can be false too, if the speaker doesn't actually think what she says she thinks. The difference seems to be that the unhedged speaker did something extra: he didn't just report his own take, he put himself forward as someone who can settle how things are. But here's what's weird: how much we hold him to it depends on the situation. In a hallway, someone can flatly say the meeting is at 3 from a hazy memory and nobody bats an eye; in a high-stakes incident, the same move gets treated as reckless. So I can't tell whether there's one hidden rule that gets stricter as stakes rise, or whether flat-out assertion is really two different speech acts wearing the same clothes. Does this distinction have a name? And how do the main views about it carve the boundary — what exactly is the extra thing the unhedged speaker is on the hook for, and why does it move around? I'm after the carving more than the label.
