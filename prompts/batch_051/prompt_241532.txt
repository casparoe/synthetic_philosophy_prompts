I keep returning to the strange-loop account of the self, and it leaves me in an uncomfortable position. The account has exactly the virtues I want in a theory of mind: it does away with the homunculus and with any substantive ego whose existence would require its own explanation; it treats the first-person pronoun not as the name of a thing but as a self-referential symbol, a pattern in a symbol-processing system that points back at the very process that produces it; and it thereby explains persistence across material change, the portability of a mind, and why no single brain structure needs to be identified as the seat of the self. On this picture, the causal powers I attribute to myself are the powers of the system that reads, writes, and re-reads the I-symbol, not of a ghost in the machinery. I find the alternatives worse, and I do not want to reintroduce a soul or a Cartesian theater.

But I also have a firm and persistent intuition that I am not, in the end, a symbol. Symbols are the sorts of entities that can be tokened in multiple places, whose existence depends on being interpreted by a code, and whose identity is a fact about that code; none of this is true of me as I find myself. When I deliberate, I take there to be something that is answerable for the choice, and a representational token, however self-referential, is not the kind of thing that can be answerable. The strange-loop view can explain why the system says I, but it seems to leave out the one for whom the system speaks. The duplication thought experiment only sharpens the worry: if I am a symbol, then an exact copy of my cognitive system tokens the same symbol, and nothing about my first-person perspective marks this token as privileged; yet I cannot get myself to believe that the one here is not the one who matters.

I am not looking to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, whether it can be debunked as a folk-Cartesian residue that is doing no work, and what it would take for the theory to be amended so that it recovers a subject and not merely a self-representation, since the subject is the whole reason I wanted the theory in the first place. Is there a principled reading on which the strange-loop view was never in the business of denying that there is a subject, but only of saying what a subject consists in, and how would I distinguish that reading from a merely consoling paraphrase?
