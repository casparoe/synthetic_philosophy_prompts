I'm a journalist working on a piece about why mathematics is so effective in physics, and I keep returning to Eugene Wigner's old observation: structures developed for their own internal reasons later turn out, with surprising frequency, to be exactly what physics needs. I want to know whether the strong version of that claim — that the fit is unreasonable rather than merely real — is testable, and what a test would show. Here is the design I keep sketching: assemble a sample of pure-mathematical results developed before any known physical use, compare the rate at which they later became physically indispensable against a matched sample of arbitrary formal constructions that lacked that prestige, and control for the obvious biases — successes are remembered, dead ends are forgotten, and once an application exists the fit gets read backward as inevitable. Tell me whether this is the right design or what a better one would be, what result would count for and against the claim, and what no test of this kind could settle — for example, whether mathematics is discovered or invented, or whether physicists simply select formalisms that flatter their methods. Keep it under 700 words, in American English.
