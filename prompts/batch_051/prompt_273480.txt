I have been trying, on my own and without any formal background in philosophy, to get my head around the sorites paradox. Take a heap of sand and remove one grain at a time. No single removal seems to make the difference between a heap and not a heap, yet after enough removals it plainly is no longer a heap. The two responses that interest me most are these. The first says there really is a sharp cut-off somewhere in the sequence, but we are simply unable to know where it lies. The second says there is no fact of the matter at the boundary, so a sentence like 'this is a heap' may be neither definitely true nor definitely false, and becomes true or false only once we sharpen the word by stipulation. I believe these views are called epistemicism and supervaluationism, though as an outsider I may not have the details exactly right.

I would like a sonnet that does real work with the distinction rather than only naming it. The octave should set up the puzzle: the shrinking heap, the missing threshold, the sense that something is wrong with the reasoning. The volta should be the moment the two responses split apart. The sestet could let each position have its say — a hidden sharp line on one side, a gap in truth that sharpening fills on the other — and the closing couplet should leave the two still in tension, since I understand neither view gets everything it wants. After the poem, add a short note in which you commit to a definite reading of the two views, say which lines voice which position and how the form tracks the argument, and flag anywhere you have simplified or stretched the ideas for the sake of the verse.
