I keep going back and forth on blackmail, which is embarrasing because it feels like one of those topics where everyone knows the conclusion and nobody can say why. Here are my verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I want you to tell me what principle, if any, captures them, which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and what it says about some cases I haven't listed. I'd rather have one precise principle than ten little ones, but a couple of candidates is fine if the choice between them is the interesting part.

Case one. Clara and Alex are coworkers, and Clara finds out Alex is having an affair. She tells him she'll tell his wife unless he pays her ten thousand. Verdict: blackmail, clearly wrong, clearly illegal. No hesitation.

Case two. Same facts, but Clara just tells Alex's wife, no demand and no offer. Verdict: not blackmail. Maybe a lousy thing to do, maybe not, but not something the law should treat as extortion.

Case three. Alex hears that Clara knows. He comes to her unprompted and offers her ten thousand if she agrees to keep quiet, and she accepts. Verdict: I don't think this is blackmail, and I'd resist criminalizing it, even though the outcome is identical to case one and the difference bothers me. If your principle says case three is blackmail too, I want to know exactly why the initiative should not matter, because my gut says it matters a lot.

Case four. Clara doesn't take money. She tells Alex to end the affair or she'll tell his wife. Verdict: not blackmail to me, though I can see a separate wrong of pressuring him. If your principle says this counts as blackmail because ending the affair is something Clara wants, I think that's overbroad, but I'll hear the argument.

Case five. Tarek has solid proof his factory is dumping waste into the river. He tells the owner to pay him twenty thousand or he'll report it to the regulator. Verdict: blackmail, or at least something equally criminal. What unsettles me is that reporting the dumping would be a good act, maybe even an obligation, and yet making it conditional on payment ruins it. I also suspect that if Tarek just shut up and took no money, nobody would call it blackmail, even though that silence is probably worse.

Case six. A journalist has pictures of a minister's affair, obtained honestly. He calls the minister's aide and says fifty thousand and I kill the story, otherwise it runs. Verdict: blackmail, even though publishing the story would be lawful and maybe even in the public interest. Publishing is allowed, asking for money is allowed, but yoking them together is not.

Case seven. A lawyer tells a company: settle this breach of contract claim for eighty thousand, or I'll sue and the filing will be public and embarrasing. Verdict: not blackmail; that's just negotiation. This is the case that keeps me up, because six and seven look structurally identical to me: a lawful disclosure or filing is coming unless money changes hands, and the money is not something the demanding party already has a right to. Yet I'm completely sure the journalist is a blackmailer and the lawyer isn't. If your principle can't separate them without inventing an ad hoc exception, I need to know that.

My running hunch is that it's blackmail just in case you threaten to do something you're otherwise entitled to do, in order to get a benefit you have no independent right to. That labels cases one, five, and six as blackmail, and lets two and three off, which is mostly where my verdicts are. Case four depends on whether "benefit" includes getting Alex to end the affair; if it does, the hunch calls it blackmail and I'm not sure I want to say that. But the real problem is seven: the lawyer is entitled to sue and has no independent right to the settlement before a court says so, so the hunch calls the lawyer a blackmailer. So either the hunch is wrong, or my verdict in seven has to go, or "entitled to do" needs changing. Maybe the lawyer's demand rests on an underlying claim to damages and the journalist's doesn't. But then five gets strange, because Tarek has no underlying claim to the twenty thousand, though he does have an entitlement, maybe even a duty, to report. Does that underlying claim distinction explain seven without wrecking five? I want you to either repair the hunch or replace it, and say plainly which of my verdicts the surviving principle can't keep. And once you have a principle, say what it thinks is actually wrong with blackmail, because the puzzle is that each ingredient looks fine on its own.
