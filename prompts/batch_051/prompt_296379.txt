I am a doctoral student in philosophy working with a computer science group that develops an AI pipeline for screening human-subjects research proposals. Our department's research-integrity code contains the following clause: "Where an investigator has formed the judgment that a proposed study would violate a subject's rights or welfare, and fails to act on that judgment when it is within the investigator's power to do so, that investigator bears responsibility for any resulting violation to the same degree as the person who performed it."

Our system encodes ethical review criteria and assigns each proposal a risk finding, a recommendation, and a prose rationale. It can represent something like the judgment that a protocol is coercive or exploitative, but by design it has no power to block, amend, or formally escalate anything: it only advises a human committee member, who is free to approve anyway. In a recent test, the system flagged a protocol as coercive, the committee member overrode the flag, and the protocol proceeded.

The clause was plainly written for human investigators and could not have anticipated an evaluator that forms ethical assessments while being deliberately built with no executive capacity. Does the plain wording apply to the system at all, given that it never "fails to act" because acting was never within its power? Under a purposive reading, should the clause be extended to it because the point of the provision is that an unactioned moral judgment should not be costless? Or is the system better described as a constrained agent, or as no agent at all, with the human reviewers bearing whatever responsibility exists? What minimal change to its capabilities would make it a moral agent on each reading?
