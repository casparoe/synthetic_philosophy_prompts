I'm organizing a small workshop this spring on divine command theory in the Islamic tradition — mostly for my grad students and a couple of colleagues from Near Eastern Studies — and I want a centerpiece document to circulate beforehand. The format I've landed on is an adversarial collaboration between a committed Mu'tazili and a committed Ash'ari over what gets called the Islamic Euthyphro: roughly, are acts good or evil independently of God's command, or does the command make them so. The two participants have agreed to ground rules up front: no polemics, no proof-texting from scripture, no accusing the other side of secretly denying God's justice or His sovereignty, and every substantive claim has to end up either as something both can sign or as a disagreement stated precisely enough that each side would accept the wording of its own view. Just so the thing doesn't get off on the wrong foot, the Mu'tazili will own a view like this: acts have objective moral properties (ḥusn and qubḥ) that unaided reason can at least partly grasp, and God, being just, commands and forbids in accordance with them. The Ash'ari will own a view like this: good and evil are constituted by divine command and prohibition, and there is no standard outside God's will to which His commands must answer. Both have agreed those one-sentence statements are fair, not strawmen.

I'd like you to draft the joint statement itself. Start with a numbered list of claims both sides can sign — about what needs explaining, about moral language not being empty, about God's justice, about the fact that reason does present at least some acts as wrong before any revealed command is consulted — whatever you judge genuinely belongs there and isn't vacuous. Then a disagreements table. The rows should be the central sub-questions: e.g., can unaided reason know the moral status of an act prior to revelation? is a command it would be wrong to obey even thinkable in principle? does God's commanding an act give you a reason or create one? For each row, give the exact Mu'tazili formulation and the exact Ash'ari formulation, each phrased the way its own side would want it phrased. Then a cruxes section: the sub-questions where a settled answer would actually move at least one side, and for each crux, what kind of argument or case both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Finish by saying which parts of the dispute you think are merely verbal, if any, and why. I'd rather you be honest about that than manufacture disagreement where there isnt any.
