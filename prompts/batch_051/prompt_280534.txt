I spent my career as a working natural scientist, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of biology. A puzzle that keeps surfacing concerns the way my former colleagues count organisms, and I want a philosopher's verdict on what those counting practices presuppose.

Consider two ordinary practices. In conservation assessment, the threat category assigned to a species under the Red List criteria used by conservation authorities depends partly on the number of mature individuals that remain. Before the assessor can count anything, they must decide what counts as one individual. A single clonal aspen stand — one underground root system, thousands of trunks — may be recorded as one mature individual or as thousands, depending on the protocol; the same dilemma arises for a coral colony, a bryozoan mat, or a clonal grass. Field workers frequently distinguish the ramet, the physically distinct module or shoot, from the genet, the genetically uniform lineage descending from one zygote, and different protocols select different units in different taxa, apparently without a single general principle governing all cases.

In the microbiology laboratory, by contrast, a colony-forming unit on an agar plate is counted as one individual even though it may have arisen from a clump of cells or from a single cell that moved across the plate; a metabolically integrated biofilm, which behaves as a coordinated whole and which some microbiologists would regard as the more individual-like object, is not counted as an individual at all. And the conceptual ground is shifting further: a mammal together with its resident microbes is now routinely described as a holobiont, and an ant nest as a superorganism, yet neither figures as a unit in any of the counting practices above.

Act as a professional philosopher of biology. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the candidate criteria of biological individuality — genetic homogeneity, the reproductive bottleneck from a single cell, physiological integration, immunological identity, evolutionary individuality, and any others you regard as live options — before committing yourself.

Then give me a committed answer rather than a survey. Take a definite position and defend it, while being explicit about the costs of that position. In particular: what theory, or family of theories, of biological individuality do these counting practices presuppose? What do they strictly presuppose, as opposed to merely fitting well with? Are the conservation practice and the laboratory practice presupposing consistent things, or do they quietly rely on different theories in different contexts? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that several defensible theories would license the same report? And what would have to change in conservation accounting and in laboratory reporting if the presupposed theory were replaced by a rival — for example, if individuality were judged by physiological integration and coordinated behaviour rather than by descent from a single cell?

Please write in British English, and please show your reasoning before settling on the position you defend.
