I'm a philosophy professor putting together a module on regress arguments, and I've noticed my own instincts about which regresses are vicious are less principled than I'd like them to be. I want you to act as an expert on the logic of regress arguments—someone who has taught this and thought hard about when an infinite regress refutes something and when it's inert—and help me extract the principle behind my own verdicts.

Here are my gut reactions to a batch of cases. Work me through them slowly and think carefully before you settle into a position.

1. An endless chain of efficient causes going back into the past, no first event. I don't think this is incoherent on its own; I can describe such a world without contradiction. So: benign.

2. The old story where the Earth is held up by something standing on something else, which stands on something else, and so on with no bottom, offered as an explanation of why the Earth doesn't fall. I'd say it explains nothing. The bill never gets paid; its just passed down. So: vicious.

3. A person believes p on the basis of q, q on the basis of r, and so on endlessly, with no foundational belief and no circle. I want to say she can still be justified in believing p. The lack of a bottom doesn't by itself defeat the justification. So: benign, or at least not automatically vicious.

4. Carroll's tortoise argument: the tortoise accepts p and accepts if-p-then-q, but refuses to move to q. We add as a further premise that if p and if-p-then-q, then q. The tortoise accepts that too but still won't take the step, so we add another premise, forever. My verdict is that the regress is vicious relative to the tortoise's demand—it shows you can't treat rules of inference as just more premises. But I also don't think it shows modus ponens is broken; it diagnoses a bad picture of what a rule is.

5. Bradley's regress. Suppose a is F. If a's being F consists in a standing in an instantiation relation to F, then we seem to need a further relation tying a and F to instantiation, and then a further one, and so on. My verdict: vicious. This shows predication can't be analyzed as a relation that always needs connecting again; you need facts, or a non-relational tie, or something of that sort.

6. Gunk: a material object whose parts all have further proper parts, and so on down forever, with no simples. This doesn't bother me at all. A gunky object is still an object; it doesn't fail to exist just because division never bottoms out. So: benign.

The thing that bugs me is that 5 and 6 feel like they should get the same verdict—both are the structure of some thing opening out into an infinite series—but I'd bet heavily on one's being vicious and the other not. And 1 and 3 feel similar in another way (infinite chains with no bottom, causes vs. reasons), and I hand them the same verdict, but I'm not sure the same feature explains both.

I've been toying with this principle: a regress is vicious iff the task, explanation, or justification at each level is incomplete until the next level is supplied, so the original demand never gets discharged; it's benign iff the new items aren't doing that deferral work—they're just more of the same structure, and the demand is already met at each level. But I can already see it stumbles on 4, and probably on 5 or 6 as well.

Here's what I want from you:

(a) two or three candidate principles that could capture my verdicts, stated precisely, with a note on which clause handles which case;

(b) an honest statement of which verdict or verdicts each principle forces me to give up;

(c) which candidate you think I should adopt and why; and

(d) a verdict, with reasoning, on a case I haven't made up my mind about yet. A truth-maker maximalist says every true proposition is made true by some entity. Let p be made true by x. Now take the proposition that p has a truth-maker. Does that new proposition need a truth-maker of its own? If it does, is the resulting chain vicious or benign? I keep flip-flopping, and I'd like to know wether the principle you extract says it's vicious or benign.

Please start by brainstorming several candidate principles and testing them against my six cases before committing to an answer. And don't tell me my intuitions are consistent if they aren't—I'd rather find out which one has got to go.
