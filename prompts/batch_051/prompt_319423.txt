I just started as the data analyst for my state's lottery commission (a friend got me the gig; mostly I audit retail sales data, but now they want me to look at whether a run of winning numbers could have been rigged). A statistician friend gave me an argument that's been bothering me, because the conclusion says the thing I'm being asked to do is impossible. It goes:

(1) A finite sequence of outcomes is random only if it could have been produced by a fair, genuinely chancy process.
(2) Any finite sequence, however patterned — 999999, 0101010101, whatever — could have come out of a fair process, since every particular sequence has some nonzero probability under that process.
(3) So a finite sequence, just sitting there, can never count as non-random; being compatible with chance is universal.
(4) By the same token, any finite sequence could have been produced by a deterministic rigging device built to emit exactly that sequence, since you could build such a device for any sequence you like.
(5) Therefore a finite sequence by itself is never evidence either way about whether the process was random or rigged; sequence-level "randomness tests" are statistically unjustified.
(6) But that can't be right: we all treat a hundred 9s in a row as suspicious, and if we couldn't, the whole fraud-detection part of my job would be pointless.

He thinks the only repair is to say a finite string is random to the degree that there's no rule shorter than the string itself that outputs it — so 999999... is compressible and not random. But that sounds to me like randomness becomes a fact about our descriptive language rather than about the actual machine that generated the draws, and I dont see why "compressible" should be evidence of fraud.

I'd like to keep (1) if I can, since randomness really does seem to be about the physical process. So: which step in the argument is weak, and if I reject it, what exactly do I lose? If the only repair is the compressibility idea, how does that connect back to whether someone rigged a lottery?
