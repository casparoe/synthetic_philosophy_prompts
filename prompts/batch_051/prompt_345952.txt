I'm an undergraduate serving as a teaching assistant in a second-year course on rationality and the emotions, and I've been asked to design the 50-minute discussion section that follows the lecture on the sunk cost fallacy. The section has about eighteen students, and I'd like the activity to work with only index cards, a whiteboard, and a few bags of tokens — no real money and no pre-session reading. The main goal is for students to feel, in their own choices, the pull of sticking with a losing investment, and then to walk into the philosophical problem this opens up: the psychological tendency is well documented, but it is a further and much more contested step to say that honoring sunk costs is always irrational.

I know the obvious design — have students invest tokens in a first phase and then offer a better second-phase option that requires abandoning the original investment — but on its own that only demonstrates the effect and points toward the standard verdict that sticking is a mistake. My professor wants the session to do something harder: it should leave room for both assessments, so that students can see why some people will want to insist the behavior is nothing but a fallacy, while others will want to say that honoring sunk costs can be forward-looking rational — for example, because abandoning a project can carry reputational or self-signaling costs, because past investment can function as a commitment device that protects a fragile project, or because the choice to abandon something is itself evidence about how one will choose in similar situations later. The activity should get students to actually face that tension before it is named, so I'd prefer no lecture up front and no use of the phrase "sunk cost fallacy" until the debrief.

Could you design the full session for me: the rules, materials, a payoff scheme that does not require real money, a timeline for the fifty minutes, and a structured debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the descriptive/normative distinction and onto the possible rational defenses of sticking? Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, how to handle that without flattening the ambiguity, and be candid about which parts of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and which parts I would need to supply myself. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in the psychological and philosophical claims the exercise relies on, and single out the point you are least sure about.
