Consider the following argument, which I encountered in a dispute about what a state gives up when it joins a customs union. I cannot tell whether it is valid, and if it fails, which step is carrying the weight.

(1) A body is sovereign over a matter exactly when, with respect to that matter, its decision is final: no other body has the standing to revise or set it aside.

(2) In a customs union, the members confer the power to set the common external tariff and common commercial policy on union institutions, whose decisions bind every member even when a member dissents.

(3) So, on trade policy, a member's own decision is not final, because union institutions can set it aside.

(4) Within a single matter, sovereignty does not come in degrees: either a body's decision on that matter is final, or some other body can set it aside. There is no condition of being partly sovereign over one and the same matter.

(5) Therefore, member states of a customs union are not sovereign with respect to trade policy.

(6) Sovereignty, however, is not the sort of thing a state can lose in one pocket of its affairs while keeping elsewhere. A sovereign state is the single bearer of final authority over its affairs as a whole; if it lacks final authority over trade policy, then it is not the bearer of final authority over its affairs as a whole.

(7) Therefore, states that form a customs union cease to be sovereign states, and the common description of them as remaining sovereign by pooling their sovereignty is at best a loose manner of speaking.

This conclusion seems to me too strong, but I cannot say where the argument goes wrong without simply refusing the conclusion. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and under what reading of its key terms does it fail? If it is valid, what should I conclude about the ordinary claim that members of such a union remain sovereign?
