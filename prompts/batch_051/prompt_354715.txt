I've been going back and forth with some people on a health-policy forum about how hospitals should allocate ventilators in a bad pandemic wave, and one of them threw out a mechanism I can't stop thinking about. Start with the standard QALY-style triage idea: every patient presenting to an ICU gets a prospective score — expected quality-adjusted life-years saved per ventilator-day — and scarce beds go down that list until the unit is full. The twist is a buy-up clause. Anyone (patient, family, insurer, complete stranger) can pay to move a below-cutoff patient above the line, at a price the regulator derives from its official value of a statistical life: the patient's expected QALY shortfall relative to the current marginal patient, multiplied by a published dollars-per-QALY figure drawn from that VSL. The payment is non-refundable and calculated on the prospective estimate, not the outcome. The money then goes into an earmarked fund that has to be spent, through a pre-approved menu of interventions, to buy back at least that many QALYs for the broader population — vaccines, lead abatement, cheap hypertension meds, whatever clears the bar. The pitch is that nobody jumps the queue for free: the rich have to make someone else statistically whole to jump it, and the scheme is QALY-positive or at least neutral by construction.

What I want isn't another debate about whether the thing is fair in the abstract. I want you to walk through how it would actually behave if you ran it for a decade at scale, the way you'd analyze a weird prediction market or a reputation system. What does it do to who actually ends up in the ICU, what do hospitals do to the scoring rubric once a price is attached to each point on it, what products do insurers invent, does the earmarked fund survive contact with a regulator who has other priorities, and does the nominal cutoff start drifting toward whoever can pay? Where does it get gamed first, and which failure mode actually kills it? Then give me a committed verdict on each main risk — not a balanced menu — plus your honest answer on whether patches like means-testing the buy-up price, or random audits of the severity scores, would fix the scheme or just relocate the game.
