Ok so here's a thing I keep going back and forth on, and I want someone to actually take a side. Plutarch is pretty upfront that his project isn't history so much as moral portraiture—that a throwaway gesture can reveal character better than a battle, because it shows what the person was like. And medieval hagiographers clearly weren't trying to be modern biographers; a saint's life is meant to make you love the saint, not to get the timeline right. Fine. But if the point is to become better by imitating, say, Francis of Assisi, and the story that actually moves me turns out to be invented, then the person I'm imitating didn't do the thing that's doing the moral work. Doesn't that make hagiography basicaly a novel with extra steps? And if a made-up Francis works just as well, why did these traditions insist on real historical people instead of inventing perfect exemplars in the first place? I'm not asking for a survey of views. I want you to commit: does the historical unreliability of an edifying biography undermine its moral usefulness, or not? Give me the position you'd defend and the strongest reasons for it.
