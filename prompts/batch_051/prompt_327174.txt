I am a PhD student in philosophical theology, and I have been asked to give a one-hour adult-education session at a local church on animal suffering before the Fall. The audience is theologically literate but not academically trained: mostly old-earth creationists and evolutionary creationists, with a small but confident minority who hold a young-earth view. For many of them the very topic sounds like a concession, so I expect the session to begin slightly defensive. My goal is not to settle anyone's view on the age of the earth or the mechanics of human origins; it is to get them to see the underlying conceptual tension and to feel why the comfortable options each cost something. A colleague in the New Testament group warned me that, if I am not careful, the room will split into a science-versus-Bible argument and never reach the theological problem at all.

The tension, as I mean to present it, is this. The doctrine of a good creation says the created order reflects God's intentions rather than being a realm of accident or malevolence, and the biblical strand, read in the usual way, says death entered the world through human sin. But the biological picture, taken at face value, says that predation, parasitism, disease, and organism-level death were structural features of life for hundreds of millions of years before any human existed to sin. If animals suffered and died before the Fall, then either the Fall introduced something other than death as such, or a good creation can contain things that look unredeemed, or pre-human animal suffering is not the kind of evil the tradition thinks it is, or the evolutionary picture must simply be resisted. Each move is coherent, but each quietly revises something the tradition wanted to say. I suspect the problem also splits into sub-questions — a biological question about whether pre-human animals suffered in a morally significant way, a theological question about what exactly the Fall introduced, and a value question about whether animal pain is the kind of evil that calls for a special explanation — and part of the art of teaching this is deciding which of these is the hinge.

I would like you to act as an experienced teacher of philosophical theology who has taught this material to non-specialists many times, and to plan the session rather than write the lecture. Please think carefully about the conceptual dependencies before settling on the plan, and begin by sketching the pieces in whatever order occurs to you. I need from you: (1) the order in which the components must be introduced and why, including which distinction has to be in place before the tension can even be felt; (2) the one concrete case or example that should carry the session, and what it must have and lack so that it makes the problem vivid without smuggling in one of the solutions; (3) the misconceptions the audience will bring or form — I suspect some will think the Fall retroactively corrupted the entire cosmos, and others will think the problem disappears once we stop reading Genesis literally — and the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than argued with at the end; (4) a check for understanding that would show the idea has landed, meaning they can state the problem as a problem internal to the doctrine of creation rather than as a science-versus-faith conflict; and (5) what I should deliberately leave out at this level and how to say so when someone presses. Do not organize this around readings or names from the literature; I want a plan for what happens in the room, not a syllabus.
