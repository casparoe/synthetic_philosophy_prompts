I have no academic background in philosophy, but I have been working through a moral question on my own, and it keeps leading me to a conclusion I find hard to accept. I have set my reasoning out below as a numbered argument. Please tell me whether the argument is valid, and if it is not, exactly which step fails and why. If it is valid, please say so, and tell me whether I should accept the conclusion.

Let T be the outcome in which one person is tortured continuously for fifty years. For any number n, let S(n) be the outcome in which n different people each get a single speck of dust in the eye, once, for a few seconds, and no one is harmed in any other way; S(0) is the outcome in which no one is harmed at all. I am using the word "worse" in the ordinary sense.

1. S(0) is not worse than T. A world in which no one is harmed is not worse than a world in which someone is tortured for fifty years; it is clearly better.

2. For any n, if S(n) is not worse than T, then S(n + 1) is not worse than T. The only difference between S(n) and S(n + 1) is that one additional person has a speck of dust in the eye for a few seconds. That is too small a difference to turn an outcome that was not worse than T into an outcome that is worse than T. A single added speck cannot be the point at which the comparison tips.

3. Starting from step 1 and applying step 2 again and again, we get: S(1) is not worse than T, S(2) is not worse than T, and so on without end. Therefore, for every number n, S(n) is not worse than T.

4. It follows that no matter how many people get a speck of dust in the eye — a thousand, a trillion, any finite number you can name — that outcome is never worse than one person being tortured for fifty years.

5. So if one had to choose between bringing about T and bringing about S(n), the torture could never be the better option, no matter how large n is. For very large n I find this hard to accept: it seems as though enough small discomforts spread across enough people ought to outweigh one person's great suffering. But I cannot see which step fails, because denying step 2 seems to force me to say that somewhere in the chain there is a particular speck — the one extra dust mote in one particular person's eye — that single-handedly tips the balance, and that also seems absurd.

Is the argument valid? If not, which step is the first to fail, and what exactly is wrong with it? If it is valid, should I simply accept the conclusion?
