I have been using a principle that I want stress-tested, because I have begun to lean on it in arguments and I am no longer sure it can bear the weight. I call it "partition debt." The idea is that a statistical association in aggregated data is never simply given: it is computed at a chosen level of grain, and whoever reports it owes the reader an account of the partitions of the data under which it holds. I also hold the stronger view that if an association reverses under a partition by a variable that is relevant to the outcome, then the aggregate association is an artifact of binning rather than a fact about the world, and the disaggregated comparisons are the honest ones. Only associations that preserve their direction across every defensible partition deserve to be called effects.

Here is my standard example. Two interviewing panels, A and B, see junior and senior candidates. Panel A hires 10% of juniors and 75% of seniors; Panel B hires 20% of juniors and 80% of seniors, so B hires better within each level. But A interviews 300 seniors and 30 juniors while B interviews the reverse, so A's overall hire rate is about 69% and B's is about 25%. I say the sentence "Panel A hires better than Panel B" is simply false: the only genuine comparisons are the within-level ones, and anyone quoting the overall rate is confusing the coarsest partition with the causal grain of the data.

I want to know whether this is a real concept or just a metaphor, whether the defensible version of it already exists under another name, what it gets right, and where it breaks. In particular: is there a case where the aggregated association is the more truthful one even though it reverses under a relevant partition, and a case where the disaggregated comparisons are themselves the artifact? And tell me what would change your overall verdict: which consideration, if I accepted it, would lead you to reject or heavily qualify the principle, and where your assessment is least certain.
