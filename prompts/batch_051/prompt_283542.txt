In climate policy you see the same analogy wheeled out over and over: caring less about a person in 2200 just because they're in 2200 is no different from caring less about someone just because they're far away. Time gets treated as if it's just another sort of distance, and since spatial distance obviously shouldn't determine whether a life matters, the conclusion is suposed to be that a "pure" rate of time preference in climate economics — discounting future wellbeing simply for being future, before you even get to uncertainty or growth — is just as arbitrary. I want an audit of how far that analogy actually holds. Give the explicit mapping first (what corresponds to what; a table is fine), then go at the disanalogies: uncertainty grows over time but not over space, causation through time runs one way where through space it runs both, far-away people exist now whereas future people's very existence may partly depend on what we choose, and whatever else you find that matters. Then say which disanalogies genuinely threaten the intended conclusion and which are just noise, and whether the anti-pure-discounting conclusion survives once they're on the table. If the analogy can't carry the weight, say what better argument, if any, gets you to the same place.
