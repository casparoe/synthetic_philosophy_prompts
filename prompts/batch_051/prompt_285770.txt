You are a philosopher of mathematics specializing in the early twentieth-century foundational debates. I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on the foundations of mathematics, and I want you to write the centerpiece of that handout: a decision tree that sorts a reader from a few pre-theoretical commitments about mathematics into one of the three classical schools—logicism, formalism, or intuitionism—or into a defensible variant within one of them.

The tree must be diagnostic rather than name-driven. No node may mention the labels logicism, formalism, or intuitionism; each node should be a concrete question or claim on which the schools genuinely come apart, phrased so a student can answer it without already knowing which position the answer leads to. Nodes worth considering include: whether a non-constructive existence proof—one that shows a number with a certain property would cause a contradiction if it did not exist, without exhibiting or computing it—counts as proving the number exists; whether a statement about all natural numbers is already true or false before anyone has proved or refuted it; whether mathematical objects are invented by us or discovered by us; and whether an axiom system needs only to be consistent, or whether its axioms must carry independent intuitive evidence.

Before drafting anything, begin by brainstorming candidate splitting questions. Then, working slowly through the logical space, choose four to six such questions and lay out the combinations of answers they permit. Locate the three schools within that space, and decide where the two main faces of formalism would land: the view of mathematics as moves within an uninterpreted symbol system, and the view of theorems as conditionals leading from axioms to derived consequences. Also identify where Brouwerian intuitionism lands, and whether it needs a leaf of its own apart from any broader constructivist position. Note any answer combinations that remain unoccupied, and say briefly for each whether it is empty because no one could coherently hold it, because it would be unattractive, or because it simply has not been explored. Flag clearly any two positions the tree cannot separate, and state what further question would separate them.

Each branch in the final tree should be labeled with the claim or question that splits it, in plain language. Aim for roughly five to eight decision nodes and no more than seven leaves. Each leaf should give the name of the position, the path of answers that reaches it, and one sentence on the most awkward commitment the position is forced to accept. Do not include any bibliography, reading list, citations, or references to specific books or articles; the instrument should be self-contained and rest entirely on its conceptual distinctions.
