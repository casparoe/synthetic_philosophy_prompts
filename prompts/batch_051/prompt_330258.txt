Question: "Skolem's paradox is often glossed as showing that first-order set theory cannot pin down uncountability. Explain why Skolem's paradox arises and how it should be resolved. Does it show that there are no genuinely uncountable sets?"

Student answer:
"Skolem's paradox comes from the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem. Since ZFC is a first-order theory in a countable language, if ZFC is consistent then it has a countable transitive model M. (This follows because any first-order theory in a countable language with an infinite model has a countable model.) But ZFC proves Cantor's theorem, so M contains a set c such that M satisfies 'c is uncountable': that is, M thinks there is no bijection between c and its natural numbers.

This looks contradictory, because M itself is countable and, since M is transitive, every element of M is a subset of M and hence countable from the standpoint of the metatheory. In particular c is really countable. But the contradiction is only apparent. When M says 'c is uncountable', it only quantifies over the sets that exist inside M. The outside bijection between c and the natural numbers is not a member of M, so M does not 'see' it. Equivalently, M's powerset is incomplete: it omits the sets that would code the enumeration. So Skolem's paradox does not show that uncountable sets do not exist; it just shows that countability and uncountability are not absolute first-order notions. It would also be wrong to conclude that every model of ZFC is countable. The downward theorom only gives at least one countable model, and the upward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem gives models of arbitrarily large cardinality."
