Okay so I've been going back and forth about this for like a week and I want an outside take. I'm a high school student, and a friend and I got into an arguement after he showed me Epicurus's claim that death isn't bad for the person who dies. The version of the arguement that I find weirdly convincing goes: for something to be bad for you, there has to be a you around at some time to be on the receiving end of the badness, and it has to be bad for you at some time. When you're dead you don't exist, so there's no subject and no time at which being dead is bad for you. And while you're still alive your death hasn't happened yet, so it isn't bad for you then either. So there's just no point at which death is bad for the person who dies. I like how clean this is — it gives a non-mysterious rule for when something counts as bad for someone, and it doesn't leave harms floating around with nobody to own them.

But then I think about a sixteen-year-old who dies in a car crash, and it seems obviously bad for that kid, not just sad for everyone else. If I were offered a choice between dying next year and dying at ninety, I'd have a clear self-interested reason to pick ninety, and I can't make that fit with the argument. I also have this asymmetry: I don't feel any dread about the time before I was born, but I do feel like dying soon would be bad for me, and I don't see how the Epicurean picture explains why future nonexistence feels different from past nonexistence. Maybe that feeling is just a bias, but it feels like evidence.

So I keep going in circles. Should I treat the intuition as something to debunk (maybe I'm just bad at imagining my own nonexistence and keep smuggling a living spectator into the scene), or should I treat it as a sign the theory leaves out something like the fact that death can deprive you of a whole future even if there's no moment when the deprivation is felt? Or is there a reading where both can be right? Give me the strongest version of each side, then tell me which one you'd give up and what would justify each move. Also, please seperate what's basically settled among philosophers who work on this from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as if it's the consensus.
