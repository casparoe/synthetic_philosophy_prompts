Consider the content-enforcement pipeline now standard at large social media platforms. A small senior team writes the platform's rules, but the volume of cases makes it impossible for senior reviewers to rule on each one. The operation is therefore organized as follows. Automated classifiers are trained on decisions made by human moderators. To make those human decisions consistent and scalable, each hard case is decomposed into a checklist of component questions: whether the post names an individual, whether it makes a threat or merely expresses dislike, whether the target is a public figure, whether a slur is present, whether there is an explicit call to action, and so forth. Moderators answer these sub-questions; a rubric combines the answers into a verdict; and the verdicts become training data for the next round of classifiers. When decisions look inconsistent, the team decomposes further—adding sub-questions, tightening definitions, relabeling examples, and retraining. In technical vocabulary, the system is an industrial version of iterated amplification followed by distillation. It embodies a bet that a holistic-looking judgment—say, whether a post is harassment or legitimate mockery—can be recovered from, or closely approximated by, an aggregation of independently answerable sub-questions, and then distilled into a model that never forms the overall impression an experienced judge would form.

What theory of judgment, competence, and evaluation does this institutional practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguishing what the pipeline strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Which component—the decomposition into a checklist, the rubric that aggregates the answers, or the distillation into a trained classifier—carries the most philosophical weight, and why? Where is the practice underdetermined between rival views, and which of those views would license the same pipeline for different reasons? Finally, suppose the decomposability assumption is false for an important class of cases: the correct verdict on some harassment cases rests on global, context-sensitive recognition that shifts when the case is carved into a checklist. In that event, what would a content-enforcement system have to look like instead, and what would it have to give up?
