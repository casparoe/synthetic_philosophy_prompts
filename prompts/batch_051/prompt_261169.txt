My reading group has spent the last month on markets in things that feel like they shouldn't be for sale, and I keep trying to work out what the person who runs it, Jules, actually beleives. The topic got personal because my cousin's been offered thirty grand for a kidney by a clinic abroad — he's not skint, he's a surveyor who wants the money to start a business, and he's done his reasearch. I'd have brought the question up anyway, but now I feel weirdly invested in guessing how Jules will react.

So far Jules has been dead against selling kidneys: when I pushed her on it she said the sellers are almost always desperate, and it makes her queasy that rich people can just buy parts of the poor. She was also dead against a shop tripling the price of bottled water and batteries just after a storm, on the grounds that the buyers had no real choice. She's against vote selling too — someone in the group mentioned a website that paid registered voters, mostly people behind on rent, twenty quid for their postal vote, and she said absolutely not. But she's completely fine with paid plasma donation (she did it at uni), fine with paid surrogacy, and fine with paying someone to queue overnight for a phone launch, which she says is just paying someone to stand somewhere.

This feels like it should add up to a single rule, but I can't get it to. Next week I'm going to ask her the case I actually care about: a healthy, fully informed person who is not desperate selling a kidney, in a country where it's legal, to fund a startup. Which verdict do you think she'll give? I'm less interested in the verdict itself than the rule she's implicitly running: lay out the different principles that fit her verdicts so far, say what each predicts for my cousin's case, and give your best guess with the reasons for it. Also tell me what further case would cleanly sort out which rule she's really running. And be explicit about what would change your mind — where your reasoning is most likely to come apart if you're wrong about her.
