I sit on the algorithmic review board of a state social-services agency. We must decide whether to approve a six-month pilot of a case-preparation system for administrative appeals. Given a claimant's file and the stated grounds of appeal, the system selects the subset of the file the hearing officer should consider; under the proposed workflow, officers would be instructed to rely on that subset unless they can state a specific reason to look outside it. The vendor's submission claims the design solves the frame problem — roughly, the problem of how an agent can determine which of its stored facts bear on a decision without considering all of them — by combining a fixed relevance function with a bounded retrieval search. One board member argues this is exactly where the frame problem bites: relevance to a fair decision is open-ended, so any finite heuristic will systematically miss facts a human would recognize as pertinent. Another replies that the objection proves too much, since human hearing officers also work from fallible relevance habits and miss facts, while the system outperforms them on the vendor's test corpora. Draft the board's decision memo — recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with an actionable bottom line and an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side.
