Last night I got into an argument with my housemate that went on way to long, and now I'm not sure which of us had the better grasp of things. I'm writing a term paper on whether human rights need the idea of dignity, and he tried to convince me the whole concept should be dropped. His version of the story, as best I can reconstruct it, went like this. Originally dignity was just a status of rank, kings and bishops had it, so it was basically a way of marking hierarchy. Kant then flipped it so every rational being has dignity, meaning a kind of absolute value that can't be traded or replaced, and that's what underwrites the imperative to treat persons as ends and never merely as means. Then a few decades ago some bioethicist in a widely cited BMJ piece argued that dignity is useless in medical ethics: once you actually specify what it's doing, it either just means respect for autonomy, or it's doing no work, or it's being wheeled in to dress up conservative stances on things like cloning. So his conclusion: dignity is either autonomy in fancy dress and redundant, or sanctity-of-life in disguise and crypto-theology; either way it can't ground human rights.

I pushed back with the standard cases, newborns, people with advanced dementia, someone in an unresponsive wakeful state, where autonomy isn't exercised but we still think there are ways you may not treat them. He said no-harm handles those: you don't need dignity to explain why you can't injure them, because a negative duty not to cause suffering is enough. I also tried to explain that my paper wants to treat dignity as a status you have regardless of capacity rather than a scalar thing you can have more or less of, and he said that distinction is exactly the kind of scholastic mess the concept invites. That's where we stalled. Something about his argument felt to quick, especially the slide from the BMJ-style critique to the claim that dignity does no justificatory work for human rights, but I couldn't pin down the move.

So I want an accuracy audit of his summary. Which parts of the history and the bioethics debate would someone sympathetic to dignity actually accept, which are overstated or conflated, and does the conclusion about not grounding rights in dignity survive the accurate parts? I don't just want the name of the paper he was referencing; I want to know exactly where the argument goes wrong or whether my unease is misplaced.
