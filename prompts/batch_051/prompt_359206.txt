I am an economist working in social choice and mechanism design; I work comfortably with preferences over outcomes and over action–outcome pairs, Pareto conditions, externalities, ex ante versus ex post evaluation, and utility representations of rule-following behavior. I keep running across the paradox of deontology in moral philosophy: the difficulty that if a deontologist regards violations of a constraint as bad, it appears they should be willing to commit one violation in order to prevent several further violations of that same constraint, and yet the point of the constraint is precisely that they may not. I cannot tell whether this is a real difficulty for outcome-based evaluation or merely a failure to write the utility function down carefully.

Restate the paradox, and the main responses a deontologist gives, in the vocabulary I actually use. Lay out the precise structure: how a consequentialist objective over outcomes fails to capture a deontological constraint; whether the constraint is best represented as a lexicographic preference, an action-indexed utility, a negative externality attached to the agent's own causal involvement, or something else; and say which representation is most faithful to the deontological position rather than merely the most convenient. Then give me a residue report. Which parts of the philosophers' debate translate cleanly into my framework, which had to be distorted to fit, and which have no expression in it at all? In particular, I want to know whether the agent-relative/agent-neutral distinction is a substantive restriction on admissible utility functions or only a choice of notation, and whether the paradox reveals a defect in outcome-based economics, in the deontological position, or in neither. If the distinction does survive translation, say exactly where it lives in the formalism, for example in the non-separability of utilities, in a restriction on the aggregation of others' violations, or in the failure of an Archimedean or anonymity axiom.
