I am a PhD student in comparative literature, trying to settle what remains defensible in a formalist view of verse translation now that machine translation has started to clear the formal bar. My object is Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot. As I read it, the book's central argument is that the formal fabric of a rhymed and metered poem — its rhyme scheme, its meter, its sound-patterning — is part of what the poem means, so a translation that renders only the propositional content has translated something other than the poem; and further, that recreating that fabric in the target language is an act of sustained inhabitation of the original, inseparable from understanding it and from a kind of authorial empathy. Suppose that argument succeeds on its own terms for human translation. Now take this development: a large language model, trained only on text and with no understanding of the poem and no experience of its sounds, can be prompted to produce in seconds translations of the Marot original that scan and rhyme. Does the argument survive? The question I want answered is not whether the machine versions are good, but whether this development severs the link between form-preservation and understanding that the argument appears to rely on, and therefore whether the argument is intact, in need of repair, or dead. Give me a committed verdict with reasons, not a survey of options. Format: exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion. The five points should be: (1) the premise the development bears on most precisely; (2) your verdict — survives, needs repair, or dead — with the decisive reason; (3) the best repair or, if dead, the strongest replacement claim, and what it concedes; (4) what the development does not touch in the value of preserving form in human verse translation; and (5) the thesis I should now defend in my own work.
