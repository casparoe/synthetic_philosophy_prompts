Most people hold both of the following. Extending a human life is good: a treatment that reliably adds healthy years is wanted, and dying at sixty strikes us as a misfortune even if dying at ninety would be a lesser one. Yet an endless life is not good: it would eventually become tedious and pointless, as the usual reading of Williams's Makropulos case suggests. These judgements appear to say that each additional stretch of life is worth having while the unending series of such stretches is not. Is there one principle that generates both verdicts — something about finitude, categorical desires, or the shape of a whole life — or is one of the two judgements mistaken? Set out the strongest defence of the asymmetry you can, give a verdict on whether it should be kept, revised, or abandoned, identify the particular argument or consideration most likely to change that verdict, and flag the point where your reasoning is least secure.
