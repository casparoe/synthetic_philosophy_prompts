I've been down a rabbit hole about Dennett's four kinds of creatures — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian — and I think I have a fifth stage, and I want someone to kick its tires before I get too proud of it.

The way I understand the ladder, each rung moves the trial-and-error one step further inside. A Darwinian creature lets its genes die. A Skinnerian creature lets actual behaviors die. A Popperian creature lets candidate actions die in its head instead of in the world. A Gregorian creature lets borrowed tools and ideas do a lot of the dying for it — words, methods, checklists, diagrams.

All four still take the scoreboard as fixed though. The standard that decides whether a candidate died well is just handed to them by evolution or conditioning or whatever culture they import it from. My fifth rung is a creature that can edit the scoreboard itself. I've been calling them umpire creatures, because they can fire the umpire.

Example, because I think in examples. I keep killing off career plans because of a rule in my head that says a plan is unserious if my dad wouldn't approve. A Popperian creature can imagine Dad's reaction and kill the plan in advance. A Gregorian creature can borrow the cultural script for what counts as a serious career. But an umpire creature can lift the dad-standard itself onto the workbench, run some trial and error on it, and demote it if it keeps killing good plans. The thing doing the review is itself an umpire, and its also up for review — just not all the way down at once.

So tell me straight: is this a real new rung, or is it just the Popperian move applied to the scoreboard instead of to actions, or the Gregorian move but with borrowed standards? Is there already a name for this that I'd understand? What's the strongest version of the idea, and where does it actually break — is the "you need a scoreboard to edit the scoreboard" thing fatal, or is there a worse problem I'm not seeing? I care more about whether the idea survives than about the name, so rename it if you have to.
