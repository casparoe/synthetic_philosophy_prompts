I keep coming back to the old puzzle about blackmail. The standard setup is that two acts that are each permissible on their own — telling the truth about someone and asking them for money — seem to become criminal when a single person combines them into a conditional demand: pay me or I'll disclose. As a software engineer, I found myself wondering whether the puzzle survives if the conditional demand is replaced by an automated default.

Suppose a person lawfully possesses true, damaging information about several acquaintances. She writes a small deterministic program that, on a fixed schedule, sends each person an offer to buy immediate deletion of their file for a flat fee; if the fee is not paid within thirty days, the program posts the file to a public website and then removes it from her local storage. There is no negotiation, no human involvement at the moment the offer is made, and no communication framed as a threat; the disclosure is built in as the program's default, and payment merely cancels a scheduled event. Stipulate, to isolate the conceptual issue, that both component behaviors are lawful in the relevant jurisdiction: publishing the true facts would be lawful, and charging a fee to delete a file one lawfully holds is lawful.

I'd like a careful walk-through of how the standard ways of dissolving the original puzzle carry over to this automated version. In particular, does the switch from a conditional demand — pay or I'll make disclosure happen — to a standing default — disclosure will happen unless you pay — change the threat/offer analysis, and with it the claim that the combination is morally worse than the sum of its parts? Which of the main rival explanations of ordinary blackmail still make this version come out as a blackmail-equivalent, and which stop applying because the harm is no longer something the person threatens to choose in response to non-payment? Does the fact that the disclosure comes from a schedule rather than from an in-the-moment decision alter the moral assessment, or is that difference superficial? As you work through the rival explanations, separate what is settled about them from what is genuinely contested, and avoid presenting one contested resolution of the original puzzle as the consensus.
