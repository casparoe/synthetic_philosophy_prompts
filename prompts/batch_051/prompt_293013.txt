The question the student was asked:

Explain the replicator/vehicle distinction. Does the gene's-eye view of selection settle the units-of-selection debate by showing that group selection is just a confused way of describing gene-level change, or does group selection remain a legitimate level of description?

The student's answer, copied out:

The main move in the units debate was G. C. Williams's critique of naive group selection. Williams drew the distinction between replicators and vehicles. Replicators are things that copy themselves with enough fidelity to persist over many generations; vehicles are the short-lived containers, like bodies, that replicators build around themselves to interact with the world. For Williams and Dawkins, the gene is the unit of selection because it is the only thing that survives across generations with anything like intact identity, while the organism is dissolved by meiosis and recombination each generation and so cannot accumulate adaptations. Dawkins's selfish-gene talk is a metaphor, but it points at a real fact: alleles that bias their own transmission can spread even when they lower the fitness of the individual carrying them, as with segregation distorters or meiotic drive.

The same reasoning is supposed to dissolve group selection. A gene for group-benefiting self-restraint would be outcompeted within its own group by a selfish rival, because the selfish individual gets the shared benefit without paying the cost. The only way group selection could be effective, Williams argued, is if groups reproduce and go extinct fast enough to override within-group selection, and in practice those conditions are so restrictive that the process is negligible. That is why the modern consensus is that group selection cannot occur: apparent cases like alarm calling or reduced clutch size are actually kin selection, where the bearer's genes benefit through relatives that share copies of them, or they are just individual-level effects in disguise.

So the gene's-eye view rules group selection out as a real process, and the units-of-selection debate is effectively settled in favour of the gene. The only respectable form of higher-level selection is species or clade selection on geological timescales, which is a different question from ordinary microevolution.
