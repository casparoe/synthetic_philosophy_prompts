Here's a position I've drifted into without fully noticing, and I want someone to actually try to break it. The view is the hedonistic imperative in its strong form: the ultimate long-run task of biotechnology should be to phase out the capacity for suffering itself — in humans eventually, but ultimately across sentient life — replacing pain with fine-grained gradients of information-bearing well-being. In other words, don't just treat pain; edit the very possibility of it out of biology. I want you to stress-test this, and not with the usual enginering worries about whether we'd botch a rollout — set those aside. Tell me what would have to be true for the view to be wrong as an ideal, not just reckless as a plan. I can think of a few candidate defeaters myself: that suffering is intrinsically valuable or partly constitutes certain goods; that eliminating it collapses the contrast that gives contentment its meaning; that the capacity to be hurt is required for genuine love, courage, or other virtues; that a life with no possibility of suffering is, in some disguised way, a kind of delusion or a cage; maybe something about humility or accepting the world as given. For each candidate, I want your honest verdict on whether a defender of the view should count it as a fatal objection, a serious cost, an awkward consequence, or a bullet they can bite without wrecking the view. Then rank the defeaters and tell me which one, if you actually believed it, would force you to abandon the hedonistic imperative entirely. And be honest at the end: have I set this position up so that nothing could in principle count against it, because the view just absorbs every objection as a trade-off it's willing to pay — and if so, is that a problem? I want to know whether I'm holding a falsifiable ideal or just a mood with a syllabus.
