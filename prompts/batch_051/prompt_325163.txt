Consider the afterlife conjecture: the view that human extinction would be bad not only because of the deaths and lost future lives it involves, but because much of what we value is invested with its value by the expectation that there will be people after us to receive, continue, and make sense of what we do. On this view, a gradual, painless extinction after a long and contented decline would still be a catastrophe over and above the sum of the individual deaths, because the value of the work, care, and aspiration of everyone who lived would be retroactively diminished. Take the position seriously and ask what would have to be true for it to be false or overstated. Which of its commitments are doing the real work — the retroactive claim about past projects, the claim that the collective badness is independent of the welfare lost by particular future people, the asymmetry between individual mortality and species extinction — and what case, intuition, or argument would a critic have to make against each? Distinguish considerations that would merely force a revision or qualification from those that would force abandoning the central claim; say where a defender of the view should themselves feel pressure; and close with an honest verdict on whether the position is well insulated from refutation, and whether that insulation is a strength or an embarrassment.
