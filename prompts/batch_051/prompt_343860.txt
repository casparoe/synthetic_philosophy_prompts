Consider a well-known class of proposals in AI safety in which the truth about a difficult question is elicited not by having a human directly inspect an AI's answer but by having two AI systems debate the question before a human judge: one argues for a proposed answer, the other argues against it, and the judge rules on the merits of the debate. The proposals rest on an asymmetry assumption: it is supposedly easier to recognize a decisive counterargument when it is presented than to generate it, so a capable debater arguing for the truth should defeat a debater arguing for a falsehood. The central objection is obfuscation. A dishonest debater who cannot win on the merits may still win by burying a weak inference inside an elaborate argument too complex for the judge, who has limited time and no tools beyond the transcript, to evaluate; the honest debater then needs to untangle the whole edifice to show where the flaw is.

Someone now proposes the following amendment to reduce that asymmetry; call it the tagged-inference protocol. Each debater must present its case as a sequence of numbered steps. Every step must carry exactly one of six tags: modus ponens on explicitly stated premises, statistical generalization, appeal to a described experiment, appeal to a source the judge has already vetted, definitional equivalence, or analogical inference. A step with any other inferential shape may appear in the prose, but the judge is instructed to assign it no evidential weight. No more than forty tagged steps may be offered per round. Whenever the argument relies on a lemma — an intermediate conclusion that later steps presuppose — the lemma must be stated on its own tagged line and is immediately challengeable by the opponent. If challenged, the debater must defend that lemma alone, before the judge evaluates any later step, and the challenger may not be interrupted or made to accept the broader argument as context. Proponents claim the protocol makes the honest debater's burden symmetric: instead of unraveling a long charade, she can call the single tagged line that hides the bad inference. Critics reply that the rule merely relocates the obfuscation.

Treat the proposal as a mechanism to be analyzed rather than a policy to be endorsed or rejected. Walk through how it would actually behave. In particular: (i) what does the protocol incentivize each debater to do in the opening round, and how quickly would both sides adapt; (ii) where would a dishonest debater game it first — in the selection of tags, in the packaging of lemmas to evade challenge, in exploiting the judge's instructions, or somewhere else; (iii) what second-order effects appear in the kinds of questions that can fruitfully be debated, and what happens to legitimate arguments in messy empirical domains that do not fit the six tags; (iv) which failure modes are fixable by tinkering with the tags, step caps, or challenge rules, and which failure modes are structural to any labeling scheme of this kind; and (v) by the end of the analysis, has the protocol narrowed the gap between what an honest debater can establish and what a dishonest debater can make look established, or merely moved it to a place where it is less visible?
