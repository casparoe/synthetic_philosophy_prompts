I've spent the last couple of weeks trying to work out whether my city should start treating the World Happiness Report's ranking as an actual policy target, and my housemate Mara has become my unofficial referee. She did three years in a wellbeing policy unit and has a pile of oddly specific verdicts about countries that tried something with the ranking: a ministry wanting to nudge people to rate their lives higher before the next Gallup wave - no, that's gaming; a country whose post-recession recovery pushed its ladder score up half a point and its enjoyment measures up along with it - yes, that's the real thing; a top-three country quietly making itself unavailable to Gallup because the label 'happiest people on earth' annoyed it - no, childish; a govenment repackaging six existing programmes as a 'happiness strategy' with no new money - no, that's just branding; a country that put real money into community centres and mental-health outreach and saw its social-support and ladder scores rise together - yes, with a caveat about sustainability; and a city that built a wellbeing dashboard on the six variables but refused to set a target for the rank itself - she liked that one.

Whenever I ask her what principle is doing all the work, she reads me the same three sentences from the World Happiness Report's own FAQ page, under 'Why do you rank countries using life evaluations rather than emotions or mental states?':

"Life evaluations capture the quality of life in a more complete and stable way than emotional reports based on daily experiences. Life evaluations vary more between countries and they are better explained by the diverse life experiences of people in different countries. Emotions are well explained by events of the day being asked about, while life evaluations closely reflect the circumstances of life as a whole."

The case I actually need to settle is a new one: a country's ladder score rises 0.3 points over a decade of genuinely better conditions (cleaner air, more secure jobs, better public transport, less corruption), and people clearly rate their lives higher when asked, but its positive- and negative-affect numbers don't move at all over the same period. Should that country keep trying to top the table?
