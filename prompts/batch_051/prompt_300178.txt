I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and I am working on a piece about decision paralysis: the way adding seemingly neutral options or fine-grained comparisons can leave people, and increasingly automated systems, unable to act. I want to anchor part of the piece in a precise variation on Buridan's ass, and I need the analysis worked through rather than the usual hand-waving.

Take the standard setup, but make it explicit. An ass faces two equally accessible bales of hay, distinct in location but identical in every property the ass values. Each bale has utility 1 for the ass, and eating nothing has utility 0. The ass maximizes expected utility and has no preference between the bales. Now add a variation: the ass has a fair coin and a binding precommitment device. Flipping the coin is costless, the commitment cannot be undone, and at the start the ass may bind itself to follow the coin's result.

Work through the following carefully rather than narrating the old puzzle. First, what does expected utility theory say the ass's permissible options are, and does it ever predict starvation? Second, add the premise often imputed to the original puzzle, that an agent may act only on a sufficient reason to prefer one alternative over the others. Does the coin's landing supply that reason? Is the fact that the coin landed left a reason to choose the left bale if the ass still does not prefer the left bale? Third, is the earlier act of precommitting to follow the coin itself a Buridan case, or is it straightforwardly rational, and why? Fourth, does this variation dissolve the original puzzle, and if so, which element of the original was doing the work: the absence of preference, or a hidden ban on arbitrary but non-preferential selection? Fifth, consider a second variation: one bale is infinitesimally closer, but proximity is not a property the ass values. Does a non-valued difference supply a selecting reason? If your answer treats such differences as reasons, say where the line falls between a selecting reason and a rationalization; if your answer does not, say how any non-preferential tie-breaker could ever count as rational.

Finally, state which premise or distinction breaks first, tell me where your reasoning about it is most likely to fail, and tell me which argument or consideration would most likely change your mind about that.
