For a commentary I am writing on the place of causation in the philosophy of science, I have been trying to settle how much of Bertrand Russell's eliminativist argument still stands. As I understand it, Russell maintained that the notion of cause has no proper place in mature science: physics states functional laws and differential equations, and the word 'cause' is a vague, anthropomorphic survivor from ordinary thought. He recommended that philosophers and scientists do without it.

The development that interests me is the rise, from roughly the mid-twentieth century onward, of a rigorous mathematical treatment of causation: structural equation models, causal Bayesian networks, the do-calculus and its relatives, now standard in epidemiology, economics, and parts of machine learning. In these frameworks 'cause' is not a piece of folk psychology; it is given precise semantics, conditions for identifiability, and algorithms for inference from observational and experimental data. This looks like exactly the sort of thing Russell claimed mature science neither needs nor possesses.

I would like a committed verdict, not a survey. Does Russell's argument survive this development? Specifically, does it still license the conclusion that causation is not fundamental, that it is reducible to non-causal structure, or that it should be eliminated? Or does the success of these formal theories show that Russell's target was only a loose everyday notion that no serious philosopher of causation needed to defend? Identify the premise of Russell's argument that the new formal apparatus puts under pressure, and either defend the argument in a suitably repaired form or explain why no repair succeeds. If your answer depends on choosing among different things one might mean by 'fundamental' or 'reducible', acknowledge that the choice is contested but then commit to the reading you regard as strongest and argue from that reading.
