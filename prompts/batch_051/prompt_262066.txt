In a legal epistemology seminar I have been taking, the tutor has used a series of problem cases to work out a position on when statistical evidence may support a legal finding. Her rulings so far are as follows. In the blue bus problem, a pedestrian was knocked down by a bus on a route on which the defendant company ran 80% of the services, but the pedestrian could not see any livery. The tutor dismissed the claim, holding that the claimant had not shown that this company's bus was involved. In the gatecrasher problem, it was proved that 1,000 people entered a paid enclosure, that only 499 had bought tickets, and that the defendant was simply one entrant selected at random; the probability that he had not paid was therefore just over one half. The tutor dismissed the claim. In the prison-yard problem, 99 of 100 prisoners in a yard attacked a guard, the guard could not identify the single prisoner who held back, and the prosecution selected a prisoner at random. The tutor held that the accused must be acquitted, notwithstanding the 99% probability that he took part. In the generic-drug problem, a claimant became ill after taking a drug manufactured by one of five companies, each with a known share of the market; she could not trace her own supplies to any one company, so she sued all five. The tutor held that each company should pay damages in proportion to its market share.

The problem to be decided next is this. A cyclist is struck by a van on a road on which only two delivery companies operate vehicles: Flare Logistics operates 90% of the vehicles on that road and Quill Couriers the remaining 10%. The cyclist cannot say whose van struck him. He does not seek a ruling that Flare's van was involved, nor does he seek his full loss from Flare; he sues Flare alone and asks for 90% of his damages, matching Flare's statistical share.

What should I expect the tutor to rule? I also want to know which principle she is implicitly applying, which rival principles fit her earlier rulings just as well, what each rival would predict for the cyclist problem, and which further problem would best discriminate among them. In giving the ruling, say which of her earlier cases is the closest analogue and why.

The five points should cover: (1) the ruling you expect, with the closest analogue; (2) the principle she is most plausibly applying; (3) the rival principles that fit her record equally well; (4) what each rival predicts for the cyclist problem; and (5) the further problem that would best tell the rivals apart.
