I have been reading a general-audience survey of Indian philosophy, and its chapter on Nyāya leaves me with the following picture of the school's epistemology, which I would like checked before I rely on it. According to the book, the Naiyāyikas claim that there are exactly four pramāṇas — valid sources of knowledge — namely perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna), and verbal testimony (śabda). Perception is simply the direct contact between a sense organ and its object, and it is reliable as long as the organ is not defective. Inference is essentially deduction: once one is in possession of a universal rule to the effect that wherever there is smoke there is fire, observing smoke on a distant hill allows one to conclude with certainty that the hill is on fire; the universal rule has itself been established by earlier perception, so nothing further remains to be worried about. Comparison is the means by which one learns the reference of an unfamiliar word: a forester tells you that a gavaya is similar to a cow, and when you later meet such an animal in the forest you know that it is what 'gavaya' refers to. Testimony consists in accepting the word of a trustworthy authority, and in Nyāya its main practical employment is religious, consisting above all in deference to the Veda and other authoritative pronouncements. The author then draws a broad moral: because Nyāya admits only these four channels of knowledge, it deliberately excludes memory, doubt, and any form of fallibilism — a genuine pramāṇa cannot produce a false cognition, so once one is genuinely using a pramāṇa, one has certainty and the question is closed.

I would like a sentence-by-sentence audit of this summary: which parts would a specialist in classical Indian epistemology accept, which parts would they want to qualify, what would a defender of the Nyāya view object to, and which parts are simply wrong? I am especially interested in whether the closing moral about excluding memory and fallibilism actually follows from a corrected version of the earlier claims, or whether it rides on one of the simplifications. My own prior is that the closing line sounds plausible, but the claim that Nyāya's testimony is chiefly a matter of religious deference already strikes me as suspicious.
