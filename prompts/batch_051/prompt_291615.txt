I work on the reliability team for a distributed platform, and in incident reviews the question of when two anomalies have the same underlying mechanism keeps coming up. It is not a purely theoretical matter: it determines whether two alerts should be deduplicated, whether a single postmortem can cover two incidents, and whether a fix that worked in one place should be expected to transfer to another. I have been collecting my own verdicts on a set of cases, and I would like to know whether there is a coherent principle behind them.

(1) Services A and B both call the same header-parsing function from a shared library that has an off-by-one error; in A this throws an out-of-bounds exception and produces 500s, while in B it silently truncates the final header and corrupts telemetry. I call these the same mechanism, despite the different symptoms. (2) Services C and D both show p99 latency spikes after a release. C's spike comes from a regex with catastrophic backtracking, and D's comes from a thread pool that grows without bound under concurrency; there is no shared code. I call these different mechanisms, despite the identical symptom. (3) Services E and F both fail with out-of-memory errors. E has a cache that never evicts entries, while F has a background worker that accumulates parsed records and never flushes them: no shared code, but the failure mode and the fix, adding a bound, are nearly identical. I keep going back and forth, and if forced I lean different mechanisms, but that feels inconsistent with my willingness to write essentially the same postmortem for both. (4) An expired intermediate certificate used by two unrelated services, G and H, makes G's outbound calls time out and makes H's calls fail fast with 401s. I call these the same mechanism, even though the downstream paths are unrelated. (5) A network partition triggers retry storms in both I and J. I's storm comes from a client library whose backoff is capped at 10 ms; J's storm comes from a business-logic loop that immediately re-enqueues failed jobs. I call these different mechanisms, even though both trace back to the same partition.

My working rule so far has been that two anomalies share a mechanism when their causal histories pass through one and the same faulty component, so that repairing that single component would have prevented both. That rule fits the five cases as I have described them, but I suspect it is close to a re-description of my gut rather than a real principle, and I expect it to strain once I look at the edges. I would like you to test that rule against my cases, repair or replace it as needed, and state the resulting principle precisely. Be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, the repaired principle cannot accommodate. Then apply it to a case I have not settled: the same memory leak recurs in service E two weeks later, after a deployment accidentally reverts the fix. Is that the same mechanism as the original leak, or a fresh instance of the same pattern, and does the distinction matter for how we should track the recurrence?
