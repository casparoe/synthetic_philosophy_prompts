The government of a federal republic is weighing a constitutional amendment that would convert each of its twelve provinces into a chartered polity. At conversion, every adult resident would receive one transferable charter share in their province. A share would carry three rights: a vote in elections for the governor and provincial legislature; an annual claim on a per-capita portion of the province's net revenues from minerals, water sales, and long-term land leases; and the right to be sold, gifted, or pledged as collateral to any person, firm, or foreign government. Non-shareholders who live in the province would continue to pay its sales, property, and payroll taxes and would be entitled to the same public services as shareholders, but they would have no vote in provincial elections and no dividend. The national government would keep the army, the currency, and a constitutional court whose judges are appointed for fixed, nonrenewable terms; the court could revoke a province's charter for armed rebellion, attempted secession, or a sustained pattern of denying basic legal protections to non-shareholders, and revocation would extinguish all shares without compensation. Provincial legislatures elected by the shareholders would set local tax rates, contract with private providers for courts, policing, and jails, and borrow on bond markets against future resource revenues.

The amendment's defenders say it only makes explicit what states already are, since territorial governments are already residual claimants on local revenues with a franchise tied to residence; its critics say it fuses sovereignty and profit in the manner of the old chartered trading companies, with the East India Company as the standing warning. Treat this as a mechanism to be analyzed rather than a position to endorse or condemn. Walk through how it would actually operate: who accumulates shares and control in the first decade and through what channels; what becomes of the median resident who sells their share; how firms, foreign governments, creditors, and non-resident shareholders would act; where the system is gamed first; and what second-order effects appear in taxation, public services, and the meaning of residence. Then say whether the system would, in equilibrium, drift back toward ordinary territorial democracy, toward company rule, or toward something distinct, and identify which provisions most determine that outcome.
