I am a retired physicist who has recently taken up philosophy, and I have become absorbed by the dispute between accuracy-first epistemology and epistemic value pluralism. I would like you to draft the document that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between those two camps should produce: a joint statement that a committed accuracy-first epistemologist and a committed epistemic value pluralist could both sign, with the residual disagreement stated exactly. The document should be self-contained and should not cite the literature or name authors; the content must do the work.

Begin with a numbered section titled Claims both parties accept. These should be nontrivial. In particular, specify what the two sides can jointly sign about the formal apparatus—scoring rules, strict propriety, accuracy dominance, and Probabilism—and about which verdicts that apparatus already fixes. Also state what they can jointly sign about accuracy being one genuine epistemic good, without smuggling in the claim that it is the only one.

Then provide a section titled Where they part ways. State each side's central claim in wording its own proponents would accept. I take the accuracy-first claim to be roughly that every epistemic norm, and every epistemic defect in a doxastic state, must be traceable to the state's accuracy profile, so that no two states with identical accuracy profiles can differ in epistemic status. The pluralist claim is correspondingly that at least one pair of doxastic states coincide in every accuracy-relevant respect yet differ in epistemic status, because at least one further feature—evidential fit, coherence, understanding, or something in that family—possesses final epistemic value.

Then produce a Cruxes section: the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side. For each crux, include a concrete case or argument that both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. I expect at least the following three cruxes to need treatment, though you may add or replace them if the drafting requires it.

The first crux concerns the normative force of the formal results: whether a theorem about scoring rules is by itself a reason for a rational agent to revise her credences, or whether an additional bridge principle is needed. A concrete bearing might be an agent who learns that her credences are accuracy-dominated by another assignment under every strictly proper scoring rule and then asks why that fact should move her; each side should predict whether that question is well-posed and what a satisfactory answer must include.

The second crux concerns ties and incomparability: whether an accuracy-based account leaves distinct doxastic states tied or incommensurable, and whether anything of epistemic significance is lost when it does. A concrete bearing might be two agents with the same total evidence who weigh that evidence differently while neither is accuracy-dominated by the other.

The third crux concerns understanding: a case in which two agents hold the same true beliefs with the same credences about a domain, but only one grasps its explanatory structure. Each side should predict whether the difference between them is an epistemic difference, and if so, whether it must be explained in terms of accuracy or in other terms.

Conclude with a section titled Is any part of the dispute verbal?, in which you say which apparent disagreements dissolve once the central terms—epistemic value, final value, and identity of accuracy profile—are made precise, and which do not.
