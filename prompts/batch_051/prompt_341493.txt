I'm a junior helping to run my school's forecasting club, where members put a number between 0 and 1 on campus propositions — whether the referendum passes, whether the volleyball team wins on Friday, whether the fire drill comes before lunch — and we score each forecast by squaring the difference between the number and what actually happened, counting a true outcome as 1 and a false one as 0. On the spreadsheet, a forecast of 0.92 on something that does not happen and a forecast of 0.08 on something that does happen come out as exactly the same error: each is 0.92 from the outcome it received, so each gets the same penalty. But the club's unofficial judgments are not the same at all. Last term, Marisa put 0.92 on a student referendum passing and it failed, and she spent weeks being called overconfident. Jonah put 0.08 on the same proposition and it passed, and his forecast was shrugged off as a long shot that happened to come in — maybe even proof that he had been sensibly cautious. Yet Jonah was exactly as confident as Marisa, only in the opposite conclusion (0.08 to passing is 0.92 to failing), and the available evidence before the vote made the referendum a genuine toss-up, so their forecasts were equally far from what the evidence supported.

I want to know whether anything can justify treating Marisa and Jonah differently on purely epistemic grounds, with downstream consequences — who acted on the forecast, who lost anything — set aside. I can think of several candidate justifications: overconfidence is a vice while low confidence is a virtue of intellectual humility; only confidence attached to the positively worded proposition gets read as confidence; our spreadsheet overlooks some dimension of good forecasting; one direction of error is inherently more damaging than the other even when its accuracy cost is the same. But each time I try to push one of these through the mirror-image numbers, it either restates the asymmetry or gives different answers if I relabel which direction is the "salient" one. I would like the strongest version of each candidate, stress-tested against nearby cases (for example, a forecaster who says 0.50 when the evidence clearly supports 0.90, or one who says 0.99 when the evidence supports only 0.60), and an honest verdict on whether the club's practice of blaming Marisa more than Jonah should be kept, reversed, refined, or given up.
