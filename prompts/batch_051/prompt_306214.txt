I lead a six-person engineering team at a mid-size software company, and I keep seeing the same two failure modes. Sometimes we act on the first plausible reading of a bug report, the first workable API shape, or the first diagnosis of a flaky test, without ever surfacing the assumption our confidence is resting on. Other times we get stuck in reflection loops: a design discussion that should take thirty minutes runs for two hours, a postmortem turns into people relitigating their own decisions without producing a single action item, and a senior engineer wants to reopen an architecture decision that was closed three weeks ago because he now has a bad feeling about it. I have no formal philosophy background, but I have picked up scattered ideas — that checking your own reasoning can improve accuracy, that directing attention at a skill you normally run automatically can make performance worse, and that reflection can curdle into rumination that feels like work while changing nothing — and I cannot turn them into a rule I can actually run. I also suspect that thinking about our own thinking is not one thing: a quick check that we know what we claim to know, a deliberate reconstruction of the reasoning that produced a decision, and an open-ended revisiting of a decision that has already been made might need different rules.

I would like a reusable decision procedure, in the form of a checklist or a small ordered set of questions, that I can use several times a week in a few minutes per decision, to decide when my team should deliberately re-examine its own reasoning and when it should stop reflecting and act. For each question, say what counts as a good enough answer to proceed, and mark the steps that are judgment calls rather than mechanical tests. I have to be able to explain the decision to people who would rather keep talking, but I also have to live with the bugs from decisions we did not examine. Then apply the procedure to three situations from my actual week and commit to a definite recommendation in each, including at least one where the procedure tells me to do something I will probably resist: (1) the senior engineer wants to reopen the settled database-schema decision on the grounds that the team underweighted a migration risk; (2) a junior engineer has shipped two bugs in a row with the same root cause, and we are deciding whether the fix is better personal reflection habits or a pre-commit checklist; and (3) a production postmortem has been running for over an hour and keeps generating more questions about what people were thinking, with no action items. I would much rather have a committed line — where the procedure says stop, reopen, or add a check — than a balanced survey of considerations, so take a definite position and defend it, and tell me where the procedure is honestly arbitrary or value-laden.
