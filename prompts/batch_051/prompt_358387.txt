Question:
Pearl's causal hierarchy is standardly described as having three rungs. Using a concrete example of your own, explain what each rung can express and what it cannot. Then (i) describe what a structural causal model consists in and what assumptions are standardly made when one is used; (ii) explain what the do-calculus is for and how it relates to the hierarchy; and (iii) discuss whether the do-calculus is complete, and if so in what sense.

Student's answer:
A structural causal model is a set of equations v_i = f_i(pa_i, u_i), one for each observed variable, together with a directed graph whose edges run from direct causes to their effects. The standard assumptions are that the graph is acyclic, that the error terms are mutually independent, and that there are no unmeasured common causes. An intervention on X replaces the equation for X with X = x and leaves all the other equations unchanged; this is what modularity means.

The three rungs are as follows. Association, rung 1, gives conditional distributions such as P(cancer | yellow fingers). In my example, a common cause—smoking—makes yellow fingers predictive of cancer even though yellow fingers does not cause cancer. Intervention, rung 2, gives P(cancer | do(yellow fingers)); this equals the baseline cancer rate, because yellow fingers is a downstream effect and the do-operator severs the edge smoking → yellow fingers. Counterfactuals, rung 3, answer retrospective questions such as: for a person who smoked and developed cancer, would they have developed cancer had they not smoked? This is written P(cancer_{S=0} | S=1, C=1).

Rung 3 is just rung 2 plus conditioning on evidence, so the do-calculus handles both alike. Its three rules rewrite do-expressions into expressions involving only the observed joint distribution, and because the calculus is complete, every query that is identifiable from the graph can be reduced this way. The upshot is that once the graph is correctly specified, a structural causal model lets you compute rung-2 and rung-3 quantities from observational data alone.
