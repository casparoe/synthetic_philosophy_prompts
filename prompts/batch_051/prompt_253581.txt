I am a novelist. My working understanding of language is practical and technical: I deal in scenes and summaries, dialogue beats, subtext, point of view, free indirect style, unreliable narration, genre conventions, and the way a reader takes up or refuses a line. I can talk about these fluently. What I do not command is the vocabulary of speech act theory, and I want it translated into mine rather than merely explained.

Please restate speech act theory in the vocabulary of narrative craft. The position to translate is roughly this: Austin argued that many utterances do not describe the world but perform an act — promising, apologizing, christening — and that such performatives are not true or false but felicitous or infelicitous; he later folded the distinction between performatives and constatives into a general account of locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary acts. Searle then systematized the felicity conditions and classified illocutionary acts. Translate each of these elements into terms I use daily: a line of dialogue as an action within a scene; subtext as the gap between the literal content of a line and what it accomplishes; genre conventions as standing conditions that make certain utterances count; point of view and free indirect style as ways of assigning, blending, or dissolving the source of an utterance; and reader uptake as the response a line is built to produce.

Then give me the residue report. I want to know which parts of the theory map cleanly onto my craft vocabulary, which parts I would have to distort to make fit, and which parts have no counterpart in how I think about prose at all. I especially want you to test the places where fiction strains the theory: a sentence in free indirect style may have no determinate speaker; an unreliable narrator's assertion is neither simply true nor simply infelicitous; a declaration may succeed within the story and fail for the reader, or the reverse; and the author is performing a second-order speech act on the reader through the characters' first-order speech acts. Is the residue a sign that my vocabulary is missing distinctions I need, or that speech act theory is built for ordinary talk and quietly assumes a single speaker and a single audience? Be as precise as you can about what did not survive the translation.
