I have been trying to pin down what it would mean for the value of an experience to be the product of how conscious it is and how good it feels. Let C be the richness of the consciousness involved — how much the experience is like something, from near-zero up to a fully awake human being's — and let H be its felt tone, from intensely unpleasant through neutral to intensely pleasant. The question is which rule combines them. An additive rule lets C and H trade off at close to constant rates, so a large enough H could in principle make up for a very small C. A multiplicative rule makes the value collapse to zero when either factor is near zero and makes compensation proportional rather than linear. A lexical rule gives one factor, usually C, strict priority, so that no amount of the other factor can buy back a shortfall once the priority factor is set. I trust my spontaneous judgments on the cases below more than I trust any of the three rules, but I also know spontaneous judgments are exactly where scope insensitivity and salience effects live, so I want the pattern pressure-tested. (For now I am comparing experiences of fixed duration in distinct subjects; I do not want intrapersonal aggregation in the picture.)

Here are the judgments. If a chemical leak threatened a pond of several hundred carp, each of which would spend hours in moderate pain, and the only available outlet would give one human a headache of roughly equal felt intensity for the same stretch, I would reroute the leak; the carp win. But given a choice between giving one cow an afternoon of genuine bovine bliss and giving one human an afternoon of mild, unremarkable contentment, I choose the human. I would not cross to the far side of a pond to relieve a sea slug whose nervous system is, as best I can tell, in its maximally aversive state, and I feel almost nothing about it. Yet I would accept one human's single pinprick to save a single carp from an hour of crushing agony. And if a deeply anesthetized human with, as far as I can tell, no moment-to-moment experience at all shows an intense nociceptive response — a pain-signal reflex — to a surgical probe, that response gets no weight from me. The pair I cannot reconcile is the blissful cow and the carp in agony: both compare an intense state in a less richly conscious subject with a trivial state in a human, and they push in opposite directions — in the pleasant case the human wins, in the painful case the carp wins.

Is there one combination rule — or a small set of them, with an explicit trigger stating when each applies — that captures all of these, and what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: preventing ten thousand sea slugs' maximally aversive states at the cost of one human's five minutes of boredom? If no clean rule gets all of my judgments, tell me which ones it requires me to overturn and why. Please also say which feature of my pattern is doing the heavy lifting if the verdicts can only be saved by adding epicycles; I would rather accept a simple rule that overturns one of my cases than a patchy one that preserves them all.
