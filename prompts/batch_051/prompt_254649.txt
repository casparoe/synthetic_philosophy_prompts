I've been turning over the old Malcolm–Dennett line about whether dreams are experiences, and I can't figure out whether a relatively new experimental finding settles it or just moves the bump. Malcolm's argument, as I understand it, is that our only criterion for saying someone dreamed is the story they tell after waking up; there's no way to catch the dream in progress, so it's a mistake to model dreaming as a temporally extended stream of inner experiences occurring during sleep. Dennett adds a different version of the worry: the vivid narrative at waking doesn't have to correspond to any real-time sequence inside the sleeper, because the whole 'experience' could be confabulated at the moment of report. What both seem to lean on is the absence of concurrent evidence from inside sleep. But now there are decades of studies in which lucid dreamers signal with pre-agreed eye movements during REM sleep, and in the last few years a handful of studies where they've answered yes/no questions and done simple arithmetic with those signals while the sleep recording shows them still asleep — afterward they say the questions arrived inside the dream, as a voice from a radio or a text on a screen. That looks like precisely the concurrent check Malcolm said was impossible.

So my question: does the Malcolm argument actually survive this development, and if it survives only in some weakened form, which premise gets repaired? Does the real-time signaling show that there is something it's like to dream while you're dreaming, or does it just move the report one notch earlier — the eye movements are still something we decode after the fact, and the subject could still be confabulating a dream around signals the body produced? I keep going back and forth on wether this is a refutation, a forced repair, or a change of subject, and I'd like a careful answer rather than the usual 'lucid dreaming obviously refutes Malcolm' move.
