For a working group on comparative development, I've been asked to write out my considered view of why some nations are rich and others poor, so that the rest of the group can stress-test it before I circulate it more widely. The position I've converged on is this: (1) The dominant proximate cause of long-run differences in national prosperity is institutional—secure property rights, credible limits on rulers, impartial courts and administration, and predictable legal systems—rather than geography or culture acting as ongoing direct causes of wealth and poverty. (2) The strongest evidence for this thesis comes from settings where geography and deep cultural background are held roughly constant while institutions diverge sharply, as in the post-1945 division of the Korean peninsula, with incomes subsequently diverging along with institutions. (3) Cross-country growth regressions are, by contrast, mostly uninformative on these matters, because institutions, culture, and geography are so entangled that no credible identification can be wrung out of them. (4) Institutions are not exogenous: they emerged through historical processes in which geography—disease environment, terrain, crop suitability—and culture—norms of trust, kinship, and authority—played large roles. Geography and culture therefore matter causally, but mostly as deep historical determinants of institutions rather than as ongoing direct constraints. (5) I remain skeptical of cultural explanations that treat a national culture as an independent, persistent cause of present-day poverty; I suspect that most measured culture effects on development are either downstream of incentives and institutions or artifacts of trying to control for institutions while culture remains entangled with them. (6) I also think that some culturally transmitted traits can outlive the institutional environment that produced them—norms formed under extractive colonial or authoritarian rule can persist for generations after the formal rules change. (7) Methodologically, I prefer discontinuity-based and quasi-experimental evidence in development and economic history, and I am suspicious of grand cross-country comparisons and long-run correlational narratives. (8) At the same time, I hold that we can make real progress on the largest questions—for instance, why the Industrial Revolution emerged in Britain rather than the Yangtze delta, and why the Great Divergence between northwestern Europe and East Asia opened up when it did.

I'd like an audit of this package. Which commitments are genuinely in tension with one another, and which pairs only appear to be so? For each genuine tension, rank it by severity, say what the conflict consists in, and identify the least costly revision that would resolve it. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set hang together, and what that revision would cost.
