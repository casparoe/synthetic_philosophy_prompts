A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether a person with psychopathy can know that what he does is wrong, and we kept talking past each other because we had not separated different things one might mean by moral knowledge. I want to bring that unresolved argument to a work of fiction. Read Patricia Highsmith's The Talented Mr. Ripley — or Anthony Minghella's film adaptation, if you know it better — through the philosophy of moral knowledge. Tom Ripley is often described as a conscience-less or psychopathic character, yet he is acutely aware of social rules and of how others judge him. Does Ripley know that murdering Dickie Greenleaf and Freddie Miles is wrong? Please distinguish at least three candidate senses in which someone might be said to know this: (a) being able to state the relevant moral rule and predict others' disapproval; (b) judging that the rule applies to his own actions; and (c) having the affective or experiential grasp of wrongness that some philosophers think moral knowledge requires. Then use particular scenes, decisions, or patterns of attention in the work to say which of these Ripley has and which, if any, he lacks. I am especially interested in whether his anxiety, his constant management of appearances, and his strange attachment to Dickie complicate the idea that he simply lacks the relevant knowledge. Where does the work resist the philosophical reading, and where does it make the distinction itself look too coarse? I want a reading that is faithful both to the novel or film and to the philosophical dispute, and I am less interested in a clinical diagnosis of Ripley than in what the work shows about what it could mean to know that something is wrong.
