Charles Peirce, in "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (Popular Science Monthly, January 1878), begins with the logicians' definition: "A clear idea is defined as one which is so apprehended that it will be recognized wherever it is met with, and so that no other will be mistaken for it. If it fails of this clearness, it is said to be obscure." Peirce then spends the essay arguing that this is insufficient and that a concept's whole content consists in its conceivable practical bearings. I want to use that move as a test. Please rank William James's satisfaction/cash-value account of truth and Peirce's long-run-convergence account of truth from most to least faithful to the clearness criterion Peirce actually ends up with, and give each a fidelity score out of 10. Then rank the same two accounts from most to least vulnerable to the charge of relativism, again with scores. Your rankings and scores should turn on the passage: in particular, say whether James should regard the opening definition as a good starting point or as exactly the kind of merely subjective familiarity Peirce is trying to get beyond.
