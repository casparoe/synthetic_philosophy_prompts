I've been arguing with myself (and occasionally with the poor grad student who offices next to me) about how to teach the simple conditional analysis of dispositions, and I'd rather turn it into a handout than keep redoing the whiteboard. The simple conditional analysis says x is disposed to M just in case, if x were in stimulus conditions S, x would M. A glass is fragile because if it were dropped it would break; a sugar cube is soluble because if it were put in water it would dissolve. Now here's the assignment I'm giving you. Rank the following three standard counterexample styles from most damaging to least damaging to that analysis: (i) finks, where the object loses the disposition as soon as the stimulus arrives (a live wire wired to a safety switch that makes it dead when touched, so it wouldn't shock you, but it's still live); (ii) masks, where the disposition is still there but something blocks the usual manifestation (a fragile glass packed in a styrofoam crate, so if it were struck it wouldn't break, but it's still fragile); and (iii) mimics, where the thing isn't actually disposed to M but would still do M in S because of some outside interference. For each item, tie the rank to the kind of failure it exposes: a case where the disposition is present but the conditional comes out false (so the conditional isn't necessary), a case where the conditional comes out true but the disposition is absent (so it isn't sufficient), or something subtler. Then, for the one you rank most damaging, say which repair you'd try first: adding a ceteris paribus clause, holding fixed the thing's intrinsic causal basis and running the conditional on that, or abandoning a conditional analysis of dispositions altogether. I don't care about the official literature for this, just a clear, opinionated ranking and reasons - and if you think two of the three are really the same problem wearing different hats, just say so, because that's my own sneaking suspicion.
