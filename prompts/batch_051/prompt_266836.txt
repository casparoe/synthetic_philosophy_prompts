As a retired natural scientist who has recently started reading philosophy, especially decision theory, I have encountered a recurring pattern of reasoning that I cannot tell whether to file under a real principle or under a formalized mistake, and I would welcome the judgment of a professional philosopher. Suppose you must play a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a partner you know to be your exact duplicate in every respect relevant to the choice: same preferences, same information, same principles of reasoning, no possibility of communication, and no enforcement. The standard analysis tells you to defect, since your choice cannot cause the other player's choice. Yet from the inside it seems the two of you cannot choose differently: whatever chain of reasoning leads you to cooperate will, being identical, lead them to cooperate as well, and likewise for defection. On that view cooperating is simply picking the better of the two available joint outcomes and defecting is picking the worse. I have seen this move dismissed as magical thinking, and I have also seen it presented as the formal skeleton of the old moral idea that one should act only on principles one could will to be universal law. Is there an established name for this kind of reasoning, and is it genuinely a distinct principle rather than a conflation of two or three neighboring ideas? A bare label would not satisfy me. I want to know how the reasoning differs from ordinary team reasoning, from treating one's choice as evidence about what the other will do, and from confusing correlation with control, and where exactly it stops being valid, for example when the game is noisy, when the players' situations are asymmetric, or when the other is merely very similar rather than a strict duplicate. Please think the boundaries through carefully and answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
