I have been working through the problem of mental causation, and I keep getting driven into a corner that I cannot see my way out of. An argument I have reconstructed from the literature runs as follows. (1) Some mental events cause physical events: my wanting to move my hand causes the movement of my hand. (2) The physical domain is causally complete: every physical event that has a cause at a time has a sufficient physical cause at that time. (3) If a mental event causes a physical event that already has a sufficient physical cause, then either the mental event just is that physical cause, or the physical event is causally overdetermined, since it has two distinct sufficient causes, one mental and one physical. (4) It is wildly implausible that every instance of mental causation involves such systematic overdetermination. (5) Therefore either mental events are identical with physical events, or mental events are causally impotent: epiphenomenalism. Each of (1) through (4) seems individually compelling, and the move to (5) looks like a modest dilemma, yet I refuse to accept either horn. Worse, the argument seems to prove too much: run the same reasoning with 'wanting' replaced by 'being solid' or 'being alive', and it appears to show that no higher-level property is ever causally efficacious, which would leave the special sciences causally idle. I cannot tell which step to blame. Which premise or inference should I give up, and what does each of the main escape routes—type identity, a principled embrace of overdetermination, interventionist or difference-making accounts of causation, and any other live option you regard as serious—actually have to pay in order to block the conclusion? Please be precise about the costs, because I have a suspicion that several responses only relocate the problem rather than dissolve it. The answer should be in British English.
