Here's a dispute I keep having and I don't know how to settle. A colleague insists that thought experiments in science never yield knowledge on their own—they are just vivid ways of re-arranging premises, and the only real epistemic work happens later, when somebody does an actual experiment or works through the mathematics. He'll happily admit that Galileo's falling bodies, Maxwell's demon, and Einstein's elevator refocused the field, but he says refocusing is persuasion, not new information about the world. I have the opposite instinct, but I can't say what observation would tell us apart, and I worry my instinct is just romanticism about dramatic examples. So: how would you test a conceptual claim like his? What would a good test look like—something like comparing what scientists do after being exposed to a canonical thought experiment versus after seeing the same claim stated as a bare formal result, or looking historically at whether a field's research priorities change more after a thought experiment than after a matched failed experiment? Design the strongest test you can, say exactly what result would count for and against his claim, and then be honest about what the test leaves untouched.
