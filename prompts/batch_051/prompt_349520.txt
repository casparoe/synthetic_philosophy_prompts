I recently joined a secular Buddhist study and meditation group after a period of upheaval that left me unsure how to think about who I am, and the teacher there repeatedly says "the self is an illusion." She uses the phrase to do quite different kinds of work: when someone describes anxiety about aging or professional failure, she says there is no fixed self for the loss to happen to; when I spoke about grieving my father, she said that both the person I am mourning and the person doing the mourning are changing processes, and that my grief rests on reifying something that was never there. The same one-liner is being asked to support conclusions about the fear of death, the rationality of grief, and everyday anxiety, and I cannot tell whether it is precise enough to bear any of that weight.

I would like you to treat this as an exercise in disambiguation rather than advocacy. Please lay out the distinct precise claims that "the self is an illusion" might express — for instance, that there is no permanent, unchanging core; that there is no single inner controller or owner of experiences; that the sense of self is a constructed narrative rather than a discovered fact; that the self is unreal in the way a mirage or a mistaken inference is, as opposed to being a real process that is habitually misdescribed. For each candidate claim, say whether it is a platitude many non-Buddhist philosophers could accept, a substantive and defensible position, or something false or overstated, and say what it would actually entail about personal identity over time, about agency and responsibility, and about the appropriateness of grief. Then tell me which of these readings the teacher's specific uses require, and whether the most defensible readings are the ones doing the work she wants them to do. I am not asking for a scholarly survey; I want the conceptual terrain laid out clearly enough that I can see what I could and could not consistently say.
