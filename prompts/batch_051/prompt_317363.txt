I've been trying to square two things in Bentham, and I can't tell whether there's a real tension or just a sloppy way of reading him. In Anarchical Fallacies, his attack on the French Declaration of the Rights of Man, he calls the bare idea of natural rights simple nonsense, and says that when you add that they're imprescriptible it becomes rhetorical nonsense on stilts; his official line there seems to be that rights exist only as legal creations, and talk of rights anterior to government is either figurative or confused. But in the Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation he grounds everything in the principle of utility, which is clearly meant to be a normative standard independent of any actual legal system, since it is the test by which legal systems themselves are to be judged. He even says nature has placed us under the governance of pain and pleasure, and that they point out what we ought to do. So he seems committed to a trans-legal moral standard, yet he also denies there are any trans-legal rights. Can these two be reconciled without making one of them a mere façon de parler? Is the distinction between a utility standard and a rights claim doing all the work, or is Bentham just using "rights" in a stricter sense than "ought"? I want the candidate reconciliations laid out and an honest verdict on which reading is least ad hoc. No headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; continuous prose only, like an email.
