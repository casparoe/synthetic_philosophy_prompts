I retired a few years ago from a career in atmospheric chemistry, and I've spent the last couple of years circling around philosophy — mostly moral psychology, because it's the part of philosophy that seems willing to engage with the empirical work I used to read. A friend who knows I've been thinking about psychopathy and moral judgment sent me a piece from a popular science site, and the article's summary of the philosophical situation goes like this:

Psychopaths are the classic test of whether you can know something is wrong without feeling it, and the evidence is in: when researchers give them the standard moral/conventional task, they draw the same distinction normal people draw, and when asked what the right thing to do is, they answer just like everyone else. They can tell you that hurting someone for fun is wrong; they just don't care. This shows that moral knowledge and moral motivation are completely independent, and that the old philosophical view that you can't sincerely judge something wrong without feeling at least some pull to avoid it has been refuted by the psychopath. It also shows how shallow that knowledge is, though: since the psychopath's only deficit is the loss of normal emotional responses, and since they pass every test of moral knowledge, what they lack isn't really knowledge at all — it's just the desires that usually come along with it. So psychopaths do know that what they do is wrong, full stop; they are simply unmoved by it. The article's conclusion is that the only mistake would be to claim they don't know right from wrong, since the studies clearly show they do.

I have a scientist's instinct to check whether the data really support the move from "they can answer the questions" to "they know, full stop," and I've read enough philosophy now to be suspicious of that final stretch. But the article moves from data to conclusion to fast for me, and its not obvious to me where the argument runs off the rails. Could you go through the summary and give me an accuracy audit: which parts are fair, which parts are subtle misrepresentations or conflations, what a philosopher working in moral psychology would want to qualify or push back on, and whether the article's final conclusion survives once the accurate parts are sorted out? I'm not looking for a reading list — just the argument checked point by point.
