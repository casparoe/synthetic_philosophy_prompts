I write about science and ideas for a living, and I'm working on a column about why Simpson's paradox keeps producing the same argument every generation without anyone moving. In reporting it I seem to have developped my own view, and I'd like a consistency check before I put it in print. Here's where I've landed:

1. There's no universal "always stratify" or "always pool" rule for Simpson cases; which table you should trust is set by what causal role the third variable plays.
2. But once you've fixed that role, the usual causal-inference rules settle it: condition on a confounder, don't condition on a mediator or a collider.
3. In the textbook admissions-style case (Berkeley-style), the department-by-department numbers are the right ones and the university-wide gap is a red herring.
4. There is a fact of the matter about the causal role of the third variable even when the data don't reveal it; it's fixed by what causes what and by what intervention you have in mind.
5. In many of the classic Simpson examples, though, the confounder-versus-mediator call is underdetermined by the data and depends on contested background theory (about discrimination, disease severity, and so on), so reasonable people land on different tables.
6. The real lesson of Simpson's paradox is that there's no such thing as "the effect of X on Y" until you fix a causal question; before that, the pooled and stratified tables are just two descriptions, neither of them the effect.

Can you audit this? Give me exactly five findings, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion: rank the tensions from most to least serious, say which commitments are in conflict, whether the tension is real or dissolves on inspection, and what the cheapest fix is.
