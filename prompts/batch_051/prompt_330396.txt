I work as a software engineer, and I keep running into a version of the doing/allowing distinction — the same family of issues as the old killing-versus-letting-die problem — that I cannot resolve, so I'm looking for a careful analysis rather than a side to cheer for.

On the one hand, I'm drawn to a clean, outcome-based picture. The systems I build optimize a cost function over expected outcomes, and if two behaviors produce the same expected cost, the system treats them as equivalent. That approach has real systematic virtues: it collapses messy options into a single number, gives a definite decision procedure, and stops me from baking what feel like arbitrary preferences into production code.

On the other hand, I have a firm intuition that a system's actively bringing about a bad outcome is different from its merely failing to prevent that same outcome, even when the expected cost is identical. If a migration script deletes a customer's data, that feels like a worse engineering failure than a backup job that silently doesn't run, even if the data is equally gone and equally unrecoverable either way. And I'd want a vehicle control system to treat "steer into one person to avoid hitting five" as different in kind from "continue on course and hit one person without intervening," even if the expected harms come out equal on paper.

I don't want to be told which side wins. I want the strongest version of each: the case that the outcome-only picture is correct and my intuition is something like a bug of human blame psychology, to be dropped rather than encoded into systems; the case that the intuition tracks something real about agency, default behavior, causal structure, or what a system is for, which the cost function leaves out; and the question whether there's a reading of "different" on which both survive — for instance, the distinction matters for responsibility, trust, or legibility rather than for which action is objectively best. Please lay out what would have to be true for each of those options to be the right response, rather than defaulting to one of them.
