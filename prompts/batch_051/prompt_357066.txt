Generic statements such as 'ducks lay eggs' and 'mosquitoes carry malaria' are accepted as true even though they tolerate exceptions: drakes and ducklings do not lay eggs, and only females of certain Anopheles species, when infected, transmit malaria to humans. Such statements also behave differently from ordinary quantified claims — they are not equivalent to 'all', 'most', or 'some', and a speaker can competently assert one without knowing the relevant proportions. Please give the five most important implications of this exception-tolerant, non-quantificational character of generics for how a public-health agency should write short advisory statements about disease vectors and reservoirs (for example, a one-line warning that 'mosquitoes carry malaria' or 'bats carry rabies'). Respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion; each item should state one implication and briefly explain why it follows.
