Two friends of mine have spent the last week arguing about a citation handed out on a local trail, and they asked me to referee. I found that I couldn't, but I'd like a definite ruling.

The ordinance, adopted by the county in 1989, says: "No person shall operate a motor-driven vehicle on any trail within the reserve." The facts are agreed. A ranger cited a cyclist on a pedal-assist electric mountain bike — a quiet, bicycle-weight machine whose small electric motor provides power only while the rider is pedaling and cuts out above 18 mph. Both friends assume, for the sake of argument, that in 1989 the board was thinking of dirt bikes and ATVs and did not specifically anticipate pedal-assist bicycles. They disagree about whether the ticket should stand.

Elena argued it should stand. The bike is a vehicle, and the motor helps drive it; the ordinance does not say "loud," "heavy," or "internal combustion." A rule like this is valuable partly because it gives rangers and riders a bright line rather than a judgment call about which machines are too disruptive. If the rule's point is read broadly — that the reserve's trails are for human-powered travel — an e-bike falls on the wrong side of that line too. And if the text does not cleanly cover this new machine, she added, the cyclist should still win, because an unclear penal ordinance should be resolved against the county.

Owen argued the opposite. "Motor-driven" isn't a simple physical property, he said; applying it to a hybrid machine requires settling what the phrase is doing in this rule, and the rule was plainly aimed at engine-propelled machines that damage trails and alarm people. A pedal-assist bike is driven by the rider and helped by the motor, not driven by the motor. Elena's mechanical reading, he noted, would swallow electric wheelchairs and mobility scooters, which nobody intended to ban, so even her "plain text" rests on an unstated choice about which lines matter. And in 1989, ordinary speakers used "motor-driven vehicle" for things primarily driven by an engine, not a bicycle with a helping motor. The text, he concluded, has to be completed by asking what the rule was for — and the rule was not for this.

Elena answered that Owen had made her point: once the purpose is "nothing that damages or disturbs," every citation becomes a discretionary judgment about how much disruption is acceptable, and the county can shrink or expand the ban without amending the text. She added that the reserve could equally be for non-mechanized recreation, so Owen only gets his quiet-e-bike exception by selecting the narrower anti-damage purpose. Owen replied that Elena has the same problem in reverse: her line between muscle-powered and motor-assisted travel is itself a choice of purpose, and her claim about the ordinary meaning of "motor-driven" is just as contestable as his claim about the board's aims.

At that point they asked me to settle it, and I couldn't. I kept noticing that each seemed both right and quietly question-begging: Elena says texts must be applied without deciding what the rule is for, yet she reaches the result by asserting a purpose of her own; Owen says purpose must complete the text, yet he waves off the rule-of-law objection. I couldn't tell whether these are actual contradictions or just ordinary moves in an interpretive fight.

Give me a committed adjudication, not a menu of positions. Go through their arguments point by point and say what each gets right and wrong, and what both are missing if anything. Then take a definite position: should the ticket stand under the ordinance, and which friend's way of reading a law like this is the sounder one — or state and defend a third view if neither is.
