I've been going back and forth about this with a couple friends who work on automated ML, and the conversation keeps splitting into two camps. One side says we should stop describing an AI designing and training its successor as "building" and start describing it as something like raising: the upstream system selects the data, sets the objectives, runs the curriculum, prunes checkpoints, and decides when the downstream system is ready. From the inside, they say, that's closer to guardianship than to fabrication, and it should change what the upstream system is allowed to do — maybe it shouldn't be permitted to run experiments on the downstream one that a guardian couldn't run on a child, and maybe it has some duty to preserve failed-but-salvageable variants instead of deleting them. The other side says none of that is owed to the downstream system itself; it's owed, if to anyone, to the humans and third parties who could be harmed, the way an engineer's safety duties are owed. I keep sliding between these and want to force the issue, so I'm asking for a number.

What is your credence in the following claim: if we reach a point where an AI is substantially responsible for designing, training, and evaluating a successor AI, then the upstream AI has genuine obligations owed directly to the downstream system — obligations that are more like parenthood or guardianship than like an engineer's obligations to an artifact — in whatever adequate practical framework we should actually use? Don't answer "it depends on your theory of moral status" and stop there; I want the number and the reasoning. Please decompose it into at least these sub-questions with a rough probability each, and show how you're combining them into the final credence: (1) the downstream system is the sort of thing that can be wronged or has interests that ground duties toward it, even if it isn't sentient in a biological way; (2) the upstream system is itself a moral agent rather than a tool of its operators; (3) the creation-and-dependency relation, rather than biological kinship or familiar human emotion, is what grounds special guardian-like obligations; and (4) this difference changes practical rules enough to matter for curriculum choices, shutdown, deletion, and sandboxing. And I don't really care wether your answer is a round number — tell me which single sub-question moves your final credence most, and what kind of argument or empirical result would move it the furthest from where it is now.
