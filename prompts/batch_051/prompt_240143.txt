I'd like you to analyse the 2010 fuss over the Venter Institute's synthetic cell through the lens of how philosophers of biology try to define life. I know the rough facts: a team synthesised a whole bacterial genome in the lab, stuck it into a recipient cell from another species whose own genome had been removed, and the resulting cell started replicating; a lot of the coverage said this was the first synthetic life, and a lot of scientists said it was nothing of the sort because the genome was copied from a natural bacterium and the cell machinery was borrowed. Take two or three serious candidate definitions of life, something built around metabolism, something built around self-replication with variation, and something built around information or coded instructions, and work through exactly which parts of the result each definition counts as alive, which parts it counts as a new life, and where each definition has nothing to say. Then turn it around: what does this episode do to the definitions themselves? Does it push you towards treating life as a cluster concept or a matter of degree, does it expose the definitions as partly rhetorical, and what is the most defensible version of both the yes and the no answer to the question did they actually create life? Finally, answer in British English.
