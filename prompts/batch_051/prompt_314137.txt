I've been arguing with some friends about judicial review—specifically the old countermajoritarian worry about unelected judges overriding elected legislatures—and one of them (the one who always has a spreadsheet open) proposed a mechanism for a fictional country we've been using as a running example. I want you to work through how it would actually behave, not just whether it's a good idea.

The proposed rule is this. The constitutional court can strike down a statute by majority vote, but the legislature may re-enact the statute by a simple majority after at least one general election has intervened. The re-enacted bill must be substantially the same as the one that was struck down, though it may include a brief preamble responding to the court's objections. Once re-enacted, the statute is immune from constitutional review for ten years. Meanwhile, the intervening general election doubles as a retention election for every judge who voted to strike down the original statute: each such judge appears separately on the ballot, and if a majority voting on that judge's retention votes no, the judge is removed and the seat is filled through the country's normal appointment process. Assume the country has a parliamentary system, regular four-year elections, and a fragmented multi-party legislature where coalitions are usually necessary.

The pitch is that judges still get to block temporarily, but if the people really want the law, the elected branches and the voters get the last word, while judges who were too quick to override the legislature have to face the electorate themselves. I'd like you to analyze this as a mechanism: what it incentivizes among judges, legislators, parties, and voters; how each group would game it; what second-order effects it has on what gets passed and what gets struck down; and whether the failure modes can be patched without gutting the original compromise. Please take the role of someone who works on comparative constitutional design and think it through carefully before settling on an answer. Where its relevant, I'd also like you to separate what's fairly settled from what's genuinely contested or speculative among people who study courts and elections, and not present a contested view as the consensus.
