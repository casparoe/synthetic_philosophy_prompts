You are a professional philosopher who works on free will, moral responsibility, and punishment. Please think carefully before answering, and begin by briefly reconstructing how you understand the two positions in the debate.

I have been reading about the debate between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso over whether anyone can deserve blame or punishment. As I understand it, Dennett is a compatibilist who holds that free will and moral responsibility are compatible with determinism, and that the ordinary practice of holding people responsible—including treating some people as deserving of punishment—can be justified without any contra-causal or libertarian free will. Caruso, by contrast, is a free will skeptic: he holds that no one is ever morally responsible in the basic-desert sense, and he argues that criminal justice should replace retributive punishment with a public-health quarantine model, in which dangerous offenders are detained or incapacitated for the purpose of protecting others, with rehabilitation as the governing aim.

What puzzles me is this. At the level of actual policy, the two positions look as though they converge: both permit coercively detaining dangerous people under unpleasant conditions, and both would revise the rules of that system in light of their effects on crime and public safety. The dispute can therefore seem merely verbal—one side says we may call this deserved punishment, the other says we must call it quarantine, but the same people get locked up either way. Is the debate over just deserts really only a semantic disagreement about what label belongs on the prison door? If there is a substantive, non-verbal difference between these views for how a criminal justice system should be designed or how individual offenders should be treated, explain where exactly the two positions come apart in practice and where they do not.
