I'm a software engineer and I've got a half-baked analogy I cant stop turning over, and I'd like someone to tell me whether it's real or whether I'm just pattern-matching. The other day I was debugging an issue where two settings objects in our code, `this.settings` and `opts`, turned out to be the same object under the hood: a function had passed one reference through three layers of config merging, so mutating one silently mutated the other. What struck me is that the machine knows `this.settings === opts` at a glance — identity checking is cheap — but for me, reading the code, learning that they're the same object was a genuine discovery. I'd been reasoning as if the two names picked out two independent things, and the program looked different once I knew they didn't. A friend who did a little philosophy in undergrad told me this is basically a puzzle philosophers call Frege's puzzle about identity statements: the claim that the morning star is the same planet as the evening star can be true yet informative, and someone can know the morning star is bright while failing to know the evening star is bright, even though both names pick out Venus. Frege's fix, as I understand it, was to distinguish the sense of a name from the thing it picks out.

In code we seem to have the same split all over the place: identifiers and their bindings on one side, the objects or values on the other; Python's `is` versus `==`; alias analysis in compilers; debug info that keeps source names around after the runtime has collapsed everything to addresses. So here's the question: is this actually the same structure as Frege's puzzle, or is it a loose metaphor that breaks as soon as you push on it? If it's real, does anything carry over in either direction — say, from compiler design to philosophy, or from Frege to how we should design languages and debuggers? If it falls apart, I want to know precisely where. And whichever way you come down, say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would flip your verdict, or where your reasoning about the parallel is most likely to fail.
