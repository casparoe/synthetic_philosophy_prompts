I have been developing a reading of Nietzsche's eternal recurrence, and I want it checked before I build anything further on it. I call it the recurrence filter. The core claim is that the eternal recurrence is best read neither as a cosmological doctrine nor only as a one-off test of whether one can affirm one's whole life backward, but as a standing filter for the patterns, habits, and standing responses one permits oneself to keep. The test, roughly: a pattern of acting is admissible only if one could will its endless recurrence — not merely tolerate it, but actually affirm it — whenever one instantiates it. The filter is aimed at the way we treat our own patterns as temporary exceptions: the deadline that justifies sharpness, the bad mood that excuses coldness, the unstated policy of not correcting a certain kind of lie. Each time I act from such a pattern I reinforce a standing disposition; the filter asks whether I could want that disposition to recur without limit, with no just-this-once left over.

An example. I have a pattern of not correcting a colleague when she misattributes my work, and I tell myself it keeps the peace. The filter does not ask whether I could endure an endless return of this particular afternoon. It asks whether I could want the pattern — letting that kind of silence stand whenever the situation recurs — to be part of what I endlessly become. If I could not, then by the theory I am not entitled to keep the pattern while still taking myself to be choosing who I become.

I know the standard readings: the cosmological thesis, the whole-life affirmation test, and the amor fati reading. I take my view to differ from all three because it is prospective rather than retrospective, and because its object is a repeatable pattern rather than the whole completed past.
