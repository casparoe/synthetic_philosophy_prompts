There's an odd asymmetry in the way people talk about forgiveness. When someone wrongs you, we treat forgiveness as a gift that is yours alone to give or withold: nobody can demand it, pushing you to forgive is seen as a kind of moral bullying, and your right to stay angry is close to absolute. But when you wrong someone else, the same voices say you need to forgive yourself — that until you do, you're stuck, and you owe it to yourself to let it go. So forgiveness is both something no one can demand of a person and something a person is expected to demand of themselves. Is this just a contradiction we live with, or is self-forgiveness a different kind of act that only shares a word with ordinary forgiveness? If different, spell out who does what to whom in each case, and what makes one optional while the other is close to obligatory. If they're the same kind of act, then defend the idea that "you owe it to yourself" can be a legitimate reason when "you owe it to the person you hurt" is treated as pressure. Give me the strongest principled distinction you can, test it against the case where the victim died without forgiving, and tell me whether the asymmetry survives.
