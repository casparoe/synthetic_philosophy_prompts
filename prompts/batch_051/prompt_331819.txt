I'm a journalist working on a long piece about old monetary ideas that suddenly look relevant again, and I've gotten stuck on Bishop Berkeley. Not the "to be is to be perceived" guy — the Berkeley of The Querist, who spent the 1730s trying to think his way out of a poor Ireland with almost no coin in circulation. His claim, roughly, is that the true idea of money, as such, is not intrinsic riches but "altogether that of a ticket or counter" for reckoning and transferring the fruits of people's labour; that an idle nation is poor even if it's sitting on a pile of gold; and that the practical fix is a national bank issuing paper notes on good security, accepted for taxes, so credit circulates and idle hands get put to work. Im flattening a book of rhetorical questions into a paragraph, but thats the gist.

I want to use the 2008 crisis as a stress test for that picture, and I want you to actually commit rather than lay out the options. I'll assume the usual facts: the housing boom built on cheap credit, mortgage-backed securities, the run on the shadow banking system, Lehman, the bailouts, and then quantitative easing. Was Berkeley's ticket-and-counter view mostly a good lens on 2008, mostly a bad one, or a specific mix? Argue your answer.

Do the mapping properly first: in his terms, who were the players, what were the tickets, what real labour or value were they supposed to count, and where did the tickets stop counting anything real? Where does his cure — keep the paper moving — become the disease? Then run it the other way: what does the episode expose about Berkeley's picture — is he naive about how credit instruments behave once they get sliced, pooled, and traded, or does he catch something the standard post-crisis story misses? Finish by giving the strongest version of the objection you rejected, and why you reject it.
