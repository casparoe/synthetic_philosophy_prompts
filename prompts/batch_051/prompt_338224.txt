Over the past several years I have watched the debate over whether insects are sentient run through the same cycle on rationalist forums and in the adjacent scientific commentary, without either side visibly moving. The skeptics—often entomologists—argue that insects lack the neural architecture, and in particular any analogue of a mammalian cortex, that we normally require before attributing experience; on their view, grooming an injured limb or withdrawing from a noxious stimulus is the work of decentralized reflexes and implies nothing about what, if anything, it is like to be the insect. The welfare side—often biologists who work on bees, ants, and flies—replies that insects show associative learning, flexible and state-dependent behavior, trade-off decisions, and nociceptive responses, and adds that the absence of a cortex is a poor argument because birds and cephalopods were once dismissed on similar structural grounds. Then the argument moves to numbers. The welfare side says: even if the probability that an insect is sentient is only a few percent, there are on the order of 10^18 insects alive at any moment, so the expected quantity of unaddressed suffering dwarfs almost everything else we claim to care about; acting cautiously on a small probability of a very large moral event is not irrational. The other side replies that this is exactly the shape of a low-probability, astronomically-high-stakes argument—a Pascal's mugging in all but name—because the probability is not measured or well-defined, the large number is doing all the work, and the recommendation would commit us to costly changes in agriculture, pest control, and land use on the strength of an ungrounded prior. New empirical papers appear; both sides cite them; credences do not move.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really a dispute about insect neurobiology or behavior. It looks to me like a dispute about how to reason when a probability is low, sensitive to theory choice, and multiplied by an enormous denominator—and, underneath that, a dispute about what should count as evidence of sentience in a nervous system organized so differently from ours. But I am unsure. It may instead be a factual disagreement about which behavioral or neural markers are sufficient for sentience, a verbal disagreement about the word sentience itself, or a difference in default priors and risk attitude that no empirical result could shift. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the impasse, not a verdict on insect sentience and not a survey of the positions. And I want an honest answer about what, if anything, would count as progress: a particular experiment, a formal result in decision theory about expected-value reasoning under unquantifiable uncertainty, a conceptual distinction among grades of sentience or welfare, or something else. If your diagnosis implies that no progress should be expected, say so and defend it.
