Here's the practice I keep coming back to. The UK, Norway, Denmark, and Japan are perfectly functional liberal democracies, yet they keep a hereditary head of state who is largely shielded from the ordinary legal process, who in theory could refuse to sign a bill or dismiss a prime minister, and who is paid for work that is almost entirely ceremonial — while everyone involved insists these powers must never actually be used. It's a wierd thing for a democracy to do, and no one in these countries believes in a divine right of kings, so what's doing the justificatory work?

I want to know what this practice presupposes about political legitimacy, authority, and the point of a state. Reconstruct the implicit theory as exactly five distinct commitments. For each one: state the commitment, explain why the institution seems to lean on it, and then say whether the practice strictly presupposes it, merely fits well with it, or is philosophically underdetermined because several rival theories could license it. Make the fifth item the exposure test: if the country became a republic tomorrow, which of these commitments would have to go first, and what would concretely change? Five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion — just the list.
