Here's a question I set on a decision theory exam, plus one of the better student answers. Could you grade it out of 20 and give me the comments you'd write, poin by point? I want the grade justified, not just a general impression.

Question: Explain the Ellsberg paradox and its challenge to subjective expected utility theory. Then take a position on whether ambiguity aversion is a rational attitude or a mistake.

Student answer: Ellsberg's example uses an urn with 30 red balls and 60 balls that are either black or yellow in unknown proportion. In the first pair of gambles, people prefer a $100 bet on red to a $100 bet on black, even though neither gamble has a higher maximum payout. In the second pair, they prefer a $100 bet on black-or-yellow to a $100 bet on red-or-yellow. This is the standard ambiguity-averse pattern: both preferences favor the gamble whose probability is known (red in the first pair, black-or-yellow in the second). No single subjective probability assignment can represent both preferences. The first preference reveals P(red) > P(black); the second reveals P(black-or-yellow) > P(red-or-yellow). Since P(black-or-yellow) = P(black) + P(yellow) and P(red-or-yellow) = P(red) + P(yellow), the second inequality implies P(black) > P(red), contradicting the first. So the choices violate Savage's sure-thing principle and show that expected utility theory descriptively fails.

The usual explanation is ambiguity aversion, which is really just risk aversion generalized to cases where probabilities aren't precisely known. Ellsberg's own reading was that the choices show people are predictably irrational, since they won't assign probabilities to the yellow event and act consistently on them. But that's too harsh. The better view is that the choices are rational: an agent can reasonably dislike not knowing the black/yellow distribution in the same way she can reasonably dislike a high-variance gamble. Thus the paradox doesn't undermine the normative core of expected utility theory; it only shows Savage's axioms are not descriptively accurate because they rule out a legitimate attitude toward ambiguity.
