I keep coming back to this stretch of Anselm's Cur Deus Homo (Book II, ch. 6, in the Deane translation):

> Anselm. But this cannot be effected, except the price paid to God for the sin of man be something greater than all the universe besides God.
>
> Boso. So it appears.
>
> Anselm. Moreover, it is necessary that he who can give God anything of his own which is more valuable than all things in the possession of God, must be greater than all else but God himself.
>
> Boso. I cannot deny it.
>
> Anselm. Therefore none but God can make this satisfaction.
>
> Boso. So it appears.
>
> Anselm. But none but a man ought to do this, other wise man does not make the satisfaction.
>
> Boso. Nothing seems more just.
>
> Anselm. If it be necessary, therefore, as it appears, that the heavenly kingdom be made up of men, and this cannot be effected unless the aforesaid satisfaction be made, which none but God can make and none but man ought to make, it is necessary for the God-man to make it.

When I try to reconstruct what he's runing there, I get this:

(1) The price paid to God for human sin has to be greater than everything in the universe apart from God. (2) If someone can give God something of his own that is more valuable than everything else in God's possession, that someone must be greater than everything except God himself. (3) Therefore only God can make this satisfaction. (4) But only a human being ought to make it; otherwise, man doesn't make the satisfaction. (5) Since the heavenly kingdom has to be made up of humans, and that requires the satisfaction in question, the one who makes it must be both God and human.

The conclusion sounds forced, and I half-suspect I've smuggled something in, but each step by itself seems right — especially (2), which almost reads like a definition. So: is this argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and what the corrected version of it would be. If it is valid, say why people still find it fishy.
