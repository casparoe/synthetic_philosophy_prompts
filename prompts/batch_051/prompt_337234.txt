Construct a thought experiment in which Morgan's canon — roughly, that we should explain animal behavior by the lowest psychological process compatible with the evidence unless higher processes are clearly required — and parsimony pull in opposite directions. The case should work as follows: a strictly "lower" explanation is available, so the canon, applied strictly, must accept it, but that lower explanation is less parsimonious and less predictive than the "higher" explanation the canon was designed to exclude. Constraints: the animal must be a species not ordinarily credited with sophisticated cognition — say a fish, an insect, or an arachnid, not a corvid, a parrot, or a primate; the case should be a single, well-described behavioral episode, with no surgical interventions and no reference to brain wiring; and the lower explanation must be a serious low-level proposal, such as chained associative learning or a fixed action pattern, not a strawman. After presenting the scenario, verify each specification: state both explanations and show why the lower one is genuinely available under a strict reading of the canon; specify exactly why the lower one is more costly, in terms of free parameters, stimulus–response links, or ad hoc generalization assumptions; and explain why the higher one remains higher on the psychological scale even though it is the leaner model. Then identify the single feature of the case that does the work, and say what happens when that feature is removed — whether the case collapses back into the familiar situation where parsimony and the canon point the same way. Finish with the strongest objection a defender of Morgan's canon could raise, and whether that objection leaves the canon standing as a scientific principle or only as a precautionary heuristic.
