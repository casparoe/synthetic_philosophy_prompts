I have spent the past few weeks trying to get clear about what I think a screenwriter or novelist owes real dead people when turning their lives into biographical fiction, and I have written down a set of commitments that I cannot get to hang together. I would rather not reveal which of these I would give up if forced, so please treat all of them as things I actually hold and audit the set as a whole.

(1) The dead cannot be harmed in the ordinary sense; once someone is gone there is no subject left to suffer, and our duties in biographical fiction should not be grounded in the subject's welfare.

(2) Nevertheless, when I draw on a real documented life, I am not free to reverse its settled public record. If the record shows that a figure refused to collaborate with an occupying power, I cannot show that figure collaborating proudly, not even in a work openly labeled as fiction.

(3) But the historical record is full of gaps, and filling gaps is part of the craft: invented dialogue, composite minor figures, and plausible private scenes are legitimate when they are consistent with what is documented and contradict nothing known.

(4) The deepest wrong in this territory is not inaccuracy but co-optation, by which I mean making the dead person a mouthpiece for values or causes they would have rejected, and thereby borrowing a moral authority they never lent. I hold this to be worse than getting central events wrong.

(5) I also hold that such co-optation wrongs the person even when no living descendant or admirer is hurt and no audience member is misled; that is why I say posthumous wrongs are possible even though I doubt posthumous harms are possible.

(6) At the same time, I think these duties track a work's implicit truth claims. A film that presents itself as the true story of a real trial faces much stricter constraints than a novel that openly recasts the same life with changed names, even when readers can see the resemblance.

(7) And I treat the recent dead differently from the long dead. I feel freer to imagine the inner life of a seventeenth-century figure than that of a person whose children, students, or victims are still alive, and I cannot tell whether this is a principle or just my discomfort with the living.

What I want is an audit of the set as a whole, not a general essay on the ethics of biopics. Please go through each pair of commitments that you judge to be in tension, say how severe the tension is and what would have to give in each case, and tell me which one change, if I would make only one, would leave the set most internally consistent and at what cost. Also flag any pair that only seems contradictory because two senses of a word are being run together, so I can see which conflicts are real and which are artifacts of my own vocabulary. Write your answer in continuous prose, as you would in a letter or long email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
