I'm rereading Camus's The Plague for an essay and I want to come at it from the non-human agency angle — germs as historical actors, the idea that a bacillus can restructure a city more thoroughly than any political decision. I'm not interested in the old allegory-of-the-Occupation reading. What I'm trying to work out is whether Camus actually lets the microbe be a genuine actor, or whether the plague is mostly an occasion that reveals what the townspeople already were. On the one hand the book gives the plague enormous causal power: it closes the port, flattens commerce, empties the streets, changes how people experience time and hope. But it's also just a mindless microbe with no intentions, and Camus keeps the focus on how people choose to respond — Rieux's sanitary squads, Paneloux's sermons, Rambert trying to leave. I'm especially interested in where the novel resists an environmental-determinist reading and where it flirts with one; Paneloux's first sermon treats the epidemic as divine chastisement, which is determinism in a theological key, and the narrator (who I think is revealed to be Rieux?) is very careful to record rather than explain. Can you help me think through which scenes you'd point to if you wanted to argue the book treats the germ as a historical actor, which scenes you'd point to for the opposite, and what the novel adds to the philosophical debate about non-human causes and agency that an argument built from concepts alone would miss? I want the reading to be faithful in both directions — not just using Oran as a case study.
