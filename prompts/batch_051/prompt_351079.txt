I have a rule I follow whenever I watch an animal do something clever, whether it is my dog working out how to knock the lid off the food bin or the crows outside my window dropping walnuts on the road for cars to crack. The rule is: if the behavior could be produced by a "lower" ability — associative learning, trial and error, cue-reading, instinct, or a fixed action pattern — then I am not allowed to conclude that the animal is exercising a "higher" one, such as forming a belief, making a plan, reasoning about causes, or feeling something like frustration or empathy. I apply the rule most strictly when the behavior is most impressive, because that is exactly when I worry I am being charmed into over-crediting the animal. I later learned that something like this rule is an old principle in animal psychology, often called Morgan's canon.

My question is whether the rule, as I actually use it, is defensible. My friend says it is rigged: after the fact, you can almost always produce some association or conditioning story that fits a single observation, so the rule always votes for the unflattering explanation. I think the crux is the word "could." A story invented after seeing the behavior and merely retold in the vocabulary of association is not the same as showing that a known lower capacity actually produces that kind of behavior. So maybe the defensible version of my rule would require that the lower explanation would have predicted the behavior in advance, and that it keeps predicting new variations without piling up extra assumptions. But I cannot tell whether that is a genuine fix or just my preference for lower explanations wearing a disguise.
