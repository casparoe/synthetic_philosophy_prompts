I have been going back and forth with a friend about the Münchhausen trilemma: the familiar dilemma according to which any attempt to justify a belief must end either in an infinite regress, in circularity, or in an arbitrary dogmatic stopping point. My friend claims this is a false trichotomy. He says the trilemma only works because it assumes that an infinite chain of reasons would have to be actually traversed, step by step, by the believer; once we notice that an infinite chain can be generated by a finite rule, he thinks all three horns can be avoided at once. He laid out his position as follows, and I cannot identify the exact place where it goes wrong, if it does.

(1) A belief is justified only if its holder can meet any challenge to it by giving a further reason.
(2) A reason given for a challenged belief is itself a belief, and can itself be challenged; a reason that is immune to challenge is simply a dogmatic stopping point.
(3) Therefore, justification cannot consist in actually completing an infinite chain of reasons, since no finite agent can complete such a chain.
(4) But an infinite chain of reasons can be specified in a finite way by a recursive rule: one specifies a first reason R0 and a function f such that, whenever a challenge is raised to Rn, the next reason is Rn+1 = f(Rn).
(5) A person who grasps the rule and can apply f is thereby able to answer any challenge whatsoever without ever performing an infinite task, since each reply requires only one finite application of the rule.
(6) Such a person's belief is justified: every challenge can be met, no answer is an arbitrary stopping point, and no answer is repeated in a circle.
(7) Therefore the trilemma is a false trichotomy: recursive justification is a fourth option, genuinely distinct from regress, circularity, and dogmatism.

Please act as a professional epistemologist working on the structure of justification. Before settling on a verdict, briefly set out what a defender of the trilemma should concede and what a defender of recursive justification needs the word "justified" to mean. Then assess the argument step by step. Is it valid? If it is valid, is the conclusion right, or does recursive justification collapse back into one of the three original horns under a more careful description? If it is invalid, identify the first step that fails, say precisely why it fails, and state which reading of the key term would be needed to repair it. I want a definite verdict I can take back to my friend, not merely a survey of possible worries.
