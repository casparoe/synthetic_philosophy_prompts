Ok so I'm a political science phd student working on affective polarization, and the thing I keep running into is that the literature talks constantly about whether partisans on the other side argue "in good faith," but I don't think anyone has actually given a working account of what that consists in. The phrase is doing loads of moral work while staying vague, and Id like to nail it down. So: give me a definition of arguing in good faith (or engaging in good faith, if you'd rather frame it that way), then run it through the following cases and tell me where it gives the wrong verdict or no verdict at all:

- someone who only reads outlets that already agree with him and dismisses the rest as biased, but who genuinely believes everything he asserts and never knowingly misrepresents anything
- someone who, when you show her she's been paraphrasing you wrong, immediately corrects herself and argues against your actual claim instead
- someone who deliberately restates your view as something dumber and then attacks that version
- someone paid to defend a position he doesn't hold, who says up front that he's being paid
- someone who keeps shifting the definition of "fair" mid-argument without noticing he's changed the topic
- a troll who knows his own points are weak and is just trying to exhaust you with endless gotchas
- an activist who publishes a fabricated statistic because she thinks the real number is less persuasive and the cause matters more than the exact figure
- someone who says up front "there is no evidence that would change my mind on this," and means it
- a relative who doesn't really hold a settled view but argues the opposite of you because he enjoys the fight
- someone who engages seriously with every point, concedes the strong ones, and at the end says "I still disagree, but I can't yet say exactly why"

Some of these feel clearly inside the notion, some clearly outside, and several leave me genuinely stuck. I have a hunch that what we call "good faith" is actually running together an epistemic virtue (being open to evidence and to being wrong) and a social or moral one (treating your interlocutor as worth engaging honestly), and that the hard cases are exactly where those two pull apart. If you buy that, show me which cases split along that seam; if you don't, tell me what you think the real joints are. Then flag which of your verdicts you're least confident in. And if your definition returns a verdict you can't stomach, either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict.
