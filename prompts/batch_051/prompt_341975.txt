I have been working out a view I call retributive discounting, and I want a candid assessment of it. The core claim is that a punishment's severity should be measured not as duration times intensity, but as a discounted sum: each later unit of suffering discharges less of the wrong's desert than an earlier unit of the same intensity, at some fixed discount rate. More exactly, for a punishment at constant intensity i continued forever, total severity is i/(1−δ) for some 0<δ<1, a finite number. The payoff, as I see it, is that the proportionality of hell becomes tractable: eternal conscious torment is not automatically disproportionate to finite wrongdoing, because it has finite severity; rather, a given wrong is matched to hell only if its desert value equals i/(1−δ), and is over-punished or under-punished otherwise. Annihilation becomes the limiting case i=0, and universal salvation becomes the special case in which no wrong carries positive desert. I therefore regard the three positions in the debate as points on one parameterized continuum rather than rival eschatologies. I know the usual retributivist alternatives: that desert must be assessed atemporally, or that a finite being can incur infinite guilt by sinning against an infinite being. I find both less satisfying than actually computing the debt. What I want from you: is discounted desert coherent, or am I smuggling a rate of temporal preference into retribution without justification? Has anything of this shape already been defended? What is the strongest version of the view, and where does it break — in particular, is there any principled, non-arbitrary way to fix δ, or does the need to choose it amount to gerrymandering of exactly the sort the proportionality debate should avoid? Be candid about whether this is a real contribution or a formalized restatement of something obvious.
