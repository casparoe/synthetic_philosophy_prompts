My department has a logic reading group, and we've been on Brouwer for like six months now. The same argumnt keeps restarting between two of the grad students, Maya and Sam, and I'm honestly not sure anymore whether they're even disagreeing about the same thing. It's about the law of excluded middle.

Maya's the constructivist-sympathetic one. Her line is: you only get to assert A or not A when you either have a construction of A or have a construction that turns any construction of A into an absurdity. So excluded middle is not justified as a general schema. Sam says that's not what excluded middle says. Excluded middle says the proposition A-or-not-A is true, not that we currently know which disjunct holds. Once you fix the meaning of quantification over the natural numbers, something like P vs NP is determinately true or false even if no one has a proof yet.

Maya pushes back: but what does "true" mean for an undecided statement, if no construction exists? Sam says it means the numbers form a fixed, completed totality, and the statement is about that totality. Maya says that's exactly the Platonist picture Brouwer is trying to get us to do without, because we don't have any intuition of a completed infinite totality, only a rule for generating it. Sam says maybe Brouwer has a weird epistemology, but rejecting excluded middle is too high a price.

The frustrating thing is they accept all the same technical facts. They both know classical logic has excluded middle and intuitionistic logic doesn't. They both know about the double-negation translation, that a classical proof of an existential statement gives you a constructive proof of its double negation but not necessarily a constructive proof of the statement itself. Maya says that's the whole point: a classical "existence" claim doesn't deliver an object, so it isn't an existence claim in the constructive sense. Sam says the translation shows classical reasoning is a harmless shorthand for reasoning about double negations, and the rest is just an epistemic restriction you're imposing on assertion. Maya says no, it's a restriction on meaning. Sam says since when was computation the goal of mathematics?

We were reading Brouwer’s Intuitionism and Formalism, and Maya kept returning to the passage where Brouwer describes mathematics as an activity of the mind that precedes language, with signs on paper serving only to communicate the mental construction. Sam said he doesn't care what Brouwer thought, he cares whether the resulting logic is workable.

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not just a verdict on excluded middle. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the words "true" and "exists," or two people answering different questions — one about the content of mathematical statements and one about when we're entitled to assert them? Or is it really a disagreement about what mathematics is for — computation, communication, description of a reality? I've started to suspect it's a normative disagreement about what a proof is supposed to do, but I'm not confident, because Maya does make claims about meaning and not only about norms. Tell me what kind of disagreement this is, why it keeps stalling despite the shared technical background, and what would actually count as progress given your diagnosis. If the answer is that no progress should be expected, say why.
