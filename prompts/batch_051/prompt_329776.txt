The debate between naturalists and normativists about health and disease has persisted for decades without either side making much headway. Naturalists hold that disease is a value-free biological matter: a condition counts as a disease when it depresses an organism's functioning statistically below species-typical levels for an appropriate reference class, such as age and sex. Normativists reply that the choice of reference class and the notion of dysfunction themselves already encode evaluative commitments, and they argue that judgments of health and disease are ineliminably shaped by judgments about harm, disvalue, and what a society is prepared to treat as medical — as illustrated by the removal of homosexuality from psychiatric classification and the progressive medicalization of infertility, obesity, and grief. Each side treats these hard cases as data for its own view, and each accuses the other of rigging the description of the cases in advance. I am not asking for a verdict on which view is right; I am asking for a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is this a verbal dispute in which a naturalist concept and a normativist concept pass under the single term 'disease'? Is it a substantive disagreement about what biology can establish? Is it a disagreement about whether the hard cases should count as evidence about the concept or as proposals to revise it? Or is it ultimately a clash of pre-theoretical intuitions that argument cannot reach? Give the diagnosis with reasons, anchored in what each side actually says about the difficult cases, and then state what would count as progress under that diagnosis — a distinction, an empirical finding, a reformulated question — or argue that the impasse is exactly what we should expect and why.
