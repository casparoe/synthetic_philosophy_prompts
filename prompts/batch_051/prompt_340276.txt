I keep coming back to a parallel between something I read about in ethics and the halting problem, and I can't tell whether it's a real structural point or just me pattern-matching on the word "unknowable."

The ethics thing is called consequentialist cluelessness, at least as far as I understood it. Consequentialism says the right action is the one with the best consequences. But for anything you actually do, the consequences ramify outward basically forever — small changes cause other changes, those cause others, and a century later the world could be very different either way. So in practice you can never actually finish the calculation of whether your action was net-good, and the theory that's supposed to tell you what to do ends up not telling you what to do, because it requires information you can't have. It's not just that we lack data; it's that getting the data would require running the whole future.

That sounds an aweful lot like the halting problem. There's no general algorithm that can look at an arbitrary program and input and decide whether it halts without running it, and even if you do run it and it hasn't halted yet you can't know whether it will halt on the next step or run forever. Both cases seem to say: the only way to find out the outcome is to execute the thing, and you can never be sure you've executed it long enough.

Is this deep or superficial? Like, is consequentialist cluelessness a kind of undecidability about the world, or is the halting problem just the formal cousin of it? Or does the analogy break because Turing's result is a proved limit on what any computation can settle, whereas cluelessness is just the messy-world problem of too many long causal chains and not enough compute? What would have to be true of the world or of consequentialism for the two to be the same kind of thing, and what does the parallel suggest that's actually false?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating the question.
