I am a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate course on the ethics and epistemology of collective memory. I need to grade one take-home essay. Below are the question and one student's answer.

Question: The town of Ransford has for over a century held an annual commemoration of what its residents call the "Burning of the Granary." According to the town's collective memory, in 1897 a neighboring village set fire to the founders' granary, a loss the town frames as both a catastrophe and a foundation for its later resilience; the memory of the fire now structures the town's identity and its relations with the neighboring village. A recently catalogued set of municipal account books and insurance records robustly indicates that no fire occurred in 1897, and that the granary's contents were lost to a failed harvest sold off over the following winter. Assume for this question that the record is genuine and strong. Should the town revise its commemoration or its memory? More generally: when a community's collective memory and the archival record conflict, which should give way? In answering, you must (i) explain what it means for a memory or a record to have authority; (ii) distinguish at least two kinds of authority that collective memory might claim; (iii) say whether your answer changes depending on whether the conflict concerns a fact or a significance; and (iv) defend a general principle about when, if ever, a community may retain a memory that contradicts the best-supported reading of the record.

Student's answer:

Collective memory and the archival record can pull apart, as they do in Ransford, but the two do not answer the same question. The account books answer the question of what probably happened to the granary; the commemoration answers the question of what that loss means for the town now. This suggests a division of labour. The record has epistemic authority over questions of fact, while collective memory has a different kind of authority — call it practical or ethical authority — over questions of significance. The question "which gives way?" therefore has a different answer on each side.

For questions of fact, memory should give way. Since the Ransford documents are genuine and robust, the town should accept that no fire consumed the granary in 1897. No community has a license to its own false empirical beliefs, and a commemoration that continued to assert the fire as a plain historical fact would no longer be honestly held. There may be cases where the record is weakened by interested parties or gaps, but here the record is strong, so the remembered facts should be corrected.

For questions of significance, by contrast, the record should give way. Whether the loss of the granary was a catastrophe that founded the town's resilience, and whether it underwrites a certain stance toward the neighboring village, are not conclusions that account books dictate. The community is the author of the meaning it finds in its past, and a historian's deflationary reading does not oblige the town to abandon that meaning. Ransford may continue to mark the loss as a founding wound, and it may continue to treat the memory as central to who its residents take themselves to be.

Matters are harder when a factual claim is woven into an identity-constitutive narrative. I believe memory should generally stand even here. Ransford's identity is organized around the fire, and the town should not be required to dismantle that identity because a ledger challenges a detail within the remembered narrative. Within the commemorative practice the detail that the neighbor set the fire no longer functions as a factual assertion; it functions as a constituent of the town's self-understanding. To insist on correcting it would treat the town as an object of inquiry rather than as a subject of its own history.

An objector might say that protecting falsehood undermines intellectual honesty. But this objection assumes the residents hold the disputed detail as an ordinary propositional belief. Collective memory is narrative rather than propositional: a community can carry a partly inaccurate narrative without its members believing the corresponding false proposition, because the retelling is governed by fidelity to identity rather than to a record. The apparent conflict is thus less a collision of two truth-claims than a collision of two projects. Each gives way only within its own sphere: memory on isolated technical facts, and the record on meaning, where the record has no jurisdiction.

Grade the answer. Give a mark out of 100 and a point-by-point commentary that justifies that mark. In the commentary, identify both what the answer does well and where it falls short, with specific reference to the distinctions and arguments in the answer rather than a general impression. Do not simply summarize the answer; the comments should explain how the mark follows from the specific strengths and errors.
