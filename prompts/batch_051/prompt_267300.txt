Define blackmail. I am not asking for a legal digest or a statutory definition, and no citations are required; I want a philosophical working definition—a set of necessary and sufficient conditions, or something close to them—that captures why blackmail is normally wrong and separates it from ordinary threats, hard bargains, and lawful demands. You should take yourself to be analyzing the ordinary moral concept of blackmail, though you may make explicit any point at which you think ordinary usage runs together two distinct ideas.

Test your proposed definition against the following cases. Treat cases (1)–(3) as clear blackmail and cases (4)–(6) as clearly not blackmail. Treat cases (7)–(12) as genuinely borderline; there I am less interested in which verdict you reach than in whether your definition yields a determinate verdict that you can defend.

Clear blackmail

(1) Ellis has photographs of a colleague's extramarital affair and tells the colleague: "Pay me $3,000 or the photographs go to your spouse."

(2) A bookkeeper discovers that her employer has underreported income and tells him: "Pay me $2,000 or I will report the underreporting to the tax authorities."

(3) An aide who has obtained a politician's private medical record tells the politician: "Find me a paid position on your staff, or I will publish the record."

Clear non-blackmail

(4) A bookseller says: "Pay the listed price and the book is yours; otherwise I will keep it."

(5) A parent says: "Finish your homework before you watch television; otherwise there is no television tonight."

(6) An employer says: "Stop padding your expense reports, or you will be fired."

Borderline cases

(7) A union announces that its members will strike unless management raises hourly wages.

(8) A journalist tells a public official: "Sit for an on-record interview about these donations, or we publish the story we already have."

(9) A security researcher tells a software vendor: "Fix the vulnerability and pay the published bounty within 90 days, or I will publish the flaw so that users can protect themselves."

(10) A client whom a contractor overcharged for years says: "Repay the amount you overcharged, or I will report your fraudulent billing to the licensing board."

(11) A company that holds a valid patent tells a competitor: "Pay a license fee, or we will sue you for infringement."

(12) An online service publishes arrest booking photographs and charges the people depicted a fee to remove their records.

Proceed as follows. State your definition, then run each case through it, saying exactly where the definition gives a firm verdict, no verdict, or the wrong verdict. Where it gives the wrong or no verdict, revise the definition or defend the classifier's surprising verdict. Then identify the borderline verdict in which you have least confidence. Finally, address the familiar puzzle that blackmail seems to combine two acts that would each be permissible on their own—making a truthful disclosure, and asking for another person's money or cooperation—yet the combination appears wrongful; explain whether your definition dissolves the puzzle, or whether the right response is to reject one of the puzzle's premises. Please write your answer in American English.
