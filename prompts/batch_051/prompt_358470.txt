Here's a thing I cant get straight. In a lot of English-speaking social practice, if a friend changes their given name—say, after transition, conversion, or just because they hate the name their parents picked—then using their old first name is not treated as a mere slip. People read it as a refusal to accept who they now are, and they get genuinely hurt or angry about it. But when someone changes their surname, say at marriage, using the old surname is mostly treated as harmless; people note it down as a maiden name in parentheses, and nobody thinks you've denied who the person is. And this stays true even when the surname change is motivated by exactly the kind of identity-heavy reason that would make a first-name change matter—someone repudiating the family they came from and trying to become someone new. Old first name: a wound. Old surname, changed for the same kind of reason: a footnote. Is there actually a principled difference between what given names do and what family names do—about reference, identity, or whatever—that justifies this, or is it just convention running on autopilot? Give me the strongest justification for the asymmetry you can build, then test it against the repudiating-the-family case, and say honestly whether we should keep the asymmetry, drop it, or reverse it. Also tell me how confident you are in that verdict and which part of it you're least sure of.
