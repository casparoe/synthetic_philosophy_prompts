I'm preparing a seminar session that reads Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through Confucian role ethics, particularly as developed by Roger T. Ames and Henry Rosemont Jr. On that view, persons are constituted by the roles they inhabit — son, employee, colleague, would-be suitor — rather than by an autonomous self behind those roles, and the moral task is to cultivate virtuosity within one's roles, not to step outside them. Stevens looks like a near-perfect test case: he identifies wholly with his role as butler, grounds his dignity in it, and subordinates every personal attachment to it, yet the novel is usually read as the story of a man who has hollowed himself out. I want a reading that says whether the novel vindicates, indicts, or complicates the role-ethical picture. In particular: is Stevens's failure best described as serving a bad master, as performing his role without the right kind of presence, or as revealing a blind spot in role ethics itself — and how, if at all, could a role ethicist criticize a bad role? Take both the novel and the theory seriously, and mark the places where each resists the other.
