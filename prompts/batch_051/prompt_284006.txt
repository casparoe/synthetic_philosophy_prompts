I've come around to the knowledge norm of assertion: you shouldn't flat-out assert that p unless you know that p, and I'm aware of the truth norm, the belief norm, and the reasonable-belief candidates. Before I teach it again, I want to know how much the view actually sticks it's neck out rather than hiding behind hedges.

So take the knowledge norm as the target and tell me what would count against it. I want a map of its exposure, not a survey of the papers. Include the familiar problem cases — the lottery assertion made on purely statistical grounds, Moore-paradoxical utterances like "p, but I don't know that p," and "selfless assertion" cases where someone asserts what they don't believe or know for good moral reasons — but don't just list them. For each, tell me whether it's fatal, merely awkward, or absorbable, and which escape routes are live rather than merely logically possible. I'm especially interested in whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — "knowledge is the norm, except in these conversational contexts, and except for these practical purposes, and except…" — and if it has, whether that's a vice or just what a philosophical thesis is allowed to do.

Also flag which direction of the norm is doing the work: is knowledge necessary for permissible assertion, sufficient, or both, and do the defeaters look different depending on whether the norm is read as constitutive of assertion itself versus as an ordinary epistemic rule for when you may assert? I suspect many counterexamples only hit one reading. Be explicit about what's settled here (is it even settled that assertion is governed by a norm in the required sense?) and what's genuinely contested among people who know the area. Don't present a contested view as the consensus.
