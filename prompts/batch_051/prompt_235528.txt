I'm taking a course this term on nuclear strategy and game-theoretic models of deterrence, and I'd like to work through a modified version of a model we've been discussing. We've studied the classic Chicken game as a stylized account of brinkmanship: two states simultaneously choose between standing firm and backing down, where mutual standing firm means a catastrophic war, backing down first carries a credibility or humiliation cost while the other side wins, and both backing down produces a compromise. We've also discussed the familiar objection that real crises are not one-shot simultaneous choices, since war can break out through accident or loss of control even while both governments are still trying to calibrate their resolve.

I want to consider what happens when the formal model is altered in line with that objection. Suppose the crisis proceeds in alternating turns along a ladder of escalation levels from 0 to 10, starting at 0. On each turn, the acting state can climb one rung, stay put, or descend one rung; descending below 0 counts as a public concession and ends the game with the usual payoffs for winning and losing. Before the acting state chooses, however, there is a chance q(k) = p0 + rk that war breaks out anyway through an accident, where k is the current escalation level and p0 and r are small known parameters. Both states observe whether war occurs before the turn proceeds. Climbing a rung therefore does not itself cause an immediate crash, but it raises the accident hazard that both sides will face at the start of every later turn, and descending lowers it. The usual Chicken payoff ordering is preserved: war is worse than conceding for each side, conceding is worse than a mutual descent, and forcing the other side to concede is best.

What would the standard Chicken analysis say about this version? In particular, do the usual asymmetric equilibria survive, or does the sequential structure with an endogenous accident risk turn the situation into something closer to a war of attrition in which each side tries to outlast the other's willingness to endure an escalating hazard? How should a rational state compute the highest rung it is willing to reach before it would rather concede than take another accident draw? I'm also curious whether the argument that it can be useful to cultivate a reputation for being willing to stand firm survives this modification, given that accidents can now preempt anyone's decision and turn an unconceded crisis into war automatically.
