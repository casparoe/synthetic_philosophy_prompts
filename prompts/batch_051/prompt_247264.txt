I keep going back and forth on a claim a designer friend made: that what makes every 'a' an 'a' isn't any shared shape but a shared structural role — something like "a closed counter with an opening on the right and a roughly vertical stem," and that style (serif, blackletter, handwriting, geometric) is just variation on that role. It sounds like it should be empirically checkable, but every time I try to turn it into a test I end up just checking wether people agree on classifications, which feels circular. Suppose I wanted to actually test whether letter-category membership is driven by an abstract structural role rather than by family resemblance among shapes. Design the strongest concrete experiment you can: what stimuli would you generate, what comparisons or measures would you use, and what pattern of results would count for the role claim and against it. Then be honest about the residue: even a clean positive result — say, a sparse set of topological features predicts classification across wildly different styles — what exactly would it settle about the "essence" of 'a', and what would it leave open (context effects, learned convention, normativity, whether the essence is in the glyph or in the reader)? And would a negative result — classification tracks holistic shape similarity instead — actually refute the role view, or just show the operationalization was bad?
