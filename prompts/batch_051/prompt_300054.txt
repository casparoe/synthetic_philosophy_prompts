I am a novelist, and I have a research question in the service of a scene I am trying to write. The novel includes Paul Dirac, who died in 1984, in a fictional late-life conversation with a much younger theoretical physicist working on the mathematical structure of string theory. The scene turns on an argument that Dirac never had to have in this form: whether the striking mathematical beauty of a physical theory can rationally support confidence in it, or justify a field's continued investment in it, when direct experimental confirmation is expected to remain out of reach for decades. I want Dirac's position to feel like a coherent, inward intellectual stance, not a set of remembered maxims.

Please reconstruct, as fairly as you can from what is known of his methods and temperament, what Dirac's considered view would most likely have been. In particular:

- Would he have regarded the modern appeal to elegance in fundamental physics as a legitimate extension of his own convictions about beauty in equations, or as a failure of nerve in which aesthetic preference is being asked to do work that only experiment is allowed to do?
- How much weight would he have given to the selection-effect objection: that the history of physics contains many elegant theories that quietly failed, while the successes are remembered partly because they succeeded?
- Is the most faithful reading of Dirac one on which beauty is evidence of truth, or one on which beauty is merely a strong heuristic for finding equations that experiment must later test? Would he have cared about that distinction, or found it unreal?
- Would he have been moved by the argument that, in the absence of direct experimental test, the lack of any comparably elegant alternative is itself a form of evidence? Or would he have treated that as the very circularity to be resisted?
- Would the particular aesthetic he favored—spare, economical, almost algebraically forced—even align with the aesthetic of contemporary fundamental theory, or would he have found the latter baroque rather than beautiful?

Give me a definite prediction, but with the reasoning behind it, and mark the places where the historical record gives you the least purchase rather than smoothing them over.

Finally, tell me what would change your prediction. Which kind of evidence about Dirac's actual views—a contrary remark in his letters or lectures, a reassessment of his scientific practice, or a development in the physics itself—would most plausibly reverse or substantially shift your verdict, and where in your reasoning do you think that reversal is most likely to occur? I do not need a bibliography or citations; I need the portrait, and a clear sense of where the portrait is fragile.
