I am a benefit-risk assessor at a national medicines regulator. Several times a month I have to recommend whether a product may remain approved when it imposes a small burden on very many people in order to prevent a severe harm for a few. A typical case is a drug that most users take preventively for years, causing frequent but mild nausea, while sparing a much smaller number of people from a fatal complication. My recommendations go to a committee that must sign off, I usually have about one working day to prepare each one, and neither I nor most of the committee members are philosophers.

Please turn the philosophical debate about aggregating tiny harms — the thought experiment often posed as whether a dust speck in the eye of a vast number of people could ever be worse than one person being tortured, together with the surrounding questions about whether harms can simply be added across persons — into a reusable decision procedure I can actually run. The procedure should be a sequence of questions to ask in order, with an explicit statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which it stops with a "no," and the points at which it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts.

Preserve the distinctions that actually matter in the philosophy — for example, whether harms are additive across persons or whether there is a severity threshold below which a harm does not count; whether extreme harms have lexical priority; whether the distribution of harms matters independently of their sum; and how to handle risk assessed before harm has landed versus harm already realized. But the procedure must remain usable by someone who has not read the literature. Be explicit about where the procedure will misfire, label any step that is really a judgment call, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do.

Then run the procedure on these three cases: (1) a preventive medication that causes mild daily nausea in most of the hundreds of thousands of people who take it while preventing a small number of deaths; (2) a food additive that prevents a few fatal cases of a rare foodborne illness each year but raises the lifetime cancer risk for tens of millions of consumers by an amount too small to detect at the individual level; (3) a road-safety change that would add an average of two minutes to the daily commute of one million drivers and is projected to prevent two pedestrian deaths per year. For at least one of these cases, ensure the procedure yields an answer a benefit-risk assessor or the committee would find genuinely uncomfortable, and explain why yielding that answer is a feature of the procedure rather than a bug.
