In the lottery paradox, a fair lottery has one winning ticket among a million, and for any particular ticket the probability that it loses is so high that it seems rational to believe that the ticket loses; yet believing this of every ticket while also believing that some ticket wins leaves a person with an inconsistent set of beliefs. In the preface paradox, an author seems rational to believe each sentence of her carefully checked book, and seems equally rational to believe, given her own history of error, that at least one sentence in it is false; again the resulting set of beliefs is inconsistent. Write a dialogue of about 1,000 words between two epistemologists who draw opposite lessons from these two puzzles. The first holds that rational belief just is sufficiently high confidence—call this the threshold view—and concludes that the puzzles show rationality does not require one's beliefs to be closed under conjunction. The second holds that rational belief, unlike high confidence, must answer to standards of consistency and closure, and concludes that the puzzles show the threshold view must be abandoned. Set the dialogue as the two discuss an author finishing the preface to her own book. Have them engage with each other's actual examples and arguments rather than restating positions. Each should be a competent spokesperson for her view and should concede at least one genuine difficulty in it; the aim is to expose why the choice between the two views is hard, not to crown a winner.
