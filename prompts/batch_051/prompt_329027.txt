I cover science and ideas for a living — mostly cognitive science, but lately I've been writing a lot about the unspoken rules that run everyday life — and I'm working on a feature about whether ordinary conventions like tipping in restaurants, queueing politely, or not jaywalking carry any real moral force, or whether they're just local etiquette that visitors can reasonably ignore. One move keeps coming up in my interviews. When I ask whether a tourist does something genuinely wrong by undertipping in New York, pushing into a queue in London, or crossing against the light on an empty street in Berlin, people say "when in Rome, do as the Romans do" as if that settles it. A city councillor I interviewed said it, more or less, to defend ticketing summer tourists for jaywalking, and a restaurant owner used it to justify a service charge on foreign diners who don't tip. It's obviously carrying a lot of weight in those arguments, but I can't tell exactly which argument it is, and I suspect it's hiding several different claims.

So here's what I'd like. Put on your professional philosopher hat — say, someone who works on social norms and moral philosophy — and think carefully before answering; maybe start by brainstorming the hidden premises someone would need to get from "locals do X" to "visitors ought to do X." Then give me a numbered list that seperates out the distinct precise claims the proverb could be used to express: claims about prudence, about manners, about respect for hosts, about tacit consent, about genuine moral obligation, about whose customs count when a person belongs to more than one community, and whatever else you find. For each claim, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or a substantive claim that's probably false. Finally, go back to the councilman and the restaurant owner: say which reading each was probably leaning on, and whether that reading actually gets them to the conclusion that a visitor is morally obligated to follow the local rule — or only to something weaker, like "it would be politer" or "don't act surprised when the locals get annoyed."
