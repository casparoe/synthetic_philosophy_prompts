A colleague of mine thinks about deception through cases rather than general theories, and I know her verdicts well. Her track record:

1. An applicant writes on a form that she holds a safety certificate she has never held. My colleague calls this a serious wrong.

2. The same applicant is asked at interview whether she holds the certificate. She truthfully says that she completed all the required coursework for the certificate, omitting that she subsequently failed the licensing exam, and she knows the interviewer will take her to be certified. He does, and hires her. My colleague calls this wrong, but clearly less serious than 1.

3. A trustee truthfully tells a beneficiary that every distribution was made exactly as the trust specified, knowing the beneficiary will infer that the trust is being competently managed and will therefore not investigate her mismanagement. My colleague judges this exactly as serious as an outright lie.

4. A friend falsely says that a bad haircut looks good. My colleague does not regard this as seriously wrong.

Now she must judge a new case. A used-car seller is asked, "Has this car ever been in a major accident?" She truthfully replies, "My mechanic inspected it two months ago and it needed no repairs at that time," and shows the report, knowing the buyer will take this to mean "no." In fact the car was in a major accident a year earlier and was cosmetically repaired. The buyer relies on her answer and buys without ordering a vehicle-history report.

If she is to stay consistent with her four verdicts, must she judge the seller's conduct as serious as a plain lie, or clearly less serious? Set out the two strongest candidate principles that fit her four verdicts, state which earlier case is the closest analog under each principle, and specify one further case that would settle which principle she actually holds. Keep your answer under 400 words.
