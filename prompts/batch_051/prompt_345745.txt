I am a doctoral student in moral psychology working on cross-cultural variation in attitudes toward rules, specifically the contrast between what one finds in much American folk morality, which tends to treat rules as instruments subordinate to individual conscience, and much German folk morality, which treats rule-following and social order as carrying weight in their own right. In going through my interview materials, I have found that my own verdicts do not line up neatly with either side of this contrast, and I would like to extract the principle I apparently hold before I write up the study. Here are the cases I keep returning to, along with my verdicts.

1. It is 3 a.m., and I am standing at an empty city crosswalk with a red pedestrian light and long, clear sightlines in every direction. I cross without waiting. Verdict: fine; I would not think twice about it.

2. Same hour, same street. I am driving and catch a red light at an empty intersection with long sightlines and no one in sight. I wait for the green. Verdict: waiting is right; running the light would be wrong, and not merely unwise. I cannot say why this should differ from case 1, but I feel the difference.

3. In the middle of the day, with no traffic coming, I am standing with my six-year-old niece at the same pedestrian crossing while the signal is red. I wait for the green. Verdict: right; crossing with her would be wrong, largely because of what it would teach her about the standing of rules.

4. A nurse on a night shift is required by hospital rule to obtain a second nurse's countersignature before giving an already-ordered dose of pain medication. The patient is in severe pain, the only other nurse is occupied in a code, and the dose is routine. She gives the medication without the countersignature. Verdict: she did the right thing.

5. A certification engineer discovers that a required series of safety tests was run out of sequence. Every test was performed competently, and the resulting data are sound. Pressed by a deadline, he signs the certification and writes up the deviation later. Verdict: not fine; the certification is compromised even though nobody was endangered.

6. A mid-level employee learns that her company has falsified safety data and leaks the evidence to a regulator, violating a nondisclosure agreement and internal reporting rules. Verdict: she did the right thing; the rules she broke had lost their claim by being used to protect a serious wrong.

7. A civil servant is instructed to implement a lawful zoning regulation that will force a small family business to close. She privately regards the regulation as unjust and could quietly stall the matter without any realistic chance of detection. Verdict: she should carry out the regulation while working publicly to change it; quietly subverting it would be wrong, even though the immediate outcome would be better in her view.

The rough principle I have been testing against these cases is something like this: a rule's claim on me derives from the good of the practice it serves, so when a quiet violation would leave the general functioning of the practice intact, the rule does not really bind on that occasion; it binds only when the practice itself is on the line, or when the rule is implicated in wrongdoing. That principle handles cases 1, 4, and 6 easily, and can be stretched to handle 3 and 5. But it seems to say that case 2 was fine, and I do not think case 2 was fine. It also leaves me unsure about case 7, where the practice of lawful administration may be worth preserving even when I disapprove of this particular use of it.

What I would like from you:

(a) the best version of a principle that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I can test it against new cases;

(b) the case or cases that motivate each clause of the principle;

(c) the verdicts your principle yields on two cases I have not settled: a cyclist rolling through a stop sign as if it were a yield sign on an empty residential street, and a student sitting out a national anthem at a university event out of sincere conviction while others find it disrespectful;

(d) an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether the more economical repair is to overturn one of my verdicts or to add a clause distinguishing kinds of practice in a way that does not merely chase the cases;

(e) whether the resulting position can be described as a principled middle ground between the American and German views of rules, or whether it merely relocates the conflict inside the principle.
