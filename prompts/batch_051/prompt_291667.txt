I'm a systems designer on live-service games — the kind of person who thinks in terms of content pipelines, progression curves, dailies, season passes, power creep, respecs, and whether an endgame holds once players have seen every biome. I have zero formal philosophy training. Lately I've been chewing on an old Bernard Williams argument, the Makropulos case: a character in a play who has lived a few hundred years is crushingly bored, and Williams's claim is that an endless life, even a healthy and pleasant one, would eventually become intolerably boring and meaningless. The reason he gives is that the things that make a life worth living — what he calls categorical desires, projects you want for their own sake and not just because you happen to be alive — get used up over time. Once they're gone, any new desire you formed would just be another instance of a type you already exhausted: a rerun, so it couldn't give you a real reason to keep going.

Restate that argument in the vocabulary I actually use. Translate it into game-design terms: content exhaustion, horizontal vs vertical progression, procedural generation, replay loops, endgame retention, novelty curves, live events, respecs, whatever fits. Go piece by piece. For each part, tell me whether it maps cleanly (the running-out-of-things-to-care-about part sure sounds like content exhaustion to me), whether you had to fudge it to make it fit, or whether it has no expression in my vocabulary at all. Then give me the residue report: I want to know whether Williams's point is just that an infinite life is a badly designed endgame — one that more content, better procedural generation, or endless horizontal progression could in prinicple fix — or whether there's a part of the argument that game-design language simply can't carry, because it's not about variety of content at all. My prior is that it's a content problem and infinite novelty solves it, so go out of your way to show me where that prior is smuggling in assumptions rather than doing real translation.
