Here are my gut reactions about when a choice reveals what a person really wants, and I want to know what account of utility they add up to. If someone orders the same sandwich every day for a year, I'm happy to say that reveals a stable preference for the sandwich. If someone hands over their wallet at gunpoint, I don't think that reveals they wanted to hand it over. If a store offers a $30 small speaker and a $70 large one, and the person picks the small one, but when an ugly $75 large one is added as a decoy they pick the $70 large one, I dont think their choices reveal a ranking; the decoy shouldn't change which speaker they really want. If someone says "I'd rather not eat the second slice" and then eats it, I still say the eating was what they wanted in the moment, but not what they judged best for themselves. If someone says they hate that they want a cigarette and then smokes one, I'd say the choice reveals a craving, not what they value. If a patient consents to surgery when told "90% survive" but refuses when told "10% die," I don't think either answer is their true preference—framing is doing the deciding. My working principle is the standard revealed-preference line, that utility is just a compact summary of actual choice, with no separate mental state of preference behind it. But these cases are pulling me apart. What principle, or small set of candidates, best captures my verdicts, and which verdicts would I have to overturn? Also, what does the resulting account say about the case I keep going back and forth on: someone who is drunk texts an ex, then says the next morning they didn't want to? Keep the answer under 800 words.
