A colleague of mine, a number theorist, keeps pressing the claim that mathematical research is structurally much closer to experimental science than to the textbook picture of theorem proving. In his analogy, a conjecture is a hypothesis, an attempted proof is an experimental run, and a counterexample is a surprising observation — one that mathematicians sometimes handle the way an experimentalist handles an anomalous reading, by adjusting the apparatus; in mathematics, he says, the apparatus is the definitions, since one can always declare a counterexample a monster and exclude it. From this he draws a quasi-empirical conclusion: mathematical claims should be appraised mainly by how well they have survived attempted refutation, so a heavily tested but still unproved conjecture deserves to be treated as a respectable item of knowledge, not merely as an open problem. I would like the analogy itself audited.

Please answer as a philosopher of mathematical practice. Start by brainstorming the main correspondences and likely disanalogies. Then give: (a) an explicit mapping from the parts of experimental science to the parts of mathematical practice; (b) the most important points where the analogy breaks; (c) a separation of the disanalogies that genuinely undermine his quasi-empirical conclusion from those that are superficial; and (d) a verdict on whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are in view, and what weaker claim about the fallibility or quasi-empirical character of mathematics would be better supported. Throughout, mark which claims are settled among people working on mathematical practice and which are genuinely contested, and please do not present a contested view as the consensus.
