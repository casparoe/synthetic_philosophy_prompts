I am a staff site reliability engineer with no formal philosophy background. My working vocabulary is incident management: service-level objectives, error budgets, observability, golden signals, alerts and pages, runbooks and playbooks, blameless post-incident review, toil, mean time to detection and recovery, and the everyday distinction between a dependency that is quietly doing its job and one that has suddenly become visible as something to be investigated. I want you to restate Heidegger's analysis of readiness-to-hand and the breakdown of equipment, as developed in Being and Time, in that vocabulary. Treat this as a translation exercise rather than an analogy or a lesson in Heidegger: work through the relevant cluster of notions — readiness-to-hand, presence-at-hand, equipment, assignment or reference, the whole of involvements, circumspection, and the specific steps through which broken equipment moves from unobtrusive to conspicuous to standing obstinately in the way — and give each the closest construct my framework has, noting where the fit is clean, where you had to stretch my terms, and where you had to introduce something my vocabulary cannot natively express. Then produce the residue report: list what did not survive the translation, and for each item say whether it is (a) a real distinction incident management is missing and should want, (b) a philosophical commitment my framework is entitled to set aside, or (c) a place where the original thought is less determinate than its reputation suggests. Conclude with a judgment on whether Heidegger's account of breakdown contains anything that a mature incident-management practice does not already know or assume, and what specifically it would add if it does.
