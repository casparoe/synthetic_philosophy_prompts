For an online philosophy of science reading group, I'm assembling a session on the counterfactual analysis of dispositions: roughly, the idea that for a glass to be fragile just is for it to be true that if it were struck, it would break. Please write a ten-question sorting quiz titled "Which Theory of Dispositions Do You Actually Hold?" together with a scoring key. The quiz should sort participants by their verdicts on concrete cases, not by the labels they like, so no question may simply ask whether someone accepts a named theory.

Build the quiz around three ways in which the stimulus for a disposition and the disposition's characteristic manifestation can come apart. First, a would-be interference that removes the disposition just before the stimulus arrives, so the manifestation would not occur even though the object currently has the disposition. Second, a mask that leaves the disposition in place while blocking the manifestation. Third, a reverse interference that bestows the disposition just before the stimulus, so the manifestation would occur even though the object currently lacks the disposition. Each question should describe an everyday item (a glass, a match, a sugar cube, a poison, a wire, a switch) in one such scenario and offer two or three forced-choice verdicts.

You must include, one per question, at least these cases: a fragile glass that a guardian would turn to steel at the instant of any impact; the same glass packed in shock-absorbing foam; a sugar cube that would be swapped for an identical-looking insoluble cube just before being placed in water; a poison that produces no symptoms because an antidote is present; and a harmless drink that would be made toxic the moment before anyone ingested it. The remaining cases are yours to choose, but each should be plainly a case of removal, masking, or bestowal.

The answer options in each question should track commitments on three things: whether the object has the disposition, whether the counterfactual "if the stimulus occurred, the effect would occur" is true, and whether removal, masking, and bestowal are equally serious problems for the simple counterfactual analysis or whether some are more easily repaired than others. The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least six positions: the unqualified simple counterfactual analysis; a normal-conditions or ceteris paribus repair; a repair that holds fixed the object's categorical basis throughout the stimulus (call it the fix-the-basis view); a graded view on which having a disposition is a matter of how the object would manifest across a weighted range of possible stimulus conditions rather than any single counterfactual; dispositional essentialism, on which dispositions are fundamental powers not reducible to counterfactuals or categorical bases; and a deflationary view on which disposition ascriptions are useful shorthand for regularities and do not report intrinsic properties of the object.

After the scoring key, add a short diagnostic note. Flag any pair of the six positions that the quiz cannot reliably separate, and for any answer pattern that appears in the scoring grid but corresponds to a view nobody defends, say whether that combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply underexplored. The audience is non-specialists who have read one general introduction to the simple counterfactual analysis but not the scholarly literature on interference cases.
