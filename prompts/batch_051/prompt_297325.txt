I have a standing policy about testimony for claims that would, if true, overturn settled science. Whenever someone — even someone I otherwise regard as careful — reports an apparently well-documented anomaly, I decline to inspect the evidence and leave my prior essentially unchanged. Last month a colleague pressed a paper on me reporting apparent telepathy in a controlled setting; I refused to read it, on the grounds that no study of that kind could move me and that reading it would simply cost me time. I have always thought of this as a rational filter, not a bias: priors matter, and my attention is finite.

Recently, however, I came across the fact that Henry Sidgwick, whose Methods of Ethics is one of the most scrupulously argued works I have read, spent years helping to lead the Society for Psychical Research, patiently collecting and critiquing testimony and experimental reports concerning just this sort of phenomenon. On the account I encountered, he was not credulous: he did not claim that the phenomena were established, and he was alert to fraud, suggestion, and self-deception. But he apparently judged that the accumulated evidence was good enough to make careful investigation a rational obligation rather than a waste of time.

I do not want to reopen the question whether telepathy exists; I want to know whether Sidgwick's example undermines my policy. Is refusing to examine evidence on grounds of prior improbability a defensible division of epistemic labor, or is it a bias protected by the very low prior it invokes? Under what conditions does such a policy fail badly, and how would I recognize those conditions from the inside? If the blanket policy is not defensible, what is the strongest revision that preserves what I care about — sparing finite attention for claims more likely to pay off — without making the relevant claim unfalsifiable for me in practice?
