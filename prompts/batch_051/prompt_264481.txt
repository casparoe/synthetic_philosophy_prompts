I'm a novelist, not a mathematician, but my current book has a once-famous set theorist as a secondary character, and I've been trying to get the way he thinks right. I fell down a rabbit hole reading about whether numbers can be reduced to sets, and I've ended up with a working rule for what makes a mathematical question a real question rather than a trick of notation. My rule goes like this: a mathematical identity or membership question counts as well-posed if (1) it can be stated in the normal notation of some settled branch of mathematics, (2) a least one standard convention fixes an answer, so a competent mathematician could just check, and (3) the answer doesn't flip when you switch between equally good conventions — if two set-ups both capture everything we care about and they give opposite verdicts, then the question was never really about the objects themselves.

Now here is the borderline case I can't shake. Under the von Neumann encoding, where zero is the empty set and each number is the set of all its predecessors, four is the set containing zero, one, two and three, so two is an element of four. Under the Zermelo encoding, where each number is just the singleton of the previous number, four is the singleton of three, and two is not an element of four. My structuralist friend says neither answer is the real one: numbers aren't sets at all, they're roles in a single structure, so asking whether two is an element of four is like asking whether the second baseman is an element of the shortstop — a catagory mistake. So I'm stuck. Does the question whether two is an element of four actually qualify as well-posed by my three criteria? If it does, what should I tell my fictional set theorist the answer is, and why? If it doesn't, which criterion or criteria should I revise, and can I revise them without throwing out ordinary questions like whether three is less than five? Also, please write the answer in American English.
