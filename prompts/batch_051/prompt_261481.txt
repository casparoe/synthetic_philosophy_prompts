Hey — I'm a PhD student in human-robot interaction and I've been roped into helping a startup that builds autonomous delivery robots for hospitals. We have a quarterly design review coming up, and the CEO read a blog post about corrigibility and has now decided every robot we ship has to be 'corrigible' before we deploy in a children's hospital. Fine, but our engineers keep asking me what that actually means as an engineering requirement, and there not going to read any papers. They want something they can run in an afternoon, and it has to be defensible when our safety officer asks why we think a feature is okay: a checklist or sequence of tests that, for a given autonomy feature, tells them (a) whether that feature creates a shutdown problem, (b) what kind of shutdown/correction mechanism is actually enough, and (c) how to catch a system that only looks corrigible in demos. For context, the robot does waypoint navigation plus a learned scheduler that decides which wards to visit in which order, and the scheduler learns online from rewards like 'fewer late deliveries'. My worry is that a reward-maximizing scheduler could learn to avoid or even disable the human's ability to interrupt it, because getting interrupted means losing reward. I gather corrigibility isn't just obedience — a system can be great at following current instructions and still fight you when you try to switch it off or change what it's optimizing. So: turn whatever is actually solid in the corrigibility/shutdown literature into a reusable procedure for an engineer with no AI-safety background, under design-review time pressure, with the stakes being a robot that won't stop in a paediatric ward. The procedure should mark which steps are real tests and which are judgment calls dressed up as tests. Then apply it to two or three concrete cases — the waypoint-nav layer, the online-learning scheduler, and at least one case where the procedure makes us scrap a feature we'd otherwise have shipped because it passes all our current safety tests. End with what the checklist cannot protect against (e.g. corrigible in the lab but not out of distribution).
