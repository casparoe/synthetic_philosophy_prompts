I am a distributed-systems engineer. My working vocabulary is consensus protocols and fault tolerance: nodes and messages, rounds and timeouts, quorums and thresholds, safety and liveness, honest versus Byzantine participants, Sybil attacks, and the difference between permissioned and permissionless membership. I am fluent in that vocabulary, but I know Habermas only in broad outline. I would like you to restate Habermas's account of communicative rationality and the ideal speech situation in that vocabulary, as faithfully as possible. In particular, translate: the distinction between communicative and strategic action; validity claims; what it means for participants to be free and equal in discussion; the idea that only the unforced force of the better argument may move participants; and the resulting notion of a rationally motivated consensus. Say what corresponds to a node, a message, a round, agreement, and a protocol violation, and what the ideal speech situation would look like if it were specified as a consensus protocol.

Then give me a residue report. Which parts of Habermas's position translate cleanly? Which parts can be rendered in protocol terms only if you distort them, and what exactly gets distorted? Which parts have no counterpart at all? In particular, discuss whether the difference between a consensus that is merely reached and a consensus that is rationally warranted survives the translation, and whether its loss is a blind spot in my vocabulary that I ought to correct or a sign that this part of Habermas's picture is less determinate than it looks. Do not smuggle normative content into the protocol description or recast "actually accepted" as "accepted because valid"; I want the translation to be honest about what the machinery cannot say.
