I'm retired now—chemistry mostly, though I ended up teaching a lot of first-year physics—and I've been reading philosophy of science and some cognitive science about student "misconceptions." One thing I can't settle is what makes a wrong answer a theory rather than just an error. I've collected these for years, and my gut reliably sorts them, but I've never been able to state the prinicple behind the sorting. Here are my verdicts.

1. A student says a thrown baseball keeps moving after it leaves your hand because the hand put a force or "impetus" into it, and the impetus gradually runs out. Theory-like. She uses it to reason: she'll tell you the ball goes straight until the impetus is spent and then falls, and she applies the same story to a hockey puck and to a spaceship.

2. A student says plants build most of their mass out of stuff they pull up from the soil. I want to call this theory-like too, but if I'm honest it's a single causal claim, not a little engine that cranks out further predictions. Hmm.

3. A student says the current in a series circuit gets used up as it passes through each bulb, so the first bulb in line gets more than the second. Theory-like. Wrong, but it generates a whole family of predictions and explains why the first bulb should be brighter in her mind's eye.

4. A student explains the Moon's phases by saying the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. Theory-like, even though it's just one story. The underlying shadow-and-light schema is doing real work here.

5. A student writes that chemical bonds release energy when they break, and that's why ATP powers the cell and why burning fuel gives off heat. I go back and forth on this one. It's systematic, and it spans biology and chemistry, but it may just be a slogan the textbooks handed him rather than a real explanatory model. Gut says not theory-like, and I can't defend why 4 feels theory-like but 5 doesn't.

6. A student looks at a distance-time graph, sees the line going up, and says the object is accelerating, even though it's moving at constant speed. Not theory-like. It's a decoding error about a representation, not about how the world works.

7. A student writes m/s for acceleration on an exam question after a flawless derivation. Not theory-like. Just a slip.

8. A student says that if you drop a heavy ball and a light ball together, the heavy one hits the ground first because it has "more force in it." Theory-like.

9. A student says a table doesn't push upward on a book resting on it; the table is merely in the way. To push, something has to be alive or at least powered. Theory-like. It's part of a whole "active agent" picture of force, and the same student applies it to rockets, walls, and why boats float.

There are more, but that's the set.

My tentative principle is: a wrong answer is theory-like if the student uses it to generate answers to new cases, rather than just reproducing it as a fixed answer. But case 4 doesn't much generate new answers and I still call it theory-like, while case 5 does generate answers and I'm not convinced it's theory-like. So the principle looks wrong, or at least incomplete.

What I'd like from you: the best principle you can give, or a small set of candidate principles stated precisely, that captures my sorting—ideally with each clause motivated by one of the cases. Then tell me which of my verdicts that principle can't accommodate and which it forces me to overturn or revise. And apply it to a case I didn't list: a student who says we have winter because Earth is farther from the Sun in winter. I think that one is theory-like, but it might just be case 4 with a different season. Please keep your answer under 900 words.
