Our local common has a deed from 1873. The clause everyone gets into fights about reads:

'For so much of the Common as lies unallotted, any member who shall by his own industry and labour improve it—by clearing, planting, building upon, or otherwise turning it to account—shall acquire the same as his private holding; provided always that no member shall take so much of the Common, or a part so placed, as leaves the rest without a portion of equal quality and quantity left in common for their like use.'

Doran, a newer member, used to design wind farms. Last year he put three 90-metre turbines along the highest ridge of the unallotted common. He fenced nothing off; sheep still graze under the towers. But the ridge was the only place were the wind is strong enough to make a turbine economical, and he's now selling the electricity and has registered the carbon credits in his own name. He says the electricity is the fruit of his labour under the first clause (surveying, hauling gravel, building the access track, maintaining the machines), and that the proviso can't be triggered because the wind still blows for everyone and nobody can be said to own wind anyway. The rest of us say the best wind sites are simply gone, and that wind and carbon credits weren't part of what 'the Common' meant in 1873.

Work through what the deed requires here. Give me the verdict you'd get from the plain words, from the purpose of the clause, and from what the drafters would have said if you'd sat them down in front of a turbine—and then tell me which approach should win and why. In particular, be honest about whether the proviso is a depletion condition or an opportunity condition, because that's where I think the whole case turns.
