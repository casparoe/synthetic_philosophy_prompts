I'm a high school student trying to think carefully about Frankfurt-style cases and the principle of alternative possibilities. Here is a variation I haven't seen spelled out, and I want to know how it changes the dialectic. In the standard case, Jones votes for candidate A on his own while Black stands ready to force him to vote A if he were about to vote B; since Black would have intervened, Jones couldn't have done otherwise, yet he seems responsible for the vote. Now remove Black. Instead, Jones himself built and activated a device months ago that detects whether he is about to vote B and, if so, directly stimulates his brain so that he votes A. At the booth the device never activates: Jones votes A on his own, for his own reasons. At the moment of voting, he cannot do otherwise, because the device he built would have closed off the alternative. He did, however, have an earlier alternative: he could have chosen not to build or activate the device. I want to know whether this self-imposed version strengthens, weakens, or leaves unchanged the Frankfurt-style case against the principle of alternative possibilities. Specifically, what should a defender of the principle say here, and what should a Frankfurt-style compatibilist say? Does the fact that the obstacle is self-imposed matter morally, given that the causal facts at the moment of choice are the same as in the original case?
