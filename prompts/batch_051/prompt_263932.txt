For a grad seminar on welfare economics, I want the list of requirements a theory of preference and utility has to satisfy before we're allowed to say that someone choosing x revealed that x is what makes her better off. For each requirement give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just the tastes of particular camps (ordinalists vs cardinals, econometricians vs philosophers, whatever). Cover at least: menu dependence, framing and default effects, intransitive choices, preferences that look constructed on the spot, choices under addiction or acute scarcity, the gap between utility as a compact summary of choice and utility as a welfare claim, and the double-counting worry where the same choice data builds the preference and then tests it. If some subset of these demands is jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, you can't treat choice data as the only admissible evidence while also ruling that some choices don't count, unless you add a further principle that isnt itself choice data — spell that out, because the conflicts are the real content. Dont waste space scoring current theories; the requirements and where they collide are the point.
