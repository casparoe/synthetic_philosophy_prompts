I am a retired natural scientist — forty years in an instrument-heavy, quantitative field, most of it spent turning phenomena into numbers that others could in principle check — and I have come to philosophy only recently, largely through C. S. Peirce. I am now trying to write the one essay I would like to leave for younger colleagues who are curious about philosophy but wary of it: an essay on what Peirce's account of inquiry — the fixation of belief, the method of science, fallibilism, and the pragmatic maxim — has to say about the way science is actually practiced today. I do not want a survey with an even temperature; I want the essay to have a spine, and I am asking you to help me decide where that spine should run.

I have been turning over a handful of candidate problems. They strike me as genuinely open rather than settled, and each bears, if sometimes obliquely, on practice. I would like you to rank them — adding or discarding as you see fit — and, more importantly, to defend the ordering.

Here are the candidates as I would now formulate them.

First, the scope of the pragmatic maxim. As I understand it, the maxim says a conception of an object is exhausted by the consequences it might conceivably have for practice. Read flatly, that seems to make the meaning of a disposition — the fragility of a glass, the toxicity of a compound, the causal power of a field — depend on occasions that may never arise. Since most of science traffics in precisely this would-be content, much hangs on whether the maxim can absorb subjunctive or dispositional meaning without quietly collapsing into the view that only what is actually measured is meaningful.

Second, the institutional question. Peirce distinguishes the method of science from the methods of tenacity, authority, and the a priori, and he claims that the method of science alone holds belief answerable to something independent of what we would like to believe, so that it carries within itself a distinction between getting matters right and getting them wrong. But the institutions that now actually regulate scientific belief — peer review, preregistration, independent replication, competitive funding, the informal authority of senior investigators — look to me like a mixture of methods. I do not know which of them are the method of science and which are authority or fashion wearing scientific clothes, nor whether the self-correcting character survives that diagnosis. If it does not, my imagined readers inherit a harder problem than Peirce faced.

Third, convergence. Peirce ties truth to the opinion that inquiry would settle on in the long run, and he makes that convergence part of the rationale for the method of science. Modern science, however, keeps producing cases in which empirically equivalent descriptions persist side by side, most visibly in quantum foundations. Should fallibilism be cut loose from the convergence claim? If truth need not be convergent, what becomes of science's ambition to force agreement; if it must be, what should a working scientist make of the apparent counterexamples?

Fourth, the maxim and theoretical entities. A working scientist routinely commits to entities whose practical bearings are entirely mediated — dark matter, fields, virtual particles, structure that may remain unobservable. Does the maxim, taken seriously, push toward an operationalism that would treat such commitments as empty until they yield a measurement difference, or does it permit posits whose significance is carried by their structural role and their delayed practical yield? Where is the line between a meaningful posit and a vacuous one?

Fifth, the psychology of doubt. Peirce makes inquiry begin in a doubt that is involuntary — an unease one struggles to escape — and he is dismissive of doubt that is merely assumed for the occasion. But science has organized itself partly around the deliberate manufacture of doubt: falsification attempts, adversarial collaborations, mechanisms designed to make people distrust results they would otherwise believe. Is manufactured doubt genuine in his sense? And if what we now suspect about motivated reasoning means doubt can be indefinitely suppressed or indefinitely feigned, does the psychological machinery behind the fixation account still carry the normative weight it was built to carry?

I care less about the final ordering than about the reasoning. The criteria I would have you apply — though I will be persuaded by an argument for weighting them differently — are these: whether progress on the problem would change how a working scientist actually practices, interprets her results, or explains to the public what science's authority rests on; whether the problem yields to conceptual work rather than to more data; how many of the other problems depend on it; and whether it is comparatively neglected outside Peirce scholarship.

Please be explicit about the trade-offs, and end with a decisive recommendation: which two or three of these problems should form the spine of the essay, and why those rather than the others. A thousand words or so is about right.
