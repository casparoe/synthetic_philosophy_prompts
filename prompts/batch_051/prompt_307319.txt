I’m trying to write out the desiderata for a theory of basic self-awareness, taking Avicenna’s flying-man thought experiment from the De anima part of The Healing as the central case. In my paraphrase: imagine a fully developed adult created all at once in a void, arms and legs spread so they cannot touch, eyes blindfolded so they see nothing, and with no sensations from outside or from inside the body. Avicenna says this person will still affirm that he exists, but will not affirm the existence of any limb, organ, or bodily thing. What does a theory of self-awareness actually have to get right if this case is possible? I want the full requirements list: the cases it has to handle, including variants where body sense is absent, distorted, or expanded; the distinctions it cannot collapse, such as self as subject versus body as object, self-attribution versus bodily location, and existence versus essence; and the empirical findings it cannot contradict, such as the rubber-hand illusion, out-of-body experiences, somatoparaphrenia, and depersonalization. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate the common contenders—for example, views that identify the self with the whole body, with brain processes, with a narrative self, or with higher-order reflection.
