A guy in my philosophy of religion reading group brought in this argument and now I can't decide whether divine simplicity survives it or not. It looks like it forces a choice between denying that God is merciful and saying justice and mercy are literally the same thing, and neither sounds survivable. Where does the chain actually break?

(1) On divine simplicity, God has no parts of any kind, including no distinct attributes: any attribute that's really in God is identical to God, and therefore identical to every other attribute that's really in God.
(2) God is just.
(3) God is merciful.
(4) Justice and mercy are not the same feature of an act: an act can be just without being merciful (collecting a debt you could have forgiven) and merciful without being just (forgiving with no regard to desert).
(5) So justice and mercy are two distinct attributes really in God.
(6) From (1) and (5), justice and mercy are identical to each other.
(7) If they're identical, then whatever is true of one is true of the other, so a purely just act would just be a purely merciful act — which (4) says is false.
(8) So divine simplicity is stuck with either God not actually being both just and merciful, or with justice and mercy being one single attribute, in which case the distinction we draw is an illusion.

My friend says that's a reductio: a doctrine that compels you to either drop God's mercy or collapse justice into mercy has refuted itself. I'd most like to keep (1) and (4), but I'm not sure I'm allowed both. If the real problem is in (5), say exactly what "really in God" has to mean for the step to fail, and what that does to claims like "God is just."
