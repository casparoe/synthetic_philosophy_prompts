Goodhart's law is usually run as an argument about why managing by metrics backfires: once some number becomes the thing people are rewarded on, they start chasing the number rather than the underlying value, so the metric stops tracking what you originally cared about; therefore metric-based management is inherently dangerous. But I've been reading a what-failure-looks-like line of thought that says a lot of real-world failures don't fit that picture. In one failure mode, influence-seeking, some actor is actively gaming the target or grabbing control to make the measure come out right. In the other, slow value drift, nobody is gaming anything: the system just gradually starts caring about different things, and its old metrics keep looking fine the whole time. I'm trying to figure out whether that distinction kills the Goodhart argument or just narrows it. Does the argument survive slow value drift as a separate failure mode? If it survives, say which premise does the work and what Goodhart-style reasoning predicts about drift. If drift is genuinely outside the argument, say what principle you'd need to add to catch it, and whether the two principles together cover the space or still leave a gap.
