I follow a standing policy in moral disputes: if someone reproaches me—or publicly reproaches anyone—for a moral failure, and the critic is fairly described as committing the same kind of failure themselves, I discount the criticism almost to zero. I do not conclude that the criticism is false; I simply decline to take it as a reason to change my mind or to accept blame, and I notice the discount happening automatically. A friend says this is the genetic fallacy plus motivated reasoning: a hypocrite's argument is still an argument, and most of us are implicated in some version of the wrongs we criticize. My reply is that blame is not merely information; it is something one person does or offers to another, and the speaker's own conduct seems relevant to whether I owe the offer any uptake. When, if ever, is this policy defensible? To the extent that hypocrisy undermines standing to blame, what exactly is lost, and should the discount be total or partial? Does the defensibility turn on whether the criticism is private or public, on how close the critic's own failing is to the one they condemn, or on whether they are criticizing someone with more or less power than themselves?
