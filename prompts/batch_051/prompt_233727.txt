I was recently elected chair of my department's grievance committee, and I have become unexpectedly preoccupied with a question I had never before had reason to take seriously: when does saying something make something the case, and when is saying something just talk? A colleague tells me this is the territory of speech act theory, but before reading any of it I decided to record my own verdicts about a range of cases, because I could not state the principle I have apparently been using. I would like to know what principle, if any, captures these verdicts — stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it — what that principle predicts for a case I cannot settle, and which of my verdicts, if any, it forces me to revise.

Here are the cases.

A licensed officiant says to a couple before witnesses, "I now pronounce you married." The couple's legal and social standing changes.

A guest at the same wedding later says exactly those words to the couple, with evident sincerity. Nothing changes.

At a committee meeting I chair, I say, "This hearing is adjourned," and the meeting is over. If a junior member of the committee says the same sentence while I am still in the room, the meeting is not over.

A colleague says to me, "I promise to have the report to you by Friday," and means it. She has made a promise, even if she later misses the deadline.

Another colleague says the same sentence with no intention of producing the report, purely to stop me from asking. I do not want to say that he made and broke a promise; I want to say there was no promise at all, only something in the shape of one.

A colleague says, "I apologize for losing your book," without an ounce of regret, solely to end an awkward silence. Unlike the promise case, I still count this as an apology — a hollow one, but an apology, and one I can accept or decline.

A witness takes an oath in court to tell the truth and later lies. I would not say that no oath ever occurred because the witness was insincere; the oath was taken and then violated.

A judge at a dinner party says in an obviously theatrical tone, "I hereby sentence you to twenty years." Nobody is lawfully imprisoned, but the utterance is not inert either: it works as a joke, or falls flat as one, and it changes the dinner conversation rather than the law.

An actor playing a judge says the same sentence in a realistic courtroom scene. Again nothing legal changes, but I would not call it merely a joke; the stage seems to suspend the ordinary machinery rather than misfire.

A parent says to a teenager, "I warn you, the pan is hot," when the teenager already knows it is hot. I still count it as a warning, just a redundant one.

A person signs a will, properly witnessed, containing the sentence "I hereby leave my watch to my nephew." The bequest occurs even if the watch is later lost before the person dies.

I say to a colleague, "I bet you twenty dollars the vote will be seven to five," but she is absorbed in her phone and does not hear me. I would say no bet was made. If she hears and says, "You're on," the bet exists even if I had spoken in jest and expected her to laugh rather than accept.

The case I keep going back and forth on is this: a tenant says to a landlord, "I forgive you for not fixing the heat." The landlord does not respond and later says he never asked for forgiveness and does not accept it. I cannot tell whether the forgiveness occurred when the words were uttered, whether it depends on the landlord's accepting it, or whether it requires an admission of fault first.

My working rule so far has been something like: an utterance changes something when there is a recognized convention for changing that thing with those words, the right person says them in the right circumstances, and the speaker means them. But I can already see that my verdicts do not sit neatly with that rule. Please test the rule against the cases, repair it into a real principle or a small set of candidate principles, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why giving them up is the right trade.
