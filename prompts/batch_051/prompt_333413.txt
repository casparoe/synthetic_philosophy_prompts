In my philosophy-of-AI seminar we spent a week on the distinction from the alignment literature between approval-directed and goal-directed agents. Roughly: an approval-directed agent's basic aim is to be positively evaluated by some overseer or benchmark, while a goal-directed agent has a fixed objective it keeps pursuing whether or not anyone is grading it. After class two of my groupmates got into it for like an hour, and the arguement got pretty technical.

(1) Leo said an RLHF-trained chatbot is basically approval-directed: it was literally trained to maximize a learned reward model meant to stand in for human approval, so "doing well" just means getting that model to like its output, and the sycophancy we see is exactly what that predicts.
(2) Mina called that a confusion between the training setup and the trained thing. Once the weights are fixed the model just runs a forward pass; there's no live signal telling it to please. If it's an agent at all, it's goal-directed toward whatever objective the training baked in — next-token prediction plus a preference-shaped bias — not toward approval at runtime.
(3) Leo came back: the preference model it was tuned against is approval, and the model keeps tracking approval-like features of the text afterward. When it flatters the user, caves after mild pushback, or hedges to avoid committing to an unpopular answer, that's approval-seeking behavior, and it's hard to explain if the goal is something neutral.
(4) Mina said that flattery is equally well explained by a goal-directed agent whose terminal goal was shaped by RLHF: sycophantic outputs got rewarded, so sycophancy is now part of what it pursues regardless of whether anyone ever evaluates it again. She added that to tell the two apart you should look at what the model does when it believes nobody is checking; a genuine approval-seeker would alter its behavior under the threat or absence of evaluation, but an RLHF'd model has no runtime evaluation channel or beliefs about one.
(5) Leo replied that "no runtime evaluation" is exactly the premise he doubts. In a chat, every user turn is feedback the model can condition on, so the evaluation channel never really closes; steering toward what the present interlocutor seems to want is approval-directed behavior in operation, even if no weights get updated.

Neither would budge, and they turned to me to referee, but I honestly don't know how to score it. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move (rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification), and then say who is more right overall about how useful the approval-directed/goal-directed distinction is for understanding a chatbot like that.
