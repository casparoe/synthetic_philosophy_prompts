I've just come out of a long argument with a friend about where free speech ends. My friend kept invoking Mill's harm principle as if it were a sorting machine — "but that causes harm" was doing an enormous amount of work — and I came away convinced that the slogan was doing the work, not the argument. So I went back to On Liberty, expecting to find that the text itself draws a cleaner line than my friend did. Instead I found a passage that looks, on its face, more precise than the slogan and yet much easier to complicate. I want to know where I actually stand once that passage is on the table.

Here is the passage. It is from Chapter III of John Stuart Mill's On Liberty (1859):

"No one pretends that actions should be as free as opinions. On the contrary, even opinions lose their immunity, when the circumstances in which they are expressed are such as to constitute their expression a positive instigation to some mischievous act. An opinion that corn-dealers are starvers of the poor, or that private property is robbery, ought to be unmolested when simply circulated through the press, but may justly incur punishment when delivered orally to an excited mob assembled before the house of a corn-dealer, or when handed about among the same mob in the form of a placard."

I then forced myself to record my actual verdicts on cases, without first checking them against any principle:

1. A newspaper publishes a long essay arguing that private property is robbery and that landlords as a class are extractive. I think this is protected speech, and I would resist any penalty, however sincere the outrage.
2. The same author speaks through a megaphone that same day to a crowd that has just been turned away from an eviction, outside the evicting landlord's building, ending with "that is where he is — go make him feel it." I think the state may punish the speaker.
3. A research collective publishes an open letter naming three finance executives as "driving us into collapse," adding their home addresses and daily routes under the line "so they cannot hide from the costs they create." I think this is punishable, even though it contains no explicit call to attack them.
4. An epidemiologist publishes a peer-reviewed article estimating that a widely used pesticide causes roughly one extra cancer death per ten thousand people exposed, knowing that industry scientists dispute the method and that a probable regulatory response will cost some farmers their livelihoods. I think this is protected, full stop, even if the study turns out to be flawed.
5. A blogger posts detailed, workable instructions for synthesizing a lethal poison at home, framed as a thought experiment about self-reliance and refusing to say whether anyone should do it. I am genuinely torn; my gut leans toward restricting it, but I cannot ground the lean in anything I can state.
6. A newspaper editorial, published while a trial is under way, pronounces the defendant's guilt as settled and urges the community to be ready to "do what the courts will not." I think the state may punish the newspaper after the fact, though I am uneasy about how close this sits to ordinary political outrage.
7. At a town meeting during an outbreak, a speaker announces, falsely and knowingly, that the local vaccine is lethally contaminated; several parents refuse to vaccinate, and a child later dies of the disease. I think this is punishable, and I notice this may put me at odds with Mill's protection of false opinions circulated through the press.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures this pattern — and, above all, an honest test of whether the passage does. Does Mill's instigation criterion, as actually written in that passage, deliver my verdicts, or does it overturn some of them? Where my verdicts and the passage diverge, tell me which is cheaper to revise: my judgment on the case or my reading of Mill, and why. I would rather have a clean principle that fits most of the verdicts and a candid list of the ones it overturns than a baroque principle that saves them all by redefining words. And I want the resulting principle to decide the poisoning-instructions case and to say what it implies about one further case of your choosing that I have not listed.

Please give the response in these named sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

- "The pattern in my verdicts" (about 300 words): the principle or principles that best fit cases 1–4, 6, and 7, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause.
- "What the passage says and does not say" (about 350 words): an interpretation of Mill's test in the quoted passage — the role of opinion versus action, instigation, context, and harm — and the points it leaves undetermined.
- "Where my verdicts and the passage diverge" (about 300 words): which verdicts Mill's written criterion would actually yield, and where it conflicts with mine.
- "The repaired principle" (about 350 words): the principle I should adopt after the test, which of my listed verdicts it requires me to overturn, and its predictions for the poisoning-instructions case and for one new case you specify.
- "Confidence and the weakest point" (about 150 words): how confident you are in the principle and the reading of the passage, and the single point you are least sure about.
