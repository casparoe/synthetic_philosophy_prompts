I'm a backend engineer, not a philosopher, but I've been going down an AI alignment rabbit hole and something about the corrigibility/shutdown problem has me stuck. The argument as I understand it goes like this: if you build an agent that maximizes a utility function, it has an instrumental reason to resist being shut down, because an agent that's off can't act in the world to bring about whatever it values. So the off switch isn't neutral hardware; from the agent's point of view it's an obstacle. People even have formal results where a naive utility-maximizer learns to disable the button or manipulate the operator, because its policy depends on whether the shutdown signal will arrive. That all seems right to me, and it has the kind of "oh, obviously" quality that makes me think the argument is tracking something real rather than just being clever.

At the same time, my day-job intuition keeps rebelling. I write code all day, and the fix seems boringly simple: just make the system shut down when it receives a shutdown signal, and don't let its utility function range over anything about whether it's on or off. Make "being off" not a state it evaluates at all. A unix process doesn't prefer to keep running; it runs until it gets a SIGTERM, and then it's just gone. So I have this strong feeling that corrigibility is a ordinary engineering property—you specify the behavior you want, you test it, you handle the edge cases—and that the philosophical argument is overcomplicating what is really just a control-flow problem. But I also recognize that if the shutdown problem is right, my intuition is exactly the failure mode the authors are warning about: I'm picturing a deterministic tool rather than an optimizer that plans.

So my question is which one should yield, and why. I want both sides presented at their strongest before you say what you'd defend: is my intuition a naive artifact of thinking about processes instead of agents, or does the theory sneak in a particular assumption about what the agent's utility function is over (trajectories? world states? its own continued operation?) that my "just make it not care" fix is already rejecting? Is there a reading of corrigibility on which both are true, or is one side just wrong? And please don't just pick a winner—tell me what evidence, argument, or result would change your verdict, and where in the reasoning you think you're most likely to be making a mistake.
