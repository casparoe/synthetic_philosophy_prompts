I've got a student in my philosophy of biology seminar who keeps insisting that the genome literally is a computer program, and that CRISPR is therefore just debugging: you read the source code, spot the buggy line, patch it, and recompile the organism. He uses this to argue that people who worry about germline editing are being superstitious, because nobody thinks twice about fixing a typo. I don't want to just dismiss him, because the analogy is doing real work and its got some grip -- there's the genetic code, codons get 'read,' genes get knocked out, there are even regions annotated like comments. But I also don't trust the conclusion. Walk me through an actual audit. Give me the correspondence table: what in the cell is supposed to be the source code, the compiler, the hardware, the programmer, the bug, the patch? Then mark the places where the mapping breaks, and tell me which breaks actually matter for his conclusion and which are just cosmetic. In particular I want to know whether DNA has anything that plays the role of a symbol that means something independent of the machinery that reads it, or whether the reader and the text are caught in a causal loop that makes the whole programmer/program picture collapse. And if the computer analogy can't carry his conclusion, give me a better one -- recipe, score, whatever -- that keeps what's right in his view while showing what the debugging argument was quietly smuggling in.
