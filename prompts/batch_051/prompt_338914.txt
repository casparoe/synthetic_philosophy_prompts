I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a piece about how institutional definitions decide what gets to count as a language. A proposal I have been following goes like this. A national research council is setting up a fund to document endangered languages. Its published criteria are deliberately modality-neutral: to qualify as a language, a system must be a conventional symbol system with a combinatorial grammar, must be acquired natively by children in some human community, and must serve as the community's primary means of everyday communication. Nothing in the criteria mentions sound, speech, or writing. But the grant manual that accompanies the criteria tells reviewers to score proposals on two kinds of evidence: a phonemic inventory and a corpus of texts in a standard orthography. Sign languages—which have phonology organized by handshape, movement, location, and orientation rather than by sound, and which generally lack a widely adopted written form—meet the three criteria but repeatedly fail the manual. A deaf-led research group has proposed a fix: count handshape, movement, location, and orientation parameters as the phonemic inventory, and accept time-aligned video annotation with glosses as the required corpus. The council is considering three options: keep the manual unchanged, adopt the group's fix, or add a clause stating that the manual's evidentiary requirements are to be read as modality-neutral and interpreted by sign-language specialists.

Take the role of a linguist who works on sign languages and on how bureaucracies classify languages. Think like a systems analyst: brainstorm internally about who gains and who loses under each option, and about where the rules can first be gamed, before settling on a bottom line. Walk through what each option would do to (a) which languages actually get documented, (b) how grant applicants, research groups, and advocacy organizations would respond and where they would first game the rules, (c) how the public and adjacent fields would come to draw the line between language and non-language, and (d) what happens to young or emerging sign languages of the sort that arise when deaf children are brought together without fluent older signers. Along the way, say whether the group's proposed fix would itself distort how sign languages get described. Then say which option has the least damaging failure modes and whether those failure modes can be fixed without quietly reinstating the spoken-language bias the criteria were meant to remove.

Begin your answer with the bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; do not preface it or restate the question. Also state how confident you are in each main prediction and identify the one point you are least sure about.
