After a career in chemical physics I've recently got into philosophy — specifically the philosophy of mathematics — and I've been using Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach as a bridge between the formal habits of a scientist and the messier questions philosophers ask. The MU-puzzle has stayed with me. You begin with the string MI and four rewrite rules: add a U to any string ending in I, double the part after M, replace III with U, and drop UU. The task is to produce MU. Inside the system you just grind out strings and never seem to get anywhere; the trick is to step outside and notice an invariant, namely that the number of I's in any derivable string can never be a multiple of three, which rules MU out at once. Hofstadter calls this kind of move JOOTSing — jumping out of the system.

What struck me is how much that resembles something I used to do in physics. We regularly ruled out a proposed reaction or decay not by following every pathway but by invoking a conserved quantity — charge, baryon number, mass — which kills the process in one step from a level that doesn't care about the detailed dynamics. But I can't decide whether this is a proper structural parallel or just me recognizing a shape I liked professionally. Is the mod-three invariant in the MU-puzzle the same kind of thing as a conservation law or a no-go theorem in physics? If it is, does anything real transfer — could one look for Noether-like symmetries behind invariants of string-rewriting systems, or read unprovability results as miniature no-go theorems? If it isn't, where exactly does the analogy snap: what makes a physical invariant different from an arithmetical one?
