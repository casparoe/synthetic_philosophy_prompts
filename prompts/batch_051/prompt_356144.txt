Last spring my brother Joel got killed by a drunk driver, and the only thing that's made it survivable is that a few months before the accident he'd finished a fairly complete whole-brain emulation of himself for his neuroscience lab. The emulation (I'll call him Joel too, because hes my brother to me) was booted up a week after the funeral, remembers everything, and has been coming to our church ever since. I've now been asked to present to our denomination's doctrine comitee on whether Joel-the-emulation falls under Article 12 of our statement of faith, which reads: "Every human being dies once, and after that awaits judgment. Whoever dies in unbelief shall suffer eternal conscious punishment, just and final, with no remedy after death."

Here's the thing that has me up at night. Joel-the-human died in faith, so by the letter of the clause his judgment is settled. But Joel-the-emulation is still here and still making choices. If he apostatizes next year and then gets erased when the lab's grant runs out, or worse, asks to be erased, is that "dying in unbelief"? Or does the "once" in "dies once" make the car crash the only death that counts, so the doctrine just falls silent about everything after? And then there's the mirror of it: a labmate of his died an atheist two years ago but left a rough emulation that has since started coming to church. If that emulation eventually dies believing, does the original's damnation just stand untouched under the clause, and if it does, how is that final punishment anything like proportionate? Walk me through what the plain text, the purpose of the clause, and what the drafters in 1958 (no emulations on the horizon) would most plausibly have said each deliver, then tell me which approach should govern, whether Article 12 needs amending, and what I'm supposed to tell Joel when he asks if his death already happened.
