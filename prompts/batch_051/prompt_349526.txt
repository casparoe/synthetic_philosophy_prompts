Hey, I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, and i'm trying to put together a "which theory of dispositions do you actually hold?" sorting quiz for an intro metaphysics seminar. The students havent read any of the literature yet, so the point is to sort them by there verdicts on cases, not by which label sounds good to them.

Could you write me a 9-question quiz, each question centered on a concrete case, with two or three answer options each, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto at least these positions:

1. the simple conditional/counterfactual account (something is fragile iff it would break if struck, or dropped, or whatever the relevant test is);
2. the standard reformed conditional account that adds an intrinsic causal basis and some kind of "normal conditions" clause;
3. a categoricalism that says disposition talk reduces to the non-dispositional microstructural basis, so the disposition itself is nothing over and above the basis;
4. a robust dispositionalism where powers are fundamental and finks/masks are just more powers in the network, not refutations of the whole idea;
5. a deflationary or eliminativist option that treats disposition ascriptions as a useful façon de parler with no deep metaphysics behind them.

If you think the logical space forces a sixth leaf, add it, but only name it if you're confident it's a real defended position and not just an empty cell.

The questions should be built mostly from fink and mask cases, introduced in plain terms: a fragile glass with a guardian who would intervene the instant it was struck; a poison that kills but not when an antidote is present; a match with a coating that stops it from lighting; something that would lose its fragility as a direct result of being struck; an object that never manifests a disposition and whose supposed basis also never does anything. I especially want the quiz to pull apart the simple conditional account and the reformed conditional account — not every "would" sentence is the same — and I don't want the cases to smuggle in the causal-basis view by talking about "the underlying microstructure" as if that were uncontroversial.

For the scoring key, give me answer-pattern logic rather than just "mostly A means..." And flag any pairs of positions your instrument can't really separate with this number of questions, and note which combinations of answers are unoccupied because the view is incoherent, versus merely unattractive or unexplored. Keep it usable in a 50-minute session, so 8–10 questions max.

And in the key or notes, please separate what's actually settled among people who work on this stuff from what's genuinely contested — don't present something like "finks refute the simple account" as the consensus when plenty of people think the simple account was only ever a first pass that needed its ceteris paribus clause filled in properly. Its fine to say a view is now a minority position; just don't make it sound dead if it isn't.
