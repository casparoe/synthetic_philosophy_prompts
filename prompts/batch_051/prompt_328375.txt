Ok so I'm a PhD student in archaeology and I keep running into this thing in how my field talks about finds, and I want you to help me work out what its actually committed to. Act as an expert in philosophy of archaeology and philosophy of mind — someone who's thought a lot about how we infer minds, beliefs, and meanings from material things. Please think carefully and don't just give the standard "we can never know for sure" answer; I want the actual implicit theory, even if you have to reconstruct it.

Here's the practice I mean. When we find a flaked stone tool, everyone writes about it in resolutely functional terms: edge angles, use-wear, cutting efficiency, raw material constraints. Nobody says the knapper "believed" anything while making it, and nobody appeals to the maker's mental states to explain the shape. But when we find something whose practical function isn't obvious — a small carved bird, a pot deliberately broken and placed over a child's grave, red ochre smeared on a skull — the same papers flip into a completely different register: the object is "ritual" or "symbolic," and the write-up reconstructs beliefs, like gifts for an afterlife or ancestor veneration. The less efficiently designed the object seems, the more confidently we treat it as evidence of ideas rather than just behavior.

So my question: what theory of mind, meaning, and inference does that asymmetry presuppose? Does it presuppose that when objects aren't optimized for practical use, the best explanation is that they express beliefs or symbolic meanings? Does it presuppose that we can recover past intentional states from objects alone, without texts or informants? And is that consistent with the functional stance we take toward stone tools — why isn't the ritual interpretation just as much a projection of our own categories (ritual, belief, symbol) onto people who might not have sliced thier world that way?

Then tell me what would actually have to change in how we write up finds if we replaced that presupposition with a stricter alternative — say, one where we can only infer constraints, affordances, skills, and social practices, but not inner beliefs. What would be lost, and would any of what's lost be worth keeping? I care more about the real intellectual structure than about the standard "ritual is a residual category" complaint; push past that. To keep yourself honest, start by brainstorming two or three different candidate reconstructions of the practice's implicit theory, then commit to one and defend it.
