I work as a machine-learning engineer on fairness for systems that allocate things, such as credit, admissions slots, and service placements. My working vocabulary is predictions, outcome labels, protected attributes, calibration, demographic parity, equalized odds, individual fairness, counterfactual fairness, and the gap between a model's loss and the decision rule built on it. I have no formal philosophy training: I know roughly what the philosophers named below say, but I want you to take responsibility for the exegesis.

Restate the equality-of-what debate, the disagreement among egalitarians about the proper currency of equality, in that vocabulary. Cover at least these three positions: equality of welfare, understood as making people's happiness or preference satisfaction equal; Dworkin's equality of resources, with its auction of impersonal resources, its hypothetical insurance against bad brute luck, and its refusal to compensate people for the expensive tastes they choose; and the capability view associated with Sen and Nussbaum, on which what matters is neither resources nor satisfaction but the real freedoms people have to achieve functionings.

For each position, tell me (a) what exactly is being equalized when the view is put into an allocative system, (b) what would have to be measured or modeled to compute it, and (c) what could not be represented in my vocabulary even in principle. Then write the residue report, which is the part I care about most. In particular: does welfare map onto an outcome label, a latent utility, or something my systems cannot access at all? Can Dworkin's distinction between bad brute luck and chosen expensive tastes be expressed in terms of features, causal pathways, or counterfactuals, or does it cut across concepts I take for granted? Does the capability theorist's objection to equality of resources, namely that the same bundle of goods gives different people different real options, correspond to anything in ML fairness, or is it exactly the kind of thing a metric defined over scores or outcomes misses? Does the choice among fairness metrics already presuppose an answer to the currency question? If so, which answer does my vocabulary smuggle in?

Mark clearly where you are translating versus where you are extending my vocabulary to say something it could not previously say. If, after translation, part of the philosophical debate looks like disagreement over model specification rather than over ethics, say so; if it looks like a genuine normative disagreement that no choice of metric could resolve, say that too.

I would rather the translation fail honestly than fit cleanly. Where a mapping would be forced, tell me. And if you think the whole exercise is misbegotten, for example because ML fairness metrics and egalitarian currencies are answers to different questions, begin by saying that plainly and then do the nearest tractable version.
