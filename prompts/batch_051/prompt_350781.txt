I've somehow ended up on my university's invertebrate-research ethics committee, and our past decisions feel less like a policy than a pile of one-off calls. Over the last few years we approved fruit-fly work on aversive conditioning without requiring anything special, mostly on the grounds that if a fly has a welfare at all it's so minimal it hardly registers. We required full anesthesia and postoperative analgesia for invasive octopus work, treating cephalopods as basically sentient. We let an Aplysia lab go ahead with no pain management whatsoever, even though sea slugs have real neurons and clearly learn. We imposed only basic husbandry rules on zebrafish larvae up to five days old, while the same lab needs full vertebrate-style review once the fish are adults. And we exempted sponges and cell cultures outright, since there's no nervous system to speak of.

Now we have a proposal involving honeybees: the researchers want to deliver mild electric shocks paired with an odor to study avoidance learning, and they also want to remove or manipulate a few bees at a time to see how the colony reshuffles its division of labor. One of the researchers says this is Drosophila all over again; a collegue of mine says the octopus and adult-fish precidents are the relevant ones, since bees learn, remember, and adjust their behavior to new information. I'm less interested in the best policy from scratch than in what we should decide if we're trying to stay consistent with our own track record. Which precedent is genuinely the closest analog, and do our past calls actually force an answer either way? If they don't, what principle would we be adopting under each option, and what future case would most cleanly reveal which principle we've really been running?
