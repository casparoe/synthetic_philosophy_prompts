In my environmental ethics seminar we have read arguments that insects may be sentient and morally considerable, but I keep noticing that actual pest-control practice seems to run on a very different theory. Conventional agriculture and mosquito-control programs routinely apply broad-spectrum insecticides in ways that kill not only the target pests but enormous numbers of non-target insects—pollinators, parasitoid wasps, predatory beetles—while the same agencies and growers also sponsor pollinator-protection initiatives and "integrated pest management" programs aimed partly at reducing insecticide use. I want to pin down the ethical view this practice presupposes. Specifically: what does it commit its practitioners to about the moral standing of insects, about whether many small individual harms aggregate into something that requires justification, and about the moral difference between intended and merely foreseen killing? Reconstruct that implicit theory, assess whether its parts are mutually consistent, and say what would actually have to change in pest-control policy if we replaced it with a sentientist view that treats insect suffering as a serious moral cost.
