I am a novelist, not a philosopher or legal scholar. I have spent the past year working on a novel about a judge during the collapse of a constitutional order, and that research led me to Pascal’s Pensées. I have accepted an invitation to present a short paper at a university colloquium on the foundations of legal authority. My paper takes as its starting point a fragment in the Pensées in which Pascal observes that justice is open to dispute while force is immediately recognizable, and that because human beings cannot endow justice with force, they have instead endowed force with the name of justice. I develop this into the thesis that the felt legitimacy of a legal order is produced less by the justice of its norms than by the stable convergence of inherited custom and effective force, with justice functioning as the retrospective vocabulary a successful order uses to describe itself.

The audience will be mostly legal philosophers, with some constitutional historians and a few political theorists. They are likely to hear this as a reductive or quietist account of law, and their commitments differ by field: the philosophers will object to the account of normativity, the historians to the lack of contextual variation, and the political theorists to the implications for reform and resistance. Please prepare me for the question period. I want a prep sheet, not a restatement of my argument: the objections I should expect, from whom and in what form, how likely and how damaging each is, what I should concede and what I should hold, how to reply in the time available, and where each reply is weakest. Include the objection I should most hope nobody raises.
