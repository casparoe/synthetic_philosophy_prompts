A friend in a formal epistemology seminar has an argument he says shows that all persistent disagreement is somebody being irrational, built out of Aumann's agreement theorem, and I can't find where it's sneaking in more than the theorem actually says. It goes:

(1) Aumann's theorem says two rational agents who share a prior can't disagree about a proposition once their probabilities for it are common knowledge; their numbers have to match.
(2) In any real argument, once both sides have talked it out and told each other what they think, each knows the other's view and each knows the other knows it. So the common-knowledge condition is satisfied.
(3) Therefore, if two rational people have fully debated something and still disagree, the theorem says the only place the disagreement can live is in their priors.
(4) But a prior is just an unargued starting point; rational people don't settle disagreements by appealing to unargued starting points.
(5) So rational people can't persistently disagree once they've talked. Any real case of persistent disagreement — two careful epidemiologists reading the same trials and disagreeing about a treatment — means at least one side is being irrational.

I won't accept (5), but I can't say exactly where the argument goes wrong or what I'm committing myself to by rejecting it. Which step is the first one to break, and what does rejecting it cost?
