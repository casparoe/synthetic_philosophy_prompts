I'm a PhD student working on the foundations of statistical inference. Before I go further into the algorithmic randomness literature, I want the specification I keep not finding: what does an adequate account of randomness for a finite binary string have to deliver, where "adequate" means adequate for judging whether such a string may be treated as random in statistical and inductive practice? Write the desiderata as a requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each. Cover, at least: the strings any account has to get right or at least rank sensibly (long periodic strings, strings output by a deterministic pseudorandom generator, strings known to come from a biased coin, very short strings, all-zero strings); the distinctions it cannot collapse (pattern of the string versus the process that produced it, random-looking versus generated by a chance process, randomness as all-or-nothing versus randomness as a matter of degree); and the constraints from computability and from the finiteness of the object that it cannot contradict. For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable and what it would cost to drop it, and flag any that are really only preferences of a particular camp (frequentist, computational, subjectivist). Then state, briefly, which single requirement does the most to exclude the usual candidates — incompressibility, passing a battery of statistical tests, known provenance from a fair coin. Most importantly, tell me whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, especially if the trouble comes from demanding that a finite string behave like an infinite sequence. I suspect the finiteness itself is doing a lot of the work, and that the list may be more interesting as a result than the theories it evaluates. A table for the requirements is fine.
