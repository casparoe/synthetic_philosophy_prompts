In my constitutional theory seminar I have been developing a case meant to bring out what, if anything, a rule of hereditary succession can still mean in a modern constitutional monarchy, and I would be glad of a careful treatment of it.

A constitution I have invented for teaching purposes—that of a fictional kingdom modelled loosely on the Scandinavian constitutional monarchies—provides in Article II, Section 1: "Upon the death of the monarch, the Crown shall pass to the lawful heirs of the body of the deceased monarch, preference among them being given to the eldest without distinction of sex; failing such heirs, it shall pass to the nearest collateral line of the royal house." The drafters, writing in the nineteenth century, intended the monarch to be hereditary but almost wholly ceremonial within a parliamentary democracy. The last monarch has now died without any born children, but during her lifetime she and her consort created three embryos by in vitro fertilisation, using her ova and his sperm, which remain cryopreserved at a clinic; none has yet been transferred to a surrogate as of the moment of her death. The monarch's first cousin, a retired diplomat, claims the crown at once, while the consort and the clinic propose to thaw and transfer an embryo and argue that the succession should be held in abeyance pending a possible live birth.

What does the clause require or permit in this case? I am less interested in the practical outcome than in the method: set out the textual reading, the purpose of a hereditary ceremonial monarchy in a democratic state, what the nineteenth-century drafters would most plausibly have said had this technology been put to them, and what a reasonable contemporary reader would take the words to mean. Show where these approaches diverge, say which should prevail and why, and explain what your answer reveals about what a succession rule in a democracy is supposed to be doing at all.
