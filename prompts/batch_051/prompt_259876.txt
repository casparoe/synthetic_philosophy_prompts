I've ended up translating a handful of poems my grandmother left, and the process has shown me I hold a set of verdicts about translation that probably dont cohere. (1) I think a haiku rendered in English that keeps the 5-7-5 count but quietly drops the seasonal word (the kigo) is less faithful than a free rendering that keeps the seasonal word even though it looks less like a haiku. (2) I think a version of Dante's Comedy that keeps the terza rima and lets the sense get padded or tweaked to land the rhymes is, on the whole, a fair trade. (3) I think that when a pun won't survive in English, it's fine to replace it with a different pun that does the same job, even though the literal meaning changes. (4) I think that for a medical consent form or a legal contract, denotation is basically all there is and stylistic texture can be thrown away. (5) But I also think a sonnet translated into rhymed iambic pentameter is worse than a free-verse version that stays close to the sense when the rhymes only get there through filler — and I can't see how to hold that and (2) at once. Is there one principle about what translation ought to preserve — meaning, effect, form, something else — that covers all five, or do I have to surrender one? If one has to go, say which and what the surviving principle predicts about translating a text under an arbitrary formal constraint like 'no letter e'. Answer in British English, please.
