At a reading group, two people argued about a passage in chapter five of Locke's Second Treatise of Government, where Locke lays out the natural limits on property. The passage contains two claims made a few paragraphs apart. First, Locke maintains that the natural measure of property is set by the extent of a person's labor and the conveniences of life: whatever a person can use before it spoils belongs to him, and this cannot prejudice another person because there remains still enough, and of equally good quality, for others to use. Second, Locke says that the introduction of money, by common consent, allows people to enlarge their possessions beyond the bounds of personal use, since money does not spoil.

Marina argued that the proviso about leaving enough for others is best read as a description of the early state of nature rather than as a standing moral constraint. On her reading, the actual ground of the right is the labor; the claim that others are not prejudiced is simply a premise that happened to be true in conditions of abundance, not a condition that must remain true for every later act of appropriation. Daniel said that cannot be right, because Locke uses the fact that others are left no worse off as the reason an appropriation counts as legitimate rather than as an injury. If later appropriation does leave latecomers worse off relative to the common store, the same reason cuts the other way, even if money removes the spoilage limit. Marina replied that Daniel is turning a sufficient condition into a necessary one: Locke says appropriation is legitimate where enough remains, but he does not say it is illegitimate wherever enough does not remain. Daniel replied that the underlying logic is that taking from the common store is harmless only while the common store is effectively inexhaustible, so once that ceases to be true the proviso must be either generalized or abandoned; he thinks Locke could not abandon it and still claim that the poor are compensated by the increase in the common stock.
