I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and one of my beats is the engineering of political institutions. A group of constitutional designers has been promoting a hybrid scheme for a country now drafting a new constitution, and I want to understand how the scheme would actually behave in practice, not what it is supposed to achieve on paper.

Under the proposal, a unicameral legislature is elected by proportional representation and a president is elected directly, each for a nominal five-year term. The president appoints and dismisses cabinet ministers without legislative confirmation, and the legislature cannot remove individual ministers. The president may veto legislation, and the veto can be overridden by a three-fifths majority. The distinctive element is an early-election rule with two triggers. The legislature may pass a motion of no confidence in the president by a simple majority of its full membership; if it does, the president leaves office immediately and the legislature is simultaneously dissolved, with new elections for both branches within sixty days, and the removed president is free to stand again. Conversely, the president may dissolve the legislature at any time, but doing so also ends the president's own term, and the president must then contest the presidential election held alongside the new legislative election. There are no waiting periods and no limit on how many early elections may occur within a nominal five-year term.

I would like a careful analysis of what this dual-trigger scheme would do in practice. What incentives does it create for individual legislators, for governing and opposition blocs, and for the president? When would each side actually use its trigger, and how would the threat of using it alter bargaining between the branches? What second-order effects would you expect for coalition formation, candidate selection, the timing of elections, and the stability of governments? Finally, where is the system likely to be gamed first, and which failure modes, if any, could be repaired by adjusting the rules without collapsing it back into a conventional presidential or parliamentary model?
