I've been trying to work out what the administrative practice around human-subjects research actually commits people to about the nature of valid consent, and I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. The practice is this. Research ethics committees—Institutional Review Boards in the United States and their equivalents elsewhere—treat informed consent as a gatekeeping requirement: before a person becomes a participant, investigators must explain the purpose of the study, what the participant will be asked to undergo, the reasonably foreseeable risks and benefits, possible alternatives, and the fact that the participant may withdraw at any time. A signature obtained through coercion is treated as worthless, and enrolling someone who cannot understand the disclosure—because of unconsciousness, intoxication, or severe cognitive impairment—without a properly authorized surrogate is treated as a serious violation. Yet the same committees routinely approve deception studies, especially in social psychology, in which participants are deliberately misled about the true hypothesis, about which behavior is being measured, or about the identity of the people they are interacting with. The standard conditions are that the deception must be necessary, that the risks must be minimal, and that participants must be debriefed afterward and given the chance to withdraw their data. And in some naturalistic observational studies, no individual consent is sought at all because asking for it would destroy the very behavior under study.

What theory of valid consent does this practice presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose that a person can validly consent while being deceived about matters that would ordinarily be considered material to the decision, or does the initial broad agreement to take part in "a study" do a different kind of moral work from study-specific informed consent? Does the practice strictly presuppose a particular analysis of consent—for instance, that consent is a token of autonomous authorization, or that it is a transfer of rights with a limited scope—or is it underdetermined between those analyses? And is the practice internally consistent, or does it quietly apply different standards of validity depending on which right is at stake and how serious the infringement? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change if the best available theory of valid consent were substituted.
