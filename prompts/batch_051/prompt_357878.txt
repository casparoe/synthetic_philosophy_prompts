I'm a philosophy PhD student in value theory, and I've spent the last week trying to make a half-formed argument from reading group rigorous, and now I think it either solves the Scheffler extinction problem or pulls a fast one. The starting point is the afterlife conjecture: many of the things we care about now — a colleague's cancer trial, a novel someone works on for decades, the archive I'm helping digitize — seem to be valued under an implicit assumption that human beings will still be around after we die. That's supposed to explain why human extinction is bad beyond just adding up the deaths. The argument that worries me goes like this.

(1) Take the conditional-value talk seriously: if these projects are valued only on the assumption of a human future, then extinction is bad over and above the deaths because it strips already-existing projects of the condition they needed. (2) But for anything to be bad in this extra way, it must be bad for someone — someone who dies in the extinction, someone who survives into the last generation, or someone who never comes to exist. (3) For a person who dies, whatever happens after her death never enters her experience, so it cannot make her life go worse for her; the trial mattered to her, wether the cure later gets used is a different fact about a different world. (4) For a survivor in the last generation, the loss is real but already counted: grief, despair, the felt pointlessness of going on. Those are ordinary harms, so once we count them we've still got nothing beyond the deaths. (5) For a person who never exists, there is no one to be harmed. (6) So the extra badness has to be either impersonal — value with no subject at all — or we have to say that a person's life can be made worse as a whole by facts after her death that she never experiences.

Now turn the dilemma on itself. The only route that keeps the afterlife conjecture seems to be rejecting (3) and letting posthumous facts into the value of a life. But once you do, where does it stop? Whether my dissertation turns out to have been worthwhile starts to depend on whether there are readers next century, and then on whether the species survives the next millennium, and then on whether anyone exists at all after that — and if humanity never ends, the question might never close. That can't be how value works. So I'm stuck between subjectless badness, a too-stingy experience requirement, and making every life hostage to the whole future. I'd most like to keep the thought that my colleague's trial isn't worthless just because she dies before the results, and I'd also like the last generation's losses to be more than just bad feelings. Tell me which step to reject, or if none of them, which bullet to bite — and give me a committed diagnosis rather than a menu, because I need to defend one position in seminar next week.
