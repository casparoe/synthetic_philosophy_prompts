I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am working on a long piece about whether the passage of time has any deep moral significance — the kind of question that quietly shapes statutes of limitations, delayed prosecutions, and the feeling that a person should not be pursued forever for what they did decades ago. In the course of interviewing philosophers and lawyers, I have written down my own verdicts on a series of invented cases, and I find that I cannot state the principle behind them. Perhaps you can help.

Here are the cases and my instincts.

1. Daniel, at twenty-five, embezzled a modest sum from a small charity where he kept the accounts. The charity absorbed the loss at the time and recovered its footing. He was never caught, and for the next forty-five years he lived honestly, paid his taxes, and did voluntary work. The old records surface when he is seventy. I would not prosecute him; it would feel almost pointless.
2. Nadia, also at twenty-five, defrauded an elderly couple out of a sum comparable to the one Daniel stole. The couple never recovered and, now in old age, still live in real hardship. Nadia has since lived an outwardly blameless life and is identified only now, at seventy. My instinct is that she should still be prosecuted, even though the offence is of the same general kind as Daniel's, of similar magnitude, and no less old.
3. Robert, at twenty-five, murdered a shopkeeper during a planned robbery. He has since lived quietly and lawfully. New DNA evidence identifies him at seventy. My instinct is that he must be prosecuted regardless of the decades.
4. Meera, a pharmacologist, falsified results at thirty to secure approval of a medicine that later caused serious harm, and some of the harmed patients are still alive. Her misconduct is uncovered when she is sixty. I think she should face a legal or professional reckoning now, though I am unsure how much her age or the delay should matter.
5. Claire, at twenty-two, acted as a lookout for a burglary in which the householder was badly injured; she was frightened and pressured by an older partner, and she had nothing further to do with him afterwards. She is identified when she is sixty-two. Charging her now feels wrong to me.
6. Jack, a schoolteacher of twenty-eight, began a sexual relationship with a fifteen-year-old pupil. He moved away soon after, and it was reported only now, when he is fifty-three and long out of teaching. My instinct is that he should still be prosecuted.

I also have a case I keep going back and forth on. Akim, in his twenties, lied under oath to protect a colleague; the lie shielded the colleague, who went on to harm others before being caught. Akim later came forward, and the truth came out. He is now sixty and has otherwise been blameless. I cannot decide whether he should now face consequences for the perjury.

I have been working with a rough principle: the passage of time matters mainly because the forward-looking reasons to punish — incapacitation, specific deterrence, rehabilitation, general deterrence through public example — weaken as the person changes; so where the offender no longer poses a threat and has lived well, the state should generally let the matter rest. But that principle seems to say Robert should be left alone, and it cannot distinguish Daniel from Nadia. I therefore find myself caught between several thoughts: that time discounts desert itself, at a rate somehow set by the gravity of the wrong; that what persists in the cases I still want to pursue is not the offender's dangerousness but the fact that the harm is still being lived by identifiable people; that for the most serious wrongs the community has an irreducible need to condemn, which does not decay; or simply that some of my instincts are inconsistent.

What principle, if any, captures these verdicts, or which verdicts ought I to abandon? Please state the principle precisely, run it through each of the cases, say what it predicts for at least one further case of your own choosing, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Please also say how confident you are in the account you offer, and single out the point you are least sure about. I would be grateful if the reply were written in American English.
