I am a professor of philosophy, and for some years I have kept an informal record of how a colleague—an ethicist working on animal minds and the scope of moral status—has judged a series of cases about whether practices involving possibly sentient animals deserve serious moral concern. Her record so far is as follows.

She treats dogs and octopuses as sentient with high confidence, and she regards large-scale practices that harm them, such as octopus farming, as raising serious moral concern.

On honeybees she is notably more cautious: she puts the probability that bees are sentient at no better than one in four, and she will not treat bee sentience as established. Yet after being shown figures suggesting that a proposed ban on a widely used insecticide would affect a number of bees of the order of a hundred billion, she endorsed stringent restrictions; I had the strong impression that the sheer scale of the number was doing much of the work in her judgement.

By contrast, she regards it as overwhelmingly likely that fruit flies are not sentient—she would put the probability at no more than a few per cent—and she does not consider the tens of thousands of fruit flies used in her own laboratory each year a serious moral matter, even though she does not quite rule fly sentience out.

She has now been asked by an agribusiness firm for her judgement on a proposal to farm black soldier fly larvae industrially for animal feed. The operation would process something of the order of a trillion larvae per year. She has said, tentatively, that she would put the probability of larval sentience at perhaps three to five per cent—low, but not vanishingly low—and she regards the larvae as behaviourally much simpler than adult honeybees.

I want to know how she will come down, but I am more interested in the principle that best unifies her earlier judgements. The candidates I can see are these. First, an expected-count principle: a practice deserves serious moral concern roughly when the expected number of sentient beings affected—probability times population—is large. Second, a de minimis or threshold principle: probabilities below some floor are set aside altogether, no matter how vast the population. Third, a behavioural-complexity bar: only creatures above some minimum of behavioural or neural organisation get onto the scale, after which numbers matter, while those below the bar count for nothing regardless of numbers.

Which of her earlier judgements is the closest analogue to the larvae case, and does it dictate her answer? Do her past verdicts genuinely underdetermine what she should say about the larvae, and what would she conclude under each candidate principle? Give me your best prediction of her actual verdict, with the reasons for preferring one extrapolation over the others, and identify the single further case that would most cleanly settle which principle she has been following. Behind the case-level question lies the scale argument itself: whether a low probability of sentience, multiplied across a vast population, should count as a moral reason at all, and where such reasoning should stop.
