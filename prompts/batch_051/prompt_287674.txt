In my political philosophy class today we did borders and the right to exclude. After seminar two people from my group kept argueing outside the building and I ended up stuck in the middle. I wanted to hear someone go through it move by move.

Mara kept saying states have a presumptive right to exclude because of collective self-determination. Her line was that a political community isn't a random crowd, it's a group of people maintaining institutions together over time, and if that group can't control who enters and becomes a member, then it can't govern its own character. She said it would be bizarre to tell a club, a choir, or even a family that they can't decide who joins, and a state is at bottom a much more consequential version of that.

Dev kept pushing back that this gets the coercion backwards. A state doesn't just say "you can't join our choir"; it threatens and physically prevents people from crossing into territory, including people fleeing violence or poverty, people who never had any say in the laws being enforced against them. He said self-determination only justifies coercion against people who are part of the self that is doing the determining; the entire question is why the excluded person is bound by that decision. Mara shot back that if that were right no state could ever enforce any law against a foreigner, even when they commit a crime in your territory. Dev said that's a different case: jurisdiction over an act inside the territory isn't the same as keeping someone out of a whole region.

Then Dev pressed on the birth-lottery point: nobody chooses where they're born, so if being born on one side of a line gives you a share in self-rule but being born on the other side means you can be kept out, we need a reason why that accident should matter so much. Mara said it's not about the accident, it's about the fact of an ongoing shared political life that actually exists, with all its obligations and institutions. Dev said that's circular: the shared life is only "shared" because you already excluded the people who didn't get in.

Mara's last real move was that Dev's position leaves self-determination hollow. If a community can't control who enters, it can't control its own future, so he's not just against borders, he's against genuine democratic self-rule. Dev said maybe self-rule that needs cages and refusals at the border to survive is self-rule in name only.

I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. Can you walk through each exchange and say who is right about which point, where each argument goes wrong or stops short, and who is more right overall?
