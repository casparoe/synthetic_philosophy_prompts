I am a third-year undergraduate currently taking a metaphysics course, and next week I have to lead a fifty-minute tutorial for first-years in an introductory philosophy module. The assigned topic is metaphysical grounding: roughly, the idea that some facts obtain in virtue of other facts, where this dependence is supposed to be non-causal. The students have done a few weeks on basic argumentative distinctions but have no exposure to this material, and I have been told to assume no formal logic beyond truth tables.

I am not asking you to write the explanation itself. I am asking for a teaching plan: the order in which the ideas must be introduced and why, the single example that should carry the session and what it must and must not be like, the misconceptions the students will bring or form and where each should be intercepted, and a final check that the idea has landed. Please do not give me a reading list, citations, or references to particular authors or articles; the plan must be teachable from your answer alone.

Organize your answer under these section headings, in this order, at roughly the lengths indicated:

1. The target idea (~100 words). State in one or two sentences the takeaway a student should be able to give back at the end, without slogan language, so I can see whether your plan is aimed at the same target.

2. Dependency structure (~250 words). What must be in place before the notion of grounding can even be introduced, and what depends on what. In particular say what distinction a student must already have between facts and other items, and between a causal because and the non-causal because grounding trades in, and why those distinctions cannot be postponed.

3. The carrying example (~250 words). Propose the one example you would return to throughout. Say what features it must have (for example with respect to time, asymmetry, synonymy, and whether one relatum sounds less real) and what it must lack. If you reject the standard red-ball-and-its-color or statue-and-clay options, say precisely what each would mislead a first-year into thinking.

4. Session sequence (~350 words). Give a timed fifty-minute plan with the hinge points marked: what I say or ask at each transition, and what I should deliberately not say yet.

5. Misconception map (~300 words). For each predictable misconception, say where in the sequence it is likely to surface, how to head it off just before it arrives, and a one-line correction if it still appears. Expect at least these: grounding is causation; grounding is entailment or mere necessary implication; a grounded fact is a second-class or less real fact; and grounding is just a word for explanation or definition.

6. Check for understanding (~150 words). Give one final question or short exercise, and say what a good answer would show and what a confident-sounding but wrong answer would show.

7. Deliberate omissions (~100 words). What should be left out at this level, for example the debate over whether grounding must bottom out in a fundamental level, and how I should say so when a student pushes on it.

I do not need prose outside these sections.
