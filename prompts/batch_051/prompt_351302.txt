I run the risk-flagging desk at a small foundation that funds pandemic-prevention and other catastrophic-risk work. A couple times a week we get a "this could be huge" warning — a lab protocol, a supplier process, a weird model behavior — and we keep falling back on a Pascal-ish slogan: tiny probability times huge stakes means treat it seriously. That slogan is useless, because every warning we see has tiny probability and huge stakes, so it just says yes to everything. I want a reusable triage procedure, grounded in whatever formal decision theory and the Pascal's-wager literature actually support, that someone without a decision-theory background can run in under an hour before our director signs off. It has to handle at least four failure modes: the probability can't be estimated at all; the huge stake is itself uncertain; acting has real costs and its own risks; and there are always rival huge-stakes stories demanding equal treatment (the many-gods problem). Mark each step where judgment can't be eliminated, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do. Then apply it to two warnings we actually got: one says a research protocol has maybe a 1-in-10,000 chance of causing a global pandemic, and shutting it down costs a lab's season of work; the other says a new AI deployment has an unquantifiable "serious" chance of enabling bioweapons, and the only response we have costs more than our annual budget. I expect to be told not to act on one of them; the point is to know which.
