In political science, a well-known rationalist argument holds that war between rational actors should usually be avoidable. Since fighting is costly, there should be some negotiated outcome that both sides would prefer to fighting, provided they share the same information and can credibly commit to the agreement. So when rational actors do fight, the argument says the cause must be one of three things: private information plus incentives to misrepresent it, an inability to commit credibly, or an indivisible object of dispute.

I want to know whether this argument survives if we move it to a different setting: an AI race. Suppose the relevant competition is a race between AI labs, possibly backed by states, to build and deploy transformative AI first. The fighting consists of cutting safety procedures, ignoring warning signs, or rushing deployment; losing the race may mean being locked out of the technology entirely rather than paying the ordinary costs of a military defeat; each side has private information about its own progress; and any promise to slow down or share the technology's benefits is hard to verify or enforce. The prize also looks closer to an all-or-nothing market position than to a divisible piece of territory.

Does the rationalist argument still go through under these assumptions? Say which premise the new setting bears on most directly, whether the argument can be repaired to fit an AI race, and what a repaired version would have to claim.
