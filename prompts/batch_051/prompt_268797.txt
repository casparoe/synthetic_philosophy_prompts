ok so in my government class we did a unit on the courts and I cannot get this one thing out of my head: sovereign immunity. the basic rule, as I understand it, is that you cant sue the government unless the government has passed a law letting you sue it. which is already wild to me, like, the government can just decide not to be sued? but then we learned about the Federal Tort Claims Act, which waives that immunity for a bunch of ordinary negligence by federal employees. so if a park ranger crashes a government truck into your fence, maybe you can get compensated. but there is a big carve-out for discretionary functions: if the injury came from a policy judgment, like how many lifeguards to staff at a federal beach, where to put a stop sign, or whether to issue a quarantine order, the government is not liable no matter how badly that decision turns out. so the government can be on the hook for driving the truck carelessly, but not for deciding how many trucks should be out there in the first place.

I was arguing with a friend about this and she said sovereign immunity is just a practical rule so the treasury doesnt get drained by lawsuits, and I should not read any deep theory into it. but I dont think that works, because the line is not between big and small amounts of money — it is between someone doing something and someone deciding something. that feels like a philosophy line, not a budgeting line. what view of the state does this practice actually presuppose? does it secretly assume the old idea that the king can do no wrong, just with the government swapped in for the king? or does it assume the state is not really a moral agent at all, only individual people are, and policy decisions are somehow not anyones actions? and if we instead treated the state as a full moral agent, the way we sort of treat corporations when we fine them, what would have to change about this whole setup? could a state be a moral agent in the way a person is, or is that exactly what the doctrine is denying? I dont need legal citations, i want the philosophy: reconstruct the implicit theory, say which parts the practice strictly needs versus which parts it just happens to fit with, and tell me where the doctrine is underdetermined so that more than one theory could license it.
