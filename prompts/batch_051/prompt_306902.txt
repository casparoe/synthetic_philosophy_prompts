I’ve been trying to put together a clear explainer for people who read rationalist-adjacent discussions of population ethics and distributive justice but haven’t worked through the formal accounts. The recurring confusion I want to dissolve is between prioritarianism and telic egalitarianism: both are offered as corrections to ordinary utilitarianism, both are motivated by the thought that the distribution of welfare matters, and in many ordinary cases they issue the same practical verdicts. The most familiar wedge is the leveling-down objection, but using that as the only illustration tends to make telic egalitarianism look like a straw man, and it doesn’t help readers see how prioritarianism can disagree with utilitarianism in a non-leveling-down direction.

Please construct a one-paragraph thought experiment for this explainer, set in a realistic institution (a hospital network, school district, housing authority, or similar), with no science-fictional elements, no stipulated utility numbers, and no perfectly informed planner. The central policy should substantially improve a small number of very badly-off people while imposing a much larger aggregate loss on many better-off people, so that total welfare falls. The magnitudes should be chosen—and justified—so that the following verdicts arise from the structure of the case rather than being stipulated: (a) an ordinary utilitarian rejects the policy because total welfare declines; (b) a prioritarian with a strictly increasing, strictly concave weighting function accepts it, because the weighted gain to the badly-off outweighs the weighted loss to the better-off; and (c) a telic egalitarian accepts it as well, but for reasons concerning the reduction in inequality. Then add a leveling-down variant of the same scenario: the worse-off remain exactly as they were, while only the better-off are brought down to create greater equality. Show that the telic egalitarian must judge this variant to be, in at least one respect, better, whereas the prioritarian rejects it outright.

After presenting the scenario, walk through each theory’s verdict on both the primary scenario and the leveling-down variant, and explain exactly which feature of the case does the work in each contrast—in particular, distinguish the role of concavity in the prioritarian weighting function from the role of intrinsic value attached to equality itself. Explain what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed or flattened. Finally, give the strongest one-paragraph attack that a defender of utilitarianism could mount against the prioritarian verdict in this case. The case is for a public explainer, so a construction that simply announces that the prioritarian weights are large enough to guarantee the desired verdict would be cheating; the magnitudes need to be motivated by the realistic structure of the scenario.
