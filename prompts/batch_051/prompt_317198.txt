I have a standing rule in how I assess quantitative or causal claims: a claim stated in prose begins at low trust, but the same claim shown as a diagram — a scatterplot, a causal flowchart, an Isotype-style pictogram — starts with substantially more credit, because the picture seems to display the structure of the matter rather than argue for it. I recently learned that Otto Neurath, who spent much of his career building a picture language for public communication, also stressed that pictures are as conventional as words, must be learned, and can mislead if treated as transparent. That unsettles my rule without by itself telling me what to put in its place. Is the rule defensible? Where exactly does it fail, and is there a revised version of it that a careful defender of Neurath's position could endorse? Keep your answer under 500 words.
