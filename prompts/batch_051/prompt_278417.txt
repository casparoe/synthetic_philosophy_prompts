Over the last few months I have been trying to work out exactly what a Scanlon-style contractualist is committed to saying about the aggregation problem, and I keep producing an argument whose conclusion I am sure must be wrong. Suppose a doctor must choose between two courses of action and cannot do both: she can save one person from dying, or she can prevent a thousand people from going permanently blind. I take it as obvious that she should save the thousand; my difficulty is seeing whether a contractualist can say so. Here is the argument I keep producing.

Consider the two candidate principles. The save-the-thousand principle directs the doctor to prevent the thousand cases of blindness, thereby allowing the one to die; the save-the-one principle directs her to save the one, thereby allowing the thousand to go blind. The argument for preferring the latter runs: (1) a principle is an acceptable basis for this kind of choice only if no affected person could reasonably reject it, where reasonable rejection is assessed from that person's own point of view against the available alternatives; (2) a person can reasonably reject a principle only if the loss it would impose on her is at least as great as the loss the alternative would impose on any other single person, and complaints may not be added together across different people; (3) death is a greater loss for the one than blindness would be for any single member of the thousand; (4) so the one person can reasonably reject the save-the-thousand principle, while no single member of the thousand can reasonably reject the save-the-one principle; (5) therefore the save-the-one principle is the only acceptable one, and the doctor is not permitted to save the thousand.

That conclusion seems to me plainly false, so something in the argument must go. I cannot determine whether the fault lies in premise (2), in the move from each person's individual complaint to the acceptability of a principle, or already in the formulation of premise (1). Please tell me whether the argument is valid; if it is valid, identify which premise is false or mis-stated and explain how a Scanlon-style view can best avoid the conclusion without giving up on the intuitive verdict about the case. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
