I am a retired natural scientist, recently drawn into the philosophical side of probability and decision theory. I have been reading about Kelly betting and geometric rationality—roughly, the idea that an agent facing repeated multiplicative risks should maximize expected log wealth rather than expected arithmetic wealth. A former colleague now runs a small research foundation and proposes the following rule: the foundation’s endowment may be invested only in a fixed set of index funds, and at each quarterly meeting the trustees must openly announce numerical probabilities for a predefined set of macroeconomic scenarios; the endowment is then rebalanced to the portfolio with the highest expected log return under the average of the trustees’ announced probabilities. No trustee may invest privately in those same index funds. Treat this as a mechanism rather than a moral position. What does it incentivize in the trustees’ stated probabilities, in their private financial behavior, and in the scenarios they choose to include? How would it be gamed—through insincere probabilities, coordination among trustees, or manipulation of the scenario menu? What second-order effects would likely appear in the foundation’s risk-taking and in who remains a trustee, and can any failure modes be fixed without gutting the scheme? If the proposal rests on a confused understanding of Kelly betting or geometric rationality, I would rather you say that plainly than analyze a mechanism that cannot work as intended.
