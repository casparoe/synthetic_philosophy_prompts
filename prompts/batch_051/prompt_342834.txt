I sit on my congregation's adult education committee, and I've been asked to circulate a short written opinion before next Thursday's meeting. No one on the committee has academic training, including me, though I have read some of this on my own, so the opinion should be readable by ordinary laypeople.

A long-time small-group leader has proposed an eight-week fall course, beginning in late September, that would teach the Bible the way historians study any collection of ancient texts: as human documents with authors, sources, edits, and uncertain dates. The course would explain, for instance, that the first five books appear to have been assembled from earlier documents over a long period, and that the four Gospels often differ in how they order and report the same events. The teacher is a devout member and insists the course would frame all of this as compatible with faith, as a way of taking Scripture seriously rather than as an attack on it.

A second group on the committee strongly opposes the course. They argue that many of our members have never encountered these claims, that eight weeks of them at once will shake confidence in Scripture, and that our education program should build people up rather than unsettle them. They want the course rejected or stripped of most of its historical content. A third group wants it approved largely as proposed, saying that treating adults as too fragile to learn what is widely known about the Bible's formation is its own kind of failure of trust and respect.

Please draft the memo I would circulate. It should recommend one concrete course of action — approve, reject, or approve with specific conditions — and say what that means for the fall calendar. It should also engage seriously with the strongest concern of whichever side the recommendation goes against, and it should end with a clear vote the committee can take. Underneath the scheduling and the syllabus arguments, I think the real question is whether learning how Scripture took shape as human documents and trusting it as revelation can be taught together, or whether one must be subordinated so that people are not left with half-knowledge and no framework for it.
