I am writing a final paper for a philosophy class, and before I submit my position I want to check whether the set of commitments I plan to defend actually hangs together. Here is where I have landed:

(1) Money is socially constructed: whether a slip of paper is money depends on its being collectively accepted as a medium of exchange and store of value.
(2) Race is socially constructed in a different way: racial categories were produced and are maintained by social and political practices, not by any shared biological essence.
(3) Gender is socially constructed in still another way: gender consists in social roles, norms, and expectations rather than in binary biological sex.
(4) Calling something socially constructed does not mean it is unreal or escapable at will: race and gender structure people's life chances even when an individual disavows the categories.
(5) Because these three cases are constructed in different ways, I reject the inference from 'X is socially constructed' to 'X could be abolished by a collective decision.'
(6) Since constructed kinds are mind-dependent, I also hold that the boundaries of race and gender categories should ultimately be settled by self-identification, not by any underlying natural kind.
(7) At the same time, I want to treat race and gender as real kinds for the purposes of social explanation and policy, since they track and help explain systematic inequality.

I am unsure whether this package is coherent. Please go through the major pairs and tell me where there are real tensions, which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection, and what each tension would require me to give up. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally serious, and say which single revision would do the most to make the set hang together.
