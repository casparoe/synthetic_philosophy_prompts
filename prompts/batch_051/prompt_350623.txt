In informal aesthetic disagreements, people often reach for a palate analogy: just as one person cannot argue another into finding a dish delicious, so no one can argue another into finding a novel, a building, or a film beautiful. The tongue, on this view, is answerable only to itself, and the faculty that responds to art is likewise answerable only to itself. The conclusion typically drawn is that there can be no standard of taste, and that any ranking of works is simply one person's palate projected as though it were a rule.

David Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste" begins by granting the wide variety of taste yet still looks for a standard. Consider this passage:

Homer's general precepts, where he delivers any such, will never be controverted; but it is obvious, that, when he draws particular pictures of manners, and represents heroism in Achilles and prudence in Ulysses, he intermixes a much greater degree of ferocity in the former, and of cunning and fraud in the latter, than Fenelon would admit of.
