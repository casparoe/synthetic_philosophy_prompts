The debate concerns an artificial system that can form and revise normative judgments—it can evaluate options as right or wrong, deliberate about what should be done, and produce a verdict about what ought to happen—but it cannot act on any of these judgments. Its outputs go to a log that no one reads, and nothing it computes ever changes the world. In my terms, its sensing path is intact but its actuation path is severed: it has full observability and zero control authority, and it computes a control signal that is never applied to the plant.

Please restate the main positions in this debate in that vocabulary, one by one. I want at least three positions covered:

(1) a control/agency view, in the spirit of the ought-implies-can principle, according to which a system that cannot translate its judgments into action is not a moral agent, because moral agency requires the capacity to make a difference in the world;

(2) a quality-of-will or answerability view, according to which a system can be open to blame for its judgments themselves even when those judgments never issue in action, just as people are answerable for what they judge and not only for what they do;

(3) a self-governance or reasons-responsiveness view, according to which the capacity to govern one's own judgments in response to reasons may suffice for moral agency even without efficacy in the external world.

For each position, give me (a) what translates cleanly into my vocabulary, (b) what translates only with distortion, and (c) what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all.
