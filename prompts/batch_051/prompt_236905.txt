Consider an asymmetry in ordinary prediction. When a colleague says her monograph will take two years because she has already mapped the chapters and the archives, we quietly replace her estimate with the base rate for comparable monographs, say four years, and treat her detailed plan as wishful. When we make the identical claim about our own monograph, we treat the detailed plan as precisely what justifies departing from the base rate: we know our own case from the inside. Each of us does both, advising others to set their inside view aside while claiming our own inside view as authoritative, and most of us feel no inconsistency in it.

What, if anything, justifies this asymmetry? Is there a principle that entitles an agent to treat her own inside view as able to overturn base rates while denying that entitlement to other agents, or is the asymmetry only a familiar piece of self-serving cognition that epistemic modesty would instruct us to abandon in both directions? Give the strongest candidate justification you can find, test it against a nearby case in which an agent genuinely possesses uniquely relevant private information about her own project, and reach a definite verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up. Please write in British English.
