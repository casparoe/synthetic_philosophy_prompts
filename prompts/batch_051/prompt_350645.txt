I run the retrospective desk at a policy magazine. Every couple weeks we assign a piece that looks back at a major failure — a public health campaign that flopped, a merger that destroyed a company, a peace process that collapsed — and explains what went wrong. Lately I've been getting unconfortable about how these pieces get written. The writers always seem to know exactly why the thing failed, and they make it sound like any sensible person could have seen it coming. But when I go back to what people were saying at the time, it's never that clean. I've heard of hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy, and I know Fischhoff and Taleb wrote about this stuff, but I don't know how to turn it into something my editors can actually use on a draft.

So here's what I'd like: Please write a decision procedure — a checklist or set of questions in order — that my editors could run on a draft before we accept it, to catch places where a writer has turned a messy, uncertain past into a neat story where the outcome looks inevitable. You're an expert on the psychology of hindsight and the philosophy of historical explanation, so ground it in that. Before you give me the procedure, spend a bit of time brainstorming the specific failure modes you want the checklist to catch.

The procedure has to be usable by editors who aren't trained in psychology or philosophy. They have maybe 20 minutes per draft. It shouldn't require them to do archival research or run experiments; it has to work on the text in front of them. For each question, tell me what a passing answer looks like, what a failing answer looks like, and where the editor should stop and hand the decision to a senior editor because it's a judgment call. Then run the procedure on three hypothetical drafts:
(a) a piece arguing that the 2008 financial crisis was obviously going to happen because of deregulation;
(b) a piece arguing that a city's decision not to evacuate before a hurricane was plainly negligent given the forecasts available at the time;
(c) a piece arguing that the failure of the League of Nations to stop WWII was inevitable given its structure.
At least one of these should be a case where your procedure tells me something I won't like.

Be honest about what the procedure cannot do — what it will miss, and where a good editor still has to use judgment.

At the very end, give a one-sentence summary of the procedure as the last line, and right before it put \answer{...} with a short label for the procedure.
