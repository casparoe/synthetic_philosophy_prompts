Pearl-style structural causal models represent a system as a set of structural equations and assume those equations are modular: intervening on one variable is a local surgery that replaces its equation while leaving the others intact—the assumption that gives the do-operator its distinct content and separates the intervention rung of Pearl's causal hierarchy from the merely associational one. One colleague claims this modularity is an empirical property of real systems, such as a gene-regulatory network or a labor market, and can be tested against observation. Another replies that modularity is imposed rather than discovered, because any apparent violation can be absorbed by repartitioning the variables or refining the equations, so the claim that a system really is modular is untestable. Design the strongest test you can of the hypothesis that a specified real system is modular in this structural-causal sense, saying what you would observe or manipulate and what result would count for or against the hypothesis. Then state exactly what the test would and would not settle: can any evidence distinguish the system's being modular from our having chosen a description that makes modularity hold? And is the modularity assumption best understood as an empirical hypothesis, a modeling convention, or something in between?
