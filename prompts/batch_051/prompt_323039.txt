I volunteer with a small group that keeps obsolete educational software working for schools that still depend on it, and I have settled into a rough checklist for deciding when a program counts as abandoned for our purposes. The checklist is: (a) the rightsholder has done no substantial maintenance, updating, or commercial exploitation for a long time; (b) a living community of users still derives real value from the program; (c) we cannot, after reasonable effort, identify a rightsholder who is reachable and able to make a decision about the work; (d) there is no functioning market in which the program is actually offered for lawful acquisition; and (e) the use we contemplate would not plausibly harm the present or future interests of any identifiable rightsholder. I have treated all five together as sufficient, and I have never settled whether each is necessary, or whether the list is tracking one idea or several.

The case splitting our group at the moment is this. A language-teaching program sold to districts in the 1990s by a two-person company was later acquired by a still-active educational publisher. The publisher let the product go dormant, removed it from its catalog, and in 2019 quietly took offline the activation server the software needs even to run. The publisher is easy to find and even has a page acknowledging the old title, but after three years of inquiries it has offered no way to license the software, no repair of the server, and no statement either asserting its interest or relinquishing it. Some of us have written a small replacement activation server and distributed it as a patch so that schools holding legitimate copies can keep using what they already paid for. The patch diverts no present revenue, because the publisher no longer sells or supports the program, and the publisher seems to have no use for the title. Yet the rightsholder is not dead, not dissolved, and not unfindable; it is a live corporation that simply does not act.

I would like a criterion-by-criterion answer. Does the case qualify under my checklist as written, and where do the criteria underdetermine the verdict? In particular, does the tension between (c) and (e) show that I have conflated two different things—the descriptive condition of the owner being absent, and the normative condition of being free to proceed? If a known but unresponsive rightsholder does not count as absent on my criteria, is there a better term than "abandoned" for what this software is, or a revised version of (c) that covers it without erasing the difference between an owner who is gone and an owner who merely will not engage? I am not asking for legal advice; I want to know which of my criteria are doing real work, which are redundant or mistaken, and whether this case shows that the whole framing needs redrawing.
