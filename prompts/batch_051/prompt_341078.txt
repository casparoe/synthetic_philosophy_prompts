So I'm giving a seminar next month in the philosophy of biology slot and I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a summary of the talk and not a restaging of the argument. The talk is a defence of the propensity interpretation of fitness: my claim is that unless fitness is understood as a probabilistic propensity to survive and reproduce in a given environment, natural selection says only 'the survivors survive', and that the usual complaints about this kind of view (propensities are spooky; adopting them just buys the tautology back one step) both miss their mark. The audience is our usual philosophy of biology crowd, all of whom have taught the tautology debate, plus two or three population geneticists who sit in on philosophy talks and think the whole tautology worry was put to bed ages ago and isn't worth dusting off. I just spent a long evening arguing with a friend who kept hammering that I've only moved the tautology up a level: if a fit individual dies young I'll just say its propensity was still high, and nothing I've said tells you what could count against 'the fitter type reproduces more'. I didn't have a good answer in the moment and it's rattled me, so I need you to be honest about the soft spots.

The spine of the talk is roughly this: (1) define fitness as actual reproductive success and selection is vacuous; (2) the relative-success coefficients population geneticists estimate are fine for tracking change but don't give you an explanatory property; (3) propensities are no more mysterious than fragility or water-solubility, they're dispositions with physical bases; (4) you get empirical content back by reading 'the fittest survive' as a population-level claim about probability distributions over offspring numbers, testable in the aggregate, not a claim about any particular individual. The part I know is weak is that I never say how a propensity gets measured except through actual outcomes, and I keep going vague when drift comes up.
