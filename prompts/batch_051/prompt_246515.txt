i keep running into the same fight in my giving group, and i think i've finally named the thing I keep reaching for. I call it tractability evaporation. The idea is that tractability isn't a stable property of a cause, the way the standard importance–tractability–neglectedness framework tends to treat it. It's more like a stock of cheap, legible interventions that gets drawn down as people actually do the work. The first dollar into a cause grabs the obvious stuff—bednets, a vaccine that already exists, a bill that already has sponsors—and each dollar after that faces a systematically harder, more expensive, lower-leverage version of the problem. So when you see "high tractability," a lot of the time you're really seeing "nobody has used up the easy wins yet." I think this makes neglectedness and tractability not two independent factors but one factor wearing two costumes: neglectedness is doing hidden work inside tractability. It also has an uncomfortable prediction: the causes that score best on the usual three-factor ranking are exactly the ones whose tractability should collapse fastest if a lot of resources move in at once, so the framework sort of punishes people who follow it in a herd.

I've even been using a rough version of it: effective tractability = observed tractability × (1 − saturation), where saturation is the fraction of known cheap wins already claimed.

Is this a real distinction or am i just restating diminishing marginal returns and adding vocabulary? If it's real, where does it break? I suspect it applies badly to research-heavy or movement-building causes, where tractability isn't a stock you deplete, and maybe my saturation term is doing all the work without actually being measurable. Give me the strongest case for treating tractability as a stable property of the problem instead of an evaporating resource, and tell me whether the herd-trap prediction is actually entailed by the idea or just something I like saying.
