I've spent the past few weeks trying to reduce a running argument to a single crux. A friend who works on prestige historical dramas and biopics — the kind that invents private scenes for recently dead public figures — keeps saying that the dead can't sue, and I keep replying that this is a fact about tort law rather than a moral argument. After tabling the legal question and tabooing phrases like defamation of the dead, the view I've converged on is roughly this: in dramatizing real people, we owe the dead themselves nothing at all. The obligations that are real all run to the living, and I think there are exactly two. First, we should not predictably leave an audience with false beliefs about the historical record that they will retain or act on; that obligation is owed to the audience, not to the person depicted. Second, we should not cause foreseeable, avoidable, non-trivial distress to survivors — family, friends, collaborators, communities of memory. Talk of violating a dead person's consent, or wronging their memory, is on this view either confused, or shorthand for one of those two living-directed obligations, or a piece of social etiquette we have mistaken for moral requirement. A person who died with no living connections, and whose story no viewer will treat as factual history, is simply available material for a writer.

I'm not asking for a defense of this view — I already have one, and I suspect I am overconfident about it. I want a systematic map of what would count against it. What possible cases, arguments, or considerations from nearby areas (posthumous interests, consent, reputation, dignity, collective memory) should most unsettle each part of the position? Which would be fatal if they held up, and which merely awkward or costly? Where should I, as someone defending the view, honestly concede that a counterexample has landed, and where am I entitled to treat an objection as an intuition pump or as already covered by the two living-directed obligations? I would particularly like an assessment of which objections someone with my starting point — trying to re-describe everything in terms of who is made worse off — should take most seriously, rather than the objections that would only move someone with different premises. And I'd like a verdict on whether the view is objectionably insulated: whether the two living-directed obligations can be gerrymandered to absorb any alleged counterexample, and whether that is a problem with the view or evidence that it is tracking something real. As you go, please say how confident you are in each part of your assessment, and single out the point you are least sure of.
