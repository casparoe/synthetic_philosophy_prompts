I chair the Civic Memory Commission of a mid-sized city: a public body that reviews petitions to remove, relocate, recontextualize, or rename statues, monuments, commemorative plaques, and street names. We hear a dozen or more cases a year and make formal recommendations to the City Council, which accepts them with few amendments. Each petition gives us sixty days to respond. We meet monthly, we are lay volunteers with no research staff of our own, and we work under an ordinance that presumes named public works older than forty years will remain, but permits removal, relocation, or renaming on broadly worded grounds we have to interpret almost from scratch each time: distinct ongoing harm, false commemoration, incompatibility with basic civic commitments.

Last month I spent a long evening arguing about precisely this with a close friend who is a historian. Her position was that monuments and street names are evidence of how past generations understood themselves and that altering them destroys the public record; the proper response to a bad commemoration, she said, is to add voices rather than subtract old ones. I argued that a standing monument is not an archive: it is a continuing honor, reaffirmed every day the city leaves it in place, so retention is as much a statement about us as it is about the commemorated person. We argued for hours and could settle none of it, because neither of us could state a test for when one of those thoughts should prevail. The argument unsettled me for an additional reason: it was an accurate description of how my Commission has been conducting itself. We decide from first principles each time, and the outcomes wobble toward whichever framing dominated the meeting.

I want you to turn whatever the ethics of commemoration can defensibly support into a standing procedure the Commission can actually run. By a procedure I mean a reusable sequence of gates or questions that any three of our members can apply in a few hours per case, with a sufficiently clear standard for what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and with explicit exit points where a petition fails, where retention is appropriate, where recontextualization or relocation is appropriate, and where the remaining question is one of values and must go to a vote rather than being crushed into a checklist.

The procedure should not assume that every object we consider is the same kind of thing. It should first sort each case into at least four functional kinds and make different justifications apply to each: primary honors such as portrait statues and named parks; event-memorials such as strike memorials and soldiers' monuments; factual markers such as plaques asserting what happened at a site; and functional labels such as street names whose address function is part of daily infrastructure. It should also distinguish the commemorated person from the specific act or role being commemorated, distinguish removing an honor from correcting a factual claim from changing a practical label, and weigh reversibility and independent aesthetic or historical value without allowing those considerations to become silent trumps. Where a fact is contested or only partially knowable, the procedure should say how to proceed rather than pretending to scholarship we cannot do. It should state plainly which steps are factual, which are judgment calls dressed as procedure, and where the procedure can be expected to misfire. And it should say what the procedure cannot settle: who counts as the public of this city, how to weigh losses to different groups, and whether a living person's repudiation can do what removal does.

Please run the procedure, in order, on these three applications. Stipulate the facts as given; the cases are invented.

First, a full-length bronze statue erected by public subscription in 1898 in front of City Hall describes an industrialist as the man who brought the city clean water and founded its oldest hospital. The waterworks and hospital are real and still serve the city. His fortune, however, was built over twenty-five years through a colonial venture that used forced labor, and afterward he was the largest private funder of a campaign to restrict voting and housing by race. A petition asks for removal; a neighborhood association defends the statue on the ground that the hospital and waterworks benefited everyone. The good works and the source of the fortune are not separable.

Second, Harker Boulevard is a major residential street named in 1954 for a mayor whose two defining acts were to route a highway through a Black business district through aggressive use of eminent domain and to block integrated housing. The street runs through the displaced neighborhood. Former residents and their descendants petition for renaming. The practical cost of renaming is genuine: hundreds of addresses, businesses, maps, and delivery systems would have to change. The mayor's only claim to commemoration is the displacement itself.

Third, a 1910 park monument to a labor organizer depicts her speaking and includes a frieze of the women garment workers she helped lead through a successful strike. It was commissioned by the strikers themselves and is one of the few public works in the city representing working-class women. The organizer later adopted and published eugenic views, including advocacy of forced sterilization, and the petitioners say that makes the honor unendurable. Descendants of the strikers and the present-day union oppose removal and say the monument is about the strike, not the whole life. A petition asks for removal.

I should be honest about my own leaning. The argument with my friend has left me inclined to think that a serious moral stain on the person should normally be decisive, and on the third case I strongly want the monument gone. That is exactly why I want a procedure rather than my reactions. At least one of the worked applications should therefore end in a recommendation I will dislike, and you should mark it and explain why the procedure is right and my inclination is wrong.

Do not write three one-off recommendations; write the procedure first, so that the applications merely illustrate it.
