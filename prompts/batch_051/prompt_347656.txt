After four decades as an agricultural entomologist—much of it spent deciding when, whether, and how to suppress pest populations, with insecticides or otherwise—I have lately begun reading moral philosophy, and I find the discussion of our duties to insects frustratingly unanchored. The literature I have seen tends to argue from a single case: the mosquito in a malaria-endemic bedroom, the aphid in the greenhouse, the locust swarm in a smallholder's field, the moth killed incidentally by a light trap, the invasive beetle in a native forest. From different cases come different conclusions, and there is no shared statement of what the competing views must get right before any of them is accepted. So before I read further, I want the specification: a list of the requirements an adequate ethical account of insect pest control must satisfy. It should include the cases it must handle, the distinctions it must not collapse (killing versus causing pain; individual versus population; disease vector versus crop pest versus invasive species; managed versus wild settings; direct versus collateral harm), and the empirical findings about insect physiology, behavior, and ecology it may not contradict; for each requirement, give the reason it is a requirement, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp.

I am not asking you to defend an account, only to state the criteria by which the accounts should be judged. I am especially interested in whether the list itself turns out to be a result: if some pair of requirements cannot plausibly be met together, or if one requirement alone excludes most of the currently popular positions, I want that said directly. Give me exactly seven numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Items one through six are the requirements with their rationales and their negotiable/non-negotiable status; item seven states which single requirement knocks out the most popular theories and whether any two requirements are jointly unsatisfiable. If you think the request rests on a mistake—for example that no single set of desiderata should be expected to cover mosquitoes, locusts, and stored-product beetles at once, or that the list cannot be drawn up before the underlying empirical questions about insect sentience are settled—then say so plainly instead of giving me a list for the sake of it.
