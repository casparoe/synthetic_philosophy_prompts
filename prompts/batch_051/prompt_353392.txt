I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in early modern philosophy, and I am drafting a take-home final worth 25% of the course grade: students have one week and a 900-word limit, and they may consult their primary texts and notes but no secondary literature. I want one essay question on Hume's account of causation, built around the following passage, which I will print on the exam:

"When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other."

That is David Hume, An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, Section VII ("Of the Idea of Necessary Connexion"), Part I. The question should force students to work out whether the passage's language is primarily epistemological or ontological: does "we are never able... to discover" warrant the stronger conclusion that causal necessity is nothing over and above regular succession plus the mind's expectation, or does it leave room for real causal powers or necessary connections that obtain independently of us but are undiscoverable? What I want to test is whether students can hold two readings of the same wording in view at once — the cruder regularity reading and the so-called New Hume, or skeptical-realist, reading — and then defend one with reasons rather than labels. The question should be unanswerable by reciting the regularity theory, and it should push stronger students to connect the passage to the principle that all ideas derive from impressions and to Hume's two definitions of a cause.

Please write four sections, in this order and with these approximate lengths. (1) "Exam question" (100–150 words): a single essay prompt that quotes or directly engages the passage and cannot be answered well by summarizing Hume's regularity theory. (2) "Model answer" (550–650 words) in American English, defending one defensible reading while addressing the strongest alternative reading and explaining why it falls short. (3) "Marking scheme" (200–300 words): name the specific moves that separate an A from a B answer, and the specific misunderstandings or omissions that should cost marks. (4) "Contested versus settled" (100–150 words): separate what is broadly agreed in Hume scholarship from what is genuinely contested among interpreters, and flag where the model answer relies on a contested reading rather than an established consensus; do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the settled view.
