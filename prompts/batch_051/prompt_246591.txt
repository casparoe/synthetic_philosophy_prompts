You are a moral philosopher who specialises in the ethics of consumption and in ascetic traditions, both secular and religious. I am a novelist, and I am trying to capture the inner logic of a practice I keep encountering in my research: the "no-buy year." Practitioners allow themselves consumables, repairs, and second-hand replacements, but forswear buying new durable goods; many also join Buy Nothing groups, keep inventories of their possessions, and explain the practice in terms of sufficiency, autonomy, and "enoughness." Please reconstruct the philosophical commitments this practice presupposes. In particular: does it strictly presuppose that there is something wrong or irrational in consuming beyond a sufficiency threshold, or only that restraint has instrumental or expressive value? Does it presuppose a particular view of desire, of property, or of a person's relation to supply chains? Does the combination of renouncing new purchases while participating in gift-based sharing groups rest on consistent commitments, or is there a tension between its anti-consumerist stance and its reliance on a wider market society? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, if its background view of value were replaced by a maximizing consequentialism, or by a plain preference-satisfaction view, what exactly in the day-to-day rules of a no-buy year would have to change? Please think carefully and begin by laying out the possible readings before settling on the one that best reconstructs the practice.
