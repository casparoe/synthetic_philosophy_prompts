I've been trying to get straight about one of those ergodicity arguments, and I think I've found a version that seems airtight, which is worrying. Here it is.

Suppose I'm offered a bet: I wager my whole bankroll each time. If a fair coin lands heads, my bankroll is multiplied by 1.5; if tails, its multiplied by 0.6. So a single play has expected multiplier (0.5)(1.5) + (0.5)(0.6) = 1.05. If I start with $100, my expected bankroll after one play is $105.

Now consider repeating the bet n times, all-in each time:

(1) After each play, expected bankroll is 1.05 times the pre-play bankroll, regardless of history.
(2) By induction, expected bankroll after n plays is 100 × 1.05^n.
(3) Since 1.05^n grows without bound, someone who takes this bet repeatedly should expect their bankroll to grow over time.
(4) So a rational person should keep taking the bet, and the longer they play, the better off they should expect to be.

But the standard ergodicity point says this bet is actually ruinous over time, because the time-average growth rate is sqrt(1.5 × 0.6) ≈ 0.949 per play, so a typical player's wealth shrinks. I can't figure out which step, exactly, goes wrong. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails and why? Please answer in American English.
