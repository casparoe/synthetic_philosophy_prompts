I recently became chair of a committee charged with brokering a compromise between two factions of an organization I care about. The factions hold different and partly incompatible value systems, and the decision before us — whether to continue cooperating with an external partner whose practices one side regards as seriously wrong — has become the point on which those systems collide. One side sees the cooperation as complicity; the other sees refusing it as a self-righteous purity that would sacrifice the organization's real work. A colleague whose judgment I trust has been urging us to model the negotiation on trade. On his view, each side arrives with a bundle of moral concerns to which it assigns different weights; a compromise is an exchange in which each side gives up a concession it values less in return for a concession it values more; and because such an exchange leaves each side better off by its own lights, anyone who refuses it is passing up gains from trade and is, by their own standards, behaving irrationally. He grants that some trades are off-limits — selling a vote, for instance — but says this only shows that compromise, like trade, operates within side-constraints.

I want this analogy audited. Set out the mapping explicitly: in the moral-compromise case, what corresponds to the traders, the initial endowments, the commodities exchanged, the prices, and the gains from trade? Then identify where the mapping breaks down. In particular, examine which features of ordinary exchange the analogy quietly imports — that the goods are commensurable enough to be priced, that each party holds title to what it concedes, that third parties are unaffected, that the pre-trade baseline is morally neutral, and that each party is the best judge of its own gain — and say which of these fail when what is being conceded is a matter of principle rather than preference. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the argument from those that are merely cosmetic. Finally, state whether the conclusion survives: that a mutually beneficial moral compromise ought to be accepted and that refusing one is irrational. If it does not survive, say what a better analogy for a defensible moral compromise would be.
