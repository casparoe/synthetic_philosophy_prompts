The dispute over whether quantum indeterminacy helps free will has, as far as I can tell, not moved in decades. Libertarians argue that if determinism holds, our actions are already fixed by the past and the laws, so an irreducibly chancy element in deliberation is the only way to leave room for genuine alternatives; their critics answer that a chancy neural event is no more under my control than a determined one, so replacing a determined world with an indeterministic one merely substitutes a roulette wheel for a puppet. Compatibilists often say the whole contrast is beside the point, because freedom is a matter of the right kind of responsiveness to reasons, not of whether the underlying microphysics is deterministic. I want a diagnosis of this impasse, not another verdict on free will. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal disagreement about the word "free," or a disagreement about what an agent—or an exercise of control—would have to be in order for the question to apply at all? My working hypothesis is that the randomness objection presupposes a picture in which the agent stands apart from the chancy events it is supposed to harness, and that this picture, rather than any empirical finding, is what keeps the debate fixed in place. Diagnose the structure of the disagreement on the basis of what each side actually says, and say what would count as progress given your diagnosis. End by saying what would change your mind about the diagnosis and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
