I've been trying to think through something about how pleasure and awareness combine, and I keep going in circles. I have a handful of gut reactions that seem right to me individually, but I can't find one rule that fits them all.

Here are the cases. (1) If someone is in a dreamless sleep and we later say their brain was in a "happy" state but they never felt a thing, I don't think that adds anything to how good their life went. (2) But if someone is deeply absorbed in fixing an engine or doing a hard puzzle, and they say afterwards they never felt pleasure during it, I still think the absorbed activity made their life better. (3) A person with advanced dementia who smiles all day and seems content — I think that happiness matters, but maybe less than the same amount of felt happiness in a fully alert person. (4) A pig wallowing in mud, clearly enjoying itself — I care about that too, but I don't know whether to count it the same as a human's enjoyment or discount it. (5) A person on a drug that makes them half-aware but feels enormous ecstacy versus a fully aware person feeling mild calm satisfaction — I genuinely can't decide which has more value, and that bothers me. (6) A million barely-conscious insects each suffering a tiny bit versus one human suffering intensely — I don't want to just add the insect pains up until they outweigh the human, but I also don't want to say they count for zero.

So my question is: is there a single principle that captures all of these? It feels like maybe the value of a feeling is something like awareness times intensity, so no awareness means zero, and low awareness shrinks the value. But that rule seems to break on the dementia and the absorbed-activity cases. What principle, if any, delivers most of my verdicts? What does it say about a fully conscious but totally flat, pleasureless life? And which of my verdicts do I have to abandon?
