I find myself pulled in two directions about phenomenal consciousness, and I want to know which side should give.

The case for epiphenomenalism seems strong to me. The physical world appears causally closed: every physical event has a sufficient physical cause. My wincing, my saying "that hurts," and my forming the judgment that I am in pain all have neural causes. The felt quality of the pain — what it is like for me — looks like something over and above those neural causes, and it is hard to see what work it does that the neural activity is not already doing. So the natural conclusion is that qualia are real but causally inert.

Yet one firm intuition resists. It certainly seems to me that the reason I judge and report that I am conscious is that I am conscious; my awareness of the feeling is what moves me to say there is a feeling. If that is an illusion, then the judgment on which epiphenomenalism itself rests was produced entirely by neural machinery, with no contribution from the fact it purports to report. A judgment that a phenomenon occurs, when that phenomenon plays no causal role in producing the judgment, looks like a suspiciously lucky mechanism: I would have made the very same judgment whether or not the qualia had been there. That seems to undercut my grounds for believing in qualia at all.

I am not asking which side is popular; I am asking how the tension should be resolved. Is the right move to reject epiphenomenalism as self-undermining, to debunk the intuition — perhaps by distinguishing the content of a judgment from its cause, so that the judgment can be justified by a neural state that carries information about the pain even if the felt quality is an idle byproduct — or is there a reading on which causal closure and my sense of being moved by pain both survive? Present the strongest case for each move and say what would justify a choice among them.
