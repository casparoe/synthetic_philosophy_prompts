A friend walked me through this arguement and now I can't find the exit. He's not denying that chaotic systems are deterministic; he's arguing that once you see how prediction actually works in them, determinism stops being a claim about the world and becomes a claim about an impossible knower. The argument:

(1) A deterministic system is one whose state at a time, together with the laws, fixes every later state.
(2) So predicting such a system is a matter of knowing its current state well enough that your uncertainty doesn't get amplified into a different macroscopic future.
(3) In a chaotic deterministic system, uncertainty about the initial state grows in a characteristic way: small errors get doubled again and again until they're as big as the whole system. Worse, the time you gain is only logarithmic in your precision—each extra decimal place buys you a fixed amount of extra prediction time, so to double the horizon you'd need to resolve the state exponentially finer.
(4) Any real measurement has finite precision, so for any measuring instrument, however good, there's a finite horizon beyond which its predictions are compatible with completely different macroscopic outcomes.
(5) Every instrument we could ever build is a real instrument with finite precision, so every possible instrument has such a horizon.
(6) Improving the instrument pushes the horizon out, but it never gets rid of it, because the new precision is still finite and the improvement only buys a fixed amount of time.
(7) So no matter how fine an instrument is, it always has a horizon; in other words, there's a finite time beyond which more precision won't help.
(8) But if no possible measurement could settle whether the system is deterministic or random beyond that time, then 'deterministic rather than random' is not a scientific difference there.
(9) So past that horizon, deterministic chaos and genuine randomness are observationally equivalent. That means either determinism isn't a scientific claim, or chaotic systems aren't actually deterministic.

Every premise on its own sounds fine to me, but (9) can't be right. I'd like to keep (1) and (3), and I also don't want to say physics can't tell the difference between a chaotic deterministic system and a noisy random process. Which step should I reject, and what do I give up by rejecting it?
