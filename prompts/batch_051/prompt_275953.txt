Act as an expert on G. E. Moore's ethics, especially Principia Ethica. I'm teaching a unit on organic unities and I need a thought experiment built to order. Take Moore's two commitments: (i) the two chief intrinsic goods are personal affection and aesthetic enjoyment, and (ii) the principle of organic unities, under which the intrinsic value of a whole need not equal the sum of the intrinsic values of its parts — the whole may be worth more, or *less*, than its parts would be worth on their own. I want the less-than case, because students always assume organic unities only ever add value.

Construct a realistic, ordinary-life scenario about two friends and a work of art (a painting, a song, a film — your pick) where, by Moore's lights, the whole consisting of the friendship-elements plus the beauty-elements comes out *less* valuable than the sum of its parts. Constraints: no morally repugnant art, no hidden malice or envy between the friends, and no sneaking in a small evil to do the work. Each component, taken alone, should still be positively good by Moore's standards. The loss in value has to come from how the goods interact, not from adding a bad thing.

Think carefully. Start by brainstorming two or three candidate mechanisms (attention, tempo, what each person notices, whatever), pick the best one, and say briefly why you picked it. Then write the scenario in one paragraph. After the scenario, do the reckoning in Moore's style: assign rough values to the parts in isolation, then to the whole, and show the arithmatic coming out below the sum. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the most work, and what would happen to the verdict if you deleted just that feature. Finish by saying how a sympathetic Moorean would push back — would they deny that one of your “parts” really has the value you assigned in isolation, or re-describe the parts so the sum equals the whole, or something else? Don't just stipulate the conclusion; the numbers have to be produced by the mechanisms you describe. I care more that the case is built honestly from his principles than that it reflects ordinary moral intuitions.
