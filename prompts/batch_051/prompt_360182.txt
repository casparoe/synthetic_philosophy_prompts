I've been reading Peirce's The Fixation of Belief and I want to stress-test his argument against something he couldn't possibly have anticipated, and I'd like the take of someone who actually works on Peirce.

Quick recap of what I mean. Peirce distinguishes four ways people settle opinion. There's tenacity, where you just cling to a belief and avoid whatever might unsettle it. There's authority, where an institution decrees what's to be believed and punishes dissent. There's the a priori method, where beliefs get adopted because they feel natural or agreeable to reason, the way a lot of metaphysics does. And there's science, where belief is supposed to answer to a reality that is independent of what anyone thinks, so there is a real difference between getting it right and getting it wrong. His claim is that the first three methods can't really fix belief: tenacity gets undermined when you run into other people who disagree; authority always leaks because no institution can regulate every question; and the a priori method just makes inquiry a matter of taste, so it ends up drifting. Science is the only one that can produce stable convergence, because it's the only one that tethers belief to something outside our preferences.

Now the development I want to throw at him. Today an enormous amount of belief-fixing happens through engagement-optimized ranking systems: feeds, recommender algorithms, autocomplete, that sort of thing. These systems don't care whether a belief tracks that external reality; they care whether the belief holds attention. Yet they sure look like they fix belief. People inside different algorithmic bubbles hold their views with huge confidence, and the disagreement that Peirce thought would destabilize tenacity and authority doesnt land anymore, because the systems filter contrary evidence out before it can do its work.

So here's my question. Does Peirce's argument survive this development? Is a feed algorithm a genuine fifth method, or is it just one of his four methods with better machinery? If it's the latter, which one, and why does Peirce's criticism of it now fail or need updating? If it survives, tell me why the algorithmic case either isn't genuine fixation or will destabilize in the way Peirce predicted. If it doesn't survive, give me the repaired Peircean claim — what should someone like Peirce say now?

I want a committed answer: pick a side and defend it, don't just lay out the options. Take your time and think through it carefully. Start by brainstorming a few ways of classifying the recommender case, then explain which classification you think is right and why. I care about what happens to the argument, not about any content moderation policy. And please write it in American English.
