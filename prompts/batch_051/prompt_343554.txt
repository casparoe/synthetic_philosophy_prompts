I'm a working type designer and font engineer, with no formal philosophy background but a durable amateur interest in debates about concepts and categories, and I keep circling back to the Letter Spirit question — what makes every 'a' an 'a' across wildly different typefaces, what separates style from identity, and whether there is anything like an essence of a letterform. My working vocabulary is the one I use daily: Unicode code points and the character/glyph distinction, glyph IDs and cmaps, outlines and control points, OpenType features, variable-font axes and interpolation between masters, hinting and rasterization, optical sizing, kerning. I would like you to restate the main positions in the philosophical debate about letterform identity in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, one by one. Roughly, the positions I have in mind are: an essentialist view on which each letter has a structural essence or skeleton that all and only its instances share; a family-resemblance or prototype view on which membership is graded and driven by partial overlaps rather than by a common definition; and a conventionalist view on which being an 'a' is, at bottom, a matter of stipulation or brute fact.

For each position, tell me what translates cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. I suspect the character/glyph distinction will do much of the work of the type/token distinction, and that variable-font design spaces look like a natural home for the idea that style is a continuous variation that preserves identity; I want to know how far those hunches survive under pressure. Then finish with a residue report: in particular, my vocabulary seems to have no native place for the normativity of 'a'-ness — the fact that a badly drawn 'a' is still an 'a' and yet is a worse instance of it — and I want to know whether that fact distorts or simply disappears in translation. Finally, give me a verdict on whether my vocabulary quietly presupposes a conventionalist answer, since code points are assigned by committees and glyph sets by foundries, and thereby dissolves a live philosophical question rather than translating it. Keep the translation honest: don't smuggle in the assumption that a letterform is just a data structure and call that a translation. The deliverable I want is the systematic translation plus the residue, not a primer on theories of concepts.
