I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I have been trying to work out whether an analogy I reach for when revising first-person narration is sound or merely private shorthand.

The relevant philosophical dispute, as I understand it, concerns the norm of assertion. To be entitled to assert that p, must a speaker know that p? Or is it enough that p be true, or merely that she believe it? The three positions predict different breaches and different critical responses. The knowledge view faults the lucky true guess: you did not know that. The truth view faults falsehood: that claim is false. The belief view faults insincerity: you do not believe that yourself.

While revising a first-person novel narrated by a woman whose husband has disappeared, I found myself classifying her statements in just this way. In one scene she claims a neighbor killed him and appears to believe it, though the scene details suggest she killed him herself. In another she tells a story she knows to be false. In a third she asserts something she has no way of knowing, and the novel later bears her out. The three views sort these scenes differently: the liar offends on any standard; the sincere-but-mistaken narrator is permitted if belief suffices but not if truth or knowledge is required; the narrator who is right without warrant is permitted by a truth norm but not by a knowledge norm.

This made me suspect the analogy is more than notational. A reader seems to admit a narrator's statement as fictional fact only when the narrator meets some such standard, and to withhold that admission, calling the narration unreliable, when she does not. If the structural identity were real, the arguments internal to the assertion debate ought to transfer. In particular, if knowledge is the norm of assertion, we would predict that readers distrust a right-but-unwarranted narrator as equally unauthorized as a liar. I doubt they do. Moreover, ordinary assertion answers to a world independent of the utterance, whereas a novel's facts are partly fixed by what the narrator says and partly by what the arrangement of scenes implies.

Is this a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion? What would have to be true of fictional truth and of ordinary assertion for the results of the assertion debate to transfer, and where precisely does the parallel break?
