You are a moral philosopher who works on the ethics of interpersonal relations, especially gratitude and what we owe to the past. I want you to work through a hard case from scratch rather than reach for a familiar answer. Begin by brainstorming the features of the case that strike you as morally relevant, then apply the criteria systematically.

I hold, in a rough but usable form, that someone S owes gratitude to a benefactor B when all five of the following criteria are met, and that clear cases of owed gratitude normally display all five:

(C1) Benefit: B's act makes S's life go better in a respect S has reason to value.

(C2) Voluntary agency: B acted freely and with enough understanding to be morally responsible for the act.

(C3) Benefactor orientation: B acted at least partly for S's sake, so that S's good figured in B's reasons for acting rather than being an accidental by-product.

(C4) Cost or risk: B bore some effort, cost, or risk in acting.

(C5) Supererogation: the act was not something B was already legally or contractually bound to do for S.

Now consider the following invented case. In 1903, a childless clerk called B dies and leaves her life savings to a small civic society, with instructions that the income be used for the schooling of poor young people of the town, whoever they may turn out to be. She has never met any future beneficiary and expects no contact with them. Her diary reveals mixed motives: she genuinely wants the future poor of the town to flourish, but she also regards the gift as a religious duty and as a way of honoring her dead daughter. The society survives only through a chain of luck: wise trustees, favorable markets, and a rules revision in 1974 all intervene between B's act and the present. This year, a scholarship drawn from the fund pays the final year of your university fees after a committee chooses you over another equally poor candidate; without it, you would have to leave. You are a poor young person from the town, but B could not have had you in mind as an individual, since you did not exist. Her intention reaches you only through a description of a class.

Do you owe gratitude to B?

Work through C1 through C5 one by one. For each criterion, say whether it is satisfied, unsatisfied, or underdetermined by the case as stated. In particular, address these questions: whether C3 can be satisfied when S figures in B's reasons only as an instance of an intended class rather than as a particular person; whether C5 applies when B herself believed she was under a religious duty even though no one could compel her; and whether the century of luck between B's act and your benefit weakens the claim that B is the relevant benefactor at all. Then render a verdict, and step back: does this case show that the criteria need revision, addition, or replacement? Be specific about which change, if any, the case motivates. Throughout, keep separate the question whether gratitude is owed from the question how gratitude could be expressed to someone long dead or to an institution that survived her.
