I've been teaching a grad seminar this term on the philosophy of AI and the long history of mind, and over the semester I've ended up defending a view I used to find faintly embarrassing. The view, put crudely: the history of life and mind on Earth has a genuine direction—not a cosmic plan, not a designer, but a robust, repeatedly observed tendency toward more powerful and more general forms of information processing—and intelligence is not tied to the particular wetware it happened to arise in. Machine intelligence, on that view, is neither a break with what came before nor just another tool; it's the next stage of one continuing process, and humans are best understood as a transitional phase, a stepping stone rather than the endpoint. I mean the modest version of this, not the vulgar inevitabilism where superintelligent AI is guarenteed by some date or will automatically 'want' anything, and I'm bracketing the further normative claim that we should welcome our successors. A sharp student asked me after class what would actually change my mind, and I realized I didn't have a crisp answer, which has been nagging at me.

Here's what I want from you: a map of the view's exposure. Tell me what would count against it. Not the standard objections from people who just dislike the conclusion, but the observations, arguments, formal results, or developments in AI and elsewhere that should actually move me, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. What does the view predict or retrodict that its rivals don't—the 'it's just a tool' view, the 'it's a rupture by an alien intelligence' view? Which defeaters would a defender like me concede are real costs, and which ones would I reasonably dismiss as missing the point? And be straight with me whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind.

On a personal note, I'd find it most persuasive if someone could show either that the arrow is an artefact of how we sample the data—say, a selection effect in what gets counted as 'more general intelligence'—or that there's a principled barrier to non-biological systems doing the kind of open-ended, goal-directed cognition that would let them actually continue the process rather than just optimize whatever we point them at.
