You are a professor of philosophy of law with a background in criminal law theory, moral philosophy, and the rule of law. I want a careful, argumentatively disciplined answer, not a summary of the case. Begin by brainstorming, provisionally and without committing, the strongest available justifications for the traditional maxim that ignorance of the law is no excuse; only then turn to the case and press each justification against it.

The episode is Lambert v. California, decided by the United States Supreme Court in 1957. The defendant had previously been convicted in California of forgery, a felony. A Los Angeles city ordinance made it a criminal offense for a person convicted of a felony to remain in the city for more than five days without registering with the police. The defendant remained in Los Angeles for more than five days without registering; she did not know that the registration requirement existed. After she was arrested on suspicion of another offense, she was charged with and convicted of violating the registration ordinance. The Supreme Court reversed the conviction. It held that due process bars convicting a person of such a registration offense when she had no actual knowledge of the duty to register and the prosecution cannot show a probability of such knowledge. The Court stressed that the violation consisted of wholly passive conduct—mere continued presence—and that nothing in the circumstances would naturally prompt a person to ask whether registration was required. It distinguished the ordinance from registration and licensing schemes tied to activities such as running a business, where the regulated activity itself gives notice that requirements may attach.

Analyze this episode through the lens of the question whether it is fair to bind people to legal rules they cannot know, and then run the analysis in the other direction: use the episode to test the principle rather than only applying the principle to the episode. In particular: (a) Distinguish three or four different rationales for the ignorance maxim—administrability, the incentive to learn the law, fair opportunity to comply, and the difference between wrongs that exist apart from the statute and wrongs that the statute itself creates—and say which rationales Lambert actually engages and which it leaves untouched. (b) Give a precise account of what, on a fairness-based view, the state legitimately demanded of this defendant and what it demanded illegitimately, identifying where the theory must speak and where it falls silent. (c) Examine whether the Court's distinction between passive status offenses and activities that put a reasonable person on inquiry draws a morally defensible line, or whether it is an ad hoc compromise between the maxim and fairness. (d) Say whether Lambert is best read as a narrow exception that proves the rule, as an anomaly the doctrine cannot absorb, or as evidence that the maxim was never a single principle about knowledge in the first place. Where your analysis forces you to choose between a reading that is intellectually honest and one that merely preserves the doctrine, say so explicitly.
