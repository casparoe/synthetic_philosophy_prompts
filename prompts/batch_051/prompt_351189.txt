i'm about to go read a bunch of positions on which computations matter morally (functionalism, integrated information, global workspace, computationalism, and the rest) and i keep seeing everyone attack everyone else one weird case at a time. before i read any more of that, I want the full spec first. You're a professional philosopher of mind who works on computational theories of consciousness and the boundaries of moral status, and your job is to think carefully and not just gesture at intuitions. Start by brainstorming a bunch of tough cases, then write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of which computations have moral status has to get right.

for each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, not just a slogan. and be explicit about which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of one camp (substrate-independence, unity of the subject, continuity, and so on). if you think some subset of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied—for instance, substrate-neutrality plus a ban on pancomputationalism plus a graded boundary line—say so and explain why; that's the part i most want. a short scorecard of the main views at the end is fine, but the desiderata themselves are the point.

make sure your list handles at least these:

- the easy anchors: awake human, dog, probably a fish, versus a rock, a thermostat, and a tic-tac-toe program—and why getting the anchors right isn't enough;
- the ladder of small nervous systems: a sea anemone or jellyfish nerve net, c. elegans with its 302 neurons, a lobster, a cortical organoid—where the line is and what property actually does the work;
- the difference between "has any status" and "how much," the difference between a moral patient and a moral agent, and why a theory can't quietly collapse either pair;
- part-whole and group cases: one cortical column, a split-brain patient's two hemispheres, a whole committee or company computing a decision, and the question of which level or levels (if any) host the subject;
- the attribution/implementation problem in particular: a physical process can be interpreted as computing all sort of things, so "it runs some computation" can't by itself settle whether it matters—otherwise either everything is a moral patient or you can gerrymander anything out of status. so say what it is for a computation to be genuinely implemented, not just loosely described;
- the simulation case: a perfect simulation of a suffering brain, or a million people passing slips of paper that implement the same program. is the simulated subject real? if yes, what stops the subject from being counted twice, once as the simulation and once as the underlying machine? if no, what exactly is a simulation missing?
- temporal gaps: general anesthesia, dreamless sleep, a paused program resumed later, maybe a computation run one instruction per year. a theory should say whether the subject flickers out and back, and whether the one before the gap is the same subject as the one after.

then tell me, with reasons, which single requirement on your list knocks out the most existing views when you take it seriously.
