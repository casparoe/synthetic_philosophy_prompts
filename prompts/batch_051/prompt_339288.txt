I am preparing a discussion section on the paradox of blackmail and keep vacillating about how much each candidate resolution is actually worth. The puzzle, as I understand it, is this. It is generally lawful for me to disclose a true but damaging fact about someone, and generally lawful for me to ask a person for money; yet if I make the disclosure conditional on payment — "Pay me ten thousand dollars or I will tell your spouse" — the combined act is criminal blackmail. The task is to explain why a conjunction of two permitted acts should itself be prohibited.

Here are six candidate accounts I have collected, stated crudely and in no particular order. (a) No-wrong: the paradox is genuine, and the correct resolution is that blackmail should not be punished; the prohibition is an indefensible protection of reputation at the expense of speech and bargaining. (b) Third-party harm: the real target of the law is the collateral damage disclosure does to spouses, employers, or the community, and the blackmailer may not use that damage as a bargaining chip. (c) Privacy: the wrong is that the blackmailer converts someone's private information into leverage, in effect selling the victim back control over their own secrets. (d) Exploitation: the blackmailer produces nothing and profits solely from a vulnerability they did nothing to create. (e) Conditional threat: the distinctive wrong is structural — making a threatened harm contingent on payment coerces the victim in a way that a bare request or a bare disclosure need not. (f) Economic monopoly: the blackmailer exacts a price for not doing something they have no entitlement to charge for, a kind of private rent on another person's exposure.

To keep the ranking honest, weigh each account against this case: someone threatens to publish a true statement that a public official privately holds a position the official publicly denies; no third party is clearly harmed, and the only person exposed is the official. Please sort the six accounts into three or four tiers according to how well they resolve the original paradox while handling that case, and structure your answer in these named sections, in order: (1) "Tier list" (four to six lines, best accounts first); (2) "Why each account lands where it does" (about 150–250 words, one or two sentences per account, explicitly testing each against the public-official case); and (3) "Hardest remaining problem" (two to four sentences on the strongest objection to your top-ranked account, or the feature of the puzzle no account handles cleanly).
