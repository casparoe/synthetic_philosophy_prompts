Here is a parallel I have been trying to decide about. Futarchy's core design is a two-stage separation of labor: citizens vote on a metric of national welfare, and then prediction markets estimate each proposed policy's expected effect on that metric, with policy decisions meant to be constrained by the market forecast rather than by unstructured debate over the policy's merits. Clinical drug approval, in its confirmatory logic, seems to me to run the same two-stage architecture: regulators pre-specify the primary endpoint a treatment is supposed to move, and then a controlled statistical procedure estimates whether it moves that endpoint; approval is meant to be keyed to that pre-specified estimate, with ad hoc reinterpretation of the evidence treated as a defect rather than a feature. Both institutions appear to be attempts to replace all-things-considered judgment with a disciplined split between a normative stage that fixes the standard of success and an epistemic stage in which a neutral mechanism identifies interventions that meet the standard.

The obvious disanalogy is that a trial can randomize, blind, and isolate a treatment from the rest of society, whereas a polity cannot be randomized and a policy has general-equilibrium effects. I cannot tell whether that is a structural difference or merely an engineering one. Is this parallel deep, superficial, or mixed? State the mapping precisely (metric to endpoint, market to trial, voters to regulators, policy to drug), say what would have to be true of both institutions for the lessons of one to transfer to the other, and identify one thing the parallel predicts about futarchy that would likely turn out false. Please also say how confident you are in this assessment and which point you are least sure of.
