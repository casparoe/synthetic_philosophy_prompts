I've been asked to help my town's rental assistance program decide whether to adopt a software tool that assigns every applicant a risk score — in this case, a guess at how likely someone is to fall behind on rent — so the office can decide who gets extra case management. I'm not a statistician, and the longer I read, the more I notice that people use "fairness" to mean completely different things. One person says the tool is fair only if its scores mean the same thing for every group: someone given a 7 out of 10 should have roughly the same chance of actually falling behind no matter which group they're in. Another says it's fair only if it flags people who won't fall behind at the same rate in every group. A third says it's fair only if it misses people who will fall behind at the same rate in every group. Then I came across a result suggesting this disagreement isn't just a matter of opinion: once you set a cutoff, a tool used across two groups generally cannot satisfy all three at once — calibrated risk, equal false-positive rates, and equal false-negative rates — unless the two groups have the same underlying rate of falling behind or the tool is essentially a perfect predictor.

Before I read another vendor's fairness report, I want the specification built from the ground up. What must a defensible standard of fairness for such a tool actually deliver? Write the desiderata: the cases it has to handle (groups of very different sizes, groups with very different base rates, a tool that is useful but far from perfect), the distinctions it cannot collapse (fairness of how the tool is built versus fairness of the numbers it produces, a false positive that burdens someone versus a false negative that withholds help from someone, the score's job versus the human decision made on top of it), and the mathematical facts it cannot contradict — especially the three-way trade-off I just described — with the consideration behind each requirement. Then tell me, briefly, which single requirement does the most to rule out the proposals people keep putting in front of us, and flag any subset of requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied given the trade-off. I want a committed answer: make the calls, give your reasons, and don't just lay out the options and leave me to choose.

End with a line \answer{...} naming the single requirement you think is most exclusionary.
