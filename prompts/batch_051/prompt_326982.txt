One of my labmates keeps telling everyone we're overcomplicating things: neural data is basicaly genetic data. You get broad consent, you de-identify the recordings, you put them in a controlled-access repository, and you're done. His whole argument runs on the genome analogy — mental privacy is just genetic privacy with a different analyte, and all the talk about cognitive liberty is ethicists and law reviews trying to invent a problem they can sell a solution to. He's used this to argue we shouldn't put any special neurorights language in the protocol.

I want the analogy audited properly rather than waved around. I don't need citations or a survey of the regulations — I want the conceptual mapping. Give me the correspondence table first: what in the neural-data domain is supposed to play the role of the genome, the raw reads, the variant calls, the "your DNA also leaks information about your relatives" feature, and the special regulatory status genetic data has acquired. For each mapping say what work it's doing in his argument. Then give me the disanalogies, and split them into the cosmetic ones and the ones that actually matter for the question of whether genetic-style data protection can carry mental privacy. Finally, tell me whether his conclusion survives — that we don't need a distinct concept of cognitive liberty because data governance covers it — or whether the analogy was doing the work the whole time. I'm fine with the answer being that the analogy works for one half of the debate and collapses for the other, if that's where it actually lands.
