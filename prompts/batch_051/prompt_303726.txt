So I’m a software engineer with zero formal philosophy background, but I’ve been reading about Nāgārjuna and Madhyamaka on and off for a few months, mostly trying to get what “emptiness” actually amounts to. The part I keep chewing on is the tetralemma. As I understand it, you take some claim about how things ultimately are and line up four options: it’s true, it’s false, it’s both true and false, it’s neither true nor false. Then the Madhyamaka move is supposed to be that all four fail—but not because the right answer is hiding in a fifth option; even “neither” is just one more place the mind tries to stand. Emptiness isn’t a fifth truth value, it’s the collapse of the whole menu. My engineer brain wants to model this as a four-valued logic with a bunch of negations, and the reading-group people keep telling me that’s exactly the move that turns it into something it isnt.

I’d like you to write a poem for me, maybe something I could read at the last meeting of that group. Form: four stanzas of four or five lines each, where each stanza is built around one of those four positions—set up a frame for “it is,” then “it is not,” then “it both is and is not,” then “it neither is nor is not”—maybe with that formula as the stanza’s closing line or a weak refrain. Then a short closing stanza, three or four lines, where the structure visibly gives way: the rhyme scheme or line shape loosens, and the poem undercuts all four without elegantly settling into “neither” or into some smug fifth option. I want the form to do the philosophical work, the way a villanelle’s refrains keep passing each other. Someone who knows the tetralemma should recognize what’s happening and feel the floor fall out at the right moment; someone who doesn’t know the theory should still just get a good poem, not a lecture with line breaks. The obvious failure mode is writing a checklist of negations—try to make it feel like the vertigo of each footstep dissolving rather than a transcript of a logic puzzle.
