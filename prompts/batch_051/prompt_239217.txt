At a rationalist meetup last weekend I ended up standing on the edge of an argument between Devon and Sasha about whether the geometry of word embeddings is real semantic structure or just a useful artifact. I've skimmed a lot of interpretability and NLP posts, but they went a few layers deeper than I usually go, and by the end I honestly couldn't tell whose right. Here's the back-and-forth as best I can reconstruct it.

Devon opened with the analogy thing. In a standard embedding space, king - man + woman lands near queen, and Paris - France + Italy lands near Rome. He said this isn't a parlor trick: a single linear read-off in a high-dimensional space lands on the right concept, so the simplest explanation is that the space encodes relational dimensions like capital-of and gender.

Sasha shot back that the famous examples are cherry-picked. There are thousands of analogy relations in those datasets, and people quote the five that look clean; nobody pastes the failures where the nearest neighbor is some random proper noun because surface form and frequency dominate. The choice of cosine as the similarity metric is under-motivated, and in a lot of real embedding spaces nearest neighbors are driven by frequency, register, and part of speech. Static embeddings can't even represent a word like bank, which is just a smear of two unrelated senses. "If the geometry can't tell the two senses apart, calling it the structure of meaning is a stretch."

Devon said polysemy is basically solved by contextual embeddings, where every token gets its own vector, so the bank objection only lands against the oldest models. And analogy datasets test hundreds of relations systematically, so the successes aren't all post hoc. Then he went philosophical: the distributional idea is that word meaning just is a summary of the contexts a word occurs in. If that's right, a space that faithfully summarizes co-occurrence isn't a proxy for meaning — it's a map of it. "All maps distort, but a distorted map isn't an artifact."

Sasha said that's too fast, because the co-occurrence statistics are shaped by the training objective and the corpus, so the distortion may not track semantics at all. Rare words cluster by spelling, in-domain words cluster by topic, and embeddings carry a huge amount of frequency and text-type information. Then she made the point that stuck with me: if you add the same constant vector to every word, cosine similarities and vector arithmetic change completely. "A real geometry should survive a change of coordinates, but here moving the origin destroys the analogies. The algebra is a property of the particular parameterization, not of meaning."

Devon answered that coordinate origins are arbitrary in physics too, but vectors and inner products are still real. What matters is the direction structure and the angles, which are invariant under rotations; the origin is fixed by the training process anyway. And the artifact story has to explain why independently trained embeddings align so well across models and languages under simple linear maps. "If the geometry were noise, different runs wouldn't share a skeleton; alignment would just fail."

Sasha said alignment is less impressive than it sounds. Procrustes alignment can make high-dimensional random point clouds line up surprisingly well, because in high dimensions random vectors are close to orthogonal and the objective has a lot of slack. She conceded embeddings are obviously useful — retrieval, clustering, translation, probing. "But utility isn't truth. A subway map is useful for navigation, and it systematically distorts. The question is whether meaning is literally vectorial, or whether the vector space is just a very good projection of something with no intrinsic metric — something built out of reference, inference, and composition." A vector has no internal structure, she said, and entailment relations like every dog is an animal aren't distance relations, which is a big part of why embeddings keep disappointing people who expect them to do logic.

Devon said Sasha was now moving the goalposts from similarity to full compositional semantics. "The original claim was never that you can do first-order logic in an embedding space. The claim is that similarity and relational analogy are genuinely captured, and cross-lingual alignment plus systematic analogy success make the artifact story really strained." Then he tried a brain analogy: if the cortex represents concepts as distributed patterns with similar things nearby in representational space, a geometric similarity space is a plausible model of semantic memory, not just an engineering convenience.

Sasha said the brain analogy cuts the other way. Cortical similarity spaces track perceptual and associative similarity much more than linguistic reference and inferential role, so they don't rescue the specifically semantic claim. "And if real neural networks do something like superposition" — she explained this as packing more features than dimensions by using nearly orthogonal directions — "then what looks like a clean geometry might be an artifact of a compressed representational format there too. I'm happy to grant that geometry is a useful interface. I just don't think it is the structure of meaning."

They both looked at me like I was the tiebreaker. I said I'd sleep on it, because each move felt right while it was being said and then stopped being obvious as soon as the other side replied. Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of the moves above, say whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between, and say how the other side should respond. Also tell me what both of them are missing, if anything, and who ends up more right overall.
