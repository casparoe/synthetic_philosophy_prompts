Van Fraassen's Reflection principle has been quietly bugging me for years, and I want help deciding what should actually give. The principle, roughly: my current credence in a hypothesis, conditional on my future self's assigning credence r to it, should itself be r. More precisely, P_t(H | P_{t+n}(H)=r) = r. What attracts me is that it looks like a coherence condition on being a temporally extended agent. If I think of my future self as me-with-more-evidence, someone who has everything I now have and conditionalizes on whatever she learns, then my present credences should already track the credences I'll later have; otherwise I'm treating my later self as systematically wrong, which is a weird kind of self-undermining. The diachronic Dutch book stuff is cute too—if I violate it, a bookie can sell me bets now and later that jointly guarantee a loss. I find that genuinely compelling.

And yet. I also have an intuition I can't shake: sometimes I should bind my future self rather than defer to her. Advance directives, Ulysses contracts, the fact that I won't answer email at 1 a.m. because I know my 1 a.m. self is about to 'decide' things with absurd confidence. If I know I'm about to take a drug that will make me highly confident of claims I now have no good reason to believe, Reflection seems to say that, conditional on learning my future self will be 95% confident in p, I should be 95% now. That strikes me as obviously wrong; the correct response is to discount that future self, not to treat her as an expert. The future self in those cases isn't me-plus-evidence; she's me-minus-something.

So here's what I want. Give me the strongest case for Reflection and the strongest case for the binding intuition, then tell me what should yield. Is the intuition just confusing deference with obedience, or sneaking in a first-person/third-person asymmetry that doesn't do the work I think it does? Is the principle saved by restricting it to future selves who are rational and at least as well informed as I am now—and does that restriction make the prinicple vacuous or merely honest? What would justify dropping the intuition, what would justify amending or abandoning the principle, and is there a reading on which both survive? I don't want a verdict so much as the shape of the negotiation.
