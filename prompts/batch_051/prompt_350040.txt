Act as an expert decision theorist and work through this carefully, starting by reducing the compound lotteries. Take the classic Allais setup: in the first pair, a sure $1M versus a 0.89/0.10/0.01 shot at $1M/$5M/$0; in the second pair, a 0.11 shot at $1M versus a 0.10 shot at $5M (and $0 otherwise). The familiar pattern — safe in the first pair, risky in the second — is exactly what expected utility theory says cannot be jointly rational. Here is the variation: after you choose but before your gamble pays, there is an independent 5% chance of a freeze; if the freeze hits, you get $0 no matter what the gamble would have paid, and if it misses, the gamble pays as usual. The same freeze risk is tacked on to all four options, so even the sure million is no longer sure.

Now work out how this changes the analysis for (a) standard expected utility and (b) risk-weighted expected utility. For (a), spell out the independence/compound-reduction step and show the freeze drops out completely. For (b), use the decumulative form: with outcomes x1 < ... < xn ordered worst to best and probabilities p1...pn, REU = u(x1) + Σ_{i=2}^{n} r(Σ_{j=i}^{n} p_j)(u(x_i) − u(x_{i−1})). Set u(0)=0 and write the four frozen gambles' REU values. Then show that if r is a power function, r(p)=p^γ, the freeze is entirely inert: non of the pairwise comparisons changes, even though the safe option has lost its certainty. Say exactly why, and state the general condition on r for the freeze to be non-neutral (and give one concrete r where it does move something). Finish with a short paragraph on what this tells us about the usual certainty-effect explanation of the Allais paradox.
