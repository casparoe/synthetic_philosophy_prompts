Consider a design choice that is now standard across streaming platforms, social networks, and online retailers: they treat a user's observed behavior as more authoritative evidence of her preferences than her own stated preferences, and they act on that epistemic ranking. A viewer may tell the service during onboarding that she wants short, substantive documentaries, but if her watch time, click-throughs, completions, and searches skew toward hour-long gossip clips, the algorithm will serve her more gossip clips, on the working assumption that her behavior has revealed what she really wants. Autoplay and infinite-scroll features are then tuned to keep her engaged, as if continued viewing were itself the preference being elicited and satisfied. This is an industrial-scale piece of applied moral psychology and preference epistemology, even if no one at the company describes it that way. What theory of preference and agency does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguishing what the design is strictly committed to from what it merely fits well with, and identifying where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. In particular, consider whether the practice must assume that preferences are stable, pre-existing states that choice behavior faithfully discloses, or whether it can survive if preferences are partly constructed by the very choice situations the platform engineers; whether collapsing what a person does into what she wants is a presupposition rather than a dispensable modeling convenience; and whether the system's dual role as measurer and manipulator of the behavior it treats as evidence makes its inferences incoherent. Finally, say what would need to change in how such systems are built if one took seriously the distinction between endorsed preferences and momentary impulses, or the possibility of systematically self-defeating behavior.
