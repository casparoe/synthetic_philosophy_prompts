I have been trying to figure out what, if anything, grown children owe their parents. The view I am testing — often called the friendship model of filial obligation — holds that a grown child's special obligations to a parent are not grounded in repayment or gratitude for the sacrifices of child-rearing, but in the relationship the two have now, so that what an adult child owes mirrors what one friend owes another. My own credence that this view is substantially right is around 0.35, and a classmate of mine puts it around 0.75; I want to figure out where our disagreement actually lives. Please give me your own credence and decompose it: assign rough probabilities to (a) the claim that a person can owe duties of reciprocity for benefits she never chose to receive and could not have refused; (b) the claim that the parent-child relationship is relevantly unlike friendship because it is unchosen, asymmetric, and difficult to leave; (c) the claim that a friendship-based account can actually require as much as we ordinarily think grown children owe — regular contact, care in old age, material help when needed; and (d) the claim that our clearest intuitions about ungrateful adult children are tracking the parent's past sacrifices rather than the quality of the present relationship. Show how these four terms combine into your final number, then tell me which single term that number is most sensitive to, which term my classmate and I most likely disagree about, and what concrete case or piece of evidence would move that term the most.
