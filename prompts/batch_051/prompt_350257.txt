I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, and next month I am presenting a department colloquium defending the following thesis: moral principles are best understood as lossy compressions of the space of particular reasons — practically indispensable summaries that do not state the truth-conditions of rightness. My argument runs as follows. Holism about reasons is true: a feature that counts in favour of an act in one context can count against it in another, so no finite set of exceptionless principles can codify right-making. But the particularist conclusion that principles drop out of moral thought is too fast, because agents evidently use principles to carry verdicts to novel cases. I argue the best explanation is that principles function like predictive models: they trade fidelity for tractability, and their value is epistemic and pedagogical rather than metaphysical. The payoff is that particularism and generalism, properly located, are not rivals — the particularist wins the metaphysics, the generalist wins the practice.

The audience is a mixed department, and I know the room well enough to expect different objections from different quarters. One senior figure is a committed particularist in the Dancy tradition who will treat any continuing role for principles as backsliding. A second is a Rossian pluralist who holds that pro tanto principles state real recurring right-making relations and will think I have conceded too much. A Kantian constructivist on the faculty takes principles to be constitutive of practical reason, not summaries of it. An empirically minded moral psychologist will be sympathetic but will press on whether the compression story is testable. And several applied ethicists want principles that can be taught and used in clinical and policy settings and will not be reassured by an account on which a principle's exception conditions cannot be stated in advance.

Prepare me for the question period. I want the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as these people would actually put them, with a note on which objections have real force and what I should concede up front. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and an honest note on where the reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me why it is the one that could undo the paper.
