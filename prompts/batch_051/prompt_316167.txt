I keep being pulled back to a version of the classical position that envy is a vice and resentment is the emotion that properly tracks injustice, and I want to take that position seriously because of its systematic virtues. It gives a clean criterion: resentment responds to a wrong done to someone, while envy responds to another's advantage over me, where the advantage need not involve any wrong at all. It explains a familiar fact about avowal — people can avow resentment, while envy is something people conceal or confess — and it seems to protect a defensible ideal of self-respect, on which my sense of my own worth should not be a function of my rank relative to others. It also gives institutions a tidy target: address wrongful acts, not the bare fact that some have more than others.

But I cannot shake the opposite intuition in a certain class of cases. Where inequalities are deep, entrenched, and inherited, some episodes of what I would unhesitatingly call envy do not feel like a shameful positional reflex; they feel apt, even moral, as though they perceive something that the wrong-based vocabulary of resentment cannot formulate — namely, that the comparative structure itself, the standing fact of who ends up with what, is the injustice. That is a stronger and different claim than the claim that some wrongful act produced the inequality, and I do not see how resentment, with its act- and agent-shape, can carry it. So I am left with a theory that earns its keep and an intuition that refuses to be debunked. I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a reading on which both survive — perhaps by treating envy's comparative character as a feature rather than a disqualification. In particular, I would like an account of when an emotion's being positional disables it as a signal of injustice, and whether Rawls's notion of excusable envy can be developed into such an account or instead collapses back into one of the two sides.
