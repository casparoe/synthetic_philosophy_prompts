been turning over the reductionist story about testimony, partly becuase I keep noticing how much of what I now treat as "found out" comes from a model rather than a person. The passage I keep coming back to is from David Hume, An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, Section X ("Of Miracles") — the setup part, not the miracles part:

"Were not the memory tenacious to a certain degree; had not men commonly an inclination to truth and a principle of probity; were they not sensible to shame, when detected in a falsehood: Were not these, I say, discovered by experience to be qualities, inherent in human nature, we should never repose the least confidence in human testimony."

His baseline case is a witness who saw the thing, remembered it, and could be shamed if caught lying. A language model churning out an answer has none of the four bits Hume lists: no tenacious memory in his sense, no inclination to truth as a settled trait, no probity, no capacity for shame. And still I catch myself updating on fluent model output roughly the way I would on a person's say-so, and in at least some cases that seems fine.

So: does Hume's reductionist argument survive the shift to LLM-generated testimony, or does it need repair, or is it just dead in that setting? My own prior is that it limps along in a weakened form — you can always run the same induction over a new source-class with some measured reliability — but that this quietly drops the thing that made the argument about testimony in the first place. I want the answer to: (a) say in the first sentence whether the argument survives, is repaired, or is dead; (b) identify which premise the LLM case actually puts pressure on; and (c) if there's a repaired version, give it and say whether it still distinguishes testimony from ordinary induction. Bottom line first, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating the question.
