I spent my career in physics, mostly thermodynamics, and now that I'm retired I keep trying to run moral philosophy through conservation laws. Here's one I've been chewing on, which I call the conservation of promises. The idea is that the binding force of a promise never just disappears. Keep the promise and the obligation is discharged, like work done. Fail to keep it — you get sick, you flake, the situation changes — and the obligation doesn't vanish, it changes form. If I promise to help you move and my back goes out, I still owe you something: a replacement helper, a raincheck, or at least a real explanation and an effort to make good. The only way to wipe the obligation out entirely is if the promisee releases you. But that's the part that bothers me, because release looks exactly like destroying an obligation, and my whole principle says obligation is conserved. Maybe release isn't destruction — maybe the promisee absorbs the obligation and forgives it, like a heat sink. I'm not sure. Is this a real idea or am I just dressing up an analogy in a lab coat? Does it already have a less physics-y name? And where does it break — for instance, does it imply that every broken promise leaves a debt that can never really be payed off?
