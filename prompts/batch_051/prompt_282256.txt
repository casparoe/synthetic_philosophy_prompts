I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about the datafied childhood: parents now begin building a child's public identity before birth—ultrasound posts, pregnancy updates, and then a running stream of photos, medical disclosures, behavioural incidents, and location traces. I keep returning to Joel Feinberg's argument for the child's right to an open future. As I understand it, the argument says that a child has a future-oriented right against having major life options foreclosed by the adults who act for her while she is too young to choose. The paradigm cases were deliberate, identifiable decisions by parents or the state: withdrawing a child from school, arranging a marriage, refusing medical treatment, or consenting to a permanent bodily intervention.

The practice I'm writing about does not fit that paradigm. No individual parental post or app permission is intended to foreclose anything, and each is normally harmless. Yet the accumulated, searchable dossier can later constrain an adult's employment, insurability, reputation, and freedom to reinvent herself, in ways that are broadly foreseeable but not traceable to any single decision.

Does Feinberg's argument survive this development? Does the right to an open future still do the needed work—perhaps by expanding parental duties from avoiding discrete, dramatic interventions to limiting the slow, cumulative construction of a child's permanent record—or does the argument depend on a model of option-closing as a deliberate, identifiable act that this situation no longer fits? If it can be repaired, name the premise that is under pressure and say what the repaired principle claims; if it cannot, say what should replace it as the ground for protecting children's future interests. I'm asking about the fate of the argument itself, not about which platform rules would be wise.
