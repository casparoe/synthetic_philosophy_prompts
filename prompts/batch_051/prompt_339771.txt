At a seminar I attended, two participants later asked me to referee a disagreement that came out of a passage from Henry Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics. The passage—which I will render in my own words, since I do not have the text in front of me—says roughly this: the bare fact that one stretch of conscious life comes earlier and another comes later is not itself a rational ground for weighting the earlier stretch's good more heavily; a rational agent aims at good generally, and to discount a future good merely because it is future is to treat a merely temporal difference as though it were an intrinsic reason.

After the seminar, the first participant said the passage settles it: once you separate pure time discounting from uncertainty about whether the future good will actually arrive, impatience is just an irrational bias. Preferring a smaller present good to a larger future good merely because the present good is vivid, or merely because the future good is later, is no more defensible than favoring a smaller good because it is nearby in space.

The second participant replied that this reads too much into the passage. The passage says only that temporal location is not by itself a reason to weight the earlier good more. But someone could coherently make pure time preference part of her final conception of her own good: she might value being the kind of temporally extended agent whose present concerns have special authority, or who cares more about what is present to her now. The passage does not prove that such a preference is irrational; it just asserts the standpoint of impartial good.

The first participant answered that this changes the subject. If you value a narrative structure, or the special authority of present concerns, then you are not discounting the future because it is future. You are valuing something else that merely has a temporal shape. The passage's target is bare temporal location, and pointing to a different value does not refute it.

The second participant countered that the real dispute is whether there can be a pure time preference that survives as a rational final end. The passage assumes the very standpoint—aiming at good generally—from which temporal location looks irrelevant. Anyone who rejects that standpoint will not find the passage persuasive, so using it as a knockdown against pure time preference begs the question.

The first participant replied that it is not question-begging but an explication of what rationality requires for a life extended through time. Rationality demands a reason for differential treatment of one own's temporal moments, and the passage makes exactly that demand: give me a reason the temporal position itself matters. If narrative structure or present authority is offered as such a reason, that is the right kind of response, but the passage has done real work by forcing the issue.

The second participant said that still leaves the passage proving only something everyone accepts—that bare time labels alone do not matter—while doing nothing to adjudicate the live cases where someone says the future self is not yet real, or that the present self has special standing because it is the one making the choice.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not.
