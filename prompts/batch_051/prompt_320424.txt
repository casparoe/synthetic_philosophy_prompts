You are a professional philosopher specializing in Indian Buddhist metaphysics, particularly Nāgārjuna, Madhyamaka, and the recent analytic reconstructions of both. I want you to think slowly and carefully, and to work through your reasoning rather than jumping to a verdict. Begin by brainstorming the candidate defeaters before you rank them.

Here is the view I currently hold, or at least find most plausible. I read the tetralemma—the pattern by which, for any candidate characterization of ultimate reality, none of the four alternatives, P, not-P, both, or neither, is assertable—not as a positive four-valued logic and not as the claim that reality is ineffable in some fifth, mystical way, but as a dialectical device. The four alternatives fail because asserting any of them covertly presupposes that the subject matter has svabhāva, a determinate nature of its own. Emptiness, on this reading, is not a property that ultimate reality turns out to possess; it is the general failure of categorical assertions about ultimate reality to gain traction. This is meant to handle the classic self-undermining objection: the claim that everything is empty should not be treated as one more categorical claim about everything, but as a withdrawal of the license to make such claims, and so the further question whether emptiness is itself empty is a category mistake. By conventional truth I mean the domain of dependent, transactional discourse that this view does not reject but says is misread when mistaken for an account of how things ultimately are.

What would count against this position? I want the serious negatives, not a generic summary of the other side. For each candidate defeater, tell me why someone attracted to my view would have to concede that it has force, and how much damage it really does. In particular, I want your treatment of the following. First, arguments that the reading does not discharge the self-undermining worry but merely relocates it, for example by exempting the statement of the view itself from the strictures it applies to every other assertion. Second, arguments that the resulting position collapses into quietism, global anti-realism, or disguised nihilism, and that it cannot explain why conventional truth remains usable, constrained, and able to support ordinary distinctions between better and worse claims. Third, any formal work on the tetralemma, or any paraconsistent or dialetheist reading, that would make the four alternatives look more like a substantive logic than a dialectical method and thereby undercut my reading. Fourth, any interpretive considerations internal to the Madhyamaka tradition that count against treating emptiness as the withdrawal of categorical assertion rather than as a statement about the way things are.

I also want a map of what this view risks. Say what it predicts or implies that its main rival readings do not, where its most exposed joint is, and whether it has been insulated—for instance by treating every apparent counterexample as merely conventional—so that nothing could in principle count against it. If it has been insulated, say plainly whether that is a problem for a view of this kind, or whether a view about the limits of assertion is entitled to that kind of insulation.

Throughout, separate what is settled among people who actually work on Madhyamaka from what is genuinely contested. Do not present a contested interpretation—such as a particular reading of the tetralemma, of the two truths, or of emptiness itself—as if it were the consensus. When you rely on a controversial reconstruction, flag it as such.
