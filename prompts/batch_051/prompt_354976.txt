Two friends of mine got into a long argument at dinner the other night about the boat case. You're the only rescuer, two boats are going down, one holds one stranger and the other holds five strangers, and you can only reach one of them before the other goes under. No one else can help, and the six people are alike in every way that might matter. Evan said obviously you save the five. Mina, who's been reading Taurek, said it's not obvious at all. I mostly sat there and now I regret not haveing a better grip on it. Here's basically the exchange:

Evan: "Five lives are five lives. One is one. You save the five. I honestly don't see what the other side is even claiming."

Mina: "You're imagining a five-fold loss, but there's no such thing. Each of the five suffers her own death, and the one suffers his. No subject suffers or loses five deaths, so the size of the group doesn't add up to a bigger reason. Taurek's point is that the numbers don't count by themselves."

Evan: "So you'd flip a coin between one person and five people? That's unserious triage. You'd let four extra people die because you wanted to be fair to a head count."

Mina: "It's not a head count, it's the only way to give each individual an equal chance. A coin flip gives the solo person 50% and each of the five 50%. Going straight for the five gives the solo person 0% purely because he's outnumbered. That's majority rule over lives."

Evan: "Think about it from behind a veil of ignorance. Before you know which of the six you are, you'd choose the save-the-five policy, because it gives you a five-in-six chance of surviving instead of one-in-two. So save-the-five is better for each person taken one by one, not just better 'for the group.'"

Mina: "But there is no veil. You're the rescuer, and the solo person is that actual guy, not a random draw among identities. You can't make an actual person bear a loss by pointing to a hypothetical lottery he never entered. That ex ante story just sneaks back in the aggregation you claimed you weren't doing."

Evan: "And yet if you flip and it sends you to the one, five actual people die who would have lived. I get that a person's claim shouldn't be outvoted like a vote. But if numbers don't provide a reason, what does? Is there any reason to choose the five over the one that doesn't treat the one as a fraction of a person?"

Mina: "I don't have a complete criterion, but 'save the five to maximize survival' literally is treating people as numbers. I'd honestly flip even in a one-versus-a-million case."

They asked me to referee and I couldn't. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what? I'd also like to know: if Mina's right, is she really committed to flipping a coin between one person and a million, or is there a coherent middle view where the numbers matter only as a kind of tie-breaker, or something else? And what's the strongest version of each side that neither of them quite got out?
