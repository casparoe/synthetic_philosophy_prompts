Can you help me figure out where this argument goes wrong, or if it actually goes through? I'm reading Adam Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments, and he says (roughly) that the pain of falling from a better to a worse condition is generally greater than the pleasure of rising from a worse to a better one. Heres the argument:

(1) Losses are felt more sharply than gains of the same size.
(2) If losses loom larger than gains, then people will sometimes demand more to sell an object they already own than they would pay to buy the very same object.
(3) That would explain the endowment effect: in the mug experiments, owners ask more to sell the mug than buyers offer to pay for it.
(4) The endowment effect is usually treated as a bias, since the object hasn't changed and the difference in prices seems to come from the mere fact of ownership.
(5) So the owner's higher price is just a psychological distortion caused by loss aversion, not a legitimate preference.
(6) Therefore policies that take the effect seriously, like demanding extra-high compensation before someone's property is taken, are deferring to an irrational quirk rather than real value.

Which step fails, if any? Could the effect be a legitimate preference for protecting your existing position—maybe losing the mug closes off options you already had, while not getting it in the first place doesn't? Or is the trouble in step (4) or (5)?
