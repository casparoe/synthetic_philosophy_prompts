In my district, the school board adopted a policy in 1998 that still governs history instruction. It reads: "History courses shall present to students the facts of the past as they actually occurred, without interpretation, opinion, or present-day judgment. The duty of the teacher is to transmit this factual record, not to impose a viewpoint." The policy has never been amended.

This year my American history teacher began a unit on Indian removal by telling us, "The Cherokee removal was genocide," and then assigning each student to write a letter dated 1838 from the perspective of a Cherokee child being forced from their home. The district has also introduced a semester course called Doing History, in which students are graded on constructing and defending an explanation of the causes of the Civil War against rival explanations supplied by the instructor. A parent has filed a complaint saying both assignments violate the 1998 policy. The principal agrees with the parent; the history department says the policy was only ever meant to stop teachers from using the classroom to push their own politics, and that these assignments make students work with evidence rather than impose a conclusion.

I am less interested in the district politics than in the rule itself. Read the policy and tell me whether the genocide lesson, the Cherokee letter assignment, or the Civil War assignment falls under it, arguing the case from the text, from the purpose, and from what the board in 1998 would likely have said if asked about these specific activities. Then say whether there is any coherent reading of the policy at all. Does it presuppose a view of historical knowledge on which the past can be reported without selection, classification, or narrative ordering? If no history course could satisfy the policy as written, what is the least revision that would make it coherent, and what view of historical truth would the revised policy commit the district to?
