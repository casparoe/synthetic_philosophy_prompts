Hey — novelist here, currently drafting a book where the main character keeps working out this theology of free will and God's knowledge, and I've realized somewhere along the way that I've basically adopted her view for myself. Before I build a four-hundred-page book on it, I want a consistency check on my pile of commitments, because I suspect they don't all fit but I can't tell which one is the load-bearing beam.

Here's where I've landed:

(1) I'm an incompatibilist about free will: when someone acts freely, nothing in the prior state of the world plus the laws settled that they'd do it. They could've done otherwise.

(2) I think counterfactuals of creaturely freedom have real truth values. In my line of work this feels natural: 'if I'd written my protagonist into the convent instead of the boarding house, she would have turned her sister in' is determinately true or false, even though that version of the chapter never existed.

(3) God, before deciding which world to create, knows all those counterfactuals by middle knowledge — so I guess I'm some kind of Molinist. Not because God makes them true, and not because the creatures exist yet; they're just true already.

(4) God actualizes a world in part by choosing the circumstances in which he already knows agents will freely do what he wants done. I think plotting works like this too: I don't crawl into a scene and move my characters' arms, I put them in the room where I know, given who they are, they'll do the thing.

(5) I hold strong-ish providence: nothing that happens is outside God's plan, and every contingent fact about the world — including our free choices, at least as permitted or intended — falls under God's will somehow.

(6) I've also got a truthmaker intuition: every contingent truth is true in virtue of something actual. I don't like free-floating facts. Even in fiction I'd say a claim about a character is true in virtue of the text or my settled authorial intentions.

(7) The book-specific one: my protagonist betrays her sister in a particular scene. I want to be able to say that God knew from before creation, via middle knowledge, that she would freely do exactly that in exactly those circumstances, and that he could set up the world so it'd happen, without him being the author of the betrayal.

So: do these hang together? Go through the pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, rank the real tensions by how bad they are, and for each real one say what the cheapest fix is and what it costs. Be blunt; I'd rather get embarassed here than hand a half-coherent theology to readers who will pick it apart on goodreads.
