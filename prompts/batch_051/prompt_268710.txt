Two friends of mine, Elena and Sam, got into it at brunch about a third friend, Dana. Dana had told an acquaintance that a movie she was about to see was shot on location in Lisbon. That's true, but Dana only half-remembered a short blurb and couldn't have given any reason; she was basically guessing.

(1) Elena said Dana asserted something perfectly fine: she said something true, and that's really the central rule of assertion — don't say false things.

(2) Sam said she shouldn't have said it flat-out: assertion presents you as knowing, and you shouldn't present yourself as knowing something you've just guessed.

(3) Elena shot back that Sam's standard would make tons of ordinary talk improper, since doctors, forecasters, and people recalling articles they read once assert appropriately all the time without really knowing. Sam answered that those people usually hedge ("I think," "the forecast says"), and hedging is fine; Dana's flat statement was the problem. Elena said Sam is just adding a "presenting yourself as knowing" requirement on top of what flat assertion actually commits you to, which is only truth.

(4) Sam then said truth alone can't be the norm, because a stopped clock is right twice a day: if truth were enough, there'd be no real difference between testimony and lucky guesses, which would break the main point of assertion — transmitting knowledge, not just true sentences.

(5) Elena replied that transmitting knowledge is only one function of assertion; sometimes the point is just to put truths on the table for joint reasoning. And anyway, she said, Sam's knowledge standard gets a worse counterexample: a biology teacher who teaches evolution because the curriculum requires it but doesn't personally believe it is, intuitively, asserting appropriately, even though she lacks knowledge (since knowledge requires belief). So neither a knowledge norm nor a belief norm seems to fit. Sam said the teacher is just a mouthpiece reciting the curriculum, not really making the assertion her own. Elena said that's ad hoc — by that trick, Dana is just reporting a blurb, and the whole dispute becomes relabeling.

They both think they won and asked me to settle it. I honestly cant tell who's right. Please go through the exchange move by move: who is right about which specific point, where each argument overreaches or mistakes the other, and then who is more right overall. Give exactly six numbered points, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion; the first five should track the five numbered moves in order, and the sixth should give the overall verdict and what both are missing.
