So here's the thing. I spent thirty-odd years running a spectroscopy lab, retired, and started reading philosophy for fun. Every time the conversation turns to AI, someone trots out Asimov's three laws — robots must not harm humans, must not stand by and let harm happen, must obey orders except when that would cause harm, and must protect themselves except when that conflicts with the earlier rules — as if that were a serious design proposal rather than a plot device. I don't want another essay explaining why they're vague. I want the standard the vagueness violates, so to speak.

Before I read any more alignment material, write me the desiderata: what would any adequate law-based (or rule-based) scheme for making artificial agents safe actually have to deliver before a working scientist would treat it as an engineering spec? For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it. I mean things like: what if the system lacks the sensory or conceptual resources to tell what counts as "harm" in the situation it's in; what if two rules pull in opposite directions and the priority ordering doesn't settle which action wins; what if the rule says "don't allow harm through inaction" but every available action risks some harm — does the scheme say how to choose? What about the difference between following a rule and actually having the goal the rule is supposed to protect, when the system can learn or alter its own rules?

Be explicit about which requirements are non-negotiable — anything that fails them just isn't a serious proposal — and which are more like tastes of particular camps (corrigibility, keeping humans in the loop, preserving human autonomy, that sort of thing). And the part I most want: tell me if any pair of the requirements can't be jointly met. For instance, is a fixed hierarchy of absolute rules in tension with the system's ability to learn from experience? Is treating the rules as unmodifiable in tension with being able to correct a system after it has gone wrong? If the list itself contains a trade-off, say so plainly — that's the result, not a bug.

I can find scorecards of Asimov's laws elsewhere; I care about the criteria theirselves. Still, please end with a short paragraph saying how the three laws fare against your list, just because it's the case everyone knows.
