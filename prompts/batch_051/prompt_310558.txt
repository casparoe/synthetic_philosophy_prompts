I've been trying to figure out what the rules around drug approval quietly assume about causation. Regulators routinely refuse to approve a treatment on the basis of observational data alone, even when the dataset is enormous and the analysis adjusts for every covariate anyone can think of; they insist on a randomised trial instead. My gloss is that this presupposes that seeing and doing are different things — that association, however rich, doesn't by itself tell you what would happen if you intervened — and that randomisation is what bridges the gap by making treatment statistically independant of everything you haven't measured. Reconstruct the implicit theory properly. Does the practice strictly presuppose something like Pearl's hierarchy, with a separate doing level and a leap from seeing to doing that needs assumptions such as no hidden confounders? Does it also presuppose that the mechanisms being studied are modular or invariant in the way structural causal models assume? And where is it underdetermined — what rival theory of causation would justify exactly the same policy? Finally, the same regulators are much happier issuing causal advice about diet or smoking from observational evidence, where a trial would be impossible or unethical: if the presupposition were right, shouldn't they be a lot more sceptical there? And can you do the answer in British English, not American?
