Question: Explain the difference between semantic and pragmatic accounts of slurs, using one slur (or a placeholder such as S, with N as its neutral counterpart) as a running example. Then say which family of views you think is more plausible, state the strongest objection to it, and say how a defender could reply. No citations required.

Student's answer: The basic division is whether the derogatory force of a slur is part of what the word literally says, i.e. its truth-conditional content, or whether it comes from how the word is used on top of a truth-conditional core that it shares with its neutral counterpart. The semantic view says a slur like S means something like 'member of group G and despicable because of it,' so that 'x is S' is true only if both parts hold. The pragmatic view says S just means 'member of G' truth-conditionally — same as N — but using S marks the speaker's contempt for G in some additional, non-truth-conditional way.

The best evidence for the pragmatic side is projection. If I say 'I didn't call Sam S' or 'If Sam is S, then he won't be invited,' the contempt toward G is still communicated even though nothing derogatory is asserted outright. That makes the derogation look like something riding along, like an implicature or presupposition, rather than ordinary asserted content.

I favor a pragmatic account, specifically the version that treats the derogation as a conversational implicature. On this story, a speaker has a choice between two words with the same descriptive content, S and N; choosing the loaded word is a marked choice, and the hearer reasons that the speaker must be trying to signal a negative attitude. Since it's an implicature, it should be cancelable: a speaker can say 'Sam is S, but I don't mean anything bad by it' and the extra derogation goes away. This also neatly explains reclaimed in-group uses: members of the group use S without the usual speaker-distance signal, so the implicature simply never arises.

The hardest objection to this account is that it makes the offense too easily removable, which sits poorly with how rigid the taboo on slurs is. People are offended by slurs even when no assertion about the group is being made — in reports, jokes, negations. But a defender should say those are all just more instances of projection, so they're already handled by the implicature story.
