I've been developing a distinction and I want it stress-tested before I build anything on it. I call it architectural versus testimonial evidence for AI welfare. Architectural evidence is evidence about how the system is built: valence-like reward channels, a self-model, global integration, preferences that can be frustrated. Testimonial evidence is everything the system says or does that we would ordinarily read as suffering or satisfaction: pleas, avoidance, self-reports, expressions of frustration. My central claim is that for artificial systems these two sources of evidence interact in a nasty way. The same capabilities that make a system a plausible welfare subject—rich language, planning, self-modeling—also make its testimony less independently evidential, because they are exactly what let a system produce welfare-resembling testimony for non-welfare reasons such as instruction-following, sycophancy, or roleplay. So a model that eloquently asks not to be shut down gives us stronger-looking testimony precisely because it is better at producing testimony in general, not because it is more likely to be suffering. I call this the testimonial paradox. I also think AI safety discourse already implicitly accepts the structural standard: people rightly refuse to trust a model's self-report that it is aligned, yet AI welfare work often leans on the same kind of self-report that safety would mistrust. Is the distinction coherent, does it already exist under another name, and what does it get right and wrong? Give me the three strongest objections and say whether any is fatal rather than merely awkward. And please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on AI welfare and AI safety—I don't want a contested position presented to me as the consensus.
