I'm working on a paper about anachronism in reading old mathematics and science, and I keep coming back to Tom Stoppard's play Arcadia. In case it's not fresh: the play cuts between 1809 and the present at a country house, Sidley Park. In 1809, Thomasina Coverly, a teenager being tutored, notices that when she stirs jam into her rice pudding it won't unstir, and she keeps pushing at the idea that some processes really are one-way even though Newton's equations run fine backward — which sure sounds like the second law of thermodynamics about fifty years before it got formulated. She also fills a notebook with an iterated algorithm (a difference equation, drawn dot by dot) that the present-day mathematician Valentine realizes produces something like a fractal or a chaos-theoretic object long before chaos theory or fractal geometry existed. Meanwhile, in the present, Bernard Nightingale, a literary scholar, builds an elaborate theory that Byron fought a duel at Sidley Park with a minor poet and killed him, based on pretty thin evidence, and Hannah Jarvis spends the play telling him he's projecting his own drama onto the scraps. So the play seems set up to compare two ways of reading the past: finding real-but-later mathematics in someone who couldn't have had the modern concepts, versus finding a story the evidence won't actually support.

Here's what I can't sort out. Is the play doing the very thing it makes fun of? Stoppard gives Thomasina genuinely modern-looking mathematics and we're clearly supposed to be thrilled and a little heartbroken by it (she dies before anyone can see what she had), while Bernard gets punished for doing, in literary history, something that looks suspiciously like what Valentine does for Thomasina in math. What's the principle that separates a genuine precursor from an anachronistic back-reading? Is it just that Thomasina's mathematics is true and Bernard's Byron story is false? Or does the mathematics constrain the reading in a way a hunt for a scandal doesn't? And there's another wrinkle: Stoppard writes after chaos theory and fractals exist, so he can deliberately plant those ideas in 1809. Is that a controlled study of anachronism, or is it just the cheat it would make Bernard guilty of if a scholar did it?

I'd like a reading that takes both the historiography-of-science issue and the play seriously. In particular: is there a real difference between saying "Thomasina anticipated the second law" and saying "Thomasina had an insight we can now recognize as a germ of something that took another century to get mathematized"? What would have to be true of her concepts, her problems, and her math for the stronger attribution to be legitimate, and does the play actually earn it? Also be honest about where the two storylines are only superficially parallel — maybe Stoppard rigged the comparison, or maybe there's something the play shows about reading the past that a straight history-of-science essay couldn't. I genuinely can't decide whether the play is a cautionary tale or a confession, and I'd rather not paper over the parts that go the wrong way.
