my mp was on the radio defending an inheritance tax hike, and when a caller asked why land can just pass down untouched while everyone else works for a living, the mp trotted out the old locke line: you own your body, you own your labour, and when you mix that labour with something unowned it becomes yours. i've since noticed the same line doing actual moral work in my own life — my brother-in-law uses it to claim a scrap of communal garden behind his flat because he's the one who weeds it, and a friend used it to argue she owns the code she writes on her own laptop in the evenings, full stop, never mind her contract. it's clearly not just a history of ideas curio.

i want you to audit the labour-mixing analogy the way you might audit 'the market is an ecosystem'. give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the labour, the thing it gets mixed into, the act of mixing, the resulting owned object, the background commons. then lay out the places where the picture falls apart, with an eye to which disanalogies are just the metaphor being a bit vivid and which ones are load-bearing for the political conclusions people draw from it. i already have a few candidate breakages in mind: the tomato-juice-in-the-ocean worry, the fact that mixing my labour with your timber seems to yield at most a claim to the added value rather than to the whole, and the way the analogy says nothing about leaving enough of comparable quality for everyone else. but i want you to seperate the breakages that actually matter from the ones that don't, and if the conclusions were being carried by the metaphor all along, say what a better analogy or a better argument would be. the conclusions i actually care about are things like: working on a resource creates private title to it even without anyone's consent, and weeding the communal garden makes it his.
