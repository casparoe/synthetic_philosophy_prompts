I serve on a three-member subcommittee of my university's tenure committee. Our task is to evaluate a single candidate against two stated criteria and report one recommendation to the full committee. The rules are explicit: the candidate merits a positive tenure recommendation if and only if both the research standard and the teaching standard are met. Each of us voted separately on the research question, on the teaching question, and on the overall recommendation, and each of us was individually consistent with the stated rule. The votes came out as follows:

- Member A: research standard met: yes; teaching standard met: no; overall recommendation: deny.
- Member B: research standard met: no; teaching standard met: yes; overall recommendation: deny.
- Member C: research standard met: yes; teaching standard met: yes; overall recommendation: grant.

Thus a majority judged that the research standard is met, and a majority judged that the teaching standard is met, while a majority judged against a positive recommendation. The full committee meets in two days and expects a single bottom line from us, not a collection of individual views. Assume for the sake of the memo that re-voting would simply reproduce this distribution.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before that meeting. It should recommend a concrete bottom line for the subcommittee to report, give the reasoning for it, and engage seriously with the strongest consideration favoring the opposite bottom line. The full committee should come away understanding why this is a substantive problem about aggregating judgments rather than a simple matter of arithmetic, and with a defensible reason to treat one mode of aggregation as authoritative.
