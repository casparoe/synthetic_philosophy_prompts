I recently joined the scientific staff of a regional fisheries management body, and I have been put in the middle of a dispute that has become hard to ignore because our next decision will lock in policy for several years. The committee relies on a catch-forecast model that treats the whole stock as a single, perfectly mixed population and treats the fishing fleet as if each vessel were an informed, single-minded profit maximizer. No one, including the model's defenders, believes these assumptions are true: the stock is patchy, and skippers actually make decisions from partial information, habit, and local knowledge. Yet the model has reproduced the last decade of landing data fairly well, and it has been used to set quotas throughout that period.

The committee's chair, an economist, says this is all a model needs to do: its job is to predict, not to describe. In his view, insisting on realistic assumptions would make the model more complicated, harder to fit, and no more useful. A field ecologist on the committee wants to rebuild the model around what is actually known about fish movement and skipper behavior. The chair says her preferred model would be realistic but useless, because we cannot estimate all those parameters from the data we have.

I have become the swing vote on whether to use the existing model's projections to evaluate a proposal to close a large part of the fishing grounds permanently. My worry is that the model has only ever been tested under the old spatial pattern of effort, and its false assumptions are exactly the things most likely to break when the fleet is forced to move and crowd into new grounds. But I also see the chair's point: we have no better-identified model, the old one has a track record, and a more realistic model could easily fail in practice.

I would like help thinking through the underlying principle. Is the chair right that a model with knowingly false assumptions can legitimately support a decision of this kind as long as it has predicted well up to now? Under what conditions would the ecologist's realism be the better guide? Please tell me where each side is strongest and weakest, and what would change your mind: which argument or consideration, if it turned out to be true, would lead you to a different verdict about relying on the model, and where is your own reasoning most likely to fail?
