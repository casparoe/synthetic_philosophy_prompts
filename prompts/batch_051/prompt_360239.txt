I am a novelist, and two writer friends of mine, Elena and Martin, recently argued about a character in my new novel. Ada, a young curator, refuses to say that a film is good until she has seen it herself. She will report what critics she trusts have said, but she never adopts their aesthetic verdict as her own. Elena insisted that this stance is principled; Martin insisted that it is a confused objection to criticism. Their argument eventually turned on a passage from Kant's Critique of Judgement, in the part where Kant discusses the peculiarities of the judgment of taste. Elena paraphrased the passage from memory as follows: Kant says that a judgment of taste is not a cognition and cannot be established by proof; if someone praises a work to me and I do not myself feel pleasure in it, no argument can talk me into finding it beautiful. The judgment of others may predispose me to look again, but it cannot determine my own aesthetic judgment.

They then had the following exchange:

(1) Elena said: That is exactly Ada's policy. She treats others' aesthetic verdicts as useful social information, but never as grounds for her own judgment of beauty. If Kant is right, aesthetic testimony cannot function as evidence for a verdict of taste.

(2) Martin replied: Kant is making a point about proofs, not about testimony in the ordinary sense. Ada could reasonably use the judgments of trusted critics as fallible evidence about whether she will find the film beautiful. That is a prediction, not a proof, so Kant's passage doesn't prohibit it.

(3) Elena answered: You are changing the subject. Ada is not making a prediction about her future liking; she is withholding an aesthetic verdict. Kant allows predictions, but says the verdict itself must wait on one's own feeling. Martin's point is compatible with hers and misses what she is defending.

(4) Martin answered: But if every aesthetic verdict must be first-personal in Ada's way, then critical disagreement becomes an exchange of private reports, with no shared reasons. Kant says taste is subjective, but he also claims that judgments of taste demand universal agreement. Elena's reading saves the first claim by sacrificing the second.

They asked me to referee, and I could not. Adjudicate the exchange point by point: for each of the four moves, state whether it correctly represents Kant's position, whether it follows from or answers the previous move, and what it overlooks. Then say whose overall reading of the passage is more defensible, and what the other side would need to concede to make the dispute commensurable.
