I keep running into people online who sum up Zhuangzi's view of death with a one-liner like "death is just going home" or "the sage neither loves life nor hates death," usually to shut down worry about dying. Last week a friend used it to dismiss my question about whether fearing a painful death is irrational. I looked up the passage they vaguely had in mind—it's from James Legge's translation of the Zhuangzi, in the chapter called The Great and Most Honoured Master: "The True men of old knew nothing of the love of life or of the hatred of death. Entrance into life occasioned them no joy; the exit from it awakened no resistance. Composedly they went and came." I want to know what this passage is actually claiming, as opposed to what people do with it. Could you lay out the different precise claims that "death is just going home" or "the sage neither loves life nor hates death" might amount to—from the harmless to the radical—and say which ones are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which reading, if any, would actually license using it to dismiss fear of a painful death? I'd like a menu, not a single interpretation, because different friends seem to mean different things by it.
