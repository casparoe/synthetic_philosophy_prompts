I sit on the editorial integrity committee of a research journal. Our author guidelines require authors to disclose "substantial verbatim reuse" of their own previously published text; manuscripts with undisclosed substantial reuse are returned for correction before review. The guideline deliberately gives no numerical threshold, so the committee has built up the following published determinations over the past three years:

1. A 9,200-word manuscript reused 736 words (8%) verbatim from the author's earlier paper, confined to a methods section describing a standard assay protocol. Decision: no disclosure required; near-identical technical descriptions in methods are what readers expect.

2. A 5,000-word manuscript reused 290 words (5.8%) verbatim in the introduction; those words stated the paper's central thesis and defined its two key constructs. Decision: disclosure required; the overlap carried the intellectual core despite the small word count.

3. A 4,200-word manuscript reused 1,176 words (28%) verbatim across the literature review and discussion, including two discussion paragraphs that restated the paper's three main findings and their interpretation from an earlier publication. Decision: disclosure required.

4. A 10,400-word manuscript reused 510 words (4.9%) verbatim in an appendix reproducing a survey instrument from an earlier publication. Decision: no disclosure required; technical apparatus does not trigger the guideline.

5. A 3,600-word manuscript reused 612 words (17%) verbatim in the introduction, all background motivation and summaries of prior work, with no overlap in thesis, definitions, findings, or conclusion. Decision: no disclosure required.

6. A 6,000-word manuscript reused 408 words (6.8%) verbatim in the introduction, again only background motivation. Decision: no disclosure required.

The new submission is a 4,000-word manuscript that reuses 920 words (23%) verbatim from the author's own earlier work, spread across five paragraphs of the introduction. The reused text is entirely background motivation and summaries of prior research; it does not overlap the new paper's thesis, definitions, findings, or conclusion, and the author has not flagged it.

I have been asked to prepare the committee's recommendation. We want to decide this case consistently with our own precedents. Please provide: (a) the decision you would recommend to the committee; (b) the precedent closest to this case and why; (c) the rule or rules that fit our past determinations, and what each rule would say about this manuscript; (d) an honest statement of what principle the committee would be adopting with either decision; and (e) one further hypothetical case that would best settle which rule we are actually following.
