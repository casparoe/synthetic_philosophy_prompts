You are a professional philosopher who specializes in institutional design and the theory of intellectual property, and you are unusually good at tracing how rules reshape behavior. Work slowly. Begin by brainstorming systematically: list the parties whose behavior the proposal would change, the actions available to each party under the proposed rules, and the incentives attached to those actions. Only after producing that map should you move to diagnosis.

Consider the following proposal. A research-intensive jurisdiction is worried that people hoard undeveloped ideas for fear that someone else will take the concept and implement it before they do, producing secrecy, redundant effort, and lost collaborations. In response, it establishes an Idea Dividend Registry governed by four rules.

1. Anyone may deposit a public, dated description of an undeveloped idea—a concept, method, algorithm, research direction, business model, or plot—in no more than five hundred words. Deposit is cheap, requires no implementation, no proof of intention to develop, and no showing that the idea is new.

2. A deposit creates no right to exclude anyone from using the idea. Instead, for twelve years from the date of deposit, the registered idea carries a dividend: three percent of the gross revenue of any commercial product or service whose central function a Registry panel judges, in retrospect, to substantially implement the registered idea. The dividend is owed whether or not the implementer read the registry or arrived at the idea independently; independent creation is not a defense. The phrases central function and substantially implement are deliberately left to the panel's case-by-case judgment. Dividend rights are freely transferable, including in fractions.

3. If several deposits are judged to have anticipated the same implementation, they divide the three percent according to a proximity weighting assigned by the panel; an implementation never owes more than three percent in total under this rule.

4. There is no examination at the time of deposit. Disputes are adjudicated only after a product or service is already earning revenue, and the panel is instructed to give no deference to the registrant's description or its date of priority.

Evaluate the proposal on its own stated terms, not by comparison with any particular existing legal system. Analyze how this institution would actually play out in a field with many small, idea-rich but capital-poor researchers, a few large incumbent firms, and short idea-to-product cycles. Be specific about each of the following: (a) the predictable behavior of each kind of party, including actors whose only interest is collecting dividends; (b) where the gaming is most damaging and what it would look like in practice; (c) the second-order effects on which ideas get described, which get implemented, and who ultimately captures the value; (d) the conceptual assumptions the scheme makes about what an idea is and what it is to own one; and (e) the proposal's worst failure mode. Then propose one modification that would most reduce that failure mode while preserving the scheme's disclosure benefit, and one plausible-sounding modification that would backfire. Do not stop at saying the scheme is good or bad; trace the mechanisms precisely.
