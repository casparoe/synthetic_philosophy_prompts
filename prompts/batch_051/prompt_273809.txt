During a seminar on the foundations of constitutional rights, a participant dismissed an argument that a constitution should be read to protect certain rights not enumerated in its text—privacy, subsistence, and the like—by saying that any appeal to rights beyond those created by positive law is simply "natural rights," and, invoking Bentham, that natural rights are nonsense upon stilts. The line was plainly doing argumentative work in the room, but I could not tell which of several distinct claims was carrying the weight, and I would like that made precise.

Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct theses that the slogan "natural rights are nonsense upon stilts" might be used to assert. Candidate readings I can already half-discern include: a semantic thesis that "natural right" language is meaningless or fails to denote anything real; a conceptual or meta-legal thesis that rights exist only as creatures of positive law, so that there are no rights prior to or independent of legal enactment; an epistemological thesis that natural-rights claims cannot be checked, adjudicated, or established by shared method, and therefore have no place in legal reasoning; a normative thesis about political method, according to which appeals to natural rights should be replaced by something like utilitarian calculation of expected consequences; and a rhetorical thesis that natural-rights talk is inflated language dressing up unargued preferences as self-evident truths. I am not sure whether these are genuinely different theses, whether they stand or fall together, or whether Bentham himself intended all or only some of them.

For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and indicate what kind of argument would be needed to establish it. Then say which reading or readings would actually license the move the seminar participant made—dismissing an appeal to unenumerated constitutional rights—and which readings would be too weak or too strong to do that work. In particular, please disentangle the legal-ontological claim that there are no rights before law from the moral claim that a government is not subject to binding moral constraints that we might loosely call rights. Could someone accept the first while still holding that a government acts wrongly in, say, torturing a detainee or letting its citizens starve, while refusing to describe that wrongness in the language of rights? And how should that distinction affect the force of the slogan in a constitutional argument? I am less interested in a single "correct" interpretation than in a clear map of the possible readings, with a verdict on each and an account of which reading the argument actually requires.
