I work as a modeler at an insurance firm, and a recent dispute with a senior colleague has unsettled me. He insisted that when two models fit the past data equally well, the more elegant one is more likely to be right. I tried to set out the reasoning behind that thought, and the conclusion it seems to force is one I cannot accept:

(1) In mature empirical science, when two theories cover the same data, the more elegant theory is more likely to be true, not merely easier to use.
(2) This could only be because elegance and truth are correlated in nature; if nature were indifferent to elegance, preferring a graceful theory over a clunky one would be mere taste.
(3) But a correlation between elegance and truth in nature should not stop at the boundary of what we can currently test. It should apply just as much to questions no experiment will ever settle: which interpretation of quantum mechanics is true, what produced the universe, what the ultimately fundamental constituents are.
(4) Therefore, in each of those domains, the most elegant candidate is the one we ought to believe, even though no observation could possibly decide the matter.
(5) That result seems to me absurd: considerations of formal grace cannot compel rational belief about unobservable interpretations or cosmologies.
(6) So elegance was never really evidence in the scientific case either; it is at most a convenience.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I would most like to keep (1) and (5), so I need to know exactly which step to reject and what that rejection costs.
