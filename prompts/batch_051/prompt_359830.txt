I am in Year 12 and preparing for my A-level English Literature coursework, for which I want to read Milton's Paradise Lost through the philosophy of the problem of evil. The question that interests me is the one I find people tend to skip: not why God permitted the fall, but how the fall could happen at all. In the poem, Satan and the rebel angels are created good by an all-good, all-powerful God, and their first sin is supposed to be exactly that — a first sin, with nothing before it to explain it. Yet the moment Milton gets inside Satan's head, the sin appears to be already under way: Satan is proud, envious of the Son's elevation, and resentful of a hierarchy he has come to see as arbitrary, and the poem presents these attitudes not merely as consequences of a choice but as the conditions in which the choice grows. So the poem seems to face a dilemma. Either the first evil choice was made for no prior reason, in which case Milton has not actually dramatised it but simply asserted it; or it was made because of motives that were already present, in which case those motives were part of Satan's created nature and the fault is pushed back onto the Creator.

I would like a reading of the poem that takes this dilemma seriously rather than treating the free-will defence as obviously adequate. Does Paradise Lost genuinely dramatise how a free, initially good rational creature could produce the first evil impulse, or does it quietly concede that the first sin is unintelligible? Please attend to the detail of the poem in answering: the speeches in Books I and II, the later account of the war in Heaven in which Satan convinces himself and his followers that they are self-created rather than creatures, and the contrast with Abdiel. Is the poem's best answer that the first sin is a false belief — Satan's denial of his own createdness — accepted before it is an evil desire, and would that solve the philosophical problem or merely move it back a step? Where exactly does the text support this reading, and where does an honest reader have to admit it will not stretch? I hope to end the essay by saying whether the difficulty Milton runs into is a poetic failure in his handling of Satan or a genuine difficulty for the free-will defence itself.
