I have recently become the primary carer for my father, who has early-stage dementia, and this has led me back to Kazuo Ishiguro’s novel Never Let Me Go with different eyes. I am struck by how much of the novel is organised around care: the carers are trained to tend the donors, and the whole system presents itself as a humane arrangement for the donors’ benefit, even as the novel is usually read as a critique of instrumentalising persons.

I want to read the novel through the ethics of care, but without reducing it to a slogan. In particular, some care ethicists distinguish between caring-for, the face-to-face labour of tending a particular other, and caring-about, the more diffuse concern for others one may never meet; some also argue that genuine care is not a duty to be administered but a relational practice that resists institutionalisation. I would like you to use those distinctions as a lens. Where does the novel show genuine care emerging between characters within a deeply exploitative system, and where does it show care being corrupted into a bureaucratic routine or a form of self-protection? Does the care ethicist’s emphasis on the moral value of partial, concrete relations illuminate the difference between Kathy’s care for Tommy and Ruth and the way the system claims to care for the donors? And where does the novel resist the care-ethics reading, perhaps because the caring itself depends on, or masks, the prior exploitation?

I am preparing an essay, so I would like a careful critical reading rather than a plot summary or an account of the novel’s reception. Please write your answer in British English.
