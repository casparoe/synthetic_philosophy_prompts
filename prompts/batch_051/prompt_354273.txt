Two members of my metaphysics study group spent an evening arguing about what it is for a thing to persist through change, and I could not tell who was winning at any point. Their recurring example was a wooden table that is painted on Monday and stripped on Tuesday. I would like a point-by-point adjudication of the exchange as it actually unfolded.

(1) A said the table is wholly present on both days: the same whole table persists through the change, and all that changes is which properties it has.

(2) B replied that A is left with a contradiction. If one and the same whole is wholly present on both days, the indiscernibility of identicals requires that whole to have exactly the same properties throughout; yet it is painted on Monday and unpainted on Tuesday. So A must either build the times into the properties or posit temporal parts — a painted Monday-part and a bare Tuesday-part of a four-dimensionally extended table.

(3) A answered that building times into the properties is not a trick. Painted-on-Monday and unpainted-on-Tuesday are compatible relational properties, just as an object can be north of Rome and south of Milan. There is no contradiction, and temporal parts are unnecessary.

(4) B answered that the north-of-Rome comparison shows A's real cost. A has exchanged the table's intrinsic colour for relations to times, and still owes an account of what makes a wholly present whole stand in the painted-relation to Monday and the unpainted-relation to Tuesday. Temporal parts owe no such further story, because the Monday-part simply is the painted thing.

(5) A said the cure is worse than the disease. At any instant only a momentary slice of the table is present — and likewise for a person. Then the slice that acts now is not the thing that made the promise or incurred the debt, and agency and responsibility splinter across temporal parts.

(6) B replied that this misunderstands four-dimensionalism. The person is the whole temporally extended object, which exists across time even if no single moment contains all of it, just as a road exists in a county even if only a stretch of it lies there. Agency belongs to the whole, not to an instantaneous slice.

(7) A answered that the road analogy begs the question. Spatial parts are acceptable because the road's segments are all there at once, and the whole road is simply that spread of simultaneous segments. But on A's view the table is wholly present at each moment, and on B's view it is never wholly present at any moment, so A does not accept the picture of an object spread through time the way a road is spread through space. The analogy takes that picture for granted.

(8) B replied that the analogy was only an illustration against a misunderstanding, not a premise, and that no observation can settle the debate anyway. The real question, B said, is which package of primitives is more economical and less ad hoc: temporal parts plus a principle of composition across time, or wholly present things with time-indexed properties. B claimed A's package is worse because it rests on unexplained relational facts.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. Assign each of the eight moves a rating from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is right about what overall, which specific concessions each side owes the other, and what both of them are missing that would sharpen the dispute. I am less interested in a final winner than in the precise points at which each argument holds up or breaks down.
