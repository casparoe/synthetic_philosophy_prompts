Political theorists have long distinguished negative liberty (the absence of interference by others), positive liberty (self-mastery or the capacity to realize one's true ends), and republican liberty (non-domination: not being subject to another's arbitrary will). The republican tradition makes a striking conceptual move here: it holds that a slave with a benevolent master who never in fact interferes is nonetheless unfree, while a person who faces a great deal of interference under a predictable rule of law may remain free in the relevant sense. A colleague of mine insists that this is not merely a stipulation about how to use the word 'free.' She claims it has genuine empirical content: for instance, that people living under unexercised domination should differ in measurable ways from people who face interference without domination.

I am skeptical, but I want to see whether the claim can be made to earn its keep. Please design the strongest empirical test you can of the thesis that what people experience or exhibit as unfreedom tracks domination rather than mere interference. Be concrete: specify the comparison, ideally separating the four cases of no interference and no domination, interference without domination, domination without interference, and both; specify the measure or measures; and state the result that would favor the republican reading over the negative-liberty reading. Then, with equal care, say what a positive result would and would not establish. In particular, would it ever license the conclusion that republican liberty is the correct concept of freedom, or only that domination is a useful predictor of something else? And what would a null result leave standing?

I want a committed response: choose one design, defend it against the most obvious alternative, and give a definite verdict on what a positive or null outcome would and would not settle — not a survey of possible measures.
