I've taught an intro-to-proofs class for a few years now, and I keep running into the same grading problem. A student can reproduce the proof that sqrt(2) is irrational, line for line, but if I ask them what breaks if we run the same argument on sqrt(4), or why it matters that p and q have no common factor, they just stare. Another student writes a messy proof with a small gap, but they can explain the whole shape of the argument, and when I point at the gap they fix it on the spot. My current grading mostly rewards accurate reproduction and docks errors, which feels like it might be measuring procedural fluency instead of whether they actually understood the proof.

I'm grading about 40 proofs a week across two sections, with maybe 10 minutes per student. The school wants a numeric grade plus short comments, and if a parent complains my department chair wants me to be able to point to a reproducible proceedure, not vibes. So I want you to turn whatever the philosophy/math-ed people mean by understanding a proof into a reusable decision procedure I can actually run at 11pm with a stack of papers: a checklist or a set of small tests, in order, that I can do with what's on the page and maybe one short follow-up question per student.

Please make sure it handles the hard cases: flawless reproduction with no apparent insight; a correct proof written in an unusual or inefficient way; a student who gives a convincing example but no general proof; a student who has a gap but seems to see the main idea; a student who uses a different valid argument than the one I taught. Mark explicitly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure and where the procedure will likely misfire. Then run it on two or three concrete cases, at least one where the procedure gives an answer I won't like, and say what the procedure can't do. Also just to calibrate: tell me how confident you are in the main claims and single out the point you're least sure about.
