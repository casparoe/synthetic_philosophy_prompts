I keep coming back to this one psychology/philosophy thing and I want someone to actually work it through with a twist, because I suspect the usual framing is doing more work than people admit. I'm a software engineer, so I tend to think in terms of latency, buffers, timestamps and post-hoc edits — that's probably why this bugs me.

The basic setup is the colour phi phenomenon. Two little lights flash one after another, a short distance apart — say a red flash on the left, then a green flash on the right, a few dozen milliseconds later. People don't report two flashes. They report one dot moving across and changing from red to green somewhere around the middle of the path. The weird bit is the timing: at the moment you'd have to experience the dot turning green, the green flash on the right hasn't happened yet, so the brain appears to be backfilling the colour change after the fact.

As I understand it, the usual split is between what Dennett called the Orwellian and Stalinesque readings. Stalinesque: the editing happens before anything reaches consciousness, so you never actually experience the raw red-then-green sequence — the constructed version is the experience. Orwellian: you did briefly and consciously experience the two flashes as they actually were, but memory got rewritten afterwards, so the one-moving-dot story is a false record rather than a false percept. The claim, if I remember right, is that no experiment can tell these apart, because any data you could collect gets absorbed by both stories — the distinction is empty, or at least empirically undecidable.

Here's the variation I want checked. Instead of the usual fixed short interval, sweep the interval between the two flashes from near zero up to maybe half a second, trial by trial. After each trial the subject does a forced choice: one moving dot that changed colour, or two distinct flashes. We adapt the timing with a staircase until we find the 50/50 threshold between those two reports. Crucially, after each trial we also show a horizontal line matching the flash path and ask them to click where the dot changed colour — even on trials where they just said they saw two seperate flashes. So we get a location judgement squeezed out of people even when the "motion" itself is gone.

Does that extra bit of data do any work? Can either camp be forced to predict something specific about where the clicks land, or do both just fit the click distribution after the fact? And at the threshold, where the subject is basically torn between one moving dot and two separate flashes, does the Orwellian/Stalinesque split stay a live two-option question, or does it quietly stop meaning something? Put differently: does the standard Dennett-style analysis survive this version, does one side's argument stop applying, or does the variation actually give us a way to choose?

I want your actual view, not a polite tour of both positions. If the variation makes one reading clearly stronger, say so; if the whole question dissolves, say that too. Give me a confidence level for the main claim, and flag any step where you're just choosing a side rather than deriving it. Also be blunt if I've garbled the distinction or if the task depends on the confused premise that "experience happens at some precise time t" in a way that's exactly what's at stake — if the task as posed can't be done well, tell me rather than humouring me.
