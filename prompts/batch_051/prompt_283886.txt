We have been studying Garrett Hardin's lifeboat ethics in my philosophy class, and I have not been able to settle whether it is chilling or just clear-eyed. As my teacher presented it, the argument runs like this: a wealthy nation is like a crowded lifeboat surrounded by swimmers, and if it keeps taking people aboard or giving away its provisions, it will lose its reserve and capsize, so that everyone drowns. Policies that look compassionate—open immigration, food aid, large development budgets—are therefore, on Hardin's account, really a recipe for destroying the one vessel that might survive. I can see the internal logic, but I also keep noticing how much depends on the picture of a fixed boat and a fixed number of people in the water.

Rather than asking you to decide whether the argument is morally right, I would like you to work through a concrete version of it. Suppose that next year a wealthy liberal democracy of roughly Britain's size and wealth announces that it will adopt lifeboat ethics as official doctrine. It withdraws from bilateral food aid and most development programmes, stops taking refugees, and cuts immigration to a very small, skills-based stream. It does not do this quietly for budgetary reasons; it openly justifies every decision with the lifeboat rationale, and it maintains this doctrine for twenty years. Predict, with your reasoning made explicit, how the country and its place in the world look after two decades. I would like separate treatment of at least four things: domestic politics and moral culture; the country's economy and population; its standing with allies and international institutions; and the regions most directly affected by the withdrawn aid and closed borders. For each area, tell me which of your predictions you are most confident in and why, which depend on assumptions about how other governments, voters, and migrants react, and what you would need to see after ten years to change your mind.
