The market now offers commitment devices for ordinary failures of self-control: savings accounts that freeze deposits until a stated goal is reached, apps that block selected websites during scheduled hours and make themselves difficult to disable, and services that impose a financial penalty whenever a planned gym session is missed. These products are explicitly sold as remedies for weakness of will, on the rationale that one's present self should tie the hands of one's future self. What view of the person, of preferences over time, and of weakness of will does this practice presuppose? Does it have to presuppose that a person really is a succession of distinct selves, with the earlier self entitled to bind the later one, or is it enough to presuppose only that preferences predictably reverse as temptations become imminent? And if weakness of will is better understood not as a conflict between earlier and later selves but as a single-moment failure—acting against one's own better judgment even while doing the act—then which features of these devices, and of their justification, would have to change?
