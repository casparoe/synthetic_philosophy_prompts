I teach an interdisciplinary undergraduate seminar on technology and politics, and in the opening discussion every year two positions pass each other without engaging: one student will say a technology is just a neutral tool and all the politics is in how people use it, while another will say technologies are political, as if that phrase settled the argument. I would like a diagnostic instrument for the first week, before the students have read any theory, that sorts them by what they actually believe on two connected questions. The first is the technological determinism question: does technical change run by its own logic, so that social arrangements are mostly downstream, or do social choices shape technical trajectories at every point? The second is the artifact-politics question: whether individual artifacts can be political in themselves, and if so, where the politics lives.

Please write a decision tree—or, if you think a scored quiz with a key would sort this material better, write that instead and briefly justify the choice—of roughly four to seven branch points or eight to ten quiz items. Every node or item should be a concrete case or a first-person claim, answerable by someone who has never seen the literature. At least these three cases should appear: (1) a public official with control over both the roads and the transit agency builds a parkway with overpasses too low for buses, so that the beaches beyond are reachable by car but not by bus; (2) a municipality must choose between expanding a centralized nuclear grid and subsidizing distributed rooftop solar; (3) a shift-scheduling algorithm must meet a staffing constraint but can be tuned to weight seniority, availability, or workers' own preferences. The instrument should sort respondents into five positions, described functionally rather than by labels: the hard technological determinist, for whom technology advances on its own and politics is downstream; the instrumentalist, for whom artifacts are neutral means and politics enters only in use; the design-politics view, on which artifacts can be built to favor some people over others, whether as a deliberate choice or as a pattern embedded in a system; the inherent-politics view, on which some technologies require or strongly favor particular political arrangements regardless of who deploys them; and a constructivist view, on which what an artifact is and does politically is settled by the social groups, technical standards, and interpretations that stabilize it, and can be reopened and redesigned. In a closing note, flag any distinctions the instrument cannot cleanly draw—in particular, say whether it can separate an isolated bigoted design decision from a systemic pattern of exclusion, and whether it can separate an inherent political requirement from a merely entrenched design choice—and indicate which leaves such ambiguous cases should fall toward. Also note any combination of answers that corresponds to no leaf, and say briefly whether that combination looks incoherent, unattractive, or simply beyond the instrument's scope. The instrument should teach as well as sort: a student should finish it with a clearer sense of where they stand and why the fault lines matter.
