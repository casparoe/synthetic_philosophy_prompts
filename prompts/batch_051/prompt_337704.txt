I have been trying to settle a question in formal epistemology about how a rational agent should revise its beliefs when evidence arrives that does not make any proposition certain, and I find myself caught between a theory I want to accept and an intuition I cannot bring myself to abandon.

The theory is Jeffrey conditionalization. It generalizes ordinary Bayesian conditioning to cases where experience redistributes probability across the cells of a partition rather than confirming one cell outright. I fix a partition, take my new probabilities for its cells as the input delivered by the experience, and then reweight each cell of my prior by those new probabilities while holding fixed my prior probabilities conditional on each cell. Its attraction is that it generalizes conditioning while keeping the update fully mechanical: once the partition and its new weights are specified, every other credence is determined, with no further judgment required about how the evidence should propagate through my beliefs.

But my intuition resists exactly that last feature. Suppose two experiences each raise my credence that a sample is toxic from 0.2 to 0.7. In the first, a well-understood chemical sensor flashes a positive reading; in the second, I catch a confusing glimpse of a readout I barely comprehend and simply find myself alarmed. I want to say these should not leave me in the same overall state of belief merely because they assign the same new probability to the proposition that the sample is toxic. The sensor result should ripple outward in principled ways—raising my confidence that the sensor is reliable, that similar samples are contaminated, that a particular false-positive process was at work. The alarming glimpse should not, or at least not in the same way. Yet once a partition and its new probabilities are fixed, Jeffrey conditionalization appears to leave no room for such a difference: all subsequent change is dictated by the old conditional structure, and the provenance of the shift drops out. That feels like losing something evidentially important.

I would like you to press on this clash rather than simply choose a side. Present the strongest case for each claim: the case that the intuition should be debunked, perhaps as a confusion between selecting a good partition and applying an update rule to it; the case that Jeffrey conditionalization should be amended or replaced, for instance by relaxing rigidity or by supplementing the rule with an account of where a change in partition probabilities comes from; and the case that the two are compatible on some reading of uncertain evidence, if one exists. Then give me a negotiated verdict: what should yield under what conditions, and what would justify each move.
