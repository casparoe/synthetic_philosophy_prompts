I've been sat in on a bunch of zoning appeals for my neighborhood association, and the same dynamic keeps happening: the people who show up to object live within a few blocks of the proposed building, and everyone else in the city who'd benefit from more housing isn't in the room. Someone floated a proposal to fix that asymmetry, and I can't tell if it's brilliant or a disaster.

Here's the mechanism. A city passes a "proximity dividend" ordinance. For any project that would build at more than twice the density the current zoning allows, the developer can skip the public hearing and the council vote, but must pay an annual dividend for ten years to every household that has had its primary residence within 400 meters of the site for at least one year before the application date. The total annual pot is set at 15% of the building's annual property tax bill, and it's split by inverse distance from the site: a household directly across the street gets several times what a household at the 400-meter edge gets. Households keep recieving their share for the full ten years even if they move away; households that arrive after the application date get nothing. Renters receive the payment, not their landlord, but only if their name is on the lease and they've met the one-year test.

Walk me through what this actually does. Who becomes the organized constituency for new housing, and who still fights it? How do developers, landlords, and wannabe neighbors game the timing, the distance bands, or the one-year residency rule? What happens to rents and to people who move into the area later? Where does the scheme fail first — and is there a fix that keeps the buy-in without turning the neighbors into a toll booth?
