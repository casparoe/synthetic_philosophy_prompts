The Mīmāṃsā tradition grounds the Veda's authority in its being authorless (apauruṣeya): a human author is subject to error and desire, and that is why authored testimony stands in need of external support. Suppose one distils from this view the rule: "A prescriptive text carries intrinsic validity just in case no person stands behind it as its author; where a person does stand behind it as its author, the text carries only such validity as that person's qualifications confer." Now imagine a generative model trained on the received Vedic corpus emits a previously unattested passage in acceptable Vedic Sanskrit that prescribes a rite, together with its mantra. The researchers circulate the passage without disclosing where it came from; a community adopts it and only later learns how it was produced. No one composed the passage's words, but it is not beginningless or revealed—it is a statistical artifact of a machine built and trained by people. Under the rule, is the passage authorless, and therefore intrinsically valid? Argue from the words of the rule, from the purpose of the authorless condition, and from what a Mīmāṃsaka would have said had the case been put to them, and say which of these approaches should decide; then consider what the difficulty, if any, reveals about the work "authorless" was doing here.
