Something's been bugging me about the way my own feelings react to made-up stories, and I cant find a way to make it consistent.

If I sit down to watch a sad movie and I know the whole time that none of it is real, the worst scenes still get me. The dog dies and I cry, and I don't feel stupid for crying afterwards, even though I always knew the dog never existed. But here's the other case. A friend tells me her dog died, I get genuinely upset, and then ten minutes later she admits she made the whole thing up to see how I'd react. The sadness doesn't just slowly fade — it collapses all at once, and what's left is something grosser, like embarrassment or anger at her. Same dog, same death, same tears. In one case, knowing it was fake from the start didn't stop the feeling. In the other, finding out it was fake destroyed the feeling on the spot.

Is there an actual principle that treats those two cases differently, or is it just a psychological accident? I want the strongest case you can make for why walking into a fiction willingly is different from being fooled first and corrected after. And if you defend the difference, you have to explain why I cant just decide, after she admits it was fake, to keep treating her story the way I treat the movie — rewind it, so to speak, and still feel the sad parts. Because I can't seem to do that.

So: is the line between those two cases one that should hold up, or is it just how my head happens to be wired? Give me the best principle you can find, and an honest verdict on whether it actually works.
