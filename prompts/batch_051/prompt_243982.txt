I've been going back and forth in my philosophy group chat about whether disgust and a sense of sacredness belong in ethics at all, and I realized we keep relitigating the same cases one at a time instead of actually forcing the issue. You're a moral psychologist and metaethicist who works on moral emotions, and I want you to think carefully and then deliver the full spec. Give me a list of desiderata that a theory of the moral status of purity-type responses has to meet to count as adequate. By purity-type responses I mean disgust, felt sacredness, taboos, desecration responses, the sense that something is degrading or contaminating rather than merely harmful or unfair. The point of the theory is practical: when someone says a practice (say, a food taboo, a biotechnology, a development on sacred land, or a sexual norm) is disgusting or sacrilegious, the theory should let us gague whether we're facing a moral datum that a decent ethical view has to register, or just psychological noise to be explained away. So the requirements should cover the relevant cases, including harmless taboo violations that produce moral dumbfounding, desecration of objects, disgust that tracks real contamination risk, and the way people treat some things as protected values they refuse to trade off against money rather than just strongly weighted preferences. It also has to respect the distinctions a theory in this area can't collapse: pathogen disgust versus moral disgust, sacred-to-me versus reason-giving-for-you, and a purity response being elicited versus being justified. And it has to sit comfortably with the empirical picture: induced disgust (fart spray, a messy room) makes moral judgments harsher; people usually can't produce good justifications for condemning harmless taboo violations; and disgust is wildly promiscuous in what it attaches to, since an aversion conditioned by nausea can stick to a food even when the person knows the food wasn't the cause.

After you lay out the list, tell me which requirements are negotiable and which aren't, and which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the obvious candidate views: disgust never counts as a moral reason, disgust is presumptive moral evidence, only harm and fairness count, and purity is a basic moral foundation on par with harm and fairness. If some of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied, say so, since that would itself be a result. Start by brainstorming candidate cases and constraints before you distill the final list.
