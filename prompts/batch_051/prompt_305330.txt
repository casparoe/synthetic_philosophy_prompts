I am a novelist, and I am in the middle of a book about a linguist sent to document a language spoken on an island that has had effectively no contact with the outside world. Because I want the protagonist's situation to be intellectually honest rather than merely atmospheric, I have been reading around in the philosophy of language, and a friend of mine, who teaches philosophy, gave me a condensed account of Davidson's radical interpretation that I cannot quite trust. As I remember her summary, it ran roughly as follows. Radical interpretation starts from the predicament of a field linguist with no dictionary, no bilingual informant, and no prior exposure to the culture: all she can go on is which sentences speakers hold true under which observable conditions. Davidson's proposal, according to my friend, is that interpretation is therefore governed by a principle of charity. The linguist must assume as a methodological constraint that most of what the speakers believe is true by our lights, and must choose her translation manual so as to maximize agreement between their beliefs and ours. On this picture, genuine disagreement becomes a kind of remainder: radical interpretation can acknowledge it only as whatever divergence is left over after charity has done all it can. My friend concluded that radical interpretation is, at bottom, a formalized version of the advice that to understand another person one should try to see things from their point of view — empathy with some logical scaffolding.

I am especially suspicious of that final gloss, because my experience writing characters has not persuaded me that inhabiting another person's point of view is either necessary or sufficient for understanding them. But I do not know enough to judge the earlier parts, and I worry I am simply finding the view unsympathetic because of the word empathy. Could you give me an accuracy audit of this characterization? I would like the accurate core separated from the common simplifications, and I would like to know what a careful defender of Davidson would object to — in particular, whether the principle of charity is best described as a rule for maximizing agreement, what it actually constrains in a radical interpretation, and whether it amounts to empathy.
