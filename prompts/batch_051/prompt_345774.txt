I'm auditing a philosophy of mind seminar that leans heavily on cognitive science, and "the brain is a prediction machine" has basically become the motto of the class. Last week it got used three different ways in an hour. The professor waved it at a question about why we don't hallucinate all the time: "Because the brain is a prediction machine; it's checking its guesses against the signal." Fifteen minutes later a grad student used the same slogan to make what sounded like the opposite point, that ordinary perception and hallucination are the same kind of process, just differing in how much grip incoming signal has on the top-down guesses — "it's all prediction anyway." Then somebody used it to explain why we see faces in air vents, and another student muttered something about "precision weighting" before the discussion moved on. I've also seen the phrase do work in arguments about placebos and chronic pain. But I honestly can't tell what the slogan commits anyone to. Sometimes it sounds like the boring claim that expectations influence what we perceive, which nobody denies. Other times it sounds like the much stronger claim that the brain literally does Bayesian inference over probability distributions, or that perception is just controlled hallucination, or that the brain's whole job is to minimize free energy. Those can't all be true, and they probably aren't all meant by the slogan.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims "the brain is a prediction machine" might be expressing, from the most trivial to the most radical? For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or probably false, and say which reading the grad student actually needs for her "hallucination and perception differ only in degree" move. A menu-style breakdown would be far more useful than a single "correct" interpretation, since I want to bring it to the next seminar and push back on both sides. Also, please write the answer in American English.
