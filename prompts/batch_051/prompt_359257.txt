I am currently deciding whether to enter into a cryonics contract, and I have run into a conceptual difficulty that I would like help working through. The case I was originally given for enrolling was an expected-value argument: even a very small probability of successful revival, multiplied by the very large value of additional conscious life, easily justifies the comparatively modest cost of preservation. I can set aside the usual objections about overestimating the probability, provider insolvency, and the technology never arriving, because those objections only adjust the size of the probability and leave the structure of the argument untouched. The change that troubles me is different. The provider I am considering has recently begun telling prospective members that, if revival becomes technically possible, the probable route will be whole-brain emulation rather than biological reanimation: the preserved brain would be destructively scanned at very high resolution and a computational model of it run as a digital person, with the original tissue consumed by the scanning process. Nothing in the expected-value argument I was given distinguishes the outcome in which I am revived from the outcome in which a high-fidelity emulation of me is created from my preserved brain while my original tissue is destroyed.

My question is whether the expected-value case for cryonics survives this changed understanding of what revival would be. The argument appears to depend on the premise that the future beneficiary is me, in the sense that makes my current reasons of self-preservation reach forward to that entity. If destructive whole-brain emulation is the mechanism, that premise is no longer obviously true; and if the process could in principle be run more than once, I do not see how to assign probabilities over the relevant outcomes for an expected-value calculation at all. Which premise does the argument need, exactly? Can it be repaired without simply asserting that a psychological duplicate is me? If it cannot be repaired, is the argument dead, or does it survive in some weaker form, for example as a reason to value the creation of my emulation rather than as a reason of self-interest for me to sign?
