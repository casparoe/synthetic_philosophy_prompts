I recently began trading in prediction markets with a fixed bankroll that I do not top up, and I keep meeting the same piece of advice from experienced regulars. They insist that, no matter how confident I am that a contract is mispriced, I should never stake my entire bankroll; instead, I should size each position by some version of the Kelly criterion — stake roughly your edge divided by the odds, and in practice perhaps only a fraction of that, such as half-Kelly. They present this as a rule of rational bankroll management rather than merely a guard against tilt or overconfidence.

What puzzles me is the standard of rationality behind the advice. If a position genuinely has positive expected value per dollar staked, a straightforward expected-value-maximizing rule would seem to say that I should stake as much as possible on it, or at least that I should restrain myself only because of diminishing marginal utility of money. But the Kelly approach instead maximizes the expected logarithm of wealth, or something like the long-run growth rate of the bankroll. It treats a large possible drawdown as a reason to pass up positive expected value even for someone whose marginal utility of money is roughly linear over the relevant range. That is a very different picture of what makes a betting policy rational.

I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of practical rationality that this practice presupposes. In particular: does it presuppose logarithmic utility of wealth, or a distinct view about time and repeated decisions — for example, that one should maximize geometric growth across a sequence of bets rather than expected value at each step — or neither? Is the practice coherent, given that real opportunities are not infinitely divisible, not exactly repeatable, and not independent in the way the classic derivation assumes? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that several rival accounts of rational choice would still license the same sizing rule. If I replaced the implicit theory with a straightforward expected-wealth-maximizing one, what else would I be committed to changing about how I treat risk, compounding, and the difference between a one-off bet and a long sequence of them? Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the reconstruction: which argument or consideration would most plausibly push you toward a different account of what Kelly-based bankroll management presupposes, or where in your reasoning you are least confident.
