Consider the following position, which I have come to take seriously after a colleague pressed the ergodicity economics program on me.

For decisions that unfold over time for a single agent — repeated gambles, investment rules, growth under uncertainty — the quantity to optimize is the time-average growth rate of the relevant resource, not the ensemble-average expected value. In an ergodic process, a long-run average along one trajectory converges to the average across the ensemble at a single time, so the two criteria coincide. But many economically relevant processes, and in particular multiplicative ones, are not ergodic: the two averages can point in opposite directions. Take a gamble that, each period, multiplies current wealth by 1.5 or by 0.6 with equal probability. The expected multiplier is 1.05, so expected wealth grows without limit as the gamble is repeated. But the time-average growth rate is one half times the natural log of 1.5 plus one half times the natural log of 0.6, which is negative, so an individual who keeps accepting the gamble sees wealth tend to zero with probability one. The position holds that expected-value reasoning is generally valid only for additive and effectively ergodic processes, or where an agent can genuinely spread risk across many independent parallel trials; that much of decision theory and behavioral economics has been applying an ensemble-level tool to non-ergodic processes; and that several supposedly idiosyncratic risk preferences, including the logarithmic form that appears in canonical treatments of risk, are better understood as shadows cast by time-average optimization than as facts about individual psychology.

I want an honest map of what would count against this position, not a defense of it and not a dismissal of it. Distinguish considerations that would be decisive — ones that would force even sophisticated defenders to abandon or sharply restrict the view — from those that are merely awkward and could be absorbed by a qualification. Include what the position's best defenders would themselves concede, not only what its critics say. Address at least these four pressure points: (i) under what conditions the ensemble average is the correct criterion after all, for example for institutions that can run many independent replicates, or for one-shot choices among prospects that are never revisited; (ii) whether the position genuinely rivals expected utility theory or only rediscovers it as a special case, such as the maximization of expected logarithmic utility; (iii) whether the injunction to maximize time-average growth is a norm of rationality, a descriptive account of actual choice, or a conditional recommendation that inherits whatever standing the goal of maximizing long-run wealth has; and (iv) which empirical or formal results would most embarrass the program. Finally, say whether the position as stated is so insulated by qualifications that it cannot be falsified, and whether that insulation, if present, is a strength or a defect.
