I am a novelist, and next month I am giving a talk to a philosophy department's ethics and political philosophy workshop about the moral psychology that animates my recent novel. The novel follows a protagonist whose envy and resentment of a more fortunate rival gradually become, in the narrative's own terms, accurate perceptions of a social arrangement rather than defects of character; the book implicitly defends the thought that such emotions can be signals of injustice and not only vices to be overcome. In the talk I plan to defend that thought in philosophical terms: that envy and resentment, at least under conditions of serious inequality or subordination, can function as warranted indicators that something about the social order is unjust, and that Rawls's admission of excusable envy in A Theory of Justice is the point at which the standard vice-theoretic reading begins to crack.

The audience will be roughly fifteen people: two senior political philosophers who work on Rawls and know the envy discussion and the stability arguments in detail; a Kantian moral psychologist suspicious of treating any passion as a source of moral knowledge; a philosopher of emotion who works on the fittingness conditions of emotions and is sympathetic to resentment but doubtful that envy can ever be fitting, since its content appears to be a wish about the other's advantage rather than a judgment about wrongs; and several graduate students who know the primary texts but not the secondary literature. They will care above all about keeping distinct three questions: whether an emotion is psychologically understandable, whether it is morally excusable, and whether it reliably indicates injustice.

My argument, in outline, is this. Rawls classifies envy proper as a vice, yet allows that some envy is excusable when the gap between the more and the less favored is so great that the less favored cannot reasonably be expected not to feel it; he treats the absence of conditions that arouse excusable envy as one of the things a well-ordered society is supposed to achieve. I want to press on what excusable can mean here. If it meant merely psychologically inevitable, it would be a fact about stability to be managed, not something a conception of justice should aim to avoid; the moment it functions as a criterion for evaluating arrangements, it concedes that the envy is a comprehensible response to something objectively wrong in the arrangement, and that concession opens the door to treating envy and resentment as fallible but informative signals of injustice. The novel, I will say, is a narrative exploration of exactly that possibility.

Prepare me for the question period. I need the objections I will actually get from this audience, not the ones a general audience would raise. Order them roughly by how likely and how damaging they are, phrase each as the relevant person would put it, and for each give me a short reply and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Do not let the answer collapse envy into resentment; this audience will insist on that distinction, and I need replies that respect it. Concede what should be conceded and hold what should be held. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me why it would hurt.
