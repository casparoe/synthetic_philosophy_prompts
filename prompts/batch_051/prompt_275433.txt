I recently spent a long evening arguing with a friend who works as a climate policy advocate. His position was that any public or philanthropic money spent on solar geoengineering research is an unacceptable moral hazard: it normalizes the technology, gives governments and private actors a reason to treat emissions cuts as less urgent, and quietly builds the political and technical groundwork for deployment without anyone ever deciding that deployment is acceptable. I found myself resisting the absolutism of that position, because refusal to study something does not make it go away, and because some work — governance studies, monitoring, analysis of termination risk — might be the only way to keep a dangerous option from developing in the dark. By the end of the evening, neither of us had a principled way to sort research we would want to see funded from research we would not.

I sit on the grant committee of a climate foundation. We screen about one proposal a month touching on solar geoengineering, ranging from integrated assessment modeling to small outdoor experiments to the design of monitoring and governance infrastructure. I would like you to write a reusable screening procedure my committee could run on every proposal. The procedure should operationalize the concerns from that argument — moral hazard or mitigation deterrence, termination shock (the risk that deployment stops abruptly and the masked warming emerges far faster than the greenhouse-driven warming beneath it), and the unresolved question of who could ever legitimately decide to deploy or even field-test — without collapsing into a blanket yes or no. Our analysts are not climate scientists, they have about two weeks per proposal, the board must sign off on rejections, and we need the procedure to say explicitly where it stops with a no, where it defers to a judgment call, and where a question is genuinely one of values rather than facts.

Please run the finished procedure on three proposals: (1) a modeling study of abrupt-termination scenarios coupled with stakeholder workshops in highly climate-vulnerable countries; (2) a small outdoor balloon release of reflective aerosol intended mainly to study plume microphysics, proposed by a university with co-funding from a private donor; and (3) a consortium that includes an aerospace launch company seeking early funding for hardware tests, framed as a step toward a rapid-response monitoring capability that would matter mainly if deployment began. In at least one of the three, I want the procedure to give me an answer I will not like, and I want you to say plainly what the procedure cannot do — especially whether it is capable of guarding against the moral hazard it is designed to address.
