A couple of friends of mine were at the pub last night, and what started as a chat about the old Sherlock Holmes telly adaptations turned into a full-blown row about whether the name "Sherlock Holmes" refers to anything. I only followed about half of it, but I've got the gist down. As near as I can recconstruct, it went like this:

(1) Dan said of course "Sherlock Holmes" refers. If you say "Sherlock Holmes doesn't exist," you're still talking about him — otherwise what exactly is it you're denying? So the name must pick out something. Not a flesh-and-blood detective, obviously, but an abstract thing: a fictional character, brought into being by Conan Doyle the way a carpenter brings a table into being, except the character isn't made of wood.

(2) Maya said that's barmy. If "Sherlock Holmes" named an abstract object, then "Sherlock Holmes smokes a pipe" would be claiming that an abstract object smokes a pipe, which is nonsense. Better to say the name just fails to refer: there's no such person. "Sherlock Holmes doesn't exist" is true precisely because the name is empty, and "Sherlock Holmes smokes a pipe" is false, full stop. What's true is only "In the Conan Doyle stories, Sherlock Holmes smokes a pipe."

(3) Dan said Maya's "in the stories" move gives the game away. "In the Conan Doyle stories, Sherlock Holmes smokes a pipe" is true, and it still contains the name, so if the name were merely empty the words "in the stories" couldn't make the sentence true — not without some account of what "Sherlock Holmes" contributes inside that phrase. Compare "In the Conan Doyle stories, Hercule Poirot smokes a pipe": that's false. If both names are empty, why does one come out true and the other false? The difference has to be something the name contributes, and a referent — the character — is exactly the sort of thing that would do it.

(4) Maya said Dan's confusing two ways of talking. "In the stories" isn't an ordinary sentence-operator; it marks off a game of make-believe. The embedded sentence is pretend-talk, the way a child pointing at a doll and saying "the baby is hungry" isn't referring to a baby at all. The names don't refer inside the game; they only pretend-refer. The Holmes/Poirot difference is a difference in what the two story-texts tell us to imagine, not in what the names pick out. And as for Dan's original point, "Sherlock Holmes doesn't exist" is just what we say when we step outside the game and note that there's no real person answering to the name — not a remark about some strange entity that lacks existence.

Please adjudicate this move by move: rate each of (1)-(4) from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who was right about which specific points, and what, if anything, they were both missing.
