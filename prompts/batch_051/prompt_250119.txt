I cover science and ideas for a living, which mostly means I sit in rooms where someone is explaining why some policy is simply impossible, and the impossibility usually turns out to have a lot of moving parts. I keep noticing a move I don't have a name for, and I'd rather have the concept carved than get a quick label.

Take Hardin's lifeboat. The setup: a boat with a capacity of 60, fifty people already aboard, and a hundred swimmers in the water. From the first sentence the number of seats is doing the moral work — nobody asks who set the capacity, why fifty made it aboard rather than forty, or whether 61 would actually capsize. The scarcity gets presented as something the arguer discovered, like gravity, when it was at least partly something the arguer set.

I've seen the same shape elsewhere. A hospital administrator says there are twelve ICU beds, as if the number twelve were geology, when the bed count is a budget decision from last spring. An op-ed says a region has exceeded it's carrying capacity and never mentions that the carrying capacity depends on which crops you plant and who gets the water. The format is always: draw a line, then talk about the line as if it had been there all along.

I know some of this is 'framing' and some is just a biased example. But I mean something narrower: the cutoff is a product of prior decisions, it could have been otherwise, and the argument leans on the cutoff while treating it as a boundary of nature. Does that move have a name? If it's just a species of something broader, tell me which species and how it differs from its neighbors — and if it has no name, give it one, with a definition that doesn't collapse into 'metaphor' or 'rhetoric.'
