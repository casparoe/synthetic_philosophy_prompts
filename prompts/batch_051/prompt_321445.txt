I'm a novelist working on a book set around a famine, and the more research I do the more I notice an asymmetry in how we talk about the deaths. When the trigger is a drought or a flood, we file the whole thing under natural disaster, and the dead sort of dissolve into the weather. When the trigger is a government decision—exporting grain, requisitioning harvests, a rationing scheme gone wrong—we file the same deaths under political crime, sometimes atrocity. But the mechanism is usually similar: people starve because they loose access to food, not because there's no food in the region. Most famines have both bad luck and human choices sitting upstream. Is the natural/political distinction doing real work here, or is it just a storytelling habit where some deaths get a villain and others don't? If you had to defend the asymmetry, what's the strongest version of it? And do the Bengal famine, the Irish famine, and the Great Leap Forward actually sit cleanly on one side of that line, or do they blur it? Please write your answer in American English.
