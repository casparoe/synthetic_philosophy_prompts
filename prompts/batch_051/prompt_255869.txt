What a listener calls one song is, in the copyright systems behind most streaming and recorded-music markets, two separately owned assets: the musical composition and the sound recording. A newly recorded cover of the same composition owes royalties to the publisher but generates no claim for the owner of the original recording; a sample lifted from the original record can require clearance from both; and a fully synthesized recording can fix the composition as a sequence of sounds without any performance of it. The same sequence of sounds is thus treated, in one proceeding, as an abstract template and, in another, as a concrete object, and the one song has two owners, two licensing regimes, and two infringement tests depending on which asset is said to have been copied.

Reconstruct the ontology of musical works that this dual-copyright practice presupposes. Does it strictly presuppose that a musical work is a repeatable abstract type, distinct from all performances and recordings and only ever instantiated by them — or does it merely fit that picture without requiring it? Distinguish what the practice is committed to from what it merely harmonizes with, and mark where it is underdetermined among rival ontologies of works, performances, and recordings. Then say what would become visible, awkward, or untenable in the practice if the presupposed ontology were replaced by a view on which only performances and recordings exist and the 'work' is nothing over and above them.

Separate what is settled about the practice's ontological commitments from what is genuinely contested among philosophers who work on the ontology of music, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
