I am drafting a short essay on a question in the ethics of frugality, and I would like comments on the argument before I revise it. The question is whether an ethic that treats frugality as a value should take a deontological, virtue-like form ("be frugal") or a consequentialist form ("promote frugality"). My working thesis is that the consequentialist reading rests on an equivocation between two senses in which a value can be realized, and that frugality exposes this equivocation more clearly than values such as happiness or welfare do. Below are my essay notes. I would like comments on three things in particular: (a) whether the central distinction in step 1 is drawn clearly enough to do the work I claim for it; (b) whether step 3 is fair to the consequentialist or instead attacks a straw man; and (c) whether the generalization in step 5 overreaches. I am less interested in prose style than in whether the argument is valid and whether a sophisticated consequentialist has an easy reply that I have not anticipated.

Essay notes.

1. The essay opens with a distinction. A value can be realized in an agent's own conduct, as when honesty is treated as a norm for one's own speech. Or it can be treated as a state of the world to be promoted, as when honesty is treated as something to be maximized across a population. If you could make a thousand people more honest only by telling one lie yourself, the first reading does not obviously instruct you to tell the lie, even though the world would then contain more honesty than before. The point is not that one reading is right; it is that they are different instructions and they can come apart.

2. The essay then applies this to frugality. Frugality is not simply spending little. It is a disposition to get more use out of what one has, to avoid waste, to distinguish needs from wants, and to resist status-driven consumption. On the "be frugal" reading, a person who buys one good coat and keeps it for twenty years is realizing the value, while a person who buys a new coat every winter but spends large sums on anti-consumerist campaigns is not—even if the second person's spending brings about more frugality in others. The second person promotes frugality but does not, in his own conduct, practice it.

3. The consequentialist reply I consider is this: if frugality matters at all, then, other things equal, more frugality is better than less, and an agent who can bring about more frugality at little cost should do so. I argue that this reply treats "frugality" as a homogeneous good that can be summed across persons. But the value of frugality is partly agent-relative: a large part of what is good about frugality is that the frugal person's own activities express discipline, attention, and independence. Those goods are realized in the agent who practices frugality, not automatically transferred into the lives of others whom one makes frugal. If that is right, the consequentialist's inference from "frugality is valuable" to "more frugality, whoever realizes it, is what the agent should promote" is too quick.

4. The diagnosis is that the consequentialist reading derives its plausibility from an equivocation. "Frugality" sometimes names a good-making feature of a person's life—the agent's own disciplined, independent relation to material things. At other times it names a good-making feature of a state of affairs—a population in which consumption is lower and waste is rarer. The ethic "be frugal" answers to the first; the ethic "promote frugality" answers to the second. The consequentialist has not shown that the first value reduces to the second, or that it is best understood as a means to the second.

5. The essay ends by generalizing. I claim that the same point holds for other values I call agent-shaped values—hospitality, courage, attentiveness—whose realization is inseparable from the character and activity of the person who bears them. I conclude that for such values the deontological or virtue-like form is the default, and that the burden lies on the consequentialist to show that a value like frugality is the kind of thing whose aggregation across persons makes sense at all.

Questions for your comments: Is step 3 unfair to the consequentialist? Could a consequentialist accept that frugality's value is partly agent-relative and still argue that the right response is to promote the conditions under which others can live frugally, without being frugal oneself? Does the essay need a worked example in which the "promote frugality" reading gives the wrong verdict, rather than merely showing that it changes the subject? And is the generalization in step 5 defensible, or are hospitality, courage, and attentiveness importantly disanalogous to frugality?
