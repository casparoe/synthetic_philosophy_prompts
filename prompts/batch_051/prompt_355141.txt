I am drafting an essay for a workshop on moral responsibility, and I would like you to give me comments on it. The essay is below. My aim is to use a passage from Hume's Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, Section VIII, "Of Liberty and Necessity," to put pressure on a common incompatibilist assumption: that a person could be held responsible for an action only if, at the moment of acting, she could have done otherwise. I suspect the passage suggests a different line of criticism than the standard "conditional analysis" reply. Please comment on whether the argument is valid as stated, whether it confuses determinism with uniformity or predictability, and where an incompatibilist is most likely to resist it. I am specifically not asking for references or literature recommendations; I want substantive criticism of the reasoning.

Passage:
"It is universally acknowledged, that there is a great uniformity among the actions of men, in all nations and ages, and that human nature remains still the same, in its principles and operations."

Here is the essay draft:

In the section of the Enquiry devoted to liberty and necessity, Hume makes a claim that looks at first like a mere empirical observation about social life: there is great uniformity among human actions, and human nature remains constant across nations and ages. The observation is easy to grant so long as it is treated as a general remark about how people tend to behave. But I want to argue that, once we take the observation seriously, it creates a difficulty for the principle of alternate possibilities that is independent of the usual compatibilist strategies.

The standard compatibilist answer to the demand for alternate possibilities is to offer a conditional reading of "could have done otherwise." According to that reading, a determined agent could have done otherwise in the sense that she would have done otherwise if she had chosen or willed otherwise. Incompatibilists reply that this conditional ability is not the freedom at issue: what matters is whether she could have chosen otherwise, given the actual past and the actual laws. The debate then turns on whether the will itself is exempt from determination.

Hume's uniformity claim suggests a different way into the problem. If we really believed that a given action was not the product of any standing traits, motives, or regularities in the agent's character, we would not treat that action as evidence of who the agent is. Responsibility ascriptions are, in large part, inferences from an action to something abiding in the agent: a disposition, a policy, a settled pattern of concern. Such inferences require exactly the kind of uniformity Hume describes. A single action that was, in the relevant sense, an uncaused or self-originating departure from the agent's prior character would be puzzling precisely because it would not reveal the agent in the way responsibility requires.

Now, the incompatibilist need not claim that free actions are random or completely discontinuous with the agent's character. Libertarians typically allow that free actions are influenced by motives and character while insisting that they are not fully determined by them. The agent adds something: an exercise of causal power that is not itself fixed by prior conditions. My worry is that this concession may be unstable. The more the free action is shaped by character, the more the uniformity needed for responsibility is doing the explanatory work, and the less the additional undetermined component seems to contribute to responsible agency. The less the action is shaped by character, the more it looks like a departure from the self whose deeds we are evaluating.

This is not quite the old complaint that indeterminism merely introduces chance. The point is specifically about what responsibility ascriptions are for. When we hold someone responsible for a cruel remark, we are not merely responding to the event of the remark. We are taking the remark to express something about the speaker, something that would, in relevantly similar circumstances, produce relevantly similar behavior. If we discovered that the remark was produced by an undetermined self-originating act that bore no regular connection to the speaker's values or dispositions, our responsibility practices would lose their grip. Hume's uniformity claim, then, is not a concession that compatibilists must explain away. It is a condition of responsibility itself, and alternate possibilities, if they require gaps in uniformity, may be incompatible with that condition.

One might object that this argument proves only that we need some predictability, not that the actual future must be determined. But I think it cuts deeper. If responsibility requires that the action be traceable to a stable self, and if the libertarian's undetermined component is needed to secure could-have-done-otherwise, then at the actual world the action must have a sufficient character-based explanation. If it has such an explanation, the alternate possibility becomes idle. If it lacks such an explanation, the action is not sufficiently rooted in the agent to ground more than a superficial reaction. Either way, the principle of alternate possibilities seems to be in tension with the uniformity requirement.

I want the comments to focus on the three questions above. In particular, I would like you to tell me whether the final paragraph commits a modal fallacy: does the argument mistakenly infer from "if the action has a sufficient character-based explanation, then the alternate possibility is idle" to "an undetermined component can never contribute to ownership"? And is there a version of the criticism that would apply even to a libertarian who rejects the principle of alternate possibilities but insists on ultimate sourcehood?
