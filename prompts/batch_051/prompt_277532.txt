I've been trying to live with a lot less after a health scare, and I've found I can't keep the three words people throw at me straight: my sister says I'm just being frugal, my roommate says I'm basically an ascetic now, and the label I actually like — voluntary simplicity — turns out to be harder to define than I thought.

The working definition I've been using is roughly this criterias: (1) your material possessions and consumption are deliberately kept well below what you could afford; (2) the main motive is some value other than saving money — time, freedom, low environmental impact, focus, something spiritual; and (3) the point isn't self-punishment or renunciation for its own sake, which is what I take asceticism to be, so there's supposed to be a positive gain you're after.

Now here's the case I keep going back and forth on. A guy in his forties rents one small room, owns fewer than a hundred things, has no car, no TV, no subscriptions, and mostly eats rice and beans cooked in one pot. He could easily afford a normal apartment and a normal amount of stuff. But he also flies four times a year to see his family and friends, pays someone to clean his small room weekly because he hates cleaning, and replaces his phone every single year because — he says — a fast, simple device frees up time and removes decisions. His total spending is still below the median for someone with his income, but the flying and the phone-churning are the opposite of low-impact, and the cleaner looks a lot like paying to keep a simple lifestyle from getting annoying.

So: is he practicing voluntary simplicity, is he being frugal, or is it actually a kind of soft asceticism? Or does he fit my definition just because he owns few posessions and meets his own stated motives? Go through my three criteria honestly and say where the definition underdetermines the answer. Then tell me whether the case shows that one of the criteria needs to be restated or dropped — I'm fine with that.

Please act as a philosopher of practical ethics who works on ideals of the good life. Start by brainstorming the strongest version of each ideal (frugality, asceticism, voluntary simplicity) so you don't just run them together, and then apply my criteria to the case. At the end, tell me what would change your mind: which argument, counterexample, or different reading of the guy's motives would flip your verdict, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
