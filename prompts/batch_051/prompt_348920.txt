For a handout in an interdisciplinary course on consent in law and ethics, write a decision tree that sorts a respondent into a position on what makes consent valid, built from the logical space of the debate rather than from a list of labels. Each branch node should be a concrete question or case about deception, coercion, or competence that a student can answer from their own judgment without already knowing which philosophical view the answer points to. The leaves must include at least these four positions: (1) consent as an actual mental state of agreement, so that the moral work is done by what the person in fact decided, not by what they said or what the other party inferred; (2) consent as a normative power that validly alters another's obligations or permissions and is effective only when sufficiently voluntary, informed, and competent relative to what is being authorized; (3) consent as a communicative act, so that validity turns on what the recipient could reasonably take the consenter to have authorized rather than on the consenter's private state of mind; and (4) a deflationary view on which consent is not a single moral power but a loose cluster of domain-specific practices, so deception, coercion, and incompetence undermine consent in different ways and for different reasons. Aim for seven to ten branch nodes and no more than nine leaves. Frame each node as a question about a case—for example, a lie about something the consenting party would have regarded as material and the other party knows this, a threat to end a valued relationship unless the other agrees, or an uncommunicated decision that someone may use one's property. Flag any two leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate, and identify combinations of answers that have no occupant, with a one-line diagnosis of whether each is unoccupied because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.
