I run a small discussion group that originated in a rationalist-adjacent reading circle, and next month I'm leading the session. The topic is machine ethics, and the specific thing I want the group to leave with is a gears-level understanding of why the hard part of implementing ethical reasoning in a machine is not settling on the right principle and then encoding it. I want them to see the difference between the objective as written in the system and the ethical predicate it is supposed to be implementing, and to see that the gap between the two is not a temporary engineering defect to be patched with better sensors or more training data, but a structural feature of asking a formally specified system to apply open-textured concepts to cases nobody enumerated in advance. Don't explain this to me; plan how I should explain it. The session is ninety minutes, about fifteen people, no background in academic moral philosophy, but they have read a fair amount of AI-safety material and will arrive with the default view that the bottleneck is choosing the objective, after which careful engineering mostly handles the rest.

Design the session's explanatory arc so that this default view is broken rather than merely contradicted. I want: the idea that has to be in place before the underdetermination claim even makes sense, and what depends on that idea; a single running example to carry the session that is not the trolley problem and not the paperclip maximizer, ideally a mundane assistive system where small changes in how the situation is described, or in what the system counts as an instance of a term like harm or interruption or clean, force visibly different outputs; and a list of the misconceptions this audience will predictably form—for instance, that the difficulty is just sensor noise, or that it can be eliminated by adding more rules, or that the system need only be uncertain about empirical outcomes rather than about the boundaries of the concepts in its objective—and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off before it hardens. End with a check for understanding that would distinguish someone who has grasped the structural gap from someone who has only concluded that machine ethics is hard. Also tell me what to explicitly set aside as out of scope for this level and how to flag it as out of scope without making the group feel the main point has been dodged. The session must stand on its own; do not assign reading.
