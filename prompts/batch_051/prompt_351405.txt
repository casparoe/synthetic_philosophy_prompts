You are a philosopher of biology who also reads nineteenth-century fiction closely, writing for a seminar that pairs Victorian novels with conceptual problems in the philosophy of science. Think step by step, and begin with some brainstorming rather than a thesis. First, spell out the distinction between the etiological (selected-effect) account of biological function and the causal-role account, and say what each would predict about a trait that has been deliberately engineered into an organism but never selected for. Then catalogue two or three plausible readings of H. G. Wells's novel The Island of Doctor Moreau before settling on the one that proves most faithful in both directions.

On the etiological account, a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected: a heart's function is to pump blood because pumping blood is what hearts were selected to do. On the causal-role account, by contrast, a function is a stable contribution that a part makes to the operation of a containing system, and no selection history is required—which is why artefacts and engineered systems sit so naturally with that account.

Now read The Island of Doctor Moreau through this distinction. Moreau is a designer rather than a breeder: he surgically sculpts animals into human-like Beast Folk, and the plot turns on the gap between what he carves into them and what persists, since his creations continually threaten to revert to their animal forms. He also imposes on them a recited Law whose prohibitions are meant to hold their animal nature in check. Does the novel dramatise the etiological account's central claim—that a structure earns a genuine function only through a selective history—so that Moreau's creations possess mere causal roles, never functions in the full sense? Or does it instead vindicate the causal-role account, by showing that manufactured speech, hands, and ritual can function perfectly well within a designed society even though they have no evolutionary ancestry? Weigh concrete elements of the novel in your answer: the vivisection and its pain; the Beast Folk's recitation of the Law and their insistence that they are men, not animals; their backsliding; and Moreau's inability to make his remade creatures stable. Say where these details push the reading.

Also ask where the novel pushes back against the philosophical lens. Wells was writing before the modern function debate, and the book is usually read as an ethical fable about cruelty, vivisection, and the human–animal boundary. Does reading it for the etiological/causal-role dispute distort it, or does the novel genuinely force that distinction on the reader? Finally, say what The Island of Doctor Moreau adds to the philosophy of biological function that the familiar swamp-creature and synthetic-organism thought experiments do not.
