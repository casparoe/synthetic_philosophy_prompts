I was talking to a friend who just took a philosophy of mind class, and he explained the higher-order thought theory of consciousness to me. His version went like this: "The theory says a mental state only becomes conscious when you have a thought about it. Like, a pain is just unconscious neural traffic until you think 'I'm in pain,' and that thought is what makes the pain conscious. So what you're really conscious of is the thought, not the pain itself. It also seems to make introspection infallible, because the higher-order thought is what makes the pain a conscious fact in the first place, so there's no gap between what you think you feel and what you feel. The obvious problem is that babies and dogs don't have the concepts to think 'I'm in pain' in the right way, so the theory has to say they're not conscious, which is absurd. And it makes conscious experience sound like silent narration rather than something felt."

I suspect some of this is a strawman—I vaguely remember that higher-order theorists talk a lot about misrepresentation, which seems incompatible with introspection being infallible—but I cant work out which parts are fair and which aren't. Could you go through his summary piece by piece and say what a defender of the view would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? Also, wich of his worries (you're only ever aware of your thoughts, you can't be wrong about what you feel, babies and dogs get ruled out, consciousness becomes inner narration) actually follow from the accurate version of the theory, and which are bad readings of it?
