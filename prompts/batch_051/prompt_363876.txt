I am a novelist at work on a book that turns on an old wrong: an embezzlement committed thirty years before the novel opens and discovered only now. In researching it, I have become preoccupied by a puzzle that reaches well beyond the plot. Many legal systems hold that the mere passage of time extinguishes the right to prosecute or sue for wrongs that would, if discovered sooner, have been straightforwardly actionable. Under such rules, a forger identified only after the limitation period has run cannot be charged, however conclusive the paper trail; a civil claim expires on the calendar even when the underlying facts are not in dispute. Yet these same systems often treat murder differently, allowing prosecution whenever the evidence comes to light, decades later, and in some places no limitation period applies at all.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose about the moral relevance of time? Does it presuppose that the moral significance of a wrong decays with its age, that the state's interest in punishment is eventually displaced by an interest in repose, that evidence and memory become unreliable at a rough rate the law may approximate, or some principled combination of these? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, say where it is coherent and where it strains, and distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Then tell me what the practice would have to look like if it presupposed instead that moral claims and criminal liability do not decay with time at all—only our knowledge of them does.
