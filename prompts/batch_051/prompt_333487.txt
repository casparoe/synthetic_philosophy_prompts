Your task is to produce the joint statement that two rival theorists could both sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration on Moore's paradox. One theorist holds that the absurdity of a sentence of the form *p, but I do not believe that p* is fundamentally a defect of assertion: the first conjunct expresses or commits the speaker to the belief reported absent by the second. The other holds that the absurdity is fundamentally a defect of the state of mind: a subject cannot rationally combine believing that p with believing that he or she does not believe that p, and the unassertability of the sentence is a symptom of this deeper doxastic incoherence.

The collaboration's point of departure is a passage in G. E. Moore's essay *A Reply to My Critics* (1942) in which he asks us to consider a speaker who says of some past event—his own example concerns having gone to the pictures on a particular night—that the event happened while adding that he does not believe that it happened. Moore observes that something would be absurd in saying such a thing of oneself, although the two parts of the sentence could both be true. The passage is presented here in paraphrase; do not attribute any exact wording to Moore.

The deliverable should have two parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims, including at least: the logical status of the sentence and whether it is ever true; the asymmetry between the first-person present-tense case and third-person or past-tense versions; and whether a speaker uttering such a sentence is necessarily lying. Second, a disagreements table, with columns for the precise contested proposition, worded so that each side accepts the wording of its own view, the assertion-account position, the belief-account position, and the case or argument both sides agree would bear on the question.

In the table, give particular attention to these cruxes: first, whether a fully competent speaker who makes a Moorean assertion exhibits a rational failing of belief management or merely violates a conversational norm; second, whether the absurdity survives in silent judgement as strongly as in speech, and what the asymmetry, if there is one, shows; third, whether the defect is best located in the speaker's relation to the proposition, the utterance, or the act of self-ascription; fourth, whether the paradox supports any transparency or self-intimation thesis about belief. Do not force agreement where the camps genuinely diverge, and do not count a formulation as shared if it is vacuous or equivocal.
