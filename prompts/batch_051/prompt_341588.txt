I am a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums rather than a professional philosopher, and I have drafted the short essay below on David Pearce's hedonistic imperative — the proposal that biotechnology should be used to abolish suffering in all sentient life and replace it with graded, information-sensitive states of well-being rather than uniform bliss. I would like comments on the essay rather than a rewrite. Please focus on these questions: whether I have stated the standard objections and Pearce's reply fairly; whether the residual objection I develop is genuinely stronger than the wireheading objection or only the same objection in new terminology; where a sophisticated defender of the imperative would most plausibly resist the argument; and whether the threefold decomposition of suffering's roles is too crude to do the work I ask of it. Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this area, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus.

Here is the essay.

Most objections to the hedonistic imperative run together two different targets. One target is the abolition of suffering itself. The other is the replacement of the whole hedonic range with a single, flat, maximal state of pleasure — the wirehead scenario, in which reward is unmoored from what is actually happening. Pearce's proposal is aimed at the first target, not the second. He asks for gradients of well-being that remain information-sensitive: better and worse states of the world are still represented in the hedonic tone of experience, and the organism still responds to those differences. The wireheading objection and the drugged-dystopia objection therefore mostly attack a position Pearce is not defending.

That does not settle the matter, because the objection can be rebuilt with the polarity of valence, rather than its informational content, as the target. Suffering as it currently occurs appears to do at least three jobs at once. It is an intrinsic bad: something a subject has reason to want to end. It is a signal carrying information about tissue damage, social loss, failing strategies, or other harm. And it is an asymmetric motivational push: negative valence biases action toward escape, avoidance, and repair, while positive valence biases action toward approach and continuation. The standard abolitionist reply addresses the first two jobs directly. The intrinsic bad is what the project exists to eliminate. The informational content can be preserved by replacing a negative-to-positive scale with a positive-only scale along which better and worse situations remain ordered. But I have not seen the third job handled with comparable care. If every point on the scale is positive, then avoiding a harm is experienced as moving from a more positive state to a less positive one, and the agent would have to treat the less positive state as the state worth moving toward. It is not obvious that a positive-only scale can supply the same asymmetry of avoidance that negative valence supplies. If it cannot, then abolishing negative valence would not merely remove a bad feeling while leaving the rest of the cognitive architecture intact; it would remove a load-bearing part of that architecture. This is an empirical claim about cognition rather than an ethical claim, and I suspect it is the claim on which the whole abolitionist program turns.

The version of the objection I most want stress-tested is this: a defender of the imperative might concede that subjective suffering need not be retained, yet deny that the avoidance asymmetry depends on negative valence itself, because avoidance could be implemented by prediction-error signals or other grading mechanisms that carry no negative affect, or because a sufficient relative drop within an all-positive scale could do the motivational work. Have I missed a decisive argument for that response, or is the issue as open as I make it sound?
