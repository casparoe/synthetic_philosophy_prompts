I've been going back and forth on this since trying to explain Pearl to a friend who works with LLMs. Pearl's causal hierarchy always struck me as one of the rare places where the math and the slogan actually line up: if all you have is observational data, you're stuck on the association rung, P(y|x), and to climb to interventions (do) or counterfactuals (what would have happened if x hadn't) you already need a causal model or an actual experiment. The usual gloss is that you can't get causes out until you put causes in, and the hierarchy makes precise why. Fine. But now LLMs look like a blunt counterexample? GPT-ish systems are trained on text, which sure looks like the biggest pile of observational data around, and they'll happily answer "what would have happened if" and "does X cause Y" questions about setups they've never seen, sometimes well. They're not running experiments and nobody hands them a structural equation model; they're just predicting tokens. So did these systems break the hierarchy, or are they taking in rung-2 and rung-3 knowledge through the training data in a way the original framing didn't anticipate? If the ladder still holds, I want to know which premise was doing the real work and how you'd restate the claim so it still says something true about systems like this. I care less about whether the model is "really" reasoning causally than about whether the hierarchy survives contact with it.
