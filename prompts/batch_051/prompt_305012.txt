I am a retired physical chemist, and since leaving the laboratory I have been reading moral and political philosophy with an attention I could rarely spare during my working life. A recurring puzzle is the way our societies draw the boundary between what may and may not be bought and sold, and I would value your help in testing whether that boundary is principled rather than merely customary.

Take the human kidney. In many societies an adult of modest means may be paid for providing blood plasma, and in virtually all of them she may be paid to do work that carries a non-negligible and well-understood risk of death or injury. Yet in nearly all of those same societies she may not sell one of her two kidneys, however willing she is and however urgent the need on the other side. The transaction is criminalised, and many people regard it as not merely illegal but repugnant. I am deliberately setting aside the empirical question of what a legal market in organs would do to supply, safety, and exploitation. My question is conceptual: is there a defensible principle that draws the line exactly where our practice draws it?

The obvious candidates seem to me to fail. If the principle is that the body is not a commodity, then paid provision of blood plasma and, in many places, compensated surrogacy are counterexamples. If the principle is that the poor cannot genuinely consent to serious bodily risk, then hazardous occupations are a counterexample, since our answer to that worry is safety regulation rather than prohibition. If the principle is that we may sell replenishable parts of the body but not parts that cannot be renewed, then the paid provision of human eggs, where it is permitted, is a counterexample, since egg cells are not replenished; and hazardous work in any case spends a resource that cannot be replenished. I want the strongest principle you can construct, and an honest verdict on whether it works.

If you defend the asymmetry, test your principle against two neighbouring cases: a shopkeeper who trebles the price of bottled water after a storm is treated as a criminal offence in many places, while ordinary scarcity pricing for essential goods is treated as commerce; and the sale of one's vote is forbidden while large donations to political parties are permitted. Does a single account of which goods may be commodified make sense of all three boundaries, or are they unrelated lines we happen to have drawn? I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for empirical findings; I want your own best reconstruction, tested against the cases. Please answer in continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or email: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
