I'm an undergraduate in a course on the philosophy of history, and a debate that keeps surfacing in our seminar has left me less interested in the two positions themselves than in the fact that the exchange never seems to move. The dispute concerns the kind of historical claim captured by the old "for want of a nail" proverb: that a small, chance-inflected event can, through compounding effects, produce a large historical difference. One side holds that counterfactual histories of this sort do genuine explanatory work. They point to sensitive dependence on initial conditions in complex social systems and to near-miss cases—commonly, the events of the July Crisis and the assassination at Sarajevo—as evidence that a rewound history with one perturbation removed could plausibly fail to produce a general European war. The other side accepts the same nonlinear picture but turns it into an objection: if everything influences everything, then the counterfactual "small rewrite" cannot be constrained, and the apparent insight of the nail proverb is an artifact of backward narrative selection. On their view, long-run structural pressures do the explanatory work, and the singular event is at most a trigger rather than the source of the divergent outcome.

The strange thing is that the two groups do not seem to differ about any matter of historical record, and both agree that historical systems are nonlinear and poorly suited to prediction. Yet repeated exposure to the same exhibits—revolutions, epidemics, the outbreak of war—hardens rather than softens their positions. I have a hypothesis, but I do not trust it: I suspect "contingency" is being used in at least three distinguishable senses (sensitivity to small causes, genuine ontic chance, and non-necessitation by social structure), and that a hidden formal problem about how to specify the minimal rewrite in a counterfactual antecedent—something like the closest-possible-world problem—is doing much of the work. But I can also see the impasse being about standards of evidence, since it is unclear what could count as evidence about a single unrewound run of history; about which cases are admissible as data; or about bedrock intuitions concerning whether individual actions or large structures are the right units of explanation.

I would like a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than a verdict for either side. Is this a verbal disagreement, a difference about what would count as evidence, a disagreement over the formal machinery used to evaluate counterfactuals, or an ill-posed question that only looks well-defined because its terms are doing different work on each side? Given your diagnosis, what would constitute progress—a distinction that disambiguates "contingency," a formal result about counterfactual similarity under chaos, a change of question, or a reason to expect the impasse to persist? Please separate what is comparatively settled among specialists from what remains genuinely contested, and avoid presenting a contested view as the consensus of the field. Please write your answer in American English.
