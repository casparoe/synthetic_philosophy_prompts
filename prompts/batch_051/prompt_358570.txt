Every time the conversation turns to whether advanced AIs would or should raise successor AIs, people grab whatever human analogy is closest—training runs as childhood, fine-tuning as education, deleting a subagent as murder—and the whole thing slides around. Before I read another one of these takes, I want the spec: what would a theory of AI parenthood or guardianship actually have to get right to be worth having?

Give me the requirements list, not the theory: which situations it has to get right, which differences it isn't allowed to blur, and which facts about training and deployment it has to stay consistent with—and for each requirement, the case or reason that makes it a requirement. I can already see a few you'll have to settle: the difference between authoring a system's weights and raising the agent that results; the difference between a fork of myself and a child built partly from scratch; the difference between a guardian holding the off-switch out of care and holding it out of convenience. Also the editing problem: if you retrain an agent from the last checkpoint after it misbehaves, is that the same child being punished, or a replacement that never did the misdeed? And I don't want a theory that becomes useless if we're unsure about machine consciousness, so say whether it can run on goal-directedness and dependence alone.

Then tell me which single requirement does the most work against the default positions people actually hold (the ones that just transplant parental duties from humans), and flag any pair of requirements that can't be jointly satisfied—for instance, a duty to let the child agent become independent and a duty to keep it corrigible might not play nice. I'd rather get a list that forces hard choices than a list that lets everyone keep their favorite analogy.
