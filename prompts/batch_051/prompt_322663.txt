One of the older faculty in our department has talked the computing committee into considering a new way to allocate the shared GPU cluster next year, and as the economics PhD student on the committee I'm suppose to think it through before the vote. I want to pressure-test it before I write my memo.

The rule: total capacity is 10,000 GPU-hours. Each lab's current (last year's) allocation is its fallback—the professor calls it the disagreement point—and those fallbacks sum to 6,000 hours, so 4,000 hours are genuinely up for grabs. Each lab submits a "maximum useful allocation" (MUA): the most GPU-hours it could productively use next year, no cap, not audited. If a lab's MUA is below its current allocation, we just treat the MUA as equal to the current allocation, so nobody loses on paper. Then, writing d_i for lab i's current allocation and m_i for its submitted MUA, the allocation is x_i = d_i + λ(m_i - d_i), where λ is the largest number in [0,1] such that the x_i sum to at most 10,000. In words: start at current allocations and move along the ray toward the submitted MUA vector until total capacity is hit; if the MUAs sum to less than 10,000, the leftover sits idle and nobody has said what happens to it. The professor calls this "Kalai–Smorodinsky with self-reported ideal points," and his pitch is that it's fair because every lab gets the same fraction of what it says it needs.

I suspect this is an incentive nightmare wearing a fairness trench coat, but I don't trust my gut. Walk me through how it would actually play out. Given what other labs report, what does a lab want to do with its MUA—inflate it without limit, or is there something that stabilizes the reports? How does the fact that this year's allocation becomes next year's fallback change things? What happens to a new lab with d_i = 0? Can labs game it by splitting into sub-labs, coordinating reports, or doing useless computation to defend a baseline? And are the obvious fixes—capping MUA at some audited technical maximum, auditing ex post usage, updating next year's fallback from realized usage—enough to make it honestly reveal need, or do they just move the gaming around?

Finally, the professor's fairness line: does "same fraction of what you asked for" survive contact with strategic reporting, or is the fairness part exactly what breaks? I want mechanism analysis, not a verdict on the old queueing system.

As you go, give me a rough confidence read on the main claims, and at the end name the one point you're least sure about. Hard cap: 800 words; shorter is fine if it's dense.
