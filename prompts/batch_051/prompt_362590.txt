Take the 2011 Bem precognition paper and the subsequent failed direct replications as a case study in the frequentist/Bayesian dispute over statistical evidence. I know the broad facts: a respected social psychologist publishes a batch of experiments that look like evidence for retroactive influence on cognition and affect, the p-values pass the usual bar, reviewers accept it, then later direct replications mostly come back null and there's a big arguement about whether the original methods were even legitimate. I want the careful mapping, not a recap. First run the original paper through a strict frequentist lens: what were the published analyses actually good for as evidence, what would a purist say about running multiple experiments and any flexibility in how they got reported, and what should Bem and the reviewers have concluded if all they cared about were error rates. Then run it as a Bayesian whose prior on time-reversed causation is not literally zero but very small: what should the original data have done to that prior, and what do the null replications do to it. Then flip the episode around and ask whether it makes the frequentist toolkit look worse, the Bayesian toolkit, both, or neither, and where each side has to quietly add complications to avoid either accepting precognition or dismissing Bem unfairly. Finally, be honest about how much of the mess is actually about statistics at all, rather than publication bias, career incentives, and the field's norms about what counts as a finding.
