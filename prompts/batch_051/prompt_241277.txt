As a novelist, I have the habit of listening to other people's arguments more than joining them, and I observed the following exchange between two acquaintances without indicating which side, if either, I considered right.

The two men were discussing a wager. A player is offered repeated plays of a fair coin. Before each play he chooses a fraction of his current bankroll to stake, from zero to one, and he may not borrow. If the staked fraction is f, then on heads his bankroll is multiplied by 1 + 0.5f, and on tails by 1 − 0.4f; if he stakes everything, the multipliers are simply 1.5 on heads and 0.6 on tails.

The first man argued that the wager is unambiguously favorable. Staked in full, each play has an expected multiplier of (1.5 + 0.6) / 2 = 1.05, a five percent expected gain per play, and expectation compounds, so after many plays the expected bankroll grows at five percent per play. Anyone who is not strongly risk-averse should be glad to take the bet.

The second man replied that expected value is the wrong lens for a repeated multiplicative bet, because it averages over parallel copies of the player rather than over the trajectory of one player. He observed that a forty percent loss requires a sixty-six point seven percent gain to repair, while the winning outcome offers only fifty percent. The relevant long-run growth of a fully staked player is therefore not 1.05 but the geometric mean, the square root of 1.5 times 0.6, about 0.949, a decay of roughly 5.1 percent per play. Over the long run a full-stake bankroll tends to zero with probability one, he said; the positive expectation is carried by increasingly rare runs of heads that no single player can depend on.

The first man conceded that staking everything is ruinous but said the second man had overcorrected. The defect lies in the stake, not in the wager. If the player stakes one quarter of his bankroll each time, the multipliers become 1.125 on heads and 0.9 on tails, whose geometric mean is about 1.0062, a positive growth of roughly 0.62 percent per play. That quarter stake, he continued, is the fraction that maximizes the average logarithm of the multiplier and is therefore the best long-run policy. The wager remains an opportunity; the correct response to the second man's arithmetic is to size the stake, not to decline the bet.

The second man replied that this was a retreat rather than a defence. Once the first man selects a fraction to maximize average logarithmic growth, he has abandoned the arithmetic-expectation criterion on which his opening move depended; maximizing expected wealth would still stake everything, and staking everything is what both now agree ruins the player. He added that the quarter-stake rule imports a new objective, long-run growth maximization, which does not follow from the bare assertion that the wager is favorable, and that whether a single play should be accepted is a further question that expectation alone cannot settle. The real distinction, he concluded, is between the ensemble average over many copies of the player and the time average over one player's successive states; the fully staked wager is favorable in the first sense and ruinous in the second, and no amount of clever sizing dissolves that distinction.

Please adjudicate the exchange turn by turn. For each turn, state which assertions are sound, which are mistaken, and which are partly right, with brief reasons. Then say what the two men jointly fail to see, and whether the underlying question — whether, and at what stake, the wager should be accepted — has a definite answer or instead turns on assumptions neither man has made explicit.
