I run a small civic-tech reading group, and I want one of those "which position do you actually hold?" sorting quizzes for our site—except instead of the usual personality-quiz fluff, I want it to actually diagnose where someone lands in the debate about government nondisclosure agreements, confidentiality clauses, and gag clauses when the state is one of the parties.

The angle I care about is this: the interesting disagreements are not about whether secrecy is ever OK, but about (a) what it can cover—illegality, mere embarrassment, operational details, the amount of public money, the identity of contractors; (b) who decides when the public interest overrides an NDA—the signer, an independent officer, a judge, the legislature, or nobody; and (c) whether a bad clause poisons the whole agreement or is just severed. Please sort people by how they answer cases, not by which label they like, so no question may ask "are you an open-government person?" or use any of the position names.

Please build the quiz so it sorts into these five leaves (you can add one or two if the logical space demands it, but don't drop any):
- strong transparency / open-government
- public-interest balancer
- procedural democrat
- whistleblower-first
- executive prerogative / strong secrecy

Eight to ten questions. Each question should be a concrete forced-choice scenario, two or three answer options. They have to be answerable by someone who doesn't know the theory.

Cases I want used, though you can adjust or add a few: a city manager's harassment settlement paid with public money that includes an NDA over both the allegations and the dollar amount; a bridge contractor whose NDA covers all information obtained during the work and who discovers the deck was poured from a known-bad batch; a national-security employee who leaks an illegal domestic-surveillance program; a blanket NDA that bars employees from ever discussing unclassified work without permission; a time-limited clause that expires after ten years; a journalist who publishes leaked documents and never signed anything; and at least one case involving no wrongdoing at all—say the suppression of routine salary data or a temporary gag on an ongoing undercover operation—so the quiz can separate people who only care about exposing misconduct from people who think government secrecy is intrinsically suspect. Include at least one question on severability (if a clause is overbroad or covers illegality, should the whole contract fall or just that clause?) and at least one on who decides the public-interest override.

For the scoring key, build from the logical space rather than from the names: first lay out the answer combinations the questions actually allow, then locate my five positions in that space. If a combination corresponds to no position on my list, say so and give a one-line diagnosis—is that corner empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored? And if two of my leaves cannot be separated by the questions, dont paper over it; flag it and say what extra question would seperate them.

Before you write the questions, brainstorm the answer combinations and check whether each question actually discriminates between at least two of the leaves; if it doesn't, cut it or rewrite it.

Please write the response in four sections, in this order:
1. "The Questions" — the quiz itself, around 400 words.
2. "Scoring Key" — which answers point to which leaf, around 250 words.
3. "Unoccupied Combinations" — the empty regions of the answer space, around 150 words.
4. "Where the Instrument Breaks" — leaves the quiz can't separate, and the extra question that would fix it, around 100 words.

Act as someone who knows both freedom-of-information law and political philosophy well, but write the quiz for people who know neither. Think carefully about the difference between a question that discriminates in theory and one that discriminates after normal people read it.
