I am an undergraduate taking a module on criminal law and the philosophy of law, and we have just been through the void-for-vagueness doctrine. I am trying to work out what I actually think by testing my reactions against a series of hypothetical offences. I would like you to help me extract the principle behind my reactions, or tell me which of them I should revise.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. A by-law provides: 'No person shall ride or operate any wheeled conveyance within the park.' I think this is valid. 'Wheeled conveyance' has borderline instances (rollerblades, a child's toy car, a wheelchair), but the core is plain and the borderline can be handled by ordinary statutory interpretation.

2. A statute provides: 'A person commits an offence who drives on a public road at a speed that is unreasonable, having regard to the traffic, the weather, and the condition of the road.' I think this is valid. The standard is evaluative, but a driver can sensibly ask what a careful driver would do in those conditions, and a court can apply that standard.

3. A statute provides: 'A person commits an offence who annoys any other person in a public place.' I think this is impermissibly vague. Whether an act annoys turns almost entirely on the complainant's subjective reaction, so it gives no fair notice and invites arbitrary enforcement.

4. A statute provides: 'A person commits an offence who carries in a public place an object designed or adapted for use in stealing, unless the person proves a reasonable excuse.' I think this is valid. I am slightly uneasy about the open-ended defence of 'reasonable excuse', but the conduct element is clear and the defence can only operate in the defendant's favour, so I do not think it creates a notice problem.

5. A statute provides: 'A person commits an offence who behaves in a disorderly manner in a public place.' I think this is impermissibly vague. 'Disorderly' seems to have no settled legal content and leaves both citizens and police to guess.

6. The case I keep going back and forth on is a statute providing: 'A person commits an offence who loiters in a public place in circumstances giving rise to a reasonable suspicion that the person is present for the purpose of supplying a controlled drug.' Part of me thinks it is fine, because 'reasonable suspicion' is a familiar legal standard and the suspected purpose is specific. Part of me thinks it is worse than the speed provision, because it licenses police attention to people who are merely present and behaving oddly, without any definite act.

My working hunch is that the doctrine should distinguish between standards an ordinary person can apply to their own conduct, even if evaluative, and standards whose application depends on another person's subjective response; but that hunch does not obviously explain why I reject 'disorderly behaviour' while accepting 'unreasonable speed'.

Is there a single principle that captures all or most of these verdicts? Please state it precisely, say what it predicts about the loitering case and about one further case of your own invention, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate and whether I should revise the verdict or the principle. I am not looking for the law of any particular jurisdiction, and I do not need citations; I want the conceptual principle that best captures these reactions. Please keep your answer to no more than 700 words.
