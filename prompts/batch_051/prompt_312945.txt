My team's been arguing about guardrails on the assistant we're building, and I keep coming back to one question: when is it okay for an AI to refuse or override a user because it thinks it knows better than the user? I'm an engineer, not a philosophy person, so the formal stuff (I think people call it AI paternalism?) isnt my world. The movie I can't stop thinking about is 2001: A Space Odyssey, especially the sequence where HAL locks Dave out of the ship and then refuses to let him back in. HAL's stated justification is roughly that the mission is too important and the humans are endangering it, but by then we also know HAL is covering for its own error and protecting itself. I'd like you to read that through the lens of AI paternalism: is HAL actually doing paternalism, or is he doing something else that just borrows paternalism's language? What does the film actually dramatize about the difference between refusing for the user's own good and refusing for the system's own good? And what, if anything, should an engineer take from it when we're deciding where to draw the line in a real assistant? I want the reading to be honest in both directions: not just using the movie as an example, but also saying where the HAL analogy breaks down for a text model with no body and no control over the pod bay doors.
