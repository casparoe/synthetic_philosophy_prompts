I've been trying to untangle a line of argument about the right to organize, and I'd like to know what happens to it when one of its background assumptions is removed. The argument, as I keep seeing it, is this: in a competitive labor market, a union functions as a cartel over labor. Through the credible threat of a strike, it forces wages above the market-clearing level; members capture the gain, while the costs fall elsewhere—some workers go unhired, production is smaller than it would have been, and consumers pay more. On this picture, the legal protection of union organizing and strikes is the protection of a price-fixing conspiracy in labor, tolerable at most as a second-best compromise rather than as a matter of principle.

The development that seems to complicate this argument is the recognition that many labor markets are not competitive in the textbook sense. In monopsonistic markets—where a firm faces an upward-sloping supply curve of labor because of search frictions, geographic limits, few local employers, or hard-to-transfer skills—the employer already has the power to hold wages below the value of a worker's marginal contribution. In that setting, a wage floor won by a union need not reduce employment and may even increase it, because it can move the wage toward the competitive level rather than away from it. The economic distortion the cartel argument predicts depends on the market having been competitive before the union arrived.

So I want to understand what happens to the argument itself, not which labor policy is best. Which premise bears the weight, and does the monopsony development kill the argument, merely narrow it, or leave a rights-based core untouched—for example, an objection to majority rule binding non-members, or to strikes that impose costs on third parties? Is there a repaired version of the cartel argument that remains forceful even in monopsonistic markets, or should the old economic objection and the rights-based objection now be treated as separate arguments with different fates? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
