I am presenting a paper at an animal-cognition seminar whose audience is mostly experimental comparative psychologists and behavioural ecologists, with a small philosophical contingent. My thesis is that Morgan's canon should be reconstructed not as a substantive claim about which cognitive capacities animals actually possess, but as a rule for allocating the burden of explanatory proof: where a lower-order psychological mechanism and a higher-order one both fit the observed behaviour, the lower-order account should be the default working hypothesis, and the burden rests on the higher-order account to specify and then produce discriminating evidence. I argue that this reading escapes the standard charges against the canon — that it is unfalsifiable, that it illegitimately equates evolutionary simplicity with psychological simplicity, and that it biases science against discovering rich animal minds — and that the canon, so read, is really a discipline on experimental design rather than a ceiling on animal cognition.

The room will not be homogeneous. Some of them work with corvids, primates, or cephalopods and think the canon has functioned as a gatekeeping device that licenses scepticism about well-documented performances; others study insect or rodent learning and suspect cognitive vocabulary is doing little explanatory work; the philosophers will care about whether my burden-of-proof rule is epistemic or merely rhetorical.

Prepare me for the question period. I want a prep sheet, not a defence of the paper: the objections I am most likely to receive, phrased the way these people would actually phrase them, ordered by a combination of how likely each is and how damaging it would be. For each objection, give me a short reply and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Also identify the objection I should most hope nobody asks. Please write the response in British English.
