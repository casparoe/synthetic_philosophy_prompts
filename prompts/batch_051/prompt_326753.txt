Act as a professional philosopher specializing in formal theories of distributive justice, and evaluate the following as carefully as you would a grad-student paper. I am writing an undergraduate term paper on prioritarianism, and a friend has argued that the standard claim that prioritarianism avoids the leveling-down objection is too quick. I have reconstructed his argument below, but I cannot tell whether it is sound or whether one of the steps is doing something sneaky. I am not looking for a literature review or citations; I want you to work through the structure of the argument and say exactly where, if anywhere, it breaks down. Please begin by briefly brainstorming the one or two most plausible failure points, then settle on a final diagnosis.

The argument:

(1) Prioritarianism evaluates outcomes with a strictly increasing and strictly concave function f applied to each person's well-being, so that total value is f(w1) + f(w2) + ... + f(wn), where wi is the well-being of person i.

(2) For any distribution, define the equally distributed equivalent level e as the level of well-being such that, if everyone were at e, the outcome would have the same prioritarian value as the actual distribution. That is, n·f(e) = f(w1) + f(w2) + ... + f(wn).

(3) Because f is concave, e is always less than or equal to the arithmetic mean of the wi, and strictly less whenever the wi are not all equal. The gap between the mean and e is therefore a natural measure of how much inequality is "costing" in well-being terms.

(4) So prioritarian value can always be redescribed as a function of average well-being minus an inequality penalty: increasing the spread among the wi lowers e while leaving the mean unchanged, and choosing a more concave f amounts to choosing a larger penalty for any given spread.

(5) But a theory whose evaluation can be represented as average well-being minus an inequality penalty has a distinct evaluative component that counts inequality as bad, and therefore counts equality as good.

(6) Any theory that counts equality as good must admit that leveling down—making the better off worse off while making no one better off—can be an improvement in at least one respect, because it increases equality.

(7) Therefore, prioritarianism has not escaped the leveling-down objection; it has just hidden the egalitarian component inside the curvature of f.

Please give your answer in American English. Tell me, step by step, whether the argument is valid and, if it is not, which exact step fails. Where a step is ambiguous between a stronger and a weaker reading, state the reading that makes it most plausible and check whether the argument still goes through on that reading. Conclude by saying what a prioritarian should say about the leveling-down objection, and whether my friend's underlying worry has any residual force.
