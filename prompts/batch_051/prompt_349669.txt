I am a PhD student in political theory working on institutional design for decentralized groups facing high-stakes, irreversible choices, and I want to know how a proposed governance rule would actually behave. The background problem is the unilateralist's curse: in a loose coalition where any member may act unilaterally on behalf of all, the effective decision threshold is set by whichever member is most optimistic, so the group's chance of a costly false positive grows with its size. Now imagine a consortium of fifteen research labs that adopts the following rule for any act that would bind the whole group, such as publishing a dual-use result. Before acting unilaterally, a lab must post its reasoning to the consortium and wait fourteen days. If no lab objects within that window, the lab may proceed. If any lab objects, the lab may proceed only if it secures a written endorsement from a second lab and then wins a two-thirds supermajority vote of all members. Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a well-intentioned policy. Who ends up acting, and on the basis of what information? What incentives does it create for strategic objection, cheap endorsement, delay, concealment, or acting outside the consortium? What does the supermajority override do to the underlying curse? Which failure modes are fixable without collapsing the rule into pure unilateralism or pure consensus?
