In my working life I was a research geologist, and I came to the philosophy of history late, with an ingrained reflex: before I credit an event, I ask what trace it ought to have left and whether some other process could have produced the trace. That reflex has exposed an asymmetry in my own historical judgments, and I have found it in most other people too. I will say without hesitation that there really was a city of Troy, destroyed around the traditional date, and that some armed conflict probably stands behind the Iliad. I will say that King Arthur is a literary fiction, or at best a name borrowed from a shadowy post-Roman war-leader. And about the Exodus I no longer know what I think. Yet in all three cases the procedure is much the same: I read a text composed centuries after the events by authors with strong motives to magnify or sanctify them, and I correct it against archaeology that is at best suggestive — a layer of burning at Hisarlik, no decisive trace of Arthur, Egyptian records that make no certain mention of any mass departure. I can state the asymmetry plainly: I grant the Trojan tradition its historical kernel while discounting the poet's exaggerations, but I refuse Arthur the same treatment, and I cannot state the rule that tells me when the kernel is owed.

Thucydides was already doing the first half of this in the late fifth century BC. In Book I, chapter 10 of his History of the Peloponnesian War, after arguing that the bare ruins of a city can badly mislead posterity about the power that once went with them, he writes:

"We have therefore no right to be skeptical, nor to content ourselves with an inspection of a town to the exclusion of a consideration of its power; but we may safely conclude that the armament in question surpassed all before it, as it fell short of modern efforts; if we can here also accept the testimony of Homer's poems, in which, without allowing for the exaggeration which a poet would feel himself licensed to employ, we can see that it was far from equalling ours."

What strikes me is that he is doing exactly what I do for Troy and exactly what I refuse to do for Arthur: he treats a poetic tradition as a report of a real expedition, grants its core, and then discounts the numbers as a poet's licensed exaggeration rather than letting them poison the whole testimony. I want to know whether anything defensible justifies my selective use of this method. If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the hardest version of the three cases: a war-leader named Arthur attested by a late chronicler who had every motive to embroider; a city burned at Hisarlik attested by a destruction layer consistent with, but not proof of, a war; and an enslaved people whose departure from Egypt left no certain administrative trace. Give me the strongest principle that lets one of these cross from legend into history while keeping the others out, and an honest verdict on whether I should keep that principle, reverse it, or give up the distinction as an accident of upbringing.
