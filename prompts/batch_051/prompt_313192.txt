Recently I have been examining how the developers of large language models try to make their systems' outputs legible rather than merely correct, and I find myself uncertain about what this project commits them to. Consider a fairly representative setup. A lab trains a model to answer technical questions, and a reward model scores each response partly on whether the final answer is correct and partly on whether the displayed chain of reasoning consists of steps that a human auditor could follow and independently verify. The model is also given a refusal policy: when it cannot produce a rationale that survives that audit, it is trained to decline, even in cases where its final answer would very likely have been right. Finally, the model is fine-tuned to produce a summary of its reasoning that is meant to be a faithful account of how it actually reached the answer, not merely a plausible after-the-fact reconstruction. One of the researchers describes the aim as wanting outputs whose support is inspectable, and she adds that on some tasks the lab would prefer an answer that is legible but somewhat less accurate to one that is accurate but opaque.

What philosophical commitments does this cluster of practices presuppose? It appears to treat an inference that cannot be rendered legible to a human audience as defective, or at least unsuitable for deployment. Is that an epistemological claim — for instance, that an inscrutable inference is not genuinely justified, or not something a user can rightly accept on the model's say-so — or is it only a practical claim about risk, oversight, and accountability? Does the practice presuppose a specific view of epistemic authority, on which the auditor's comprehension is what licenses acceptance of the output? Does it presuppose that a model's verbalized steps bear a determinate relation of fidelity to the computational process that produced the answer, and what exactly would that relation need to be for the training signal to make sense? I would also like to know what the practice strictly presupposes, as opposed to what it merely fits well with: which rival views of understanding, justification, or testimony could equally endorse these design choices, and which would have to reject them? Finally, there is an apparent inconsistency in the practice itself: the same engineers train a model to account for its answer in step-by-step prose while acknowledging that the underlying computation is not fully captured by any such transcript. What view of understanding or explanation would make that tension tolerable rather than contradictory, and what would have to change in the practice if that view were replaced?
