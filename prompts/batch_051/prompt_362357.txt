I'm a therapist, and I keep hitting the same fight between a Stoic-flavoured view that emotions are mostly noise and a Romantic view that emotions are the point. I've been rereading Spinoza's Ethics and got stuck on the passage near the start of Part V where he claims, as I understand it, that an emotion which is a passion stops being a passion once you form a clear and distinct idea of it. His idea seems to be that if you trace out the causes and the bodily changes involved in, say, anger, the emotion no longer merely happens to you; you become the adequate cause of it, or at least you understand it clearly enough that it stops dominating you. I want to turn that into something I can actually use in weekly individual therapy, across maybe six to eight sessions, with clients who are not going to read Spinoza and don't have years to spend philosophising. Write me a reusable session-level decision procedure for helping a client move an emotion from passion to activity in that Spinozist sense. It should be concrete enough for a therapist without a philosophy background: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a satisfactory answer to each, where the procedure should stop or refer out, and the points where a human has to make a judgment call instead of following a rule. Then apply it to two clients. One has recurrent grief after a death years ago; the other has workplace anger they say is justified but draining. Be honest about whether the procedure actually operationalises Spinoza's distinction or just replaces it with ordinary cognitive reappraisal, and tell me what the procedure cannot do for either client. Also tell me one outcome the procedure might endorse that a warmth-and-acceptance therapist would dislike.
