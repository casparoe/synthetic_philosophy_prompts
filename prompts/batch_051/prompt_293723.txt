I've been going back and forth about something a friend mentioned after a seminar on the ethics of communication. She said it was an idea from Édouard Glissant, something about people having a right to opacity — that being legible to others isn't automatically owed to anyone, no matter how much they want it. I don't know the original context, but it made me realize my actual verdicts about when people owe each other an account of themselves are all over the place. Here they are, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures them.

1. A friend cancels on a group dinner for the fourth time with just "can't make it, sorry" and no reason. I think that's not fine, but mostly because the rest of us kept planning around them, not because they owe us their private business.

2. A coworker quits and tells the team only "leaving for personal reasons," even though we'll be stuck covering their projects for months. I think that's fine, or at worst annoying, not a real violation.

3. A partner of a year ends things with "this isn't working" and won't say why. I think that's not fine — after a year, some kind of account is owed.

4. A politician runs on "trust me" and won't give policy specifics. Not fine; voters have to arrange their lives partly around what the candidate would actually do.

5. A close friend asks me "what's wrong?" when I'm visibly upset, and I say "nothing." I think I'm allowed to do that, even if it's a little unwise.

6. A professsor gives a C with no comments and won't meet. Not fine — the student can't act on the grade without knowing what the problem was.

7. A parent refuses to tell their 12-year-old why they're getting divorced, saying "you'll understand later." Not fine.

8. Someone close to me talks in heavy irony and ambiguity, and when I misread them says "that's just how I talk" and doesn't adjust. Not fine — being chronically illegible like that in an intimate relationship is a way of withholding, not just a style.

My provisional principle is something like: you owe others whatever legibility they need to update their expectations and plan around you, but you don't owe them your inner life on demand. But case 3 seems to break that, since ending the relationship already lets me update my expectations; and case 5 fits it only if I write the friend off as nosy, which feels wrong. Is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? State it precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop if any, and apply it to a case I keep hesitating on: an adult child asking an aging parent for their reasons for a long-ago decision that shaped the child's whole life.
