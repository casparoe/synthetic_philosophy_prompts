He lent me a well-thumbed introduction to pragmatism that summarises William James's essay 'The Will to Believe' (1896), his reply to Clifford, and I copied out the summary because it seemed to settle things:

James is attacking Clifford's rule that you should never believe anything unless you have proof. He argues that the rule is fine as scientific practice but is the wrong standard for religion, since religion is just the sort of question on which the evidence will never be conclusive. When the evidence can't settle a question, James thinks belief becomes a free choice: you're entitled to believe whatever you find most appealing. In fact, the real test of a belief is whether it works for the person who holds it, so a useful belief is a justified one, and all that ultimately matters is that your faith is sincere. That's why he says we have a right to believe, not because we have evidence, but because waiting for evidence is itself a decision to let the moment pass.
