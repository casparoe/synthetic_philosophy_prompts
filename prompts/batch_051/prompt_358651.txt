I am a retired natural scientist, and my working life was spent with the vocabulary of experimental practice: instruments, calibration, error budgets, propagation of uncertainty, reproducibility, and the long chain of trust that runs from a raw detector signal to a published result. I have recently been reading about the philosophical unease over computer-assisted proofs, such as the Appel–Haken proof of the four-colour theorem and the proof of the Kepler conjecture, where the checking is delegated to software and hardware and no single mathematician can survey the whole argument.

I would like you to take the main positions in this debate—roughly, that such results are nonetheless genuine proofs and mathematics has simply acquired a new instrument; that they are not proofs in the traditional sense but something closer to experimental or empirical results; and any intermediate position that wishes to preserve proof status while accepting that the verification is unsurveyable—and restate each position in the vocabulary of experimental science described above. For each position, tell me what translates cleanly, what can be rendered only with distortion, and what has no expression at all in that vocabulary. Then say whether the residue points to a real feature of mathematics that the experimental vocabulary cannot capture, or to an unclarity in the philosophical position itself. Please write your answer in British English.
