I've been going around in circles on a friend's argument about graded moral status and I can't find the exit. Every time I try to taboo "sentience" and cash it out in terms of valence or something computational, I land back in the same trap. Here's the argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Sentience and moral status come in degrees. There's no sharp line in nature where things suddenly start to matter: human, chimp, dog, mouse, fish, bee, and on down. And the gradient doesn't obviously stop at biology. A thermostat has a target state and acts to bring the room in line with it; a trained RL agent has a value function it's maximizing. If "having interests" just means having states of the world you act to bring about, then these are the same kind of thing as a bee's preferences, just much simpler.

(2) Adjacent points on this gradient are morally close. A bee isn't that much more of a subject then a fruit fly, and a fruit fly isn't that much more of a subject than a simple light-seeking robot. Whatever the right measure is — richness of experience, complexity of preference structure, amount of valence — each local step is small.

(3) Small local differences shouldn't compound into a large total difference. If A differs from B by epsilon, and B from C by epsilon, then A differs from C by at most 2 epsilon, and so on. So a bee and a thermostat, linked by finitely many epsilon-sized steps, must be morally close.

(4) Therefore either the thermostat has almost the moral weight of a bee, or the bee has almost the moral weight of a thermostat.

(5) But both of those are crazy. A thermostat has no inner life — nothing it is like to be a thermostat — so giving it even epsilon moral weight seems wrong. But I also don't want to say a bee is morally equivalent to a bimetallic strip.

(6) So something between (1) and (3) has to go: maybe moral status isn't graded all the way down (there's a threshold after all — subjective experience, actually felt valence, whatever), or epsilon-sized differences can add up to big ones after all, or step (1) is quietly equivocating between "preference" as felt valence and "preference" as mere goal-directed behavior.

I'd most like to keep (1), because I actually think moral weight should track the sophistication of whatever system we're looking at and that nature doesn't draw a magic line. But keeping (1) seems to force me to bite the bullet on thermostats, and I don't want to be the guy whose ethics implies my Nest has interests. So: where exactly does this argument go wrong, if it does, and what does rejecting each candidate step cost? Give me your confidence in the diagnosis and single out the move you're least sure about.
