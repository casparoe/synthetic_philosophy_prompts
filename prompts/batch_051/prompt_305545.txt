Here's a scenario I keep coming back to. In 2030 a mid-sized parliamentary democracy amends its constitution to require that every election to a single office — president, mayor, governor, party leader — be conducted by a strategy-proof voting rule: no voter can ever get a candidate they prefer elected by submitting a ballot that misrepresents their actual preferences, no matter how everyone else votes. The clause was pushed through by good-government idealists, its now more or less unamendable, and the constitutional court is packed with literalists who will genuinely strike down any election law that doesn't satisfy their reading of it.

You already see the trap: Gibbard–Satterthwaite says that with three or more candidates and unrestricted rankings, any onto, single-winner, strategy-proof rule is dictatorial. Take the theorem as a given for this exercise. So the country has just banned every non-dictatorial method for a race with more than two choices. I want your reasoned prediction of how this plays out over the next twenty years, not a story but a mechanism-by-mechanism argument. Which escape hatches do parties, legislatures, and courts settle into — collapsing all contests into two-candidate runoffs, letting nominations or primaries do the real choosing, redefining what counts as a sincere ballot until the clause is empty, switching to approval or score ballots and arguing the theorem doesn't cover them (does that actually save them?), or deliberately tolerating a low-level hint of dictatorship in formally strategy-proof rules? Which of these do you expect to dominate, and where are you confident versus where does the prediction hinge on assumptions about how literally the court reads the theorem, how creative party lawyers get, and how much voters actually care about sincerity? Say explicitly what would have to be true for your prediction to be wrong.
