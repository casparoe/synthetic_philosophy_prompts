I cover transitional justice for a science and ideas magazine, and after several years of sitting through amnesty hearings I have adopted a working rule: when a perpetrator apologizes, weeps, or speaks of remorse, I assign that expression no evidential weight in judging whether the person has genuinely changed, unless it is accompanied by something costly to the speaker over and above what the amnesty process itself already requires—naming accomplices who might retaliate, surrendering assets investigators have not yet found, or confessing to crimes the commission has not charged them with. My rationale is that an amnesty process gives every incentive to perform remorse and almost none to be sincere, so an uncostly display carries little information about the person's character. Last week an applicant described an execution in disturbing detail, met every legal condition for amnesty, and showed what looked like genuine distress; my editor wants the profile to describe him as a changed man, and I refused. Is my rule defensible as an epistemic policy, or is it cynicism protecting itself with methodology? Where does the rule fail, and how could someone in my position tell the difference from inside?
