I am currently taking a course on formal pragmatics, and we have just finished David Lewis's "Scorekeeping in a Language Game." I keep noticing a parallel that may be either illuminating or misleading. Lewis describes a rule of accommodation: if at a point in conversation an utterance requires a component of the conversational score to fall within a certain range, and the score is not already in that range, then, barring conflict, the score is adjusted at that point so that the utterance is acceptable. My other coursework is in motor neuroscience, and I was struck by how anticipatory postural adjustments work. Before a person makes a voluntary movement such as raising an arm, the central nervous system activates postural muscles to shift the body's center of mass, because the movement itself would otherwise create an unstable position. The motor system appears to adjust its own postural state so that the impending action does not violate a balance constraint, rather than treating the action as defective.

I would like a structural audit of this analogy before I put it in an essay. In particular: state the exact mapping between the components—conversational score to postural state, utterance constraint to balance constraint, accommodation to anticipatory adjustment—and say whether the match is genuine or loose. For the analogy to be deep, what would have to be true on both sides? What would a real transfer of results look like? For example, does the distinction between presuppositions that are easy to accommodate and those that provoke challenge have a motor analogue in the distinction between postural adjustments that are energetically cheap or contextually constrained? And where does the parallel break down? I can already see one possible disanalogy: in conversation, accommodation is normative and hearers can refuse to accommodate, whereas the motor adjustment is largely automatic and may fail only under pathology or extreme perturbation. I want you to say how damaging that disanalogy is, and also to identify something the analogy predicts that turns out false on one side.

Finally, be explicit about what would change your mind: which argument, empirical result, or theoretical consideration would lead you to a different verdict about whether the parallel is deep, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
