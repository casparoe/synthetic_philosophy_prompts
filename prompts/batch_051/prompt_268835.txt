In my computer science class we were taught a set of criteria for deciding whether a problem is computationally tractable. A problem counts as tractable if (a) there is a step-by-step procedure that solves every instance correctly, (b) the number of steps the procedure requires grows at worst polynomially with the size of the input, and (c) for inputs of ordinary size the procedure finishes within a reasonable amount of time using ordinary resources. Problems that fail these criteria are called intractable. Scheduling and assignment problems often belong to a class called NP-hard, which is treated as the standard marker of intractability: no one has found a polynomial-time procedure for any NP-hard problem, and finding one would give polynomial-time procedures for an enormous family of other hard search problems.

Now consider a case that seems to me to sit on the border. Every spring, the student government at my school assigns roughly four hundred students to eighteen elective workshops. The assignment must respect room capacities, time conflicts, and prerequisites, and it is meant to satisfy as many students' ranked preferences as possible. As a formal problem, this task is NP-hard: in the worst case, known exact methods may have to search through a number of possible assignments that grows exponentially with the number of students and workshops. Yet three members of the student government, using rules of their own devising — fill the most oversubscribed workshops first, give upperclassmen priority, run a second pass in which students may swap with one another — produce a workable assignment in one afternoon. It satisfies every hard constraint and gives most students one of their top choices, though no one can prove it is optimal.

Does the student government's scheduling problem qualify as tractable under the three criteria above? Go through the criteria one by one, distinguishing where necessary between the general problem of that form and the single annual instance, and mark the places where the criteria fail to give a clear verdict. Then say whether this case shows that the criteria themselves need to be revised — for example, by replacing the demand for an exact solution in the worst case with a demand for good-enough solutions on the kinds of cases that actually arise. Finally, address the philosophical implication: if the criteria do need revision, what becomes of the common claim that the human mind, like any finite machine, can only feasibly carry out tractable procedures?
