I've been trying to untangle an argument that came to me after reading W.K. Clifford's essay where he introduces mind-stuff (I think it's called On the Nature of Things-in-Themselves, but I don't have the text in front of me). As I remember it, he says a bit of inorganic matter doesn't have consciousness as a whole, but its ultimate parts each have a tiny share of feeling, and these little bits combine into unified minds in organisms. The argument I want checked is this:

(1) If Clifford's mind-stuff view is right, every fundamental physical unit has some tiny amount of experience.
(2) Composite physical things are just arrangements of fundamental units.
(3) So every composite physical thing has the combined experiences of its parts.
(4) Some of these tiny experiences are unpleasant; even a faint unpleasant experience is a kind of suffering.
(5) So if Clifford is right, ordinary material objects like rocks and water have at least some faint suffering in them, since they contain many tiny experiences.
(6) Therefore, Clifford's view implies there is vastly more suffering in the world than our ordinary moral thinking counts, including in inert matter.

I see the move from experience to suffering in (4) is doing a lot, but maybe it's fine: if the parts really do have feeling, then some of those feelings are bound to be bad. And (3) looks like composition, but Clifford's whole point is that mind-stuff combines. I want to know: is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and what is the strongest repair of that step? If the argument is valid, does the ethical conclusion in (6) actually follow from Clifford's view as I described it, or does it read something extra into his view?
