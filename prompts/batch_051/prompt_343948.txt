I'm an undergrad in a seminar on moral uncertainty, and I've been chewing on this asymmetry. Take a doctor who is factually uncertain whether a patient is allergic to a drug, or whether a drug will interact badly with another one. Everyone expects her to hedge: run the test, pick the option that's safe under every live possibility, or delay the decision. But if the same doctor is morally uncertain about whether a legally available treatment is permissible—say she has read the arguments on both sides and gives each moral view about 50% credence—allmost nobody tells her to hedge between the two moral views. The standard advice is to form her best all-things-considered moral judgment and act on that, wholeheartedly. So factual uncertainty gets treated as a reason for caution and compromise, while moral uncertainty gets treated as a reason to commit to your favored view.

I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher who works on normative uncertainty, and think carefully before you answer. Start by brainstorming the strongest candidate justifications for this asymmetry, then test each one against nearby cases, not just state them. My question: is there a genuine justification here, or is it just a bias we have because we think moral views are "personal" in a way factual beliefs aren't? If you defend the asymmetry, you have to face this less convenient case: an aid worker in an emergency is morally uncertain between a utilitarian way of allocating scarce food and a prioritarian one, and she knows that whichever view she lets win, someone will be seriously harmed if the other view is right. Are her moral uncertainties less reason-giving than her factual uncertainties would be, and if so, why? Finally, say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or dropped.
