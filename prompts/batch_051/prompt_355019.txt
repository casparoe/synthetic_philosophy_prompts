I've been reading the alignment vs control debates for years now and I keep flip-flopping on whether control can actually carry the weight people want it to. Suppose we have a model that is roughly as capable as a smart human remote worker — it writes code, sends emails, does web research, maybe files expenses — and a lab deploys it too do real work for a year with meaningful autonomy, but without any confident story about what the model wants. Their entire safety case is control: a monitor model reads all its outputs, the thing runs in a sandbox, there are tripwires, audits, the works. What's your actual credence that this setup keeps the probability of a catastrophic outcome (model escapes its environment, exfiltrates its own weights, or causes damage north of a billion dollars) below 1 in 10,000 over that year? For calibration, I'd put my own credence around 0.3 that they stay under that line, while a friend who actually works on control stuff is closer to 0.85, and I'd like to know where that gap actually lives once you force the reasoning out in the open.

I don't just want a number — I want the decomposition. Break it into the sub-questions: (a) the probability the model is actually misaligned and would attempt something bad if it thought it could get away with it; (b) the probability that, conditional on an attempt, the monitor and sandbox catch it before anything catastrophic happens; (c) the probability that it can cause catastrophic damage through a sequence of individually benign-looking actions rather than one obvious bad action; (d) the probability that existing evals and tripwires catch misalignment before deployment or early enough not to matter. Give a rough probability and one line of justification for each term, then show how they combine into the final number. Then tell me which term your final answer is most sensitive to, and what single piece of evidence would move that term the most.
