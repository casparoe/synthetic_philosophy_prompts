I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about moral agency, working from my gut on cases instead of theory. A person who deliberates and then does the thing: definitely an agent. A person with locked-in syndrome who can form judgments about what should be done but cannot move or speak: I still count them as an agent, just a reduced one. A corporate ethics committee that issues non-binding recommendations to a CEO, which the CEO sometimes follows and sometimes ignores: not an agent, even though the people on it are. An AI that advises a government and whose recommendations are in practice never overridden: this one gets me. Its just advice, but it reliably changes the world; I keep almost saying yes and then taking it back. A soldier who judges an order wrong but obeys it: an agent, but compromised. A robot with a minimize-harm rule that can only ever propose actions to a human operator and never carry them out: not an agent. A person who can't move but can compose and send messages through a brain implant: agent, though maybe only because that one channel exists.

Is there one principle behind this, or at least a plausible simple one that gets most of it? Which of my verdicts does it force me to give up, and why? Then apply it to the case that's really bugging me: if the CEO had a standing policy of always following that ethics committee, would the committee become an agent? My gut says still no, but then I need to know what, if anything, makes the always-followed AI different; if my gut is wrong, I need to know which earlier verdict broke first.
