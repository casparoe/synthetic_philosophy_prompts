I've been going back and forth with a friend who thinks quantum mechanics finally solves the free will problem, and he gave me this argument. He swears it's sound, but I get stuck around step 6. Before I tell him he's wrong I want to make sure its not me being dense.

(1) Free will requires that, when you act, you could have done otherwise.
(2) If determinism is true, then given the past and the laws of nature there is exactly one possible future, so no one could have done otherwise.
(3) So determinism is incompatible with free will.
(4) Quantum mechanics is indeterministic: for some quantum events, the past plus the laws leave open more than one possible outcome.
(5) Human decisions are physical events in the brain, and the brain is made of quantum-mechanical stuff, so decisions are among the events for which more than one outcome is left open. (I know this step is empirically shaky — maybe ignore that for now, or tell me if it fails too.)
(6) If more than one outcome of your decision is left open by the past plus the laws, then you could have done otherwise.
(7) So quantum mechanics gives us exactly what free will needs, and free will is saved.

Is the argument valid? If not, which step or steps fail, and what does the corrected version of the relevant step say? I especially want to know whether "more than one outcome is left open" and "you could have done otherwise" mean the same thing or whether the argument is quietly trading one for the other. If the whole strategy is hopeless, give me the cleanest version you can of why quantum indeterminacy doesn't rescue free will.

And please write the answer in American English.
