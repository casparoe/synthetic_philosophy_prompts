Reconstruct, as carefully and internally consistently as you can, the position David Hume would have taken on the following policy question, which postdates him: whether the law should treat orphaned or abandoned software as lapsing into a common pool, so that after a diligent search for the owner turns up no one able to exercise the rights, users may lawfully patch, archive, and redistribute the program. Proceed from Hume's commitments as you understand them: that justice, including the rules of property, is an artificial virtue instituted to remedy scarcity and limited generosity; that property rests on convention rather than on natural right; and that the rules of justice are to be assessed by their general tendency to public utility rather than by the merits of individual cases. On that basis, work out what he would likely say about a blanket lapse rule, a rule preserving the vanished owner's copyright indefinitely, and an intermediate mechanism such as a public registry or a source-code escrow with a fixed reversion term. Address two objections in his voice: first, the claim that a disappeared owner cannot be harmed by losing an exclusive right she is not exercising, and second, the worry that an abandonment rule would invite firms to disappear strategically in order to shed warranties or liabilities. Make sure the prediction respects the distinction between the value of the institution of property taken as a whole and the value of any particular enforcement or exception.
