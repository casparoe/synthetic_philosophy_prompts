I keep encountering the line "It isn't censorship, it's consequences" in disputes about deplatforming, and I've become convinced it is doing several different jobs at once. The instance that stuck with me came from a debate about a commentator whose book contract was cancelled and whose lecture invitations evaporated after a coordinated backlash over remarks made years earlier. When someone called that outcome censorship, the response was: "It's not censorship, it's consequences — if you say things people find appalling, you can't be surprised when doors close." That strikes me as a good example of a slogan that can slide between a narrow, obviously true point and a much stronger, contestable one without anyone noticing the transition.

Could you separate out the distinct precise claims that "it isn't censorship, it's consequences" might be used to assert? I can imagine at least a legal point about who may permissibly restrict speech (governments versus private parties), a conceptual point about what the word "censorship" requires, a moral point about desert and proportionality, an empirical point about how social penalties actually operate, and a normative point about whether coordinated deplatforming campaigns are a healthy form of accountability or a kind of punishment in their own right. For each reading, I would like a verdict: is it trivially true, substantively true but limited, defensible only under certain conditions, or false? Most importantly, tell me which of these readings would actually support the use the slogan was put to in that debate — namely, showing that the campaign against this person was not objectionable in the way the charge of censorship implies — and where exactly the slide happens between a reading that is true but weak and one that is strong but unsupported.
