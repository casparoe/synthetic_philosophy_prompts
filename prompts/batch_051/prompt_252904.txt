Two people in my political theory seminar got into a long argument after a guest lecture on voting systems, and afterward each of them asked me to say who had won. I have reconstructed the main moves of their exchange as faithfully as I can remember.

Lin began: "The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem is an impossibility result for ranked voting. Take any rule that is defined for every possible profile of complete strict rankings over at least three candidates, that picks a single winner, and that can in principle elect any candidate. If the rule is non-dictatorial, then the theorem says there will be some profile of true preferences on which a voter does better by submitting something other than her true ranking. That means ranked methods are unavoidably manipulable. Approval voting sidesteps this because it never asks for a ranking: each voter simply marks every candidate she finds acceptable, and the candidate with the most approvals wins. There is no ranking to misreport, so the theorem's premise never even gets a grip."

Priya answered: "All you have shown is that approval voting falls outside the letter of the theorem, not that it avoids strategic voting. Suppose my true ordering is A above B above C. The approval ballot does not let me say that. I have to decide whether to approve only A or also B. If I judge that A cannot win and C might, I will approve B too, even though my ballot then makes A and B look equally acceptable. That decision is strategic, not sincere. You have moved the manipulation into the threshold."

Lin replied: "Only if preferences are not dichotomous. Suppose a voter genuinely has a binary evaluation: each candidate is either acceptable or not, and any acceptable winner is just as good for her as any other acceptable winner, while every unacceptable winner is just as bad. Then approving exactly the acceptable candidates is weakly dominant—whatever everyone else does, she cannot get a better outcome by adding or dropping a candidate. On that domain, approval voting really is strategy-proof. And the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem is silent, because it assumes the unrestricted domain of strict rankings."

Priya came back: "That is silent because you have deliberately shrunk the domain small enough that the theorem has nothing left to say. Real voters choosing among three candidates do not have binary values; they have orderings and intensity. Forcing them to translate that into a yes-or-no judgment is where the strategic act hides. And domain restrictions can always dodge a theorem—if there is only one possible preference profile, no rule is manipulable, but that is not a useful fact. So your defense protects a toy version of approval voting, not the method anyone is actually proposing for elections."

Priya then concluded: "The deeper lesson of the theorem is not about the shape of the ballot. It is about what happens when you try to aggregate individuals into a single collective winner while retaining honest expression of preference information. Approval voting discards most of that information before anyone votes. Discarding information is not an escape; it is a capitulation. A voter who uses a cutoff to treat A and B as equally acceptable even though she prefers A is no more sincere than a ranked voter who buries B. So the theorem's core problem survives any change of ballot format."

The argument ended there because we all had to leave for another seminar. I genuinely cannot tell where the valid points are in this exchange; they seemed to be talking past each other at times, but each of them also made remarks that struck me as clearly right.
