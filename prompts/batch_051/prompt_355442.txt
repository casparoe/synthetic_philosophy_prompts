At a conference last spring, two colleagues of mine argued about whether “democratic backsliding” is a respectable diagnostic concept or a partisan label. I was asked to referee and could not find an obvious answer; the exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) The first said the term presupposes one canonical trajectory for democracy and is applied so selectively that it functions chiefly to recast substantive policy disagreement as a defense of democracy.
(2) The second replied that backsliding is tracked by institutional indicators—judicial independence, electoral integrity, media pluralism—and that selective or hypocritical application is abuse of a concept, not an argument against the concept itself.
(3) The first answered that the indicators are not neutral: a government that weakens a constitutional court to enact its program is called a backslider, while a government whose program the same court blocks is being defended from illiberalism—so the label tracks the observer’s politics rather than any neutral benchmark.
(4) The second said that objection targets the application of thresholds, not their existence; a concept can have contested boundaries and still detect real differences in whether power can be contested.
(5) The first responded that here the contested cases are the paradigm cases, not the margins, and that the baseline against which “backsliding” is measured is itself set by the analyst: liberalizing changes count as “deepening democracy,” while reversal of that liberalization counts as “backsliding,” so the checklist already smuggles in the substantive verdict.
(6) The second said every normatively loaded concept—health, negligence, cruelty—has contested boundaries that get weaponized, but that does not make the concept merely expressive; the test is whether it predicts and explains, and institutional decay has distinctive mechanisms and consequences.

They gave up and asked me to settle it. Please go through the exchange move by move. For each numbered move, state whether you regard it as clearly right, plausible but partial, or clearly mistaken, with a brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify the strongest consideration available to each side that neither articulated.
