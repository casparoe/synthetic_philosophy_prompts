I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and every few weeks I'm drafting or editing a story in which the concepts of determinism, chaos, unpredictability, and randomness get blurred. A climate scientist tells me a weather system is "chaotic and therefore unpredictable." A neuroscientist tells me a subject's decision was "already determined" seconds before the subject reported making it. A tech company tells me its quantum device generates "truly random" numbers rather than merely hard-to-predict ones. These remarks then get compressed into headlines about free will and the limits of science, and I worry we are printing claims stronger than the work supports. Please write me a reusable editorial procedure, grounded in the philosophy of physics and the philosophy of science, that I or a nonexpert editor can run in ten minutes before filing. It should decide when a system or event may be called deterministic, indeterministic, unpredictable, or random, and it should distinguish practical unpredictability from unpredictability in principle and from the absence of a determinate outcome. Lay out the procedure as a checklist or decision tree with the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it must stop and refer to an expert, and which steps are judgment calls rather than rules. Then run the procedure on the three cases above, stating for each what it licenses us to print and what it forbids, including at least one case where the procedure gives an answer an editor will not like. Finally, say what the procedure cannot settle, such as which interpretation of quantum mechanics is correct.
