I'm trying to get clear on what my gut verdicts about unbounded gambles actually commit me to. Here are the cases I keep returning to.

1. Standard St. Petersburg: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, and I receive 2^n dollars if the first heads is on toss n. I would not pay more than about $25, even though the expected monetary value is infinite. I want to say the expected value calculation is not a decision procedure for gambles with unbounded upper tails.

2. St. Petersburg truncated at a known finite toss limit k: if no heads occurs within k tosses, the game ends. I would pay the finite expected value, and my willingness to pay tracks that value as k increases. The infinite limit feels different in kind from any finite truncation.

3. Pasadena game: same coin process, but if the first heads is on toss n, the payoff is (-1)^(n-1) times 2^n/n. The positive and negative contributions to the expectation each diverge, so the expectation is undefined. But the terms in their natural order sum to ln 2. My gut says this gamble is fundamentally different from St. Petersburg: I would not pay to play it, and I would not accept any positive payment to take the other side, because the natural-order sum seems like an artifact of how I happened to sequence the outcomes.

4. Pasadena truncated at a known finite toss limit k. The expected value exists and approaches ln 2 as k grows. I would pay the finite expected value for each specified k. The limit of these finite truncations does not convince me that the infinite Pasadena game has a well-defined price.

5. Re-described Pasadena: I keep the same gamble but enumerate its possible outcomes in a different order when writing the expected-value series. The series can now be made to sum to any real number or to diverge. I feel that the underlying gamble is unchanged, which reinforces my view that the infinite Pasadena game has no expectation and is not priceable.

What principle about risky choice, if any, captures all of these verdicts? More specifically, can I coherently hold that St. Petersburg has an infinite expectation but a finite reservation price, that Pasadena has no expectation at all, and that finite truncations are fully well-behaved? If some verdict has to go, which should it be, and what does the resulting view say about a gamble whose expected-value series is conditionally convergent but sums to zero in its natural order?
