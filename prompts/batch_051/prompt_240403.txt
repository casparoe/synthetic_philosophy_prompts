So I was talking with a friend in law school about blackmail, and neither of us could get past the basic puzzle. If I happen to learn some true, embarrassing fact about you, I'm legally in the clear to publish it (true speech), I'm legally in the clear to ask you for money, and I'm legally in the clear to just keep my mouth shut and give you nothing. But the second I say "pay me or I'll publish it," it's criminal. Each building block is legal and the combination is a crime. I know the law has all these labels like "wrongful coercion" or "obtaining property by extortion," but to me thats just naming the thing, not telling me what the wrong actually consists in.

What I'd like is for you to treat the blackmail prohibition as a practice and reverse-engineer what theory of the wrong it presupposes. The part that confuses me most is that structurally similar threats are perfectly legal: a prosecutor can tell a defendant "plead guilty or face much worse charges," a patent holder can tell a small firm "pay a license or we sue," an employee can tell her boss "match this offer or I walk and take my clients." So if blackmail is just "a threat to do something lawful unless paid," why do those not count? That suggests the law is implicitly committed to something fairly specific about what is being transacted when a blackmailer sells silence—about secrecy, about information as leverage, about turning a right you happen to have into a marketable asset. I want you to say what that presupposed theory is, which rival accounts (e.g., economic/libertarian, commodification, privacy-based) it rules in or out, where the practice is genuinely underdetermined so multiple theories would license the same statutes, and what would actually change in the law if you swapped in one of the rival accounts. And less of the "it's bad and the law knows it" stuff, more on the exact commitments the legal line between blackmail and plea bargains quietly draws.
