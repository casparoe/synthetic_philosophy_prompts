I've been arguing with someone about the absurdity doctrine — the courts' power to refuse to apply a statute's plain language when the literal result would be absurd — and they proposed the following substitute for judicial discretion. Before a court can depart from the literal wording of a provision, a panel of twelve citizens drawn from the jury roll is shown the provision and told what the case at hand would require; the court may ignore the plain text only if at least ten of the twelve say applying it literally would produce an absurd result. The panel's verdict is published and can be cited only as to that specific provision, never as general precedent. Walk me through how this would actually behave: which cases get litigated or settled differently, how each side tries to game the panel, what judges do in the run of small cases where convening a panel isn't worth it, and whether the rule ends up relocating judicial discretion to twelve people with no training in the canons of construction rather than cabining it. Where does it fail first, and is there a fix that doesn't just hand the discretion back to judges?
