Consider a letter-string analogy microdomain of the kind studied in the Copycat modeling tradition. A problem has the form "source-string -> target-string; probe-string -> ?" For example, "abc -> abd; ijk -> ?" is naturally solved as "ijl": the transformation replaces the rightmost letter by its alphabetic successor, and applied to "ijk" that same transformation replaces "k" by "l."

Structural roles matter throughout. The microdomain includes sameness groups (e.g., the doubled kk in iijjkk), positional roles (leftmost, middle, rightmost), and relational roles (successor, predecessor, first, last). The interesting cases are those in which a literal transfer of the source transformation is blocked or pressured, so something must slip. Two stock examples:

- "abc -> abd; iijjkk -> ?" admits at least "iijjll" (the rightmost group, kk, is replaced by its successor group, ll) and "iijjkl" (only the rightmost letter, k, is replaced by its successor, l).
- "abc -> abd; xyz -> ?" admits at least "wyz" (when z has no successor, the roles "rightmost" and "successor" slip to "leftmost" and "predecessor") and "xya" (if the alphabet is treated cyclically).

Suppose two researchers disagree about what this microdomain shows about concepts. The fluid-concepts researcher holds that the graded, context-sensitive competition among interpretations in this microdomain is the visible signature of conceptual slippage: concepts such as letter, successor, and group are not fixed atoms but dynamically constructed, partly overlapping structures, and a system that solves the domain fluently must represent concepts that way. The symbolic-structures researcher holds that the same behavior can in principle be produced by a classical architecture with explicit structural descriptions, a space of discrete transformation rules, and stochastic competition among hypotheses under evaluation heuristics; on this view "conceptual slippage" is at most a convenient abbreviation for a probability distribution over discrete interpretations, and positing irreducibly fluid concepts adds no explanatory or computational content.

Draft the joint statement these two researchers could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The deliverable is not a verdict but a constructive document: claims both sides accept, residual disagreements made exact but not slanted, and cruxes stated so that settling a crux would move at least one side. Use the following sections, in this order:

1. Agreed claims (roughly 500–700 words). A numbered list of claims both researchers will sign. Cover: what the microdomain demands of any adequate model; what the ambiguity examples establish and do not establish; what counts as data versus interpretation; and any methodological constraints both accept on appeals to human performance.

2. Disagreement table (roughly 4–6 rows). A table with columns: Issue; Fluid-concepts wording; Symbolic-structures wording; Verbal or substantive? Each camp's wording must be one its own side would accept as a precise statement of its view, not a caricature by the other side. Keep the issues specific to this microdomain rather than to concepts in general.

3. Cruxes and pre-registered tests (roughly 250–350 words per crux; two or three cruxes). For each crux: state the sub-question such that a settled answer would move at least one researcher; give a concrete problem or experiment in the microdomain both agree bears on it; and record in advance what each researcher predicts for that problem or experiment, including what pattern of behavior each would count as evidence against their own view.

4. Where the disagreement stands after the collaboration (roughly 200–300 words). Say which disagreements the drafting process revealed to be verbal, which remain substantive, and what, if anything, each side conceded.
