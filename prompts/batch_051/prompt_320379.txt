I have a standing rule for deciding what counts as "what careful reasoning would conclude" about a messy, underdetermined question: I refuse to defer to an idealization unless I can spell it out as a concrete process I could in principle run, and the only idealization I currently allow is iterated amplification / HCH and the factored-cognition picture behind it — a base human with a large time budget, who can decompose each question into subquestions, delegate those to other instances of the same setup, and reassemble the results, with each level of oversight only modestly more capable than the one below it. I deliberately exclude cleaner idealizations, such as a perfect Bayesian with unlimited compute or a fully informed utility-maximizing singleton, on the grounds that they are black boxes that smuggle in a conclusion rather than showing me a deliberation that could actually reach it. I apply this rule when deciding whether to update on arguments I cannot fully check, mostly about high-stakes questions like alignment strategy, long-run priorities, or what future minds might be like. Lately I've begun to suspect that HCH does the same kind of smuggling in the opposite direction: it anchors "ideal deliberation" to the decomposition habits, blind spots, and incentive structures of present-day humans, so my rule may just be a bias toward an idealization that looks reassuringly like me. Is this policy defensible, and can it be restated so as to keep whatever it gets right while avoiding that failure mode? Or am I protecting my current beliefs by only trusting a process I have rigged to reproduce them?
