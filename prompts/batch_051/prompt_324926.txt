I've spent a fair amount of my free time brooding over the fact that, in practice, the companies whose apps and devices I use own the records of my behavior — where I go, what I click, what I buy, whom I message — while I have almost no say over any of it. I tried to imagine the opposite arrangement, and found I genuinely couldn't tell whether it would be liberating or unworkable, so I want to hear someone reason it through in detail.

Here is the scenario. Suppose a wealthy, mid-sized democracy — the Netherlands, for concreteness — enacts a statute effective January 1, 2030, declaring that any recorded information that can be linked to an identifiable person is that person's property, carrying the full ordinary bundle of rights: to exclude others from using it, to license it for a fee or for free, to sell it outright, and to pass it on by inheritance. Processing a person's data without a license becomes a civil wrong treated like trespass to property, with statutory damages; existing data holdings are not grandfathered in, so companies must obtain a license within two years or delete the data. Narrow exceptions cover emergencies and ordinary journalism, but not advertising, credit scoring, insurance pricing, or research conducted without consent.

Over the following twenty-five years, how does this play out? I want a chain of reasoning, not a story or a list of predictions. Walk me through the mechanisms: who adapts by building licensing platforms or data brokers that act for individuals, which industries shrink or exit, what happens to free ad-supported services, how ordinary people actually behave once their data is a tradeable asset (do they sell it for pennies, hold out, or ignore it?), and how courts and regulators cope as the definition of personal data collides with inferences, aggregate statistics, and observations made in public. For each link in the chain, say how confident you are, which effects seem robust, which are guesses, and what hidden assumption would most change the answer if it turned out false.
