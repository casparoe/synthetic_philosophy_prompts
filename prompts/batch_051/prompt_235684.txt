I'm preparing a proposal for a 6,000-word term paper in a graduate seminar on population ethics, and I want to work on the repugnant conclusion without re-running the familiar arguments about whether it should be accepted. My half-formed idea is this. The standard presentations of the repugnant conclusion are deliberately sparse: they compare two populations described only by their size and by a single welfare level per life—one population of excellent lives, another much larger population of lives barely worth living—and they leave everything else about those lives unspecified. I suspect that part of the intuitive repugnance comes from how readers silently fill in that blank, imagining the barely-worth-living lives as monotonously similar, socially disconnected, and not ones the people living them would affirm. The project would ask whether the usual intuitive resistance to the repugnant conclusion survives once those unstated features are made explicit and varied one at a time, while holding the population size and stipulated welfare levels fixed.

Turn this into a proposal that does the following: (1) states the question precisely and in a way that has a determinate answer; (2) explains why existing discussions leave it open—as I see it, they attack or defend the underlying principle, not the presentation of the case; (3) lays out a method, including the concrete set of case variants I would construct and compare; (4) says what the possible findings are, which one I should expect and why, and what would count as the project failing; and (5) gives a realistic assessment of what would make it too big for one semester and what would keep it tractable. Commit to the most defensible version of the hypothesis and argue for it, rather than surveying options and leaving me to choose. Don't include citations or a bibliography; I'll add references myself. If the proposal leans on a contested reading of the repugnant conclusion, flag that in a brief closing note rather than assuming it.
