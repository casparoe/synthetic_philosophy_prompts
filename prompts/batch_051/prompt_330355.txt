I am an undergraduate taking a course this term on the social construction of kinds — we have covered race, gender, and money — and I keep noticing a pattern in the cases we read that I do not yet have a name for.

Consider a classificatory term introduced by some institution to pick out a kind of person: a psychiatric diagnosis for a certain pattern of disruptive behavior in children, for instance, or a legal status such as refugee. At first the term is treated as straightforwardly descriptive; it is supposed to name a population that exists independently of the label. But once the label begins to circulate, it becomes available to the people classified and to the institutions around them. Parents learn to describe their children in the vocabulary of the diagnosis; schools build procedures for identifying and accommodating such children; and the children themselves start to understand their difficulties through the category. The people classified begin to adjust their behavior and their self-presentation to the category as they understand it. After some years, the professionals who use the term notice that the people now being brought under it no longer quite match the original case descriptions, and they revise the criteria. The revised criteria pick out a somewhat different population, whose members in turn respond to the label, and the category moves again.

The same shape appears with administrative and legal categories: people discover what a status such as refugee officially includes, and they organize their testimony and their lives around that definition — often not insincerely, but because the category supplies a structure within which experience can be articulated — while courts and agencies revise the definition in response to how it is being used, which in turn changes who can be recognized under it.

I am not sure what to call this. It is not just that social categories are constructed or that classification is interest-laden; it is a particular two-way process in which the kind and the people classified are modified together over time. I can see several nearby concepts — self-fulfilling prophecy, labeling theory, stigma, performativity, nominalism about kinds — but each seems to capture only part of the process, and I do not want to misuse a term that belongs to a different debate.

Does this configuration have an established name in the philosophy of social kinds? If it does, what exactly is the mechanism supposed to be, and how is it distinguished from each of those neighboring concepts? If it does not, or if it fits an existing concept only imperfectly, what would you call it, and where would you draw its edges — for instance, does the process require that the classified people be aware of the label, or can institutions alone do the responding, and are there classifications of people that are causally insulated from it and so should not be counted?
