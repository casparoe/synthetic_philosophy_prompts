Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether a large open-source software foundation’s conduct-enforcement system should count as a legal order in Pascal’s sense. My friend kept insisting that the foundation is a better example of Pascal’s point than any state, because it did not start by asking what was just and then trying to make it powerful; it started with power over code, access, and reputation, and then built courts, rules, appeals, and sanctions on top of it. I resisted, but I realized we had never written down exactly what we took Pascal’s criteria to be. By the end we agreed on this reconstruction from the Pensées: a coercive order is legitimate only if it unites force with justice; justice without force is powerless because people will ignore it; force without justice is mere tyranny; and because what is just cannot reliably be made strong, the only stable arrangement is to make what is already strong just — to clothe established force in the forms of justice.

Here is the case we disputed. A large foundation controls an open-source project with thousands of contributors. It owns the trademarks, controls the main repository and release infrastructure, and employs several core maintainers. Its code of conduct is enforced by a committee with a written code, an escalating ladder of sanctions (warning, suspension, removal of commit access, permanent ban from all foundation spaces), and a right of appeal to a separate review body. The committee can enforce its decisions because exclusion from the project can end a person’s ability to work on the code they depend on and can damage a professional reputation built in the community. But participation is voluntary: anyone can fork the code, leave, and start a parallel project. My friend says this is exactly Pascal’s fusion made visible — force first, then justice — and that it therefore meets the criteria. I think it is closer to force wearing a justice costume, since the rules themselves are changed through informal negotiation among the powerful, and the appeal body is not really independent. I would like you to settle which of us is right. Go through the criteria one by one; where the answer is no or uncertain, say whether the problem lies in Pascal’s criteria or in the case; and end by saying whether this case shows the formula needs amendment. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning with no preamble.
