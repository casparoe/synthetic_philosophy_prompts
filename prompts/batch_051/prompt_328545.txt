I direct the review office in a county health and human services department that evaluates proposed population-level interventions from contractors, hospital systems, and behavioral-design firms: automatic enrollment in benefit programs, loss-framed screening notices, appointment reminders using social-norm comparisons, gamified self-management apps, emotionally charged public-health campaigns, and similar material. Our current standard is that an intervention is acceptable unless it is outright deceptive or coercive, and everyone involved knows this misses most of the ethically difficult territory. I am asking you to write a reusable decision procedure my staff can run to determine whether a proposed influence is legitimate persuasion or has crossed into manipulation. The staff have no philosophical training, and the determination has to be reached in a single ninety-minute meeting and turned into a one-page recommendation for the director who signs off. The procedure must be genuinely operational: it should specify the questions to ask and in what order, what counts as a minimally sufficient answer to each, where the review stops with a no, where a yes merely moves the review forward, and which steps are judgment calls no procedure can eliminate. It should also encode, rather than paper over, the main philosophical divergence in this area: whether what makes an influence manipulative lies in the method itself (hidden influence, bypassing the target's rational faculties, emotions that do not track the merits), in the manipulator's intent or stance toward the target (exploiting a vulnerability, treating the person as a means, a specific kind of disrespect for autonomy), or in something about the relationship between the parties. Where the underlying accounts genuinely fork, the procedure should tell my staff how to proceed in light of that fork.

I especially need the procedure to address two gray zones. The first is emotional appeal—fear, shame, hope, guilt, identification—that is neither false nor deceptive. The second is default architecture and similar design that works through inertia, salience, or effort rather than through convincing anyone. For each, the procedure should state the exact feature, if any, that separates a permissible use from a manipulative one, and it should be candid about the cases it cannot classify.

Then apply the finished procedure to three proposals: (1) a retirement-savings plan that enrolls employees at a 6% contribution rate unless they return an opt-out form within ninety days; (2) a hospital's pre-surgical campaign using individualized loss-framed letters and a graphic image, along the lines of "in the last year, patients with your risk profile who delayed this screening experienced X"; and (3) a medication-adherence app that awards points and streaks and shows users a leaderboard with the names of other users in their postal code. Include at least one application in which the procedure returns a verdict my team—generally sympathetic to behavioral nudges—will not like, and explain why the procedure forces that verdict anyway. End by listing the kinds of proposals the procedure cannot settle, where the office should instead convene a values discussion among the people the intervention would affect.
