I am a novelist working on a book about how ordinary people negotiate the built world, and I keep noticing an asymmetry in my characters’ judgments. A park bridge with a low clearance that keeps out city buses is quickly described as political—even as hostile—while a sidewalk too narrow to accommodate a wheelchair, or a public square with no benches at all, is usually treated as an inconvenience or an aesthetic fact, not as a political decision. Yet all three distribute access and comfort in predictable ways. I want to know whether anything justifies this asymmetry, or whether my characters, and my readers, are simply more willing to see politics in an artifact that carries an identifiable designer’s signature than in one that does its work through absence or default.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating of the question. In your answer, test the strongest principle you can find for the asymmetry against at least one case where the same exclusionary outcome is produced once by an added feature and once by a missing one—for instance, a hostile armrest on a bench versus no bench at all—and say whether the ordinary judgments should be kept, reversed, or given up.
