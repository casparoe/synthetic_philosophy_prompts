I have no academic background, but for a long time I have been thinking on my own about the arguments people have over whether one historical atrocity can be said to be worse than another. One side says that such comparisons are plainly possible: you look at the number of people killed, the depth and duration of the suffering, the intent behind it, and the damage left after, and you can rank; in fact, there are situations in which refusing to make such a comparison is itself an evasion. The other side says that the attempt to rank is not just difficult but obscene, because it tells the victims of the event that ends up lower that their loss was bounded, and because no single scale can capture what made each of those events evil in the particular way it was.

I would like you to write the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two positions. Please first write a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table. The agreed claims should be substantive rather than merely procedural: facts about what comparisons require, what both sides refuse to say, what both sides worry about when a comparison becomes public, and where each side grants the other something. The disagreements table should state each remaining disagreement in wording that its own side would accept, not in the opponent's caricature of it.

I have a half-formed suspicion, and I want you to test it in the document. My suspicion is that the two sides are mostly right about different acts: one side is right about the arithmetic of comparing, and the other is right about the damage that public rankings do, so the real fight is over whether a comparison can be made without being used. I would like the document to say, in words both sides accept, whether this suspicion dissolves any of the original argument and, if it does not, where the genuine residue is.

Please identify three or four cruxes — sub-questions such that answering one of them would make at least one side change its position — and for each one, name a concrete case or piece of evidence that both sides would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction about it in advance. The cruxes I suspect are worth testing are: whether there is any single measure, or a small set of measures, that actually tracks what people mean when they say one atrocity was worse than another; whether a public ranking adds a new wrong that the underlying arithmetic does not contain, and whether that wrong can be removed by framing, by adding caveats, or by keeping comparisons private; whether the refusal to compare is itself a moral failing, and if so, whether it can be made up for by comparing on one axis without declaring a final overall rank; and whether the views of survivors settle these questions, and what happens when survivor communities themselves disagree.

For these cruxes, use at least the following cases, though you may add others: the common comparison between the Atlantic slave trade and the Holocaust; a public memorial or school curriculum that arranges atrocities by the number of victims; a truth commission that must decide which of two historical campaigns to investigate first with limited resources; and the observable fact that some survivors compare their own suffering with that of others while some refuse. For each case, I want each side's prediction recorded in wording that side would stand behind.

Do not include citations, footnotes, or a reading list, and do not quote from any books; the document should stand entirely on its own reasoning. If you end up judging that part of the dispute is only about words, say exactly which part and why, and put the remainder in a form both sides can sign without embarrassment.
