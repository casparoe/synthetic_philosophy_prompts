I am taking an upper-division ethics course that has just finished a unit on deontological constraints, and in seminar a classmate presented an argument he thinks shows that agent-centered constraints are not merely counterintuitive but internally incoherent. I have reproduced his argument below, with the steps numbered as he gave them.

(1) A deontological constraint forbids an agent from performing an act of a certain type even when performing one such act is the only way to prevent several other agents from performing acts of that same type. For example, an agent may not kill one innocent person even if that is the only way to stop someone else from killing five.

(2) The only plausible ground of such a constraint is the badness of the act type it prohibits—in the example, the badness of an innocent person's being killed.

(3) If the badness of an act type grounds a prohibition against performing acts of that type, then an agent has decisive reason to rank states of affairs with fewer instances of that act type above states of affairs with more instances, other things being equal.

(4) So, in the example, the constraint requires the agent to choose the course of action whose foreseeable result is a state of affairs in which five innocent people are killed, even though the alternative would contain only one such killing.

(5) A norm that requires an agent to choose the state of affairs it ranks as worse, by the very evaluative ranking that is supposed to justify the norm, is practically incoherent.

(6) Therefore the constraint in the example is incoherent, and since nothing in the argument turns on the particular act type or on the numbers, every deontological constraint is incoherent.

I find the conclusion unacceptable, but I do not want to reject it merely because it is revisionary. My question is: which premise or inferential step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I would prefer to keep the constraint itself rather than the ranking principle in premise (3), but I want to know whether that preference can be made coherent and what else it forces me to give up. I would like a diagnosis of the argument that names the step, explains why it looked compelling, and lays out what the main ways of resisting the argument each have to sacrifice.
