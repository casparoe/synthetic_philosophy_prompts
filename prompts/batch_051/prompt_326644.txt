I spent my career in a lab and only got into philosophy after retiring, so when my reading group asked me to write up my "decision profile" I figured it would be straightforward. It wasn't. I've landed somewhere in the expected-value/fanaticism territory, and I want to know whether the thing actualy hangs together before I present it. Here's where I am:

(1) I'm an expected utility maximizer. Rank actions by expected value using your best credences and your best utilities, and pick the top. No extra rules.

(2) The value of saving lives is linear. Saving 2n people is exactly twice as good as saving n people. I don't accept diminishing returns over distinct lives: each added life counts as much as the last.

(3) I reject fanaticism. Pascal's mugging is a reductio, not a deal. A one-in-a-billion chance of saving some astronomically huge number of lives should not swamp the certainty of saving, say, a hundred lives.

(4) My main safety valve is bounded utility. I think the utility function has to level off at some point, so no outcome can have infinite or arbitrarily high value. This is also how I keep the St. Petersburg paradox from undermining my confidence in expected utility.

(5) I also think ordinary risk-averse verdicts are mostly a mistake. If you genuinely face a 20% chance of saving ten lives versus a certainty of saving one life, the expected-value answer (two lives) is exactly right, and the queasy preference for the certain one life is just probability distortion.

(6) That said, in practice I'd still turn down the mugger, but for epistemic reasons: at some point my credences like "this stranger can deliver an astronomically huge number of lives" are so flimsy and model-dependent that I shouldn't cash them out literally.

What I'd like is a consistency audit, not a lecture. Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and rank the real tensions by severity, each with the cheapest fix. I genuinely can't tell whether the problem is in (2), in (4), or somewhere subtler about when expected value is supposed to apply.
