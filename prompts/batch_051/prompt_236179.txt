I'm a philosophy of technology PhD student trying to turn a half-baked idea into the core of a paper, and I'd like comments on the argument rather than references or framing suggestions. The paper is about a slide I keep seeing in the Winner-style discussion of whether artifacts have politics (the old Langdon Winner piece and everything that cites it). Here's the claim in note form:

- Winner's examples actually do two different argumentative jobs, and people quietly slide from one to the other.
- The low overpasses on Long Island — the story where Robert Moses supposedly had the clearances set so buses couldn't get out to the beaches — shows that a design parameter can carry a discriminatory decision: the racism is in the concrete, so to speak. But that politics is still relative to a whole context of buses, road networks, who drives, who rides, where people are trying to go. Change that context enough and the same bridge height stops doing that political work.
- The other claim that sometimes rides in on this example is stronger: that some technologies just are or require a certain kind of politics, full stop, regardless of context. That's the inherent-politics reading.
- My suspicion is that the first claim is true and important, the second is usually overclaimed, and the literature drifts between them because the bridge example makes the weak claim vivid and then lets the strong claim borrow it's force.

So the paper would defend a split: artifacts are non-neutral because they sediment decisions and change the space of live options (the weak claim, which I think is defensible), but we shouldn't say a given artifact is inherently authoritarian or democratic (the strong claim, which I think mostly isn't). I'm using sediment deliberately because I want it to sound geological: layers of decisions build up in the thing, and you can dig for them but you can't read them off the artifact alone.

Where I'm stuck, and what I'd like comments on:
1. Is the distinction between the two claims doing real work, or am I just relabeling the familiar distinction between artifacts that order social life and artifacts that are supposed to be intrinsically political?
2. The blurring worry: the context I'm allowed to vary in order to dissolve a politics is itself full of artifacts. If contexts are artifact-saturated, does the weak/strong split collapse?
3. What would a smart defender of the strong reading say about the Long Island case specifically — is there a version of it that gets them further than I'm giving it credit for?
4. Is there a better word than sediment? It risks making the politics sound passive and finished, which is maybe not what I want.

Also, when you comment, say how confident you are in each claim you make and flag the one point you're least sure of.
