Ok so here's the thing I'm working on. I'm collecting the cases that make trouble for the simple view that gratitude is just the fitting response to someone's benevolently intended benefit, where the central case is a friend going out of their way to help you. So far I've got:

- The botched favor: someone genuinely tries to help, even sacrifices real time or money, but the help never lands. People still want to say some gratitude is due, even though there's no benefit.
- The accidental benefit: someone improves your life purely as a side effect of their own plans, without ever intending to. You might be glad, but calling it gratitude feels off.
- The mixed-motive benefactor: the help is real and partly out of genuine concern, but partly self-interested or aimed at putting you in their debt. Does the gratitude get diluted, or do the good motives do all the work?
- The meddler: they intend to help and by their lights it works, but you never wanted the help and it makes your life worse. Is gratitude still owed?
- The dead benefactor: you learn years later that someone quietly paid for something big in your past, and they're dead now. Gratitude feels apt, but there's nobody left to thank or repay.

I've got this nagging sense I'm forgetting one more standard kind of case, one that doesn't just amount to turning a dial on the benefactor's motives or on whether the benefit actually reached me. Tell me the one I'm missing and what it pushes a theory of gratitude to say that the cases on my list don't. By a new case I mean one with a different structure, not just a rewording or a combination of two of mine.
