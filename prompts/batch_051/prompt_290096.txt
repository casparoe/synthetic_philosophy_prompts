Critical culture treats two kinds of dramatic invention about real dead people very differently. If a biopic or novel gives a historical figure a fictional act of kindness, a nobler motive, or a late change of heart, it is usually accepted as harmless dramatic license, sometimes even praised as a generous rounding-out of the person. If the same work instead gives that figure a fictional act of cruelty, treachery, or bigotry, it is often condemned as unfair to the dead—as a wrong done to someone who cannot answer back. But both involve fabricating facts about a person who actually lived; only the valence of the fabrication differs. Is there a principle that justifies treating flattering falsehoods about the dead as more permissible than defamatory ones, or is this asymmetry a mere bias carried over from defamation law and a taste for consoling stories? The strongest answer will formulate a candidate principle—reputational interests, posthumous harm, truthfulness to history, consent, something else—and test it against nearby cases: a neutral invention about private life; a flattering invention that erases the person's actual convictions; a darkening invention about someone whose reputation is already secure; and the difference between dramatizing the recent dead and the long dead. End with a verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or revised.
