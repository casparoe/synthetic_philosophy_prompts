Legal systems that aspire to the rule of law commonly do two things that seem to pull in opposite directions. They treat fair notice as an essential requirement of valid criminal law: statutes so vague that an ordinary person cannot tell what is forbidden are struck down or narrowed, and secret or unpublished rules are regarded as illegitimate. Yet the same systems, with only narrow exceptions, refuse to let a defendant plead that she did not know her conduct was criminal, even when the relevant statute was genuinely obscure or had only recently received an authoritative interpretation. Reconstruct the implicit theory of law, and of the citizen's relation to law, that would make these two commitments hang together. In particular, what does the doctrine that ignorance of the law is no excuse presuppose about where a citizen's obligations come from, about the function of publicity in lawmaking, or about the difference between law's being knowable and its being actually known? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say where it is philosophically underdetermined, and explain what would have to change in the practice if the underlying view of legal obligation were replaced. Please write your answer in British English.
