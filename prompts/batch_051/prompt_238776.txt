I teach an upper-division undergraduate seminar on truth and paradox. The class has fourteen students and one 75-minute meeting; they have already worked through the liar paradox and Tarski's hierarchy but have not seen Curry's paradox or Yablo's sequence. Design a classroom activity—no lecture up front—that makes them feel the force of the claim that paradox does not depend on direct self-reference, and that avoiding the result forces a choice among logical principles they have been using without comment.

The centerpiece should be Curry's paradox. Arrange the activity so that the students themselves construct, or are led step by step to construct, a Curry sentence—something in the spirit of a sentence that says that if this very sentence is true, then some arbitrary claim F is true—and then derive F under rules they endorse in advance. Let them notice afterwards what they have done. Then debrief by mapping each step of the derivation onto a possible escape route: rejecting the T-schema, restricting or rejecting conditional proof, rejecting modus ponens, rejecting contraction (the structural rule that permits using a premise more than once), or treating the relevant sentence as truth-valueless. The debrief should make clear that each escape route is live and that each has costs.

Follow this with a shorter second phase on Yablo's sequence: an infinite list of sentences, each of which says that every later sentence is false. Have the students work through the back-and-forth and see how contradiction seems to arise without any sentence referring directly or indirectly back to itself—or, if you judge that this cannot be made vivid in the remaining time, say so and tell me what to cover instead.

Give me concrete rules, materials, team roles, and a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes. Include a debrief script in outline, an explanation of what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it (for instance, one team refusing the T-schema too early and so missing the pressure on the other principles), and how to handle that. Finally, be frank about which feature of the underlying idea the activity cannot reproduce and what I will need to supply explicitly in a short closing remark.
