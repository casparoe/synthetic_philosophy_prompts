I am a machine learning engineer who spends most of my time building classifiers for clinical risk prediction, and I think naturally in terms of label functions, training distributions, decision thresholds, calibration curves, prevalence, and dataset shift. I have recently been reading the philosophy of medicine, and the standing dispute between naturalist and normativist accounts of disease looks to me like an argument about exactly this machinery, though the participants rarely say so in my terms. As I understand them, the naturalist holds that disease is a value-free matter of statistically subnormal biological functioning relative to an appropriate reference class, something discovered rather than decided; the normativist holds that classifying a condition as a disease ineliminably involves judgments about harm, disvalue, or how a human life ought to go.

I would like this debate translated into the vocabulary of supervised learning as faithfully as you can manage. Restate the naturalist position as a set of claims about a reference distribution, a generating process, and a label function; restate the normativist position as a set of claims about who supplies the labels, under what loss, with what decision threshold. Pay particular attention to the reference-class problem, the choice of what counts as normal variation, and the placement of the threshold between health and disease: on the naturalist picture the threshold should presumably fall out of the distribution, while on the normativist picture it is imposed for reasons external to it. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the philosophical exchange translate cleanly, which translate only under distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in the way I think about classifiers. End by saying whether you think the residue points to something my vocabulary is missing, or to something the philosophers' disagreement has not yet made precise. Keep the translation honest; in particular, do not quietly assume that the classifier's labels are ground truth, since whether such ground truth exists is part of what is at stake.
