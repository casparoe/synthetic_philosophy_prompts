A friend and I argued long into the evening about the two-envelope problem and could not settle the following variant. The smaller of the two sums, S, is drawn with equal probability from the finite set {1, 2, 4, 8, 16}; one envelope is filled with S and the other with 2S; and you choose an envelope by a fair coin, open it, and observe the amount A before deciding whether to switch. In the usual unbounded presentation, the reasoning is that the other envelope is equally likely to contain A/2 or 2A, so switching has expected value 1.25A and one should always switch. Please work through the bounded version precisely: for which observed values of A does that reasoning survive, and for which does it break down? Show that the strategy of always switching nevertheless has zero expected advantage over never switching, even though switching is conditionally favorable at every observed amount except the largest possible one. Finally, explain how the standard paradox reemerges as the support of S is allowed to grow without bound.
