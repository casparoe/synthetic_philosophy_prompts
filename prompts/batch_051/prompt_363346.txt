Below is a stretch of Plato's *Parmenides* (trans. Harold N. Fowler); I'll say "Form" where Fowler says "idea":

“I fancy your reason for believing that each idea is one is something like this; when there is a number of things which seem to you to be great, you may think, as you look at them all, that there is one and the same idea in them, and hence you think the great is one.”
“That is true,” he said.
“But if with your mind's eye you regard the absolute great and these many great things in the same way, will not another great appear beyond, by which all these must appear to be great?”
“So it seems.”
“That is, another idea of greatness will appear, in addition to absolute greatness and the objects which partake of it; and another again in addition to these, by reason of which they are all great; and each of your ideas will no longer be one, but their number will be infinite.”

A colleague extracts a single line of reasoning from this passage and then presses it harder than Parmenides himself does. His reconstruction runs as follows.

(1) Whenever a plurality of things are all F, there is a single Form, F-ness, in virtue of which they are F; this is the one-over-many principle, and it is supposed to explain why each Form is one.
(2) The Form of F is itself F: the Great itself is great, which the passage takes for granted when it treats the absolute great as one of the great things in view.
(3) The Form of F is distinct from the many F-things that participate in it; otherwise it would merely be one of them and could not be the one thing common to them all.
(4) Now consider the predicate "is a Form." Many things are Forms — Justice, Beauty, Greatness, and the rest. By (1), there must be a Form of Formhood; call it F1.
(5) By (2), F1 is itself a Form.
(6) By (3), F1 is distinct from the Forms that participate in it.
(7) But then the plurality consisting of all those Forms together with F1 is itself a plurality of Forms. By (1), there is a further Form, F2, in virtue of which they are all Forms; by the same reasoning F2 is distinct from F1, and the demand for a new Form recurs without end.
(8) So there is no single Form of Formhood. The theory yields an unending hierarchy F1, F2, F3, . . . and leaves its own central predicate — "being a Form" — without the one over many it was meant to supply.
(9) Therefore either that unending hierarchy is somehow acceptable, or at least one of the steps above must be given up.

I would most like to keep the one-over-many principle in (1), since it seems to be the engine of the whole theory, and I also find (3) hard to surrender. Which step should be rejected, and why? Explain why that step looked compulsory in the first place, what each of the main escape routes costs a Platonist, and whether any repaired version of the theory can avoid the regress while still doing the explanatory work Socrates wanted the Forms to do.
