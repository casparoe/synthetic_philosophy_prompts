I'm taking a jurisprudence seminar on the Hart–Fuller debate, and last week a classmate kept falling back on the maxim "an unjust law is no law at all." The case on the table was a duly enacted statute that required citizens to report one another to the authorities on pain of punishment — a statute I would call both legally valid and gravely unjust. His move was this: if the statute is unjust, it isn't law at all, and therefore it creates no legal obligation and a judge should treat it as void. I am fairly sure several distinct claims are being compressed into that one sentence, but I couldn't cleanly separate them in the moment, and the discussion moved on before I could pin it down.

Could you lay out the distinct precisifications the maxim could be given, as a menu I can take back to the seminar? For each reading, state in a sentence or two what it actually asserts — about legal validity, about obligation, about what courts should do, about the concept of law, or whatever it amounts to — then mark it as trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false, and finally say which reading or readings would actually license the inference from "this statute is gravely unjust" to "it creates no legal obligation and courts may disregard it." I especially want to know whether the readings that are plausible give him his conclusion, or whether the readings that give him his conclusion are the implausible ones. Part of what is confusing me is a suspected slide between a rule's being defective as law and its failing to be law at all, and between a court's having to apply a rule and the rule's binding a person in conscience; if those distinctions do the work, say so explicitly.
