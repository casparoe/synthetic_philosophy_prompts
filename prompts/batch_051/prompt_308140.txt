I teach a philosophy of emotion course every couple of years, and I've realized my own view is mush. I keep giving the students three neat boxes — emotions as judgments, emotions as perceptions of bodily changes, emotions as feelings — and then hedging. So I want to get my own house in order by working backwards from what I actually say about cases. Could you do the reflective-equilibrium work for me? I'll give you my gut verdicts, and I want you to tell me what principle (or small set of candidate principles) best captures them, test it against a few more cases, and flag which of my verdicts you'd have to overturn.

Here are the cases, roughly as they come to me, not carefully ordered:

1. A car backfired behind me the other night and I jumped. I don't want to call that startle an emotion. It's too fast, there's no aboutness, nothing evaluated.

2. I can stand under a ladder and judge that the ladder is dangerous while feeling completely flat. That's an evaluative thought with no feeling in it. I dont think it's an emotion.

3. Waking up with a sense of dread before I even remember what's wrong. I want to say that's an emotion — maybe the paradigm case — even though it has no clear object.

4. Fear of the snake behind the glass at the zoo. I know it can't get me; I tell myself this; the fear doesn't budge. Still an emotion.

5. Too much coffee: racing heart, sweats, the same bodily profile as mild anxiety. I don't want to call that anxiety or an emotion. It's just arousal.

6. Admiration for a colleague who quietly did the right thing and took a professional hit for it. I can't locate much in my body, mostly the thought that what she did was admirable and a sort of pull. But I'd call it an emotion.

7. I'm still resentful about a friend's betrayal from years ago, but I can go a full week without it surfacing. Is it an emotion the whole time? I'm inclined to say yes, as a standing state, not just when it flares up.

8. The irritability I get after a bad night's sleep. Feels more like a mood than an emotion, and I want to withhold the word emotion for it, though I can't say exactly why.

My default hunch is something like this: an emotion is a felt evaluation — both sides are necessary. Evaluative content (safe or dangerous, admirable or contemptible, loss) without the feeling isn't an emotion, and a feeling or arousal without evaluative content isn't one either. But I can already see cases 3 and 5 putting pressure on that formula, and cases 2 and 6 doing opposite things to it.

So: what's the principle that gets the most of these, stated precisely enough that it actually rules on new cases? Where does my felt-evaluation formula need repair? Which of my verdicts does the best principle force me to drop? And please rule on two cases I left out: surprise at an unexpected party — emotion or not; and the grief someone feels at a fictional character's death while knowing full well it's fiction. Tell me if your principle treats those two the same or differently, and why.

Hard limit: 1,200 words. Lead with the bottom line — your one or two candidate principles — before the case-by-case work. Don't cite anyone; I want your reasoning, not the literature. I know the literature. I'm asking what I should think.
