For the past several semesters, our department's logic colloquium has hosted a running dispute between two colleagues. One is a classical set-theoretic Platonist who works in ZFC and holds that the Axiom of Choice is not merely convenient but true of the cumulative hierarchy of sets. The other is a constructivist who holds that mathematical existence means existence in principle of a construction or proof, so the unrestricted Axiom of Choice, which asserts choice functions without supplying any rule for them, is not assertable as a universal principle even though many countable instances are acceptable. They have finally agreed to stop talking past each other and to produce an adversarial-collaboration document. Write the document they should produce after a good-faith negotiation.

It should contain three parts. First, a numbered set of claims both can sign, substantive rather than vacuous — about what is provable in ZF and ZFC, about the equivalence of Choice with the well-ordering theorem and Zorn's lemma, about what the Banach–Tarski decomposition and similar results actually establish, about the familiar shoes-and-socks illustration (a rule selects the left shoe from each pair, while no rule selects one of a pair of indistinguishable socks), about the theorem that unrestricted Choice entails excluded middle in extensional constructive settings, and about which restricted or definable instances of Choice work without the axiom. Second, a disagreements table whose rows state the genuine residual disagreements, each row phrased so that the side whose column it appears in would accept that wording — I expect rows on the meaning of the existential quantifier in mathematics, on whether nonconstructive consequences of a principle can ever be evidence against its truth, and on whether full Choice does indispensable work beyond countable and dependent choice. Third, a cruxes section, giving for each crux a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case written down in advance and a specification of what would make that side revise. Conclude with an explicit statement of which part of the dispute, if any, is verbal rather than substantive, and which part is not.
