Two colleagues of mine, a philosopher and an economist, spent a lunch hour arguing about a mutual friend's decision. The friend has to choose between two postgraduate offers: a fully funded philosophy PhD at a well-regarded program, and a two-year fellowship in a government office working on criminal justice reform. She told our table that she finds the choice agonizing, and that she suspects the two options are incommensurable.

The economist immediately said that incommensurability is usually just uncertainty in disguise. His claim, as I understood it, was that the two options have different expected values, and although the relevant probabilities and cardinal utilities are hard to estimate, there is in principle an answer to which option is better for her. Calling them incommensurable, he said, is a way of refusing to do the calculation.

The philosopher replied that the economist was answering the wrong question. The problem is not that the friend lacks information; it is that the values at stake — roughly, intellectual freedom and a contemplative life on one side, practical impact and political responsibility on the other — are not measured on a single scale. He said this is the point of Joseph Raz's account of incommensurability: two options can be such that neither is better than the other and they are not of equal value, because there is no common measure along which they could be ranked.

The economist came back: even if there is no cardinal common measure, a rational agent can still have an incomplete or lexicographic preference ordering, and the friend must in fact have some ranking, because if she eventually chooses, that very choice reveals what she prefers. If an option is chosen over another, he said, that is the best evidence we have that it was, after all, better for her.

The philosopher denied that choice reveals a pre-existing comparison. He argued that in hard choices the will can create reasons rather than merely reflect them. He appealed to Ruth Chang's distinction between being on a par and being exactly equal: if two options are on a par, neither is better, but they are also not equal, and the rational response is not to search for a hidden ordering but to commit to one option, thereby making it the one that was best for the agent.

The economist said this last move makes him nervous. If commitment can make an option best, he asked, then what prevents any arbitrary choice from being rationalized after the fact? He said the philosopher owes us a criterion for when it is rational to commit, because otherwise the difference between the options being on a par and the agent simply not having settled on a precise ranking is doing no work.

At that point they turned to me. I honestly could not referee it. Please adjudicate the argument move by move: say which specific claims are right, which are overstated, and who is more defensible overall. Also, so I can calibrate how far to trust the verdict, say which argument or consideration would most likely have changed your mind, and where your own reasoning is most vulnerable.
