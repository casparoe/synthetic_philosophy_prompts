I have been trying to hold two commitments together, and I want a negotiated verdict rather than a vote for one side. On the one hand, a broadly retributivist picture of punishment seems right to me: wrongdoing generates desert, and a justified punishment is one whose severity is fitted to the gravity of the offense rather than chosen for the future goods it might produce. This picture takes wrongdoing seriously, treats the punished person as a responsible agent rather than a tool of social engineering, and gives a principled account of why the innocent must never be punished. On the other hand, I have a firm intuition that eternal conscious torment can never be a fitting sentence for any wrong a finite creature commits in a finite life. However grave the offense, a punishment that goes on forever seems to outrun any possible desert; the very proportionality commitment that makes retributivism intelligible appears to rule the doctrine out. I know the standard moves on the doctrine's behalf: that an offense against an infinite being has infinite gravity, and that the condemned's rejection continues without end so that fresh desert is always being generated. The first has always struck me as a stipulation dressed up as a premise, and the second as changing the subject away from the original finite act; I want to know whether those reactions are defensible or are themselves the thing that should give way.

Please present both sides at their strongest and then say which can give. Specifically: what would it take for the intuition to be debunked — a product of proportionality heuristics shaped by finite harms and finite punishments, perhaps combined with the tendency of felt appraisals to track the intensity of suffering far more than its duration — so that the intuition misleads when asked to judge an unending case? What would it take for the retributive theory to be amended so that it yields annihilation or universal reconciliation rather than endless torment, and would that be a natural completion of the theory or a disguised abandonment of it? And is there a reading on which the two are compatible — for instance, that hell is self-imposed separation rather than a positive infliction, or that the punishment's endlessness is not a duration in the ordinary sense? I am not asking for a survey of the standard theological positions. I want an argument for what would justify each move — dropping the intuition, amending the theory, or reconciling them — and a closing judgment about which you would give up and why.
