I work in the risk office of a regional power grid operator. For years our reliability forecasts have used point estimates for every failure scenario — a 4% chance this substation overloads during a heat wave, a 1% chance we lose that transmission line in a storm — and we plan around the scenarios that clear a threshold. Recently a friend from a stats department has been telling me a single number is dishonest when the evidence is thin, and argues we should use imprecise probabilities instead: represent our credence as an interval, like 2–9% (a lower probability and an upper probability) rather than pretend we know it's 4%. That sounds right in the abstract, but I can't take "let's be more imprecise" to a board meeting. Can you write me a procedure my team could actually use, week to week, for when to use an interval rather than a point estimate, how wide an interval to use, how to combine intervals from different engineers, and how to make a spending or load-shedding decision from intervals at all? The people running it have engineering backgrounds but no formal philosophy, we've got about an hour per forecast, and whatever we do has to be explainable to a public utilities commission. Please give the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and says "we don't have enough to give a number," where it hands off to a person because the issue is risk appetite rather than evidence, and which steps are judgement calls disguised as calculation. Then run the procedure on two scenarios: (1) a summer peak demand day with thirty years of good weather and load data, where the team puts a 4% point estimate on one substation overloading; and (2) a first-of-its-kind cyberattack scenario on our control systems, where the engineers point estimates range from 1% to 35%. Flag any place where the procedure would push back on what we'd normally do, even if it's uncomfortable, and say what the procedure cannot do.
