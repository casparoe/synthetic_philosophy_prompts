I supervise a system built on top of a large language model, and I have a fixed policy for deciding how much to trust its answers to important questions. I always require the model to give a step-by-step explanation before I accept its output, and I make my confidence almost entirely a function of the explanation's internal coherence. If the stated reasoning is clear and consistent, I accept the answer even when I cannot check it myself and even when the answer looks suspicious by superficial indicators; if the explanation is muddled or contradictory, I distrust the answer even when independent grounds suggest it is correct. A colleague says I am treating the model's self-reports as though they were literal descriptions of its underlying computation, when they might be fluent rationalizations produced after the answer was reached. Is my policy defensible? What would a model's chain of thought have to be like for its coherence to be genuine evidence about the correctness of the answer, and what revision of my policy would keep whatever is reliable in it while avoiding the risk my colleague identifies?
