I'm a reporter who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about the way scholarly societies are starting to police the old online lives of their members in response to public pressure campaigns. The case giving me trouble concerns a mid-career historian of science—call him M.—who is a Fellow of a small learned society. Its bylaws include this clause:

"Fellowship may be terminated by a two-thirds vote of the Board upon a finding that a Fellow's public statements or conduct are incompatible with the Society's purposes of fostering rigorous scholarship and public trust in the field."

The clause dates from the mid-1980s. In 2019 an anonymous account began circulating a deleted blog post that M. wrote in 2003, when he was an undergraduate and years before he joined the Society. In the post he was criticizing a eugenicist's claims and, to show what he was up against, quoted a passage from a 1920s pamphlet that included a racial slur without euphemizing it; the excerpt is now being passed around with that context stripped. A podcast network dropped him a few weeks later under pressure, and then a faction of Fellows started urging the Board to invoke the clause. The Board genuinely can't tell weather the clause reaches a pre-membership, pre-web, deleted-then-archived undergraduate blog post, or whether "incompatible with the Society's purposes" was mostly aimed at things like data fabrication, plagiarism, or misconduct at conferences.

I'd like you to work through how the clause applies on the usual approaches: what the words themselves support, what the clause's evident purpose supports, what its drafters would probably have said if asked in 1986 whether it covered a student's old blog comment that would only surface decades later, and what a reasonable Fellow reading it today would expect. But I'm also chasing a larger question for the piece: should any of this be analyzed as punishment at all—retribution for the words, deterrence, incapacitation—or is a learned society's decision to expel someone closer to a private association deciding who it wants to keep, where penal categories just mislead? Is the podcast network's deplatforming different in kind from the Society's vote, and does it matter that the context was stripped? If I'm conflating "does the clause cover this" with "is the outcome justified," or if the punishment framing is the wrong lens for a membership decision, push back on that directly rather than humoring the setup.
