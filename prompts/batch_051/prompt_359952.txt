I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I tend to think about knowledge in terms of dependency graphs: beliefs are nodes, justification is a directed edge, and any finite graph of supporting relationships either bottoms out in some nodes that need no support, cycles, or goes on forever. The traditional empiricist answer is that raw perceptual experience supplies the bottom nodes — the given. But I worry that a node has to be the right type to appear in a justification edge at all, and raw sensation seems to be the wrong type: it causes belief without supporting belief. I reconstructed that worry as follows.

(1) If perceptual experience grounds empirical knowledge, then some perceptual states justify beliefs without themselves standing in need of justification.
(2) A state can justify a belief only if it has propositional content, because justification is an evidential relation between contents, and only propositional contents can be evidence for or against something.
(3) Raw perceptual states, as classically conceived, are non-conceptual — a color patch, a sound, an itch — and therefore lack propositional content.
(4) So raw perceptual states cannot justify beliefs.
(5) Therefore perceptual experience cannot ground empirical knowledge.

I keep thinking the whole weight is on premise (2), but I'm not sure the move from (4) to (5) is as clean as it looks. Is the argument valid as stated? If not, identify the exact step that fails, and say what premise would need to be added or revised for the conclusion to follow.
