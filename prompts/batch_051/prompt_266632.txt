I am a novelist, and my particular weakness in worldbuilding concerns the relation between the fine grain of an invented world and the large story. I am drawn to projects in which I fix the deepest laws first — what a magical practice conserves, whether matter is granular, what a soul can transmit to a descendant, which quantities are exactly invariant — and then I attempt to derive the visible world from those laws. My recurring failure is that I either let every micro-level rule echo through politics, economics, and private life, which flattens the world into a diagram, or I ignore the rules whenever they become inconvenient, which attentive readers catch. I want a reusable procedure, grounded in what physics actually says about how levels of description relate to one another — effective field theory, separation of scales, the renormalization group, decoupling, universality — that I can run for each new book before the series bible is locked.

I understand the central lesson to be that higher levels have real autonomy: much fine-grained detail gets suppressed or screened as one moves to larger scales, and only a small number of surviving parameters constrain the macroscopic story. But I also gather that some features do propagate upward and cannot be ignored — conserved quantities, broken or unbroken symmetries, exact constraints, and behavior at phase transitions or thresholds. I want that turned from a physicist's slogan into a working writer's checklist.

The procedure should be usable by a novelist with no formal physics training in one afternoon, teachable to a co-author or a writers' room, and defensible to an editor and to continuity readers who will test the world harder than I do. It should take as input a statement of a world's low-level laws and yield a decision about which aspects can be left indeterminate, or even mildly inconsistent, at the scale of a body, a city, and a century without damaging the world's coherence — and which must be held fixed because they would be felt at those scales. Include a set of questions to run in order; a plain-language way of distinguishing features that are irrelevant from features that are merely small but accumulating; explicit treatment of the dangerous middle cases, such as effects that are suppressed but not erased, decoupling that holds for typical conditions but fails at a threshold, and fine detail that becomes relevant at a phase transition; and two or three worked examples, at least one of which must be a case where the procedure tells me that a plot device I like cannot be made visible at the narrative scale and should be cut. Finally, say plainly where the analogy with physics breaks down and where authorial judgment — theme, character, mood, reader comprehension — has to take over, and mark the steps at which the procedure cannot eliminate judgment.
