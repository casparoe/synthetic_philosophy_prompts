Take the standard thought experiment in which natural selection is modeled as a population climbing a fixed fitness landscape. On that picture evolution may be a stupid optimizer—it takes noisy local steps, has no access to gradients, and can get stuck on local peaks—but it is at least ascending a single objective function, and the comparison with gradient descent is a comparison of two ways of moving uphill on a landscape. Now change one feature: let fitness be frequency-dependent, so that a phenotype's expected reproductive success depends on its current frequency in the population, as in sex-ratio or mimicry dynamics, and the success of a type shifts the landscape that will judge the next generation. Work through what, if anything, is being optimized in this variant. Does the notion of a local optimum remain well defined, and what happens to the standard moral that the difference between evolution and gradient descent is mostly a matter of efficiency and noise, not of what the two processes are fundamentally doing? State the weakest extra assumption under which the original fixed-landscape picture is recovered.
