I keep drawing a parallel and I want to know whether it is deep or superficial. Before giving your verdict, start by brainstorming the most plausible ways to draw the mapping; then settle on the fairest version and stress-test it.

The independence axiom of expected utility theory says, roughly, that a preference between two gambles should survive embedding in any shared context: if I prefer gamble A to gamble B, then I should also prefer a compound gamble that yields A with probability p and some common gamble C with probability 1-p, to a compound gamble that is identical except that B replaces A. The common component is the same on both sides, so it should not reverse the local comparison.

This reminds me strongly of referential transparency in programming languages: the principle that two expressions with the same meaning can be substituted for one another inside any larger program without changing what the program does. A local semantic judgment is supposed to survive arbitrary embedding. I realize the local relations differ: preference is a ranking, substitutability is an equivalence. But the abstract shape looks the same. In both cases, context is forbidden from reversing a local relation, and local reasoning is supposed to extend to arbitrary embeddings.

Under this analogy, the Allais-type reversals are a context-dependence bug in human valuation. In the classic pattern, people prefer the sure moderate prize in one pair of gambles, then reverse their preference in a second pair formed from the first by mixing each alternative with the same common chance of some third outcome. An identical shared context flips the local ordering, much as an expression whose behavior depends on hidden state or side effects may no longer be substitutable once embedded.
