I've settled into the view that, for a single rational agent facing a decision, information can't be bad in expectation. Good's theorem says that the expected value of free information is always non-negative: if you're allowed to see a signal and then choose your act, your expected utility is at least as high as if you had to commit to an act before seeing it. I generalize from that to claims like: a rational person should never pay to remain ignorant, and you can't rationally dread finding out a test result when the only thing at stake is what you do afterwards.

So here's what I want: the full map of where this position is exposed. Which assumptions of the theorem could fail, and what would the world have to look like for each failure to be a live escape route rather than a mere logical possibilty? I don't just mean the textbook caveats. Walk through things like preferences that change when you receive the information, utility that depends directly on your state of belief rather than on outcomes, situations where your knowing affects what other agents do or what they know about you, non-Bayesian updating, and an agent whose future self won't follow the plan. Which of these are genuine defeaters for the informal claim that you should never pay not to know, and which just show that the theorem's assumptions were doing all the work? Could a rational person ever be right, with eyes open, to pay to remain ignorant? Then give me the honest meta-assessment: has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability? Is the statement information is never bad under the assumptions of Good's theorem just a tautology, and if so is that a problem for using it as a guide, or is the theorem doing different work than people usually think? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkard, say which ones a committed defender would themselves concede count against the view, and tell me which single consideration you would use if you were trying to talk me out of it.
