"Distinguish manipulation from legitimate persuasion. Then apply your preferred criterion to the following case, explicitly defending it against at least two rival criteria.

Case. A patient refuses a low-risk diagnostic procedure because he greatly overestimates its risks. His physician knows the patient's fear is not supported by the evidence. In the consent discussion she provides the correct risk statistics, but she deliberately uses a calm, reassuring tone, foregrounds the most favorable comparison figures, and ends by saying: 'If I were in your position, I would have the procedure.'

Question: Is the physician's influence manipulation or legitimate persuasion?"

Student answer:
"The key difference is whether the influencer engages the target's capacity to evaluate reasons. Legitimate persuasion offers considerations the target can weigh, whereas manipulation bypasses or distorts that capacity. An intention-based criterion is insufficient here, since a benevolent physician can still manipulate and a self-interested advertiser can give someone perfectly good reasons. The physician does appeal to emotion through her tone and her closing remark, but she also supplies the accurate statistics; because the patient's fear is itself the product of distorted reasoning, she is not subverting his rational capacities, she is helping to correct them. Admittedly, had she withheld unfavorable information or misstated the probabilities, this would have been manipulation, but she did neither. Her influence is therefore legitimate persuasion; the emotional tone is merely the packaging in which accurate reasons are delivered. An autonomy-based criterion would also get the case wrong, because it would condemn any influence that changes what the patient decides, and every successful persuasion changes the target's decision."
