I'm a science and ideas journalist, and I've been going down an idealism rabbit hole for a piece about why old philosophical problems keep resurfacing in simulation and virtual-world debates. The part I keep getting stuck on is Berkeley's so-called master argument. The general drift of his idealism — that we only ever directly perceive our own ideas, and that a physical object just is a bundle of sensible qualities that all turn out to be mind-dependent — is straightforward to restate. But the master argument seems more aggressive. As I understand it, Berkeley asks you to try to conceive a tree existing entirely unperceived and unconceived, and then points out that in doing so you're still conceiving it, so the best you've managed is a tree being conceived. My editor wants me to go beyond the standard dismissal — that he's confusing the object of thought with the act of thinking, or equivocating on "conceive." I've never found that dismissal fully satisfying, but I also don't want to be the journalist who writes 3,000 words only to fall for a 300-year-old trick.

So here's what I'd like: a committed explanation of what the master argument actually adds over Berkeleys earlier argument that sensible objects are collections of sensible qualities and those qualities are ideas. Does the standard equivocation/confusion objection land, and if it does, where exactly is the illegitimate step? Or is there a defensible core to the master argument that even someone who rejects idealism should take seriously? Don't survey every interpretive option; pick the strongest reading, give your reasons, and say plainly where you think the argument overreaches and where it doesn't. And if it helps, connect it to the modern move of saying we cannot conceive of unperceived existence in debates about simulated worlds — I keep hearing something like that and want to know whether it's just Berkeley again.
