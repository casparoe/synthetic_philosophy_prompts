I teach an intro logic course, first half syllogistic and second half modern quantifier logic, and the students keep messing up the handoff: they either try to cram a relation-heavy argument into all-A-is-B form, or they symbolize something in Frege-style notation that Aristotle could have handled in two steps. What I want is a reusable decision procedure, not another explanation of the two systems. You're an expert on the history and philosophy of logic, in particular on what Frege actually changed. Before you settle on the final checklist, take a few minutes to brainstorm the failure modes a student would hit; then give me the clean version, without the brainstorming.

The deliverable: a checklist my students can run on an ordinary-English argument to decide whether it can be evaluated inside syllogistic logic without distortion, or whether it needs modern machinery — relations, nested quantifiers and scope, function-ish expressions like "head of," identity. For each test, say what counts as a clear pass, what counts as a decisive fail, and where the right result is "unclear, stop and ask a human" because the English is ambiguous in a way that's a reading matter rather than a logic matter. It has to be usable in under five minutes by students who only know the four categorical forms plus singular statements. They'll run it a few times a week on homework, and I'm grading them partly on whether they pick the right tool, not just on the final answer, so it has to be defensible when a student argues with me. It also has to be defensible to a colleague of mine who thinks syllogistic is all intro students need. The checklist should stop early with "needs modern logic" as soon as that's obvious; don't make them fill out the whole form once the game is up. Mark which steps are genuine tests and which are judgment calls dressed up as tests — especially the step where you decide whether it's safe to paraphrase a relational sentence into monadic predicates, since that's exactly where alot of the distortion sneaks in.

Then run the procedure on these three, showing the sequence of tests and where it stops:

(1) All humans are mortal; Socrates is a human; so Socrates is mortal.

(2) All horses are animals; so every head of a horse is a head of an animal.

(3) One more case of your choosing where the procedure gives an answer I'm not going to like — either it flags something as needing modern logic that I'd have happily left in syllogistic, or it lets me keep something in syllogistic that I'd have sworn was too fancy. Tell me briefly why I wouldn't like that answer, and whether the procedure is actually right.
