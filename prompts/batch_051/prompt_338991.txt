I've read enough Pearl-through-rationalist-blogs to know the do-calculus at a hand-wavy level, and lately I've been reading counterfactual history — the what-if-the-assassination-had-failed, what-if-the-Nazis-had-won genre. I can't shake the feeling that its Pearl-style causal inference with the model left out. The standard move seems to be a minimal rewrite: you change one event, keep as much else fixed as possible, then trace the downstream consequences. That's just a do-intervention, right? You surgically set a variable and leave the rest of the graph alone. And when a historian says an event was overdetermined or inevitable, that reads like a robustness claim: the outcome is insensitive to do() across a whole range of plausible antecedents. But I've also been around rationalist forums long enough to know how easy it is to pattern-match on a shared word, and "counterfactual" is doing a lot of work in both places. So: is this a genuine structural identity or a loose analogy? What would have to be true on the history side for the causal-inference apparatus to actually transfer — a well-defined graph, stable variables, a joint distribution over possible worlds? And where does the mapping come apart? In particular, is there some result or property in causal inference that the parallel predicts should show up in counterfactual history but plainly doesn't, or the reverse? I'm more interested in the failure points then in another list of similarities.
