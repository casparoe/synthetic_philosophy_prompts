Some friends and I have been meeting monthly to argue about the golden rule, and after about a year we've got exactly nowehere, and I'm starting to think we're not even arguing about the same thing. The fight is over whether the rule can be fixed once you notic that doing to others as you would have them do to you goes wrong whenever people's tastes differ. One of us says the only salvageable version is negative: don't do to others what you wouldn't want done to yourself. Someone else says that's too weak, since it lets you do nothing while people suffer, and it still makes everything depend on your own dislikes. A third wants to flip it: treat others as they themselves want to be treated. That gets hit with the objection that people sometimes want bad things or incompatible things, and that anyway it turns the rule into a big empirical project of finding out everyone's preferences. A fourth says we should idealise the preferences — treat people as they'd want to be treated if they were informed and rational — at which point everyone else says we've stopped talking about the golden rule and are doing full-blown moral theory with a slogan on top. And underneath there's a recurring murmur that the rule was never a decision procedure at all, just a rough empathy check; but then, the others say, it can't settle anything. My working theory is that we're having two disagreements at once: one about what 'the golden rule' even names, and one about whether a moral rule can be purely formal and still tell you what to do. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, substantive, about which cases count as data, or something else — and tell me what would count as progress, including the possibility that none should be expected. Please write the answer in British English.
