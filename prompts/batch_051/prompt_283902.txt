I am drafting a short essay on what we owe the real dead when we dramatize their lives, and I would value comments on the argument at this early stage. The essay begins from a distinction that strikes me as overlooked: the usual complaint that a film or novel “gets a real person wrong” runs together an epistemic question about the audience and a moral question about the person depicted. The first asks whether the work misleads viewers or readers about what actually happened. The second asks whether the work wrongs the dead person herself, even when no one is misled. My claim is that these come apart in both directions. A work can mislead without wronging—say, by presenting a speculative but morally innocuous episode as fact. And a work can wrong without misleading—for example, an openly invented scene in which a real historical figure is shown doing something flatly contrary to a commitment she spent her life defending, where the audience is clearly meant to understand the scene as fiction. If this is right, then the familiar demand that dramatizations merely avoid defamation, or correct the historical record, does not yet capture what we owe the dead.

The positive proposal I am exploring is that the relevant obligation is not primarily an obligation of accuracy but an obligation I am provisionally calling non-usurpation: roughly, one may not annex a real person’s life to a narrative arc that makes that life mean something the person herself worked to prevent it from meaning. I distinguish this from truthfulness to documented fact and from fidelity to character, since a work can be scrupulous about the record and plausible about temperament while still conscripting a life into a moral the person would have rejected. My questions are these, and I would rather hear criticism than encouragement. First, does the opening distinction genuinely carry weight, or does one side of it collapse into the other under pressure? Second, does the invented-act example establish the separation, or does it smuggle in the assumption that the act depicted is independently objectionable? Third, is non-usurpation as sketched too strong, too vague, or both—and can you supply a case in which it gives the wrong verdict?
