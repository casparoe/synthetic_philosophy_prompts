I am the director of emergency operations for a county health jurisdiction. In every declared emergency my office imposes measures—international airport entry screening, night curfews, isolation orders, venue closures—and for months afterward I must recommend, several times a month, whether to continue, narrow, or end each one. The stakes run both ways: lifting a measure too early can expose the county to real harm, while keeping it on normalizes extraordinary power, and the ratchet mostly turns one way. Legality and legitimacy also come apart in my world: a measure can be fully lawful and yet be losing its grip on the people it applies to, or be legally thin and still clearly owed obedience for now. My staff are not lawyers or philosophers, each review gets about an hour, and the elected county board must approve any continuation beyond the declared emergency and wants reasons rather than intuitions.

Write me a reusable decision procedure for deciding whether to continue an emergency measure once the acute phase has passed. It should state which questions to ask and in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure returns a presumptive "end it," where it returns "this is a value judgment, escalate to the board," and where legality and legitimacy must be assessed separately rather than merged. Ground the procedure in the real conceptual distinctions between legal authorization and legitimacy, separating what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this topic, and not presenting a contested view as the consensus. Then run the procedure on two cases: continuing traveler health screening at the international airport six months after disease rates have returned to baseline, and keeping a post-disaster night curfew in force. Flag the step where the procedure gives an answer I will not like, and state plainly what the procedure cannot settle. Write in American English.
