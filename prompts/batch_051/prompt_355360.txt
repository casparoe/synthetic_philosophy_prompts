I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about why some people vanish from the historical record. One of the people I interviewed, a computational historian who works with fragmentary document collections, kept falling back on an analogy: an archive is basically a fossil record. Her translation goes like this — fossils and archives are both wildly biased samples of something much bigger, because soft-bodied things rot before they can fossilize, just like the unrecorded poor, enslaved, or non-literate people decay out of history; the parts we get are the hard shells and bones (contracts, tax rolls, administrative documents) laid down by the state and the literate. Her payoff is that since paleontologists don't treat a missing species as proof it didn't exist, historians shouldn't treat archival silence as evidence that someone didn't matter or didn't act.

I want to push on this analogy before it goes in my piece. Start with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the organism, the skeleton or shell, the taphonomic filter (all the processes between a creature dying and its bones ending up in a collection), the rock outcrop, the excavation, the museum drawer? Then where does it break? I've got a hunch it quietly imports natural, purposeless processes into a thing — building and keeping an archive — that is saturated with decisions, power, money, and incentives, and maybe it also imports a clean split between the surviving part and the person who made it that doesn't hold for records. But I don't want my hunch to do all the work, so seperate the disanalogies that actually matter for her payoff from the ones that don't. Does her conclusion survive once they're on the table? And if the fossil record is the wrong picture, is there a better analogy from the sciences or elsewhere for how archives get shaped — one that keeps the lesson that silence isn't absence without turning deliberate erasure into erosion?
