Im working on a novel set in a small breakaway city-state whose founders are obsessed with the idea usually attributed to Abelard, that the sin lies in the consent rather than in the outward deed. They push it further than Abelard might have: after their revolution they rewrite the criminal law from scratch so that a person is punishable only for what they willingly consented to, not for what actually happened. A shooter who misses is punished exactly like a murderer; a driver who kills a child through a pure accident, with no wicked will, has committed no crime at all, however much the family wants someone held to account. Courts stop asking what happened and instead hold something like an inquest into what the accused truly consented to, relying on diaries, confessions, character testimony, and whatever counts as a lie detector in this world.

As a novelist I want the institution to feel concrete and warp the way real institutions warp, not just serve as a moral lesson. So predict how this legal system actually behaves after a generation or two. What happens to deterrence, to how people plan and talk about crimes, to the detective's job, and to negligence and recklessness—do those get reinterpreted as willful blindness and pulled into the consent inquiry? What happens to victims, who mostly want to know who hurt them and whether they'll recieve anything like justice? Does the system start punishing people for seeming to harbor bad intentions, or for confessing them, and does a black market in false confessions or faked intentions emerge? I care less about whether it's a good idea than about which parts of the transformation are robust, which are guesses, and what facts about human guilt and testimony would have to be different for the whole thing to collapse or to settle into a stable equilibrium.
