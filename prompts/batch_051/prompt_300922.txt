I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science, and the foundation that funds my position has asked me to draft an internal screening guide for its small-grants program. The program's mandate says it will fund projects whose central claims are scientific in roughly the sense Karl Popper gave the term: open to empirical refutation rather than protected from evidence by design. Each round, three non-specialist program officers triage roughly sixty short applications, each application gets about thirty minutes of attention, and the board signs off on every funding decision. The foundation needs a reusable procedure, not a theory seminar: something an intelligent generalist can apply cold and defend to the board.

A literal falsifiability screen, applied one application at a time, breaks down on familiar problems. No hypothesis is tested alone; when a prediction fails, an applicant can always revise an auxiliary assumption and keep the core claim, so simple falsification gives no usable routing rule. Many legitimate projects make probabilistic, model-relative, or early-stage exploratory claims that are not cleanly falsifiable, while a good deal of pseudoscience arrives pre-dressed as crisp, falsifiable-sounding prediction. The guide therefore needs to preserve the point of the criterion—separating projects whose central claims carry genuine empirical risk from projects engineered to survive any evidence—while being honest about where the theory hands off to judgment.

Please design the screening guide as a step-by-step protocol. It should be executable by a non-philosopher reading an application for the first time; it should identify the standard moves applicants use to make unfalsifiable positions look testable; it should separate mechanical checks from judgment calls, with instructions for what an officer should do when uncertain; and it should produce a decision and a short rationale the board can act on. Work the procedure through at least three hypothetical applications, including one the board is likely to like but the procedure recommends rejecting, and one the board is likely to dismiss but the procedure recommends funding.

Return the guide as one JSON object conforming to the following schema, with no text outside the JSON.

- procedure_name: string, a short working title for the guide.
- principles: array of strings, the falsificationist commitments the procedure actually operationalizes, stated in plain language, at most four.
- steps: array of objects, each with step_number (integer), name (string), practitioner_question (string, the plain-language question the officer asks), what_to_look_for (string, what in the application bears on that question), outcomes (array of objects, each with outcome (string) and routing (string, what to do next)), time_minutes (integer), judgment_call (boolean), and uncertainty_guidance (string, what to do when the officer cannot decide).
- terminal_verdicts: array of objects, each with verdict (string), definition (string), and recommended_action (string). Include at least the verdicts fund_as_science, fund_as_nonempirical_inquiry, revise_and_resubmit, reject_outside_mandate, and escalate_to_board.
- red_flags: array of objects, each with flag (string), how_to_spot_it (string), and recommended_response (string). Cover at least: vagueness that absorbs any outcome, post hoc rescue, auxiliary hypothesis proliferation, moving target, and unfalsifiable core with testable fringe.
- worked_applications: array of objects, each with case_label (string), application_summary (string, one paragraph), step_results (array of strings, one entry per step applied, saying what the step found), final_verdict (string), and uncomfortable_implication (string, what the procedure forced that the foundation will not like). Include at least three cases, with the two specified cases among them.
- limits: object with what_the_procedure_cannot_decide (array of strings), residual_judgment (array of strings), and override_conditions (string, describing when an officer may depart from the procedure).
- board_note: string, at most 250 words, explaining whether the foundation's stated falsifiability criterion had to be modified and how to defend the procedure to the board.
