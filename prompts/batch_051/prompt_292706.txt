Last night I had a long argument with an old friend about Sartre. He kept pressing the charge of incoherence: once you deny that there is a human nature and a pre-given order of values, he said, you seem to be left with no grounds for condemning anything or anyone, so the thesis that existence precedes essence cannot do the universal, normative work Sartre wants it to do. We went round in circles for hours, and by the end I was less confident than I would like that I could lay out the position in a way that survives that objection. In a few weeks I have to give an introductory session on Sartre to first-year undergraduates who have read almost no philosophy, and I want to anchor the session on the following passage from Jean-Paul Sartre, Existentialism Is a Humanism (translated by Philip Mairet):

"From the Christian side, we are reproached as people who deny the reality and seriousness of human affairs. For since we ignore the commandments of God and all values prescribed as eternal, nothing remains but what is strictly voluntary. Everyone can do what he likes, and will be incapable, from such a point of view, of condemning either the point of view or the action of anyone else."

I want the session to use this passage as the objection the students must hold in view while we work through what the thesis actually says, so that by the end they can see which versions of the objection bite and which rest on a misunderstanding.

Please act as a professional philosopher who has thought hard about how beginners first meet existentialism. Think carefully about the dependency structure: which ideas must be in place before the claim that existence precedes essence can even be stated precisely, and which ideas must wait until later. Spend a few minutes brainstorming two or three possible carrying examples before settling on one. Then give me a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself. Start with a single sentence stating the one idea the session is designed to land. After that, set out the order in which the components should be introduced and why each depends on the previous one; identify the single example or case that will carry the idea and say what it must have and what it must not have; list the misconceptions the students will predictably bring or form and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off; describe how I will know by the end whether they have understood the question rather than merely learned to repeat the slogan; and say explicitly what should be left out at this level. The whole plan should be at most 800 words, and it should be written in British English.
