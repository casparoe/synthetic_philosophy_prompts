Just got back from a long argument with a friend about whether death is actually bad for you, and now I'm supposed to lead a book club discussion on Tolstoy's The Death of Ivan Ilyich and I want to use our argument as the lens. My friend kept pushing the Epicurean line that the fear of death is a mistake about the future—he made me read this bit from Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus:

"Foolish, therefore, is the man who says that he fears death, not because it will pain when it comes, but because it pains in the prospect. Whatsoever causes no annoyance when it is present, causes only a groundless pain in the expectation."

So what I want to know is whether the novella gives that view a fair run or just stacks the deck against it. Ivan Ilyich spends the whole back half of the story terrified of dying while everyone around him refuses to think about death, and Tolstoy pretty clearly treats his fear as the honest response and their denial as the real self-deception. Read through Epicurus, where does the novella actually support him (if anywhere) and where does it push back hardest? Is Ivan Ilyich afraid of death itself, or of a life that went wrong, or of being erased? And does the ending—whatever you make of it—settle my argument with my friend, or just change the subject?
