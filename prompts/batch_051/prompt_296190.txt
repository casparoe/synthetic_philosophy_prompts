Suppose a laboratory claims that, using patterned stimulation delivered through a cortical visual interface, it can produce a genuinely novel colour experience: a hue subjects describe as reddish-green, one experienced as uniformly reddish and greenish at the same time rather than as a mottled patch, a rapid alternation, or a mixture of two familiar hues. The sceptical response is that human colour experience is organized around opposed channels — red versus green and blue versus yellow — so that a simultaneous reddish-green is excluded by the structure of the system itself, and any report of it must be a misdescription, a memory artefact, or an effect of suggestion. How could one test whether a subject has actually undergone a new kind of colour experience? Design a concrete protocol; it may combine forced-choice discrimination against the nearest familiar hues, re-identification of the target experience across sessions, colour-matching under different adaptation states, interference with naming or memory for ordinary colours, and neural criteria for a distinct representational state. Say which results would favour the novel-hue reading and which would favour the misdescription reading. Finally, be precise about what even a strongly positive result would leave unresolved — in particular, whether the subject now occupies an enlarged colour space or merely a damaged or shifted one, and whether any behavioural or neural criterion can settle what the experience is like for the subject.
