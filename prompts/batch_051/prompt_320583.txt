For a seminar I am teaching on well-being, I have written out my current commitments about the place of novelty and variety in a good life, and I want a consistency audit before I present them. Here they are:

(1) I hold an experientialist account of well-being: nothing makes a life better or worse for the person except the felt character of that life, understood broadly to include pleasure, interest, mood, and affect.
(2) I think a life of adventure and variety is better for the person than a life of comfortable routine, even when the two lives contain the same sum of pleasant experience.
(3) I explain the value of novelty instrumentally, through hedonic adaptation: repetition drains the affective returns of an experience, so variety matters only because it keeps the felt quality of a life from decaying.
(4) I take the modest side on Nozick's experience machine: a simulated life whose experiences are identical to a real one is not worse for the person.
(5) I nevertheless regard the fear of a boring life as rational even when the person knows in advance that the life will remain pleasant throughout — so the fear is not merely a fear of lower hedonic returns.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, and which apparent tensions dissolve once the commitments are stated precisely. For the genuine tensions, rank them by severity, say what the cheapest repair would be in each case and what it would cost, and tell me which single revision would remove the most strain overall. If you think some commitment is too vague to assess, say what precisification would matter most. And if the task as I have posed it is itself misdirected, I would rather be told that plainly than receive a neat ordering that ignores the problem.
