I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a name for, and I'd be grateful if you could help me pin it down. The clearest case is the old kidnapping scenario: someone puts a gun to your head and says they'll let you go if you promise to pay them later. If you're the transparently self-interested type, they know the promise is worthless once you're safe, so they don't believe you, and that's very bad for you. If you could somehow be the kind of person who keeps promises even when keeping one is costly and no one is holding the gun anymore, you'd be much better off. Yet from the inside, at the moment when you'd have to actually pay, keeping the promise looks like a pure mistake. A flaw would be an asset, but you can't just decide to have it.

I've seen the same shape in other places. A negotiator who genuinely overvalues their own bottom line, or who genuinely gets angry enough to walk away from a deal they should in fact take, can sometimes get better terms than someone who only fakes those attitudes, because faking leaks. It isn't quite the usual trick of removing your own options in advance, like Ulysses tying himself to the mast; in these cases the options stay available, and the useful thing is that the person's actual preferences or emotions are, moment to moment, running against their own interest. Does this general configuration have an established name? If the field has several nearby labels, I'd like to know how they divide the territory. Specifically, is this the same thing as the Ulysses case or importantly different? Is it the opposite of the ordinary situation where someone knows they shouldn't do something and does it anyway? And can the decision to cultivate such a disposition itself be called rational, or does that stretch the word rational past usefulness? I won't be satisfied with a bare label; I want to know exactly what falls under the concept, what is adjacent but different, and where the edges are. If the thing I'm describing has no settled name, say so and propose one, with a definition and boundary cases.
