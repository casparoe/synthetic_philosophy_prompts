I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and next week I'm giving a departmental workshop talk where the room will be our usual philosophy people plus a few behavioral ecologists and a primatologist who loves optimal foraging models. My thesis is that the "just-so story" charge against evolutionary psychology is mostly misdirected: sure, you can cook up an adaptationist tale for almost any trait after the fact, but that's an indictment of sloppy practice, not the research program itself, and the standard EP cases (cheater detection and the Wason selection task, sex differences in mate preferences, disgust, the use of an environment of evolutionary adaptedness to generate predictions) do make risky predictions once you pin down the ancestral conditions carefully enough. The problem is my audience is basically Gould-and-Lewontin sympathetic: they think EP is unfalsifiable storytelling, that talk of an EEA is pseudo-precision, that "reverse engineering" the mind is spandrel-blind, and that adaptationist thinking is fine for livers but not for minds. One senior ecologist likes to say he's a Darwinian about everything except human psychology. I want a prep sheet for the Q&A, not a defense of the paper: the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way this crowd would phrase them, which have real force, what I should concede up front rather than get nailed on, where each reply is weakest, and the one question I should be praying nobody asks. And please commit: tell me what you think I should concede and what I should hold, with reasons, rather than laying out the menu and leaving me to pick. My own fear is that my framing — that the just-so story objection is itself a just-so story about how EP gets done — is to clever by half and is going to blow up in front of people who know the empirical literature better than I do.
