Suppose that starting next month an inscrutable device—call it the Box—begins publishing each morning a list of precise propositions about the future, some hours away and some decades away, each paired with a number between 0 and 1. The propositions are ordinary in form: that it will rain in a particular city tomorrow; that a named patient will survive a surgery already scheduled; that a specific bill will pass within the year; that a given person, when asked, will agree to a particular request; that a certain house will burn down within five years; that a particular defendant, if released, will commit a violent offense within a year. The Box's designers guarantee—and, after testing, everyone is convinced—that each number is the genuine objective chance of its proposition, in the sense in which a fair die has a one-in-six objective chance of landing on six: not a fallible prediction, not a measured frequency, and not anyone's subjective confidence. No one can query the Box or influence which propositions appear. It reports each number once, never revises it, and is unaffected by anything anyone does in response. Grant for the sake of the scenario that objective chances of this kind exist for all of these propositions. Everyone can see the list, and nearly everyone accepts the standard rule for using such numbers: your rational degree of confidence in a proposition should equal its known objective chance, unless you possess what philosophers call inadmissible evidence—information about the very outcome, or about your own future conduct, that gives you a reason to depart from that chance. That rule is the Principal Principle.

Now imagine the world has lived with the Box for thirty years. Give me your reasoned prediction of how it has reshaped betting and insurance, criminal law, medicine and clinical research, markets, politics, and ordinary self-management. In particular, work through the cases where deference to the Box strains or seems to break down, and say what a coherent agent should actually do in each. When the Box assigns 0.2 to the proposition that a man will donate to a specified charity and he knows he has not yet decided, can he resolve to make the number wrong, and what confidence should he end up with? When a bail judge sees that the Box assigns 0.4 to the proposition that an arrested defendant will commit a violent offense if released, and the statute permits detention only on clear and convincing evidence of dangerousness, what should the judge—and eventually the doctrine—do? What happens to an insurance market when one person's five-year fire risk is printed as 0.9 and everyone, including the person, knows it? How does clinical research proceed when the Box happens to print chances for both arms of a treatment comparison, one clearly favorable? And how should a market respond when the Box assigns 0.7 to one proposition and 0.6 to a second proposition that, by the public's best logic, is equivalent to the first?

Above all I want the chain of reasoning rather than a story. Separate the consequences forced by coherence alone from those that depend on institutions' incentives, legal conventions, and how quickly people learn to distinguish information they should ignore when deferring to a chance from information that genuinely licenses setting the number aside. For each major prediction, say how confident you are and which assumption—about human behavior, the reach of the chance reports, or the surrounding law—would have to fail for the prediction to be wrong.
