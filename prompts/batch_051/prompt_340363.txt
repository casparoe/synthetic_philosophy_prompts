I spent thirty-odd years as a field ecologist and retired last year, and now I've gone down a philosophy rabbit hole; lately it's social choice theory. Sen's liberal paradox has been rattling around in my head because, if I'm reading it right, it's a clean proof that a group can be set up so that whenever everyone prefers one option to another the group follows, and each of at least two people has a protected personal sphere, and yet the group's preferences can still cycle and leave no coherent decision. That sounds like an abstract curiosity, but I've become the person on my housing cooperative's board who writes up decision rules, and we hit a version of it every few meetings: one resident wants to do something to their own unit that they call private, and everyone else on the board wants to block it. We currently alternate between following the majority and following the resident depending on who argued better, and I'd like to replace that with a proceedure that respects what Sen actually showed.

So write me a reusable decision procedure for the board — a checklist or flowchart in words — for deciding when an individual owner's claim of a personal sphere should win and when a unanimous preference of the other owners should win. Base it on the actual conditions of the theorem (the Pareto principle, minimal liberalism, unrestricted domain), but translate them into plain questions the board can ask. The procedure has to be runnable in about ten minutes during a montly meeting by people who have not read Sen and are not going to, and the stakes are real: fines, forcing someone to repaint, changing house rules. Please give the questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, say where the procedure stops with a decision and where it hands the call back to the board as a value judgment rather than an application of the rule, and include an explicit pre-vote check for whether the case is about to produce the kind of cycle the theorem guarantees.

Then work the procedure through three disputes that actually came up in our building: (a) one owner wants to paint her front door bright purple while every neighbor prefers the current grey; (b) a night-shift owner wants blackout curtains that change the facade and all other owners prefer the standard curtains; (c) an owner wants to smoke on his balcony and the two downwind neighbors object. Be honest if the procedure returns an answer we won't like, and say clearly what it cannot settle.
