I teach a 20-person upper-division seminar on group decision-making (mostly philosophy majors, plus a few politics people). I have 80 minutes next week, and I want an in-class game that makes them feel the discursive dilemma in their bones before I ever name it. The structure I want them to run into is the classic three-judge case, which I'd describe like this: a committee decides whether something goes through, and the outcome requires two conditions, A and B, both to hold. Three members each reason perfectly consistently — one thinks A but not B, so no; one thinks B but not A, so no; one thinks both, so yes. If you vote on A alone, the majority says yes. If you vote on B alone, the majority says yes. But if you vote on the overall conclusion, the majority says no. So the group endorses both premises and rejects the conclusion built from those same premises — a position no single member actually holds. No one's being irrational; the agregation itself produced it.

But I want them to feel that, not hear it as a mini-lecture. Design a runnable game or role-play for 20 people in 80 minutes, no real money. Give me exact rules, materials (cards, handouts, score sheets, whatever), a payoff scheme using points or candy or something similarly small, and a debrief that maps what happened to premise-based versus conclusion-based voting, and to why there's no neutral way to decide which procedure deserves to be called the group's real view. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm especially worried that people will coordinate and vote strategically once they see the contradiction coming, or that the content will get too politically charged — and how you'd handle those. And be straight with me about which part of the idea the game can't reproduce: I suspect the fully general point (that this isn't a quirk of one weird agenda) is something I'll still have to supply in lecture. And don't pad the answer with citations or a reading list — I want a runnable activity, not sources.
