I'm a science journalist working on a long piece about Stephen Wolfram's A New Kind of Science and the fight over whether his principle of computational equivalence is a scientific claim, a metaphysical one, or just a way of talking. As I understand it, the principle says roughly this: once a system gets past a pretty low bar of visible complexity, it has the same computational power as any other system past that bar. A turbulent fluid, a brain, a rule 30 cellular automaton, the weather — all sitting, Wolfram claims, at the same maximal level of computational sophistication, not just similar but equivalent. If that's right its a huge claim about the natural world, not a claim about our programming preferences.

But every computer scientist I call either tells me the principle is unfalsifiable or tells me it's vacuously true because any physical process can be encoded as a computation by an observer. Before I write the piece, I want to know whether that's fair. So: what would an actual test of the principle of computational equivalence look like? Give me the strongest concrete designs you can — experiments, systematic simulations, surveys of rule space, measurements of natural systems, whatever — and tell me exactly what result would count for the claim and what would count against it. I know universality of a system is undecidable in general, so part of what I'm asking is whether a statistical or partial test can still bite. Then be honest about the residue: after the best possible result comes back, what part of the claim remains untestable, and is that because it's vague, because of undecidability, or because it's secretly a definition of what counts as a system being worth paying attention to?
