I've been collecting my verdicts about eating animal products when alternatives are scarce, and I want to know whether a single principle underlies them. A pastoralist family on semi-arid rangeland where crops fail most years keeps goats, drinks their milk daily, and slaughters one for a wedding feast: I think this is fine. A comfortable urbanite in a rich country with cheap beans, lentils, and tofu on the shelf who buys factory-farmed chicken out of habit: I think this is wrong. A resident of a food desert in a rich country, without a car and with the nearest real grocery an hour away, who buys a frozen supermarket burger because that is what the corner store stocks: wrong, but only slightly, and far less wrong than the urbanite. Someone with severe allergies to legumes, soy, nuts, and wheat, for whom a workable vegan diet is genuinely difficult, who eats eggs from a neighbor's free-ranging hens: fine. A guest in a remote community whose hosts set out the only shared meal they have, containing goat meat, where refusal would leave the hosts with nothing else to offer: fine. A professional at a business dinner in a rich city who orders the meat dish only to avoid seeming difficult, though a vegan option was on the menu: not fine, though only a small wrong. A coastal community whose farmland has gone saline and now fishes the lagoon because little else will sustain them: fine. A person who hunts and eats feral hogs that are destroying a local watershed: fine, perhaps even good.

The case I keep going back and forth on is buying sturdy leather work boots for a cold, wet climate when I can manage the inexpensive synthetic pair now but not the durable leather pair unless I save for many months; the synthetic pair needs replacing every season, while the leather would last for years.

Is there a principle that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts, what does it imply about the boots, and what does it say about some further case I haven't considered? If some of my verdicts have to go, tell me which and why.
