So my brother has spent the last year trying to convince me that charter cities and seasteads are a way to genuinely opt out of the state, not just switch to a different state. He recently got a job with one of those companies that leases a big chunk of land from a developing-country government and runs it as a semi-autonomous zone, and now he wants me to invest. I got annoyed and wrote out an argument that the whole thing is a bait-and-switch. I think its sound, but the conclusion is so tidy that I half-suspect I've pulled a fast one somewhere, so I want it stress-tested.

Act as a political philosopher who works on state legitimacy, jurisdiction, and property rights, and who can also handle the institutional-economics side (enforcement, credible commitment, polycentric law). Think through this carefully. Start by brainstorming four or five distinct ways the argument could fail — equivocations, false premises, scope shifts, whatever — before you commit to a diagnosis; write the brainstorm out. Then go through it step by step.

The argument:

(1) In a charter city or seastead, the thing residents are really buying is security for their persons and whatever they own, because property without reliable enforcement is just stuff that other people can take.
(2) Reliable enforcement there has to take one of two forms: either defense and dispute resolution are unified under a single authority with final say over that territory, or they are left to a competitive market of protection and arbitration providers, with no single body having final say.
(3) If it's the competitive version, then when two providers disagree about who was wronged — or when a resident wants to sue her own provider — there is no higher forum both sides are bound to accept, so disputes either escalate to force or never get settled. That is not "reliable enforcement"; it's the thing you pay to avoid.
(4) So reliable enforcement in a charter city or seastead requires a single authority with final say over disputes and coercion within its territory.
(5) But a single authority with final say over disputes and coercion within a territory is a state in the only sense that matters for the argument: it makes rules, enforces them, and its residents cannot appeal outside it except by physically leaving. It may be small, contractual in origin, or called something else, but functionally it is the state again.
(6) Therefore the resident of a charter city or seastead has not exited the state; they have moved to a smaller, newer, usually less accountable state.
(7) If "opting out" of the state is really just moving to a smaller and less accountable state, then it is not the absence of state that these projects advertise, but at most a choice among states.
(8) So charter cities and seasteads cannot deliver their core promise: you cannot opt out of the state.

Is this argument valid? If yes, is it more than just a definitional trick? If not, say exactly which step or steps fail, under the strongest reading on which they would arguably be true, and give me the corrected version of the argument that survives — or tell me if the corrected version proves something weaker than (8). Also tell me how much of this I can still honestly say to my brother.
