I've been reading around the old Nyāya debate about the pramāṇas — the instruments of knowledge, basically the distinct routes by which a new item of knowledge gets produced — and I'm trying to work out what I actually think by running a set of cases past my gut.

Here are my verdicts, roughly as they stand.

Seeing flames in the fireplace in front of me: I know there's a fire there. Clear yes, and it arrives just by looking.

Seeing smoke on the hill and concluding there's a fire on the hill: I know that too. Clear yes, but it feels like a different route from the flames case, because it runs through a general rule that smoke means fire.

A reliable forester tells me there's a fire in the valley, and I can't go and look. I'd say I know there's a fire in the valley. At first I want this to be its own route — trusting someone's word. But then, thinking about it coldly, trusting the forester might just be an inference from his track record, so maybe it collapses into the smoke case. I'm torn.

The same forester tells me that an animal he calls a gavaya — some kind of wild ox — looks like a cow but wilder, with a longer muzzle. Later I see a cow-like animal and think: there's a gavaya. I'd say I now know something I didn't know before. This one really doesnt feel like plain perception, and it wasnt just testimony either, since the forester never told me about this particular animal; I put his description together with what I was seeing. Gut says this is a genuinely different route.

I look at an empty table and say I know there's no butter-knife on it. I'd count that as knowledge. But it doesnt feel like seeing a positive object, and it doesn't run on a general rule like the smoke case either — it seems to have its own character. This one nags at me.

My neighbour is fat, and I know for a fact he never eats during the day. I conclude he must eat at night. I'd say I know that. But I'd be fine saying this is the smoke case in fancy dress, since I'm leaning on a background general rule about how a fat person who doesn't eat by day must eat at night.

So here's the pull. I'd love the tidy story to be that there are exactly two fundamental routes — perception and inference — and everything else is some mix or shorthand. But my own verdicts keep resisting it: the gavaya case refuses to collapse, and the empty-table case keeps niggling as something separate. Is there a principle that captures when something is a genuinely distinct instrument of knowledge rather than a derivative of the others? If there is, I'd like it stated precisely, applied to the cases above, with an honest note on which of my verdicts it forces me to drop or demote. And what does it say about the two I keep going back and forth on: knowing something because I remember it, and knowing something because I read it in a good encyclopaedia?
