I am a law clerk to a judge on our state's intermediate appellate court, and she has asked me to prepare an analysis of a void-for-vagueness challenge to a new municipal ordinance. Our court's own recent vagueness decisions, with their holdings, are these:

1. In the Noise Ordinance Case, we upheld a law making it an offense to operate sound-amplifying equipment on a public street between 10:00 p.m. and 7:00 a.m. at a level above 60 decibels measured at the property line. We said the numerical limit, the hours, and the measurement point together provide an objective standard.

2. In the Park Loitering Case, we struck down a law making it an offense to loiter in a public park in a manner annoying or disturbing to other park users. We said the law defined the offense by the subjective reaction of bystanders and left enforcement to an officer's own annoyance threshold.

3. In the Sidewalk Display Case, we upheld a law prohibiting the placement of tables, racks, or signs on a sidewalk so as to leave less than six feet of clear passage. We said the six-foot rule is a determinate and measurable standard.

4. In the School Grounds Case, we struck down a law making it an offense to remain within 100 feet of a school entrance during school hours without legitimate business. We said the quoted phrase has no statutory or contextual definition and licenses judgment about a person's reasons for being present.

5. In the Hospital Noise Case, we struck down a law making it an offense knowingly to make an unreasonable noise within 150 feet of a hospital. We said the term gives neither citizens nor officers a criterion to conform to, and the distance limit did not supply one.

6. In the Jackhammer Case, we upheld a law banning the operation of a jackhammer on a street adjacent to a residence between 7:00 p.m. and 7:00 a.m. except with an emergency-repair permit. We said the law specified the conduct, the hours, and the location, and that the permit exception was administrative and objective.

The new ordinance before us provides: "No person shall, within the indoor public areas of a public library, engage in conduct that a reasonable person would regard as disrupting the quiet use of the library for reading and study." The challenger argues that the ordinance is void for vagueness; the city argues that it is saved by its confinement to a single context and by its use of a reasonable-person standard.

How should our judge decide if she wants to be consistent with our court's own case law? Identify the closest analog among our precedents and explain whether it actually controls. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the result, set out the principle we would be adopting under each possible ruling and say which principle fits the pattern of our prior holdings better. End by specifying a further hypothetical ordinance that would cleanly tell the two principles apart.
