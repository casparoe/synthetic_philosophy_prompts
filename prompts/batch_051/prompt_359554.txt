The free-will defense was built to answer the logical problem of evil, and its whole engine is a value claim: worlds containing creatures with morally significant freedom, who sometimes choose badly, are valuable enough that the evil those choices produce is worth permitting. The debate, however, has moved on. The version of the problem of evil that now does most of the work is the evidential one, and its hardest cases are not the cruelties of free human beings but prehuman and nonhuman suffering—a fawn dying slowly in a forest fire, predation, disease across evolutionary history—where no human freedom is exercised and no moral lesson is learned by anyone. Does the free-will defense survive this change in the paradigm case? Say exactly which premise the shifted debate puts under pressure, whether the familiar fallback of tracing natural evil to the free choices of fallen nonhuman agents does real explanatory work or merely insulates the defense from evidence, and whether there is a repaired descendant of the defense that preserves its central idea while conceding that the original formulation was answering a different question from the one now being asked. If no such repair exists, say what the residual problem should be called and why the free-will defense is no longer an answer to it. Keep the answer in American English.
