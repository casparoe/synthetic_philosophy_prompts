I am a journalist who covers science and ideas. Before I became a journalist, I worked as a software engineer, so the vocabulary I reason in most precisely comes from type systems and data representation: types and values, equality across representations, equivalence classes and quotient types, canonical forms, serialization and deserialization, and invariants that survive changes of representation. I have no formal training in the philosophy of physics, but I keep encountering a position about gauge theories: that a gauge-dependent description of a field contains surplus structure, that configurations related by gauge transformations represent one and the same physical possibility, and that what is physically real lives in the gauge-invariant data carried by closed loops, such as holonomies, rather than in the potential at each spacetime point. Restate that position, along with the main alternatives to it, in my software-representation vocabulary as faithfully as you can: treat gauge transformations as operations on representatives, gauge orbits as equivalence classes, gauge fixing as the choice of a canonical form, and gauge-invariant observables as functions that are well-defined on the quotient. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the physicists' and philosophers' debate translate cleanly, which parts get distorted, and which parts have no counterpart at all in how software handles redundant representations? In particular, I want to know whether my vocabulary reduces the question of what is real in a gauge theory to an uncontroversial point about representation, or whether something physical resists the translation and thereby shows that the philosophical dispute is sharper than a mere choice of coordinates.
