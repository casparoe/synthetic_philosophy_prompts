I recently served on a jury, and I have not been able to stop thinking about the instruction the judge read us. I have no training in law or philosophy, so I have had to work through the problem in my own words. The instruction came from an old statute and read:

"A person is not criminally responsible for an offence if, at the time of the act, he was suffering from a mental disorder that prevented him from understanding what he was doing, or from understanding that what he was doing was wrong."

The defendant had been diagnosed with psychopathy. The psychiatrists did not dispute that he had planned the killing, or that he understood in a practical way what he was doing, or that he knew the law forbade it. What they disputed was whether he understood that it was wrong. They described a man who can say, fluently and accurately, that hurting people is wrong, and can also say that his own act was wrong, but is entirely unmoved by saying it. The word seems not to have the weight for him that it has for other people. One psychiatrist put it this way: his reasoning works, but the feelings that normally tell a person what matters are missing.

I am less interested in what my particular jurisdiction would decide than in the underlying problem. Does the word "wrong" in that rule mean "against the law" or "morally wrong," or something else again? If it means "morally wrong," what exactly is missing in a person who can use the word correctly but does not feel its force, and is that missing thing a kind of knowledge? The statute predates the modern clinical description of psychopathy; its words seem aimed at a person whose thinking has fallen apart, not at a person whose thinking is intact but whose feelings are absent. Should the rule be extended to this later-described condition, or should its text, its original purpose, or what its authors would have said decide the matter? I would like a careful argument about which of these approaches should govern here, and about what it means to know that something is wrong.
