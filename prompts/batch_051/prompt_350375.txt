I am a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on distributive justice and the moral emotions. We have been working through Rawls's contrast between resentment, which is supposed to answer to actual wrongs, and envy, which he treats as a vice even when it is psychologically understandable — though he allows that some envy is excusable under conditions of severe inequality. I find myself torn between two ways of developing that material. One position holds that envy is in its nature a vice: a painful or hostile response to another's advantage that is not made appropriate by the fact that the advantage is undeserved, and that treating envy as a signal of injustice flatters a corrosive emotion and confuses a moral sentiment with a merely self-regarding one. The opposing position holds that the vice-label individualizes what is often a structural problem: much of what gets called envy is really resentment at unjust inequalities, and even envy in the strict sense can be excusable, and can carry information, because it registers that a social arrangement has made some people's flourishing feel like an injury to others. Please write a short dialogue between two equally competent members of this seminar, one defending each position. Have them respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talk past one another, and have them try to pin down exactly where their disagreement lies — over the definition of envy, over whether the emotion is reliable evidence of injustice, or over what practical response follows. Neither character should be a strawman, and the dialogue should not settle the question by authorial fiat.
