A friend and I have spent the past two evenings arguing about whether the standard anti-corruption toolkit — disclosure rules, criminalization, conflict-of-interest laws — simply encodes a distinctively Western picture of the impersonal public official, and whether it is bound to misfire in places where gift exchange and personal obligation are among the main ways trust and loyalty get built. That argument produced a concrete proposal, and I want to see it analyzed rather than defended.

Imagine a city in a middle-income democracy where giving an official a substantial gift at a wedding, funeral, or holiday is common and widely regarded as respectful rather than transactional, but where the very same channels are also used to buy permits, contracts, and favorable rulings. The city adopts a new rule, called the relationship test. Every transfer above a modest value threshold from any private person to a public official — or to the official's spouse, parent, child, or sibling — must be reported to an ethics registry within fourteen days. For each reported transfer the official files a sworn account of the relationship, and the registry assigns one of two classifications. If the giver and the official can show a documented history of non-pecuniary reciprocity that predates the official's time in office — years of mutual attendance at family events, shared membership in a long-established community association, or a record of small two-way favors that began before the official took the post — the transfer is classified as a personal gift and allowed without limit, but the official must recuse themself from any decision affecting the giver for the following two years. If no such prior relationship can be shown, the transfer is classified as a public receipt: the item must be surrendered to a public asset registry, which auctions it within ninety days; the proceeds go to the municipal budget; and both giver and official receive a receipt. The official may not attend the auction, and the giver may not bid.

Walk through, with your reasoning visible at each step, what this mechanism would actually do in such a city. Trace the likely effects on the total flow of gifts, the forms gifts take, the choice of recipient, and the timing with which personal relationships are formed or documented. Identify where the system is gamed first, and by whom — donors, officials, relatives, intermediaries, registry staff, or local organizations. Then assess whether the personal/public distinction can survive sustained pressure or whether it degrades into something else. Pay particular attention to whether the rule inadvertently penalizes officials who behave with Western-style impersonal probity — no gifts at all, no cultivated community ties — while rewarding people who are good at manufacturing a documented history of closeness, and whether a city with strong clientelist norms would simply shift the exchange into services, favors, and future employment that never pass through the registry. Conclude by separating your robust predictions from your fragile ones, and state what would have to be true about local social structure for the rule to produce its intended result rather than a new, better-documented form of corruption.
