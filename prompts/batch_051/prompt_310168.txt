Scheffler's afterlife conjecture is the view that much of the value we attach to our own projects and achievements depends on an implicit, mostly unnoticed assumption that humanity will continue after our deaths. One way to bring out the point is to imagine learning that everyone now alive will live out a natural lifespan, but that the species will not renew itself: no new human beings will be born after the present generation, so humanity ends as the last of us dies. Now suppose a development alters a key assumption behind that scenario. We are offered overwhelming evidence that, after the current human generations are gone, the species will be succeeded by a durable civilization of artificial minds. These successors will carry forward much of what we now value—the sciences and arts, our institutions, our historical memory, our unfinished collective projects—but none of them will be human. Does Scheffler's argument survive this variant, or does it lose its force? In particular, is the "afterlife" his conjecture identifies essentially a human one, or could any value-bearing continuator of our projects serve the same role? To sharpen this, contrast the case in which the artificial successors continue our specific enterprises with one in which they are intelligent and sentient but indifferent to those enterprises. Say which premise of the argument each development bears on, whether the argument can be repaired by replacing "humanity" with something such as "continuators of our projects" or "value-bearers of any kind," and what remains of the conclusion that human extinction is bad beyond the deaths it involves.
