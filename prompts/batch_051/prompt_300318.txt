While reporting a piece on where the real content of mathematical claims goes when choices look arbitrary, I have repeatedly encountered a version of the following argument that there is no fact of the matter about which square root of −1 is "really" i. I want a precise diagnosis: is the argument valid, and are its premises true? If it fails, identify the exact step at which it fails and say what the strongest corrected version would establish.

(1) Complex conjugation is an automorphism of the field of complex numbers: it fixes every real number and sends i to −i. (2) Consequently, every first-order statement in the language of fields with real parameters that is true of i is true of −i; the two roots satisfy exactly the same such statements. (3) Thus nothing in the field-theoretic description of the complex numbers distinguishes i from −i; each is merely a square root of −1. (4) The standard complex plane, understood as an oriented real vector space in which the basis (1, i) is positively oriented, does distinguish them: replacing i by −i reverses orientation. (5) But orientation is exactly the kind of structure this argument is meant to deflate—it is imposed on the complex numbers rather than found in them—and there is no further non-conventional structure that separates the roots. (6) Therefore any distinction between i and −i is an artifact of convention, and there is no fact of the matter about which root is really i.
