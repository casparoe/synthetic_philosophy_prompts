I'm a PhD student in a psych program and one of the older grad students keeps trotting out the line that evolutionary psychology is just reverse engineering the mind. His version: an engineer finds a gadget with no manual, looks at it's parts, sees what they're good for, and infers what the gadget was built to do; evo psych does the same with mental mechanisms—obsessions, mate preferences, whatever—treating them as components designed to solve ancestral problems. He uses the analogy specifically to answer the just-so stories charge: reverse engineering is a real method, so inferring function from structure isn't inherently unscientific, it's what good engineers do. I've heard this enough that I want it properly stress-tested rather than just nodded along to. Can you audit the analogy? Spell out the mapping first: what corresponds to the artifact, the engineer, the blueprint, the test bench, the environment it was built for. Then go through the disanalogies—we don't have most of the relevant fossil or archaeological record, there was no designer with explicit specs, drift and neutral processes leave structure too, traits get co-opted for new jobs, development and plasticity blur what 'designed' even means, and what was adaptive in the Pleistocene may not be what the mechanism is doing now. Which of those are cosmetic and which ones actually break the inference he wants to run? And be blunt at the end: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the reverse-engineering analogy actually do any work against the just-so charge, or does it mostly re-brand 'I found a story that fits' as 'I reverse engineered this'? If there's a better analogy that would actually carry the load, tell me what it is and where it'd still fail.
