I keep going back and forth on Schopenhauer's account of music and I'd like you to sort me out. I spent my career in the labratory rather than in a philosophy department, so forgive me if I've got the terminology a bit off.

Here is the puzzle. In the third book of The World as Will and Representation he gives his general account of art: when we contemplate something aesthetically we stop being an individual driven by wants and become a pure will-less subject, just looking at the Idea. Art is meant to give us a short holiday from willing, and that is precisely what makes it valuable, because willing is the thing that causes all our suffering.

But then in the same book, when he gets to music, he says almost the opposite. Music is not like the other arts, which copy the Ideas; it is a direct copy of the will itself, and that is why it is the most powerful of the arts and why it moves us more deeply. He also says it can arouse every emotion in us without the usual motives being present.

Those two claims look to me like they are in real tension. If art's whole job is to get us out of the will for a moment, why would the highest art be the one that gives the will its most direct voice? Is he contradicting himself, or is there a reading on which the account of music fits comfortably with the will-less contemplation story? I want you to lay out the candidate reconciliations — different senses of will, the difference between feeling an emotion and being blindly driven by it, music as objectification rather than incitement, and anything else you can think of — and then actually commit to one, or argue that there is a real hole in the theory. Do not just list options; tell me which reconciliation is honest and which ones are special pleading.
