I write about art and ideas for a general audience, and I keep noticing a tension in the way reviewers, editors, and ordinary viewers talk about politically engaged work. A film that pleads too openly for a cause gets dismissed as propaganda, as if its message disqualifies it as art; a week later, the same critics will praise a novel or mural as courageous because it speaks truth to power, and will cite its social impact as evidence of its value. The line gets even harder to hold once you notice that the ideal of art valued purely for its own sake has historically been easier to claim for artists who are not depending on their work to change their social standing, whereas artists from subordinated groups have often insisted that their work must do something in the world. W. E. B. Du Bois pressed one version of this challenge: he argued that all art inevitably advocates, and that Black artists of his moment should openly enlist their work in the struggle for racial equality rather than pretend to stand outside politics.

I want to know whether the asymmetry in our ordinary judgments is defensible. Can we coherently celebrate the politics of one artwork while dismissing another as mere propaganda for doing the same kind of thing, or is the distinction between art and propaganda mostly a way of legitimating messages we already accept and delegitimating messages we do not? As a professional philosopher working on aesthetics and political criticism, I would like you to think carefully about this. Begin by brainstorming the strongest justifications for treating openly persuasive art differently from art that is not, then test whether those justifications survive the Du Boisian point. You may conclude that the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned, but please end with a clear verdict rather than a survey of views.
