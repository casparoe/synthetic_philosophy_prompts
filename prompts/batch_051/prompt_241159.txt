Consider a commercial virtual world with the following two enforcement policies. When one player uses the platform's avatar and chat tools to harass and degrade another player, the operator permanently bans the offender and explains that the conduct caused real harm to the person behind the avatar. When one player fraudulently induces another to hand over a rare virtual item acquired through hundreds of hours of play, the operator bans the fraudster but refuses to restore the item or compensate the victim, explaining that the item is only data and no genuine loss occurred. Reconstruct the theory of virtual action, virtual objects, and virtual harm that this pair of policies presupposes. Is that theory coherent, or does the operator treat the same kind of underlying fact, an event occurring only inside the virtual world, as real in one case and unreal in the other? Distinguish what the policies strictly presuppose from what they merely fit with, and explain what would change if the operator instead held that virtual objects are genuine property, or that virtual harms are only harms to the conditions of play.
