I'm a novelist, and for a book I've been reading Newton's theological writings—not the physics, the prophecy stuff. I keep coming back to the chapter Of the Prophetic Language in his Observations upon the Prophecies of Daniel and the Apocalypse. He lays out a method there that I find weirdly attractive as a formal system: the prophets, he says, all wrote in a single figurative dialect, where the same image always means the same thing, so that Daniel and Revelation decode eachother. A beast is a kingdom, horns are kings or governments, days count as years, and once you've fixed a correspondence in one place you can apply it everywhere. That gives a definite, non-arbitrary answer to questions that otherwise feel unanswerable—why these numbers, why these beasts—and it makes the whole thing feel like a clean grammar rather than a fog of private associations.

But I also can't shake a different feeling, and this is where I'm stuck. When I actually read Daniel or Revelation, the images seem to do more than carry fixed referents. The fourth beast with iron teeth isn't just Rome; it's also a recurring shape of terror in the mind. The woman in the wilderness isn't just the persecuted church; she's an image of something I keep recognizing in my own characters without being able to say what. I have this firm intuition that prophecy, at least as literature, works by excess and overflow—that the moment you assign one-to-one meanings you've turned a living symbol into a label, and something important is lost. I don't think Newton would deny the images are vivid; he would say the vividness is the garment, not the body, and the body is the historical meaning.

So I'm torn. The systematic view is genuinely compelling because it refuses to let prophecy mean whatever anyone wants, and it produces clear results across two diffcult books. The intuition is also firm, and it's not just aesthetic whining: it says the images derive power precisely from their openness, so treating them as code strips out what makes them prophetic rather than merely predictive.

What I want is not a verdict but a negotiated one. What would it take for me to debunk the intuition—to say it comes from a modern readerly habit of prizing ambiguity, not from anything the texts are doing? What would it take for the theory to be the thing that goes, or to be amended into something Newton himself would reject? And is there a reading on which both survive—maybe the code is real but local, or the excess is part of the code's own rhetoric? Tell me honestly whether those debunking moves are real arguments or just ways of not listening.
