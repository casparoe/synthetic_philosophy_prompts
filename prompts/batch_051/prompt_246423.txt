Since I retired from experimental physics, I have been reading my way through the philosophy of promising, and I keep being stopped by the same asymmetry. A courtroom treats a witness who has stood up and promised to tell the truth very differently from a witness who has taken an oath. If the first witness lies, most of us would call it ordinary promise-breaking and perhaps a contempt of the court's expectations; if the second witness tells the identical lie under identical pressure, we call it perjury, and we regard it as a categorically graver wrong. The Greek poets projected the same asymmetry upward onto the gods: an Olympian's oath sworn by the river Styx is treated as binding in a way that no bare undertaking is, and the penalty for forswearing it is described as a year without breath and nine years banished from the councils of the gods. Yet the Styx, as far as any natural scientist can see, is a river. It does not hear the words, alter the content of the promise, or change the chance of detection. So I want to know whether the practice of treating oath-breaking as worse than promise-breaking rests on anything defensible, or whether it is a remnant of magical thinking about words and rivers.

The particular question is this. Give the strongest justification you can for treating oath-breaking as more serious than breaking a promise with exactly the same content. Then test that justification against several nearby cases: an atheist who knowingly takes a religious oath while believing the divine witness does not exist; a legal system that allows a non-religious solemn affirmation and attaches the same perjury penalties to it; a private informal oath to a friend, sworn on something the friend holds sacred, with no institution to enforce it; and a person who mouths an oath without any intention of keeping it, so that the oath itself adds a fresh layer of deception. At the end, say whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned. Throughout, please separate what is settled among people who work on promising, oaths, and speech acts from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
