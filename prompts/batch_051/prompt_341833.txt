You are a professional philosopher of biology. I need help resolving a conflict between a theory I find compelling and an intuition I cannot dislodge, and I want a negotiated verdict rather than a one-sided defense.

The theory is the etiological, or selected-effects, account of biological function. On this view, a trait has the function F just in case the trait is present because, in the organism's ancestors, doing F was selected for: hearts have the function of pumping blood because hearts were selected for pumping, not because each current heart happens to pump. I am attracted to this view because it solves several problems at once. It draws a principled line between a function and a fortunate side effect: the nose does not have the function of holding up glasses, even when it holds up glasses every day. It makes malfunction possible: a heart can be supposed to pump while currently failing to pump, because its function is fixed by its selection history rather than its present performance. And it does all this with ordinary evolutionary history, without appealing to unexplained teleology. These virtues seem hard to replicate.

The intuition that troubles me is this. Suppose a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a frog spontaneously assembled itself in a swamp from ambient materials, by a staggeringly improbable physical accident. It has a heart, lungs, kidneys, and a nervous system arranged exactly as in an ordinary frog, but it has no evolutionary history at all: no ancestors, no population, no selection. I find it impossible to say, as the selected-effects account seems to force me to say, that its heart has no function. Pumping blood is not an incidental thing the heart does; it is what the heart is there for. I have a similar reaction to a synthetic bacterium assembled de novo in a laboratory so that its genome was produced by a random sequence generator rather than copied from any existing species, where one of the chance sequences codes for an enzyme that degrades a toxin. In a toxic medium the bacterium flourishes while a control strain without that sequence dies. Here too my intuition says the enzyme has the function of degrading the toxin now, even though no selective history has accrued.

The causal-role account seems to capture this intuition: a trait's function is whatever it does that contributes to a capacity of the containing system. But I also know what that view costs. Causal-role functions are relative to an arbitrarily chosen system and capacity, so a nose can be assigned a function within the eyeglass-holding system; the view multiplies functions without a principled stopping point; and it seems to lose the normative dimension — the fact that a heart can malfunction, can be supposed to pump while failing to pump — which was one of my main reasons for caring about functions at all.

So I am stuck between a theory with systematic virtues and an intuition that will not budge. Walk me through each possible resolution and say what would justify it, without simply picking a side. Should I debunk the intuition as a confusion between a current causal contribution and a function? Should I amend the selected-effects account — for instance, by allowing current or forward-looking contributions to survival to confer functions even without selection history, or by treating design as a kind of selection — and would either amendment still solve the function/accident problem? Should I adopt a two-sense view on which the etiological account and the causal-role account are each correct about a different but legitimate notion of function? Or should I abandon the etiological account, and if so, what do I say about the function/accident distinction that made it attractive? I want the costs of each option made explicit.
