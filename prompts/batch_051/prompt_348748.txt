I'm working through a section of Simone de Beauvoir's The Second Sex with a small study group, and we keep going in circles over her well-known anti-essentialist claim that womanhood is not a biological condition one is born with but something one becomes. On my reading, if womanhood is something one becomes, then it must be something like an identity one can take up; being a woman would then be a matter of self-understanding, will, or choice rather than anatomy. But one member of the group objected that this makes Beauvoir sound like a liberal individualist, and another said she is describing a process of social formation, not a project of self-creation.

Now I am worried I have been reading the line as a more familiar claim that people can identify however they want, when her actual target was something deeper about freedom and situation. If one becomes a woman rather than being born one, why is not the most natural conclusion that womanhood is optional, or at least a matter of how one relates to one's own conditions? Where exactly does that inference go wrong, if it does?
