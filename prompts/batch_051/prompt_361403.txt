Ok so I'm not sure if this is too in the weeds, but I went down a rabbit hole and its been bugging me. At school we did the interwar period and I ended up reading about the socialist calculation debate. Mises and Hayek argued that a socialist economy can't work because without real markets there are no prices for capital goods, so planners have no way of knowing if resources are being used well. Hayek's version was a bit different though: he said the relevant knowledge is scattered across millions of people in little local bits, some of it the sort of thing people couldn't even write down, and the price system is what gathers it all up. Then Lange replied that you don't actually need real markets for that. A Central Planning Board could set prices itself, raise them when there's a shortage and lower them when there's a surplus, and tell managers of state-owned firms to produce where price equals marginal cost and to keep costs as low as possible. So in theory the board can find the right prices by trial and error.

I gather Hayek later said Lange was assuming the data were already sitting there waiting to be used, when the real problem is that competition discovers them in the first place, plus managers would have no incentive to actually follow the rules. And I've seen people say Lange was mostly replying to Mises rather than to Hayek's later version.

Anyway, here's my actual question. Back then no central planner could watch every transaction in a country. Now suppose a government had real-time data on basically every purchase and every factory's inputs and outputs, plus a modern AI/optimisation system that could actually process it all. Does Hayek's argument still go through? Which specific premise gets hit by that development? Is it just the calculation side, or does the incentive side survive as well? And if the original argument doesn't fully survive, is there a weaker Hayek-ish claim that does?
