I'm a retired chemist who has recently gotten into philosophy, and I've been trying to turn Davidson's essay What Metaphors Mean into the kind of derivation I'm used to from the lab. Here is the argument I end up with, using the sentence My lawyer is a shark as the example.

(1) If the sentence had a metaphorical meaning over and above it's literal meaning, a competent listener could in principle put that extra meaning into other words.
(2) But every proposed paraphrase — he's predatory, he's ruthless, he's tenacious, he gets what he wants by intimidation — either narrows the metaphor down or changes the subject; none of them captures what the sentence actually does to a hearer.
(3) If something can't be captured in a paraphrase, then it isn't a determinate meaning at all.
(4) So the sentence has no metaphorical meaning; it just has its false literal meaning plus whatever non-semantic effects it happens to produce.
(5) Therefore the Lakoff and Johnson picture must be wrong — the one where metaphor is systematic conceptual mapping (argument understood in terms of war, time in terms of money) that helps constitute how we think and mean.

I keep feeling premise (2) is doing two jobs at once, but I can't say exactly where the argument slips. Is the argument valid? If it is, which premise should a conceptual metaphor theorist reject? If it isn't, where exactly does it go wrong? And either way, how should a Lakoff-style theorist push back? Please give me the answer in American English.
