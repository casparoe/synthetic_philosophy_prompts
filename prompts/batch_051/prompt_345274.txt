As a novelist trying to write a scene between two readers of Maimonides’ Guide of the Perplexed, I need a dialogue that actually scratches under the surface of his view on animal sacrifice. The passage I care about is in part III, chapter 32, where Maimonides argues that the sacrificial laws were a concession to the Israelites’ long habit of worshiping through animal offerings—he says something like God didn’t demand they drop sacrifice all at once, since that would have been as unrealistic as asking someone to suddenly stop eating solid food. One of my characters reads this as flatly deflationary: the sacrificial system has no intrinsic value, it’s just a tolerated phase God used to wean people off idolatry. The other reads it differently: Maimonides still sees the system as genuinely transforming, training people’s minds and bodies away from external worship toward something purer, so it’s not merely a concession to weakness but a kind of therapeutic discipline. Write the dialogue between them, with actual back-and-forth, neither one a strawman. The question I want them to sharpen through the exchange is whether Maimonides is stuck saying God commanded something not good in itself, or whether the “concession” reading can be defended without making sacrifice worthless. Let one of them probe what the other does with the fact that Maimonides still bothers to explain the details of the sacrificial laws in the Guide at all.
