I'm a software engineer, and my professional default when I hit an unfamiliar technical problem is to find the settled consensus among experienced practitioners rather than re-derive everything from first principles. Some time ago I noticed I had started carrying the same policy over to moral questions. When I'm morally uncertain — whether a practice around user data is permissible, whether I should be giving away much more of my income, whether a work decision is exploitative or merely competitive — I look up what professional ethicists tend to conclude, weight their views toward whichever position is more cautious, and act on that. I don't try to reconstruct the underlying arguments myself: I have no formal training in philosophy, and I can't do that competently in the time available. My actual rule is stricter than it sounds. My default credence in a moral claim is the expert consensus as I understand it, and I only depart from that consensus when the stakes are small enough that I can afford to be wrong on my own terms.

A friend says this isn't humility but a category mistake with extra steps. On her view, philosophers have no special authority on moral questions, so what I call a consensus is really just a sample of people who are unusually good at articulating whichever conclusion already feels least uncomfortable to me; the weighting-toward-caution is where my own preferences sneak back in. I take the point, but I can't tell whether dropping the policy would leave me with something better or just with overconfidence in my own untrained judgment. So I want a verdict on the policy itself, not on any particular moral issue: under what conditions, if any, is this a defensible way for a non-specialist to form moral beliefs under uncertainty? Where would it fail systematically? Is there a repaired version — say, treating expert consensus as one piece of evidence rather than as a default — that keeps the benefit of not having to become an ethicist while avoiding the charge that I'm laundering a preference for caution? And is there any way to tell from the inside whether I'm genuinely deferring or just rationalizing whichever conclusion lets me keep my job and my spending habits?
