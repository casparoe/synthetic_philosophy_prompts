So I'm a software engineer with zero philosophy background, and I keep thinking about a legal idea I came across: a criminal law can be struck down for being too vague (void for vagueness, I think). To me a law is basically an API spec—if the caller can't tell which inputs are legal, the person who wrote the spec screwed up. But when I go through concrete cases, my own verdicts dont line up, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them.

My verdicts:

- A posted speed limit of 30 mph: fine. That 31 is illegal and 30 is legal is just an edge case, not a vagueness problem.
- A law that says no vehicles are allowed in a park: fine. Skatebords and e-scooters are edge cases, and every term has edge cases.
- A law against reckless driving: fine. "Reckless" is fuzzy, but juries seem to apply an ordinary-careful-person standard and that works well enough.
- A law against driving faster than is sensible for the conditions: fine, even though it's almost pure judgment. This one should bother me, but it doesn't.
- A city ordinance against loitering with intent to commit a crime: not fine. It smells like it gives cops a license to stop whoever looks suspicious.
- A law against annoying conduct on public transit: not fine. That's basically criminalizing being irritating.
- A law against unreasonable noise after 10 pm: fine. Which is strange, because "unreasonable" is doing a lot of work here.
- A law against disturbing the peace: not fine. And this feels almost like the same law as the noise one, so now I'm confused.

My working rule is something like: vague laws about physical safety and noise are okay; vague laws about annoyance, suspicion, and taste are not. But that feels ad hoc, like I'm just sorting cases by what I happen to dislike.

Is there a principle that gets all of these except maybe one or two? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn? And what does your principle say about a law against disrupting a public meeting, which I keep going back and forth on?
