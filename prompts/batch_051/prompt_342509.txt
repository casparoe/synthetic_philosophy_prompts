I'm a high school student in a philosophy elective, and we have been reading P. F. Strawson's classic essay about freedom and resentment; one part of it has been stuck in my head. As I understand him, Strawson argues that the long debate over whether moral responsibility is compatible with determinism mostly misses the point. Blame and praise, he claims, do not wait for a metaphysical answer; they are carried by the reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation, gratitude, forgiveness — that are embedded in ordinary human involvement with other people. A hard determinist may announce that nobody is ever truly blameworthy, but she will still resent a friend's betrayal and still expect an apology, because the participant stance is not a theory she can simply decide to leave behind. For Strawson, the real question is not whether responsibility is possible but when it becomes appropriate to suspend that participant stance and adopt the objective stance, treating someone as a thing to be managed or cured rather than as an agent to be reasoned with and blamed.

But here is a development I keep returning to, and I cannot tell whether it damages the argument or leaves it untouched. Suppose mood- and impulse-regulating medicine becomes precise enough that a person can, without resolving the metaphysics of determinism at all, selectively dampen or strengthen these very reactive attitudes — weaken her resentment after being wronged, or heighten her indignation at injustice, much as a doctor can already modulate an overactive immune response. If the reactive attitudes can be controlled in this targeted way, does Strawson's argument still go through? I am trying to figure out which premise is actually under pressure: the empirical claim that these attitudes are inescapable features of human life, or the conceptual claim that holding someone responsible just consists in the appropriateness of these attitudes. Is there a repaired version of the argument that survives even if the attitudes become partly optional, and what would it say? I am interested in what happens to the argument itself, not in whether such drugs should exist or be used.
