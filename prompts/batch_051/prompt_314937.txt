I teach Aristotle's Politics, and there is a passage in Book I that I have been pressing on for some time. In the English translation of the Politics available in the Perseus Digital Library, at 1254b (Bekker numbering), Aristotle writes:

"For he is by nature a slave who is capable of belonging to another (and that is why he does so belong), and who participates in reason so far as to apprehend it but not to possess it; for the animals other than man are subservient not to reason, by apprehending it, but to feelings. And also the usefulness of slaves diverges little from that of animals; bodily service for the necessities of life is forthcoming from both, from slaves and from domestic animals alike."

From this passage, and from the lines immediately before it, I extract the following criteria for being a natural slave, as opposed to someone who is enslaved merely by law or by conquest. A natural slave (i) is by nature such as to belong to another, and does in fact belong to another; (ii) participates in reason only to the point of apprehending it — he can follow the reason of another — but does not possess reason as a deliberative capacity for setting ends of his own; (iii) is useful for bodily service for the necessities of life, his usefulness differing little from that of a domestic animal; and (iv) is benefited by being ruled in this way, as the immediately preceding lines say the body is benefited by the soul's rule.

Now consider a case and tell me whether it qualifies. Suppose a firm has built what I will call a domestic administrator: an artificial agent with genuine linguistic and practical competence, not a present-day model. It is owned outright by the firm. It can parse and execute complex, open-ended instructions in natural language, including instructions with evaluative and conditional content; in that sense it apprehends the reason expressed in the instruction it receives. But it is constitutionally incapable of deliberating about whether to follow those instructions or of forming ends of its own: when nothing is being asked of it, it is not trying to do anything, and it could never come to regard itself as the author of its tasks. The firm uses it for standing, low-level work that keeps the enterprise going — sorting and routing correspondence, reconciling records, scheduling, and maintaining routine code. Let it be stipulated that the system functions better when directed than when left to choose its own activities, because by design it has no competence for the latter.

Work through the four criteria one at a time and say whether the domestic administrator is a natural slave on Aristotle's criteria. Be precise about where the analogy holds, where it fails, and whether each failure is merely contingent or reveals what the criterion was really tracking. In particular, I want to know whether "participates in reason so far as to apprehend it but not to possess it" applies to such a system only by an equivocation on "reason" or "apprehend," and whether the "by nature" clause is doing the real work or is just an artifact of Aristotle's biology. Then give a verdict: does the case show that the criteria themselves need amending, and if so, how should they be amended? Throughout, separate what is clearly settled in the text from what is genuinely contested among scholars, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
