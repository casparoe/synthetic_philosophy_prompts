The passage is from John Maynard Keynes, *A Treatise on Probability* (1921), Chapter VI, "The Weight of Arguments." In it, he writes:

> The question to be answered, however, is this—if the evidences upon which a probability is founded are of such a character that they do not admit of being weighed one against another, is it still possible to compare the weights of different arguments?… The theory of probability and the theory of induction are but one theory; for the probability of an argument is not a property of the argument itself, but of the relation between the argument and the conclusion. The weight of an argument is, however, a property of the argument itself.

I am not certain this quotation is letter-perfect, since I last transcribed it some years ago, so I want the exercise to treat the wording as the student's own paraphrase and not as a verbatim quotation from Keynes. The student is then asked to answer, in essay form, whether Keynes's distinction commits him to a two-dimensional picture of rational confidence, and whether weight can matter for action if the probability is unchanged.

"Keynes's distinction between probability and weight is clear enough: probability measures the support a conclusion gets from evidence, while weight measures how much evidence there is. So if I flip a coin and have only seen it land heads once, my probability for heads on the next toss is 1/2, but my confidence has low weight because my evidence is thin. If I have seen a thousand flips split evenly between heads and tails, probability is still 1/2, but the weight is high. Since weight tracks the amount of evidence, it is really just the confidence interval around a probability estimate. For that reason, Keynes is committed to a two-dimensional account of rational belief, because an agent needs both a probability and a weight to know how stable the probability is. However, the distinction has no real consequence for action. If two agents assign 1/2 probability to heads, they will be willing to bet at the same odds, regardless of whether their evidence is thin or abundant. Weight affects only how much they might revise if new evidence arrives, not what they should do now.
