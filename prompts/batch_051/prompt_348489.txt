I'm a third-year economics student, and in my history of economic thought seminar we've been reading the socialist calculation debate. A classmate keeps defending the Hayekian side with an analogy: the market is basically a communications network. Each price is a compressed signal carrying dispersed local knowledge—where a crop failed, what a new furnace costs, what households can no longer afford—and the price system as a whole computes an allocation that no central planner could compute. On this picture, a Lange-style planning board that adjusts prices by trial and error is just running an intercom system disconnected from the actual information. I'd like a careful audit of how far this analogy holds. Map the correspondences explicitly: what plays the role of sender, receiver, message, channel, noise, and the computation itself. Then identify where the mapping breaks, what the analogy quietly imports that a communications network doesn't have—property rights, money, default, and especially the incentive to act on the signal rather than merely receive it—and whether the conclusion, that no planning board could replace markets even in principle, survives once the disanalogies are in view. If I'm blurring Mises's calculation argument together with Hayek's knowledge argument, or if the analogy can support only one of them, say so rather than making the comparison work by force.
