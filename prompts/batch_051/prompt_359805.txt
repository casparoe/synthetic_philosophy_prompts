For a seminar I teach on the concept of democratic backsliding, I would like you to work through a problem in miniature: how a monitoring body's past rulings commit it to a verdict on a new case. Over the past two decades, an imagined body, the Meridian Democratic Standards Panel, has classified national episodes each year as either "democratic backsliding" or "democratic stress falling short of backsliding." I have condensed its rulings below. The Panel insists that it applies a single, non-partisan standard; its critics maintain that the label is, in practice, applied on partisan grounds. The parties involved are fictional composites, but the pattern will be familiar.

1. Alba. A right-nationalist party with a parliamentary supermajority rewrote the constitution, enlarged and packed the constitutional court, redrew districts to its own advantage, and converted the public broadcaster into a party organ. Ruling: backsliding.

2. Boralia. A right-nationalist government with a narrow majority lowered the judicial retirement age in order to purge senior judges, replaced senior civil servants, and turned state media into government mouthpieces; opposition parties remained competitive, large protests continued, and outside observers judged the ballot count itself accurate. Ruling: backsliding.

3. Carith. A long-serving right-authoritarian leader won a referendum establishing an executive presidency, governed by emergency decree after a failed coup, and imprisoned opposition politicians, journalists, and judges; elections continued under heavily skewed conditions. Ruling: backsliding.

4. Dunmara. A right-populist president attacked the courts and the press, leaned on election administrators, and refused in advance to commit to accepting the result; the courts and subnational election officials held firm, the opposition won the next election, and power was transferred. Ruling: democratic stress, not backsliding.

5. Ephrata. A left-populist government with a narrow majority lowered the judicial retirement age to replace senior judges, installed allies at the public broadcaster, and used emergency decrees to bypass a hostile legislature; it then lost the next election, and the incoming government reversed much of what it had done. Ruling: democratic stress, not backsliding.

Now consider a new case, Fenwick. After a clean and well-administered election, a left-populist coalition takes office with a two-seat majority. It passes a judicial reform retiring most senior judges and replacing them with appointees loyal to the coalition, and it shifts constitutional review to a chamber staffed by those appointees. The electoral commission remains independent, the private press remains pluralistic, opposition parties campaign freely, and there is no violence against opponents. The government has now been in office four years without a further national election. Its reformed court has occasionally ruled against the government in minor matters but has upheld the judicial reform itself.

Please tell me what the Panel would rule about Fenwick, or should rule for consistency with its own precedents, and lay out your reasoning. I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the following: (i) the competing rules that fit all five precedents equally well; (ii) what each rule predicts for Fenwick and why; (iii) your best guess about the Panel's ruling, including the closest precedent and the principle the Panel would be adopting either way; and (iv) the single further fictional case that would most cleanly discriminate between the surviving rules.
