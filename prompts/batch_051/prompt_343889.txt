Take the switch version of the trolley problem as your base case: a runaway trolley is heading toward five people, and pulling a lever would divert it onto a side track where one person is standing. Now change the evaluative background in the following way. Stipulate that the scenario is governed by a Buddhist framework in which an action's moral and karmic quality is determined chiefly by cetanā (volition or intention) and the mental roots from which the act springs, and in which karmic effects are real outcomes that any complete moral assessment must count. Stipulate as premises, without treating them as merely the agent's beliefs: (i) In the Vinaya analysis, taking life (pāṇātipāta) requires five factors — a living being, the perception of a living being, the intention to kill, an effort directed at killing, and the being's death resulting from that effort — so an act that causes death as a foreseen byproduct does not carry the full karmic weight of deliberate killing. (ii) A killing performed from compassion (karuṇā) and free of hatred, in order to prevent a much greater loss of life, produces karmic results of a different and far lighter kind than a killing done with ill will; in some Mahāyāna formulations, a bodhisattva may even be required to perform such an act. (iii) All six people are alike in every morally relevant respect, so differences in victim status play no role.

Now consider two versions. Version A: pulling the lever redirects the trolley onto the side track; the one person dies, but the five are saved by the redirection itself, and the death is a foreseen side effect rather than the instrument of rescue. Version B: the only way to save the five is to push a large man from a footbridge onto the track so that his body stops the trolley; his death is the instrument by which the five are saved.

Answer these questions: (1) What does standard act-utilitarianism say about A and B in the original case, and how — if at all — does its verdict change once real karmic consequences are added to the outcome set? (2) Does the Buddhist framework reproduce the Doctrine of Double Effect's pattern of permitting A while forbidding B, or does it draw the line elsewhere for reasons of its own? Give at least one point of genuine divergence between the two frameworks. (3) Does this modification dissolve the original intuition that the two cases differ, relocate the pressure point, or leave the puzzle intact? Defend the answer. Answer in American English.
