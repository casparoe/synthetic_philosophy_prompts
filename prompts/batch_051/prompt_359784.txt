After thirty-some years building fisheries and ocean models, I retired and, to my old colleagues mild amusement, started reading philosophy of science. The thing I keep chewing on is Milton Friedman's F-twist — the claim that a model's assumptions don't need to be realistic, and that models should be judged by their predictions rather than by whether their premises describe the world. I want to defend it. Some of the best models I ever used were built on assumptions we knew were false: fish treated as identical particles, an ocean reduced to a few well-mixed boxes, mixing coefficients pulled out of the air. They were still useful, precisely because we judged them by what they got right, not by how true they were. There's a clean criterion there: don't ask whether the model is true, ask whether it works. Demanding realistic assumptions seems like a recipe for models that are either intractable or useless.

Yet I also can't shake the opposite intuition, and it comes from the times our models let us down. We'd calibrate a stock-assessment model in a normal decade, it would predict catches beautifully, and then a warm-water regime would arrive and the errors would blow up — not randomly, but because the false assumptions we'd shrugged off (no temperature dependence, no species interactions, constant catchability) were exactly the things that mattered in the new regime. I had no way, from predictive success alone, of telling whether I had a model that would transfer or one that was overfit to its calibration window. The intuition I'm stuck with is this: unrealistic assumptions are harmless when you stay inside the tested domain, but for extrapolation and intervention you need the assumptions to be right in the relevant respects, and "right in the relevant respects" is not something prediction alone tells you.

Here's my actual question. I'm attracted to the F-twist, but I don't want to just wave off this intuition as sour grapes from a modeler who got burned. Tell me, with a definite position and reasons, which should yield. Should I debunk the intuition as some kind of selection effect, or as overgeneralizing from a few failures? Should I amend or abandon the F-twist? Or is there a reading on which "assumptions don't matter" and "assumptions matter for extrapolation" are compatible? I want you to commit to a resolution and defend it, not lay out the options and leave me to pick.
