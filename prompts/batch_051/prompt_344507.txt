Ive been reading William James's Varieties of Religious Experience, the chapters on mysticism, and he lists four marks of mystical states. Two of them seem to me to be in real tension. He says mystical states are ineffable: the person who has them can't give an adequate report of what the experience was like in words, the experience defies expression. But he also says they have a noetic quality: they are states of knowledge, they give insight into depths of truth the person didn't have before. That seems like a weird pair to put together. I made a little argument that the knowledge part has to be pretty hollow. Here are the steps:

(1) If an experience is ineffable, its content can't be put into words.
(2) If its content can't be put into words, the person having it can't say what it is they learned.
(3) If the person can't say what they learned, then either they didn't learn anything you could call a fact, or what they have is just a feeling of certainty that they learned something.
(4) A feeling of certainty by itself isn't knowledge, since you can feel certain about false things or about nothing in particular.
(5) So a mystical experience can't give someone knowledge unless they can express at least some of its content afterward.

Is this argument any good? If not, which step exactly is the weak one? I keep going back and forth between step 2 being too demanding and step 4 being too harsh about what knowledge is. Maybe James means something else by noetic that I'm flattening out. Be specific about the reading of each step and where a defender of the knowledge claim should dig in.
