I am a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist is a philologist appointed to a commission charged with reforming the orthography of a small European language in the years around 1900. The central disagreement among my characters concerns what a script ought to encode: one faction urges that an alphabet should represent pronunciation directly, with one symbol for each distinctive sound; a second insists that spelling must preserve the identity of stems and affixes even when pronunciation shifts across contexts; a third argues for minimizing disruption so that older documents remain legible. I want the commission's arguments to be technically serious even when they are wrong, and I find that I need the underlying specification before I can dramatize the dispute convincingly.

Please write for me the desiderata that an adequate theory of a writing system must satisfy. By a writing system I mean any conventionalized mapping between a language and visible marks, whether alphabetic, syllabic, morphosyllabic, or otherwise, and by a theory I mean an account of what such a system encodes and what makes an instance of it successful or defective for a stated purpose. Set aside taxonomy and give me the specification: the phenomena the theory must handle, with the case or consideration behind each — allophony, sandhi, homophony, morphophonemic alternation, borrowed sounds and loanwords, tone and stress, and the difference between phonemic and phonetic representation. Include the distinctions a theory cannot collapse, such as a script's inventory versus its mapping rules, spelling conventions versus sound change, encoding a language as a system versus transcribing an utterance, and what a reform should accomplish versus what an existing orthography does accomplish. Also specify the cases any adequate theory ought to be able to discuss intelligibly, at least as they are standardly described: the deep orthography of English, the shallow orthographies of Finnish and Spanish, the consonant-only systems usual in Arabic and Hebrew, the featural design of Hangul, and the broadly logographic character of Chinese.

Then state which of these requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of particular camps — phonemic transparency, morphological stability, etymological visibility, ease of acquisition for readers and writers, and continuity with the existing written record. Finally, address what I most need for the novel: whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, so that no single orthography can be at once fully phonemically faithful, morphologically stable, and historically continuous, and identify which requirement does the most to exclude an otherwise attractive design. Keep the emphasis on the criteria themselves and on the reasoning that makes each a genuine requirement, rather than on grading real writing systems; I will apply the list to the invented language myself. I would appreciate the answer in American English.
