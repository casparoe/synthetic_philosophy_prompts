I've been rereading the part of Wealth of Nations where Adam Smith says commerce does more than enrich people, and I got into an argument I can't settle. The passage is from Book III, Chapter 4:

"Commerce and manufactures gradually introduced order and good government, and, with them, the liberty and security of individuals, among the inhabitants of the country, who had before lived almost in a continual state of war with their neighbors, and of servile dependence upon their superiors. This, though it has been the least observed, is by far the most important of all their effects."

My friend says Smith's explanation right after this is that, before commerce, a great proprietor has nothing to do with his surplus except maintain a crowd of retainers and dependants, which is why the countryside is full of people who have to obey him. Once commerce gives him something to buy, he spends the surplus on that instead, dismisses the retainers, and the whole servile dependence starts to unravel. So far so good.

But then my friend constructed this argument:

(1) If Smith's mechanism is simply "redirect the lord's surplus away from maintaining armed dependents," then the civilizing effect should not depend on what the lord spends it on.
(2) So if the first trade goods available in a feudal region were morally corrupting things—say, a gambling house, opium, imported relics, or paid access to a fashionable court—the same mechanism should produce order, good government, liberty and security.
(3) But that's absurd. We wouldn't say a region was civilized because its barons bankrupted themselves at a casino or became opium-addicted.
(4) So either Smith's argument proves too much—vice civilizes whenever it drains surplus from retainers—or his explanation is incomplete and needs a hidden moral condition on what kind of commerce counts.

I want to say the argument goes wrong somewhere, but I can't figure out where, because Smith's own text right after the passage really does sound like the mechanism doesn't care what the lord buys. He even says the lords traded the upkeep of a thousand men for diamond buckles or things equally frivolous. If frivolous diamond buckles can civilize, why not a casino? Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me if I want to keep Smith's basic idea that commerce can civilize without making people selfish?
