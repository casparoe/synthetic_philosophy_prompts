I've ended up defending the claim that a language could in principle lack indexicals — no 'I', 'now', 'here', 'this', and no tense devices anchored to the speech situation — without semantic loss, provided it has rich descriptive resources, names, explicit coordinates for times and places, and gesture. I mostly got here by thinking about tenseless languages and about 'pronouns' in Japanese that behave like ordinary nouns, not through any deep commitment to descriptivism. I want a map of what would count against this view. I know the usual suspects — Perry's amnesiac shopper, Lewis's two gods, Kaplan's character/content apparatus — but I want to know which of those actually threaten my claim as opposed to threatening the stronger claim that there's no self-locating belief at all, and which further results, about belief reports, animal communication, perception, or other context-sensitive expressions, would be decisive rather than merely awkward. I'd also like to know whether the view collapses into 'indexicals are just convenient abbreviations,' and if it does whether that's fatal. Be honest about weather I've qualified the view into unfalsifiability, and say what a defender should concede and what they can dismiss. I'd personally be inclined to bite the bullet on belief reports, so tell me if that's a bullet the view can't survive.
