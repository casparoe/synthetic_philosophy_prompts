Epistemic status: my own working view, assembled from years of reading rationalist-adjacent arguments about long-run trends in violence, and never tested as a set. I would like an audit.

Here are the commitments I currently hold on whether the world has become less violent, what should count as violence, and how the trend should be measured.

1. There is a determinate fact of the matter about whether the world as a whole is less violent now than, say, two centuries ago. How eras rank is not merely an artefact of the definitions and denominators one happens to select.

2. An act counts as violence when it is physical injury or death intentionally inflicted by an agent on another person, either by direct force or by force aimed at identifiable victims. War deaths and homicides count; insults, property damage, and ordinary market competition do not.

3. For the same reason, I regard so-called structural violence as a metaphor, not a species of violence. Harms that flow predictably from institutions, inequality, or background economic arrangements are not violence unless some agent directs them at identifiable victims.

4. For the long-run trend, the right quantity is the per-capita rate: the annual risk of violent death or serious violent injury faced by a randomly chosen person. Raw totals mislead because the population has grown enormously, so "the world is less violent" is best read as a claim about the risk to a typical individual.

5. Absolute numbers are not negligible, though. When I compare two episodes, or decide where to spend resources to prevent deaths, I compare expected absolute deaths, and I am willing to call the episode with far more dead "the greater violence" even when its per-capita rate is lower. I take (4) and (5) to answer different questions, so I do not think they conflict.

6. I exclude suicide from my violence statistics. This is not because suicide is unimportant, but because the phenomenon is self-directed rather than interpersonal.

7. I include in my violence statistics deaths from an industrial disaster that followed from a company knowingly ignoring serious safety defects, even though the harm was not aimed at named individuals.

8. I also include deaths from a deliberately imposed famine — one caused by a naval blockade or a forced collectivisation, for instance — even when the policy's goal was extraction or military victory rather than the deaths themselves.

I can already see places where these might rub against one another, but I suspect some of the friction is illusory and some of the genuine tensions are not where they appear. I have no strong attachment to any single item in the list. Please go through the set and tell me:

- which pairs are in genuine tension, and how severe each tension is;
- for each genuine tension, what the least costly repair would be and what it would cost me;
- which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and why;
- which single revision would remove the most tension for the lowest cost;
- whether any one commitment is quietly doing much more work than the others.

Please write your answer in British English.
