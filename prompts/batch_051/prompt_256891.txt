So my wife has started taking our kids to her grandma's church, and my son came home last week asking why I don't think God exists. Instead of doing the dinner-table version, I've been re-reading Anselm's Proslogion and Gaunilo's reply, becuase the only thing I actually remember is the lost island joke, and I'm no longer sure why it's supposed to land. Before I read another twelve papers on whether the argument works, I want the spec: what does a Gaunilo-style parody actually have to get right to count as an objection to Anselm's argument, rather than just a punchline? I keep seeing people either wave the island around or dismiss it as "not the same logical form" without ever saying what the form is.

Here's the passage I keep getting stuck on, from Gaunilo of Marmoutier, In Behalf of the Fool, replying to Anselm:

"Hence, your example of the painter who already has in his understanding what he is to paint cannot agree with this argument. For the picture, before it is made, is contained in the artificer's art itself; and any such thing, existing in the art of an artificer, is nothing but a part of his understanding itself."

Write me the requirements list. For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it — which step of Anselm's argument it has to engage, and which distinction it can't collapse (like the difference between having a phrase in your understanding and understanding that the thing exists, which is what I think Gaunilo is getting at with the painter). Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just camp preferences, the way some people insist the parody has to be about a finite kind of thing while others think that's beside the point. Then tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidate parodies, flag any pair of requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, and briefly run the lost island against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria, not the scorecard.
