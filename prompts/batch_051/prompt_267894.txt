For the past year, my reading group has been working through Adam Smith’s The Theory of Moral Sentiments, and our discussions now stall at exactly the same place every few weeks. The focal point is the passage in Part III where Smith distinguishes the man without, whose jurisdiction rests on the desire for actual praise and the aversion to actual blame, from the man within the breast, whose jurisdiction rests on the desire for praiseworthiness and the aversion to blameworthiness. One camp reads the man within as an internalised social spectator: I imagine how a real other would judge me, then abstract from local bias until I have a corrected representative of public judgment. A second camp reads it as a normative ideal: the impartial spectator is not any actual social voice, however corrected, but a standard of what an informed and disinterested spectator ought to feel. A third camp argues that Smith deliberately blends these roles, moving between a causal account of how conscience arises and a normative account of what conscience demands. The strange thing is that all three camps agree on the translation, the surrounding argument, and the broader chapter, yet no one has moved in months. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about Smith’s text at all, but about whether an account of moral judgment can be derived from an account of social psychology. I’d like you to diagnose the structure of this dispute rather than adjudicate it. Is it a substantive disagreement about Smith’s intended concept, a conceptual disagreement about whether impartiality can be reduced to idealised social agreement, or a case of the parties asking different questions under one heading? In particular, are they disagreeing about what Smith believed, about what the impartial spectator must be if his theory is to work, or about whether moral judgment is ultimately an explanatory or a justificatory notion? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a sharper distinction, an argument that Smith himself runs the two functions together, or an honest acknowledgment that the text underdetermines the answer and the real debate is a philosophical one about conscience? Please answer in British English.
