I run the engineering review unit at a regional transit authority. My reviewers examine inspection records—photographs, ultrasonic readings, corrosion measurements—from bridges, tunnels, and rail lines, and assign a risk level to each reported defect; those levels determine whether we keep a line running, impose speed restrictions, or take a component out of service. The problem I have not been able to solve is disagreement. Two qualified reviewers regularly look at the same record and reach materially different judgments: one calls a feature an active crack with a high failure probability, the other calls it a benign casting flaw with a low one. At present we settle these disputes by whatever happens in the room—seniority, argumentative stamina, who did the most recent field visit—and I do not think that will look defensible when we are audited after an incident. I know there is a philosophical literature on how a rational inquirer should respond to disagreement with an epistemic peer, with conciliationist views on one side and steadfast views on the other, and that it has developed distinctions between first-order evidence, higher-order evidence supplied by the disagreement itself, and questions about whether two people who reached different conclusions from the same record really were peers or really did assess it independently.

Write me a reusable decision procedure my review leads can actually run in the weekly review meeting, where we have roughly an hour per disputed finding and the lead must record which step determined the outcome, with any departure from the procedure justified in writing to our safety committee. The procedure should take them through a fixed sequence of questions: whether the two reviewers had the same evidence, whether each judgment was reached independently, whether the dispute is about the probability of failure or about what the feature is, whether the gap matters for the action we would take, and anything further you think the epistemology requires. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer and what to do with a no or an unclear. The users are engineers with no philosophical training, so the procedure must operate in terms of records, photos, measurements, and our risk tables, not in terms of credences and peerhood—though you may keep the theory visible in explanatory asides if that helps. Be explicit about which steps are genuine judgment calls that cannot be reduced to a rule, and about the points where conciliationist and steadfast approaches would pull the procedure in different directions and which one you are following there. Then run the procedure on three disputes: (1) two equally credentialed engineers independently review the same forty photographs and measurement logs; one assigns a seventy percent chance that a weld indication is an active crack, the other assigns fifteen percent and says it is a lamination artifact. (2) The same kind of dispute arises between an engineer with twenty-five years of experience and one with three; the senior engineer rests much of his judgment on a pattern he says he has learned to recognize and cannot fully articulate. (3) An engineer who walked the bridge and inspected the area in changing light disputes a colleague who works only from photographs, insisting the light revealed a crack that the photos flatten out. In each case, show what the procedure outputs—a risk level, a decision to gather more evidence, a referral to a named person—and tell me plainly where the procedure gives an answer the team will resist and why it gives that answer anyway. Close by saying what the procedure cannot do: the disputes it cannot settle, and the failures it will not prevent.
