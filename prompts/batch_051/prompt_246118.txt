A jurisdiction's corporate sentencing council is considering a new penalty for serious corporate wrongdoing such as price-fixing, large-scale fraud, or an environmental disaster. In addition to any fine, a court could impose a narrative accountability order. A court-appointed examiner, paid by the company and given access to internal correspondence, meeting records, and current and former employees, would write a public statement in the first-person plural, as though the company were speaking: how the offending course of action was chosen, what incentives and lines of authority produced it, which alternatives were considered and rejected and on what reasoning, and which positions or committees did what. The statement would not be an apology and would not be required to express remorse. The chief executive would have to read it aloud in full at the next annual general meeting and remain to answer questions from shareholders about it, and the statement would have to be displayed prominently on the company's website and reproduced in its annual report for five years. Misleading the examiner, or later repudiating the account without evidence, would be a separate offense carrying additional penalties.

The proposal is defended on the view that if a corporation can properly be blamed for anything, it ought to be able to give an account of what it did and why, and that forcing the corporation to do so through its own senior leadership is more honest than a fine, which a large company can absorb as a cost of doing business. Assume for the sake of the analysis that the legal machinery functions as described and that procedural appeals are not the issue.

I would like not an abstract verdict on the proposal but a careful account of how it would actually play out if it became a normal part of corporate sentencing. What incentives would it create before misconduct is contemplated and after an order is imposed? How would companies, executives, and their lawyers game it, soften it, or turn it to their advantage? What second-order effects would it have on internal documentation, organizational memory, and the distribution of blame inside firms? Where would it fail first, and which of those failures could be repaired without gutting the mechanism? The underlying philosophical question is whether a corporation, as distinct from the individuals who compose it, can be the object of blame, and I would like the analysis to return to that question rather than treat it as decoration.
