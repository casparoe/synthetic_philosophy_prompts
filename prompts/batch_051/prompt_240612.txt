I'm putting together a unit on the idea of societal collapse and its critics, and I want to bring a work of fiction into dialogue with the scholarly debate. The popular narrative about collapse — the one attached to Easter Island or the Classic Maya — treats it as a terminal event: a society overreaches its resource base, its institutions fail, and what follows is a decisive rupture, after which the society is gone. Over the past couple of decades, though, archaeologists and historians have pushed back on that picture. They argue that what looks like collapse from a distance often looks, up close, like transformation: populations relocate rather than die off, political systems are replaced while everyday practices continue, people rework old institutions under new conditions, and the label collapse itself smuggles in assumptions about what counts as a society persisting through change.

I'd like to read Cormac McCarthy's The Road through this debate. On its face the novel seems to give the collapse narrative its starkest form: an unnamed catastrophe has stripped away agriculture, government, law, and settled community, and most survivors appear reduced to bare predation. Yet the whole book is also about what survives that stripping away. The man and the boy maintain small rituals of washing, naming, and memory; they insist on a moral distinction between themselves and others; they act as though they are preserving something through the ruin rather than merely enduring it. I want to know whether the novel ultimately endorses the classic picture of collapse as a terminal break into a state of nature, or whether it complicates that picture by showing the break itself as a kind of transformation in which ethical life persists. Which details of the novel support each side? Where does the novel's moral psychology rest on exactly the assumption about collapse that the critics would challenge, and where does it supply material for their critique on its own? I'm asking for an honest reading that takes the novel and the debate equally seriously, and that is willing to say where the book resists the lens rather than merely illustrating it.
