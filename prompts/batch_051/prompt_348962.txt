I'm retired now, after a career in the physical sciences, and I've been reading my way through the philosophy of AI and risk. My study group keeps coming back to an argument that seems to prove too much, and I'd be grateful for a diagnosis of where it goes wrong.

(1) If we put an AI system in charge of something consequential, we ought to have a proper guarentee that it will not do serious harm.
(2) A guarantee only counts if it holds in the worst case: a system that behaves well on average, or on typical inputs, is not guaranteed at all.
(3) For any realistically trained model there will be some input—adversarial or simply out-of-distribution—on which it does something seriously harmful, and we cannot rule such inputs out in advance.
(4) So, if we insist on worst-case guarantees, no consequential AI deployment is ever justified.
(5) The same reasoning applies to humans: any surgeon, pilot, or control-room operator faces rare conditions—fatigue, ambiguous cases, unusual failures—under which they would make a catastrophic error, and we cannot rule those out in advance either.
(6) So, by parity, no human should be deployed in a consequential role either.
(7) But that conclusion seems absurd; we plainly do, and plainly should, deploy humans in such roles.

So which premise or step should we reject, and why does it look right before you reject it? What do we give up by rejecting it? And if there are recognisable positions in the debate—the safety-engineering view, the average-case or probabilistic view, the 'humans are different because they can be held to account' view—what does each of them have to pay to make this argument come out right? For what it's worth, I'd be reluctant to give up (1) or (3), though I can be talked out of that.

If you could write your answer in British English, I'd appreciate it.
