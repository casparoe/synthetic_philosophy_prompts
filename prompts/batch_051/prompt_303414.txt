My one-sentence thesis: Chalmers's hard/easy distinction is a point about the type of explanation being offered, not a prediction that neuroscience will fail. So you can accept the hard problem as a real explanatory gap without buying property dualism, and finding the neural correlates of consciousness won't close the gap.

The passage I'm pinning my reading to is this, from David Chalmers, "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" (1995):

"If these phenomena were all there was to consciousness, then consciousness would not be much of a problem. Although we do not yet have anything close to a complete explanation of these phenomena, we have a clear idea of how we might go about explaining them. This is why I call these problems the easy problems."

My reading, in short: "easy" doesn't mean "easy for us right now"; it means "the kind of thing a cognitive or neurophysiological model can in principle deliver." I'll argue the hard problem is whatever is left over after every functional property has been given that kind of model, and that the leftover is real even if physicalism is true. Then I'll say the right response to it is to live with it, not to dissolve it.
