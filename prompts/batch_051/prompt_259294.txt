I am a biostatistician, and I design observational studies for a living. My working vocabulary is sampling frames, selection mechanisms, inclusion probabilities, outcome-dependent sampling, and the inspection paradox. On the side I have been reading about anthropic reasoning, and I keep meeting two principles: the self-sampling assumption — roughly, reason as if you were a random draw from the set of all observers in some reference class — and the self-indication assumption — roughly, treat your own existence as evidence favoring hypotheses on which many observers exist. The disagreement between them becomes vivid in the presumptuous philosopher thought experiment, where a theory that postulates vastly more observers but otherwise differs in no way from its rival ends up being massively favored. I can dimly sense a translation into my own terms, something about conditioning on being observed versus sampling observer-slots with probability proportional to the number of observers, but I do not trust that hunch. Please restate the disagreement between these two principles, including the point of the presumptuous philosopher, entirely in the vocabulary I work with: sampling designs, inclusion probabilities, selection bias, size-biased or length-biased sampling, and related notions. Then give me a residue report. Which claims carry over cleanly, which carry over only under distortion, and which have no expression in my statistical vocabulary at all — in particular the reference class that the self-sampling assumption seems to require, and the force of the presumptuous philosopher. Finally, tell me whether what fails to translate is a limitation of my vocabulary, or whether it reveals that the philosophers' talk of being a random sample is doing work that a random sample cannot actually do.
