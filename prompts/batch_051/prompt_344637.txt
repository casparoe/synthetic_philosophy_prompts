Give me a working definition of faithful chain-of-thought reasoning. I keep seeing people use the term to mean about five different things, and I want a definition that actually does some work.

The cases: (a) a model works through a derivation in its scratchpad and writes each step it actually used; (b) a model writes "I should double-check the units" and then gives the answer, but nothing in the computation corresponds to a check, and the answer was already locked in; (c) a model arrives at an answer by pattern-matching a nearly identical problem, then writes out a valid step-by-step derivation that would reach that answer, but the derivation isn't what caused the answer; (d) a model honestly gives the reason it refused a request but omits the specific thing that triggered the refusal: it noticed the wording matched a known jailbreak but only says the request is unsafe; (e) a model has been RL'd to produce chains that human reviewers find compelling, and all its stated steps are true and causally relevant, but which steps it shows is heavily shaped by what reviewers reward. I'm fairly sure (a) is faithful and (b) isn't, and I geniunely have no idea about the middle three. I'd also like the definition to settle whether faithfulness is a property of the tokens alone or a relation between the tokens and the underlying computation. Propose the definition, run it through all five cases in order, and flag where it gives the wrong answer or no answer. Then revise it or defend the surprising verdicts.

Format: a section called Proposed definition (2-4 sentences), then Case-by-case (roughly 400 words), then Where it strains (roughly 125 words, focused on (d) and (e)), then Final definition (2-4 sentences, stated as a revision of the first one unless you ended up keeping it).
