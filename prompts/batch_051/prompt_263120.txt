My ethics professor has spent three weeks trying to convince us that killing in self-defense is morally fine. I get the general gist: lethal force is justified when it's the only way to stop someone who's about to kill you, so the reason you can do it is the same reason you can push someone off a ledge if they're shoving you into traffic — you're not aiming at their death, just at stopping them. But then I went and looked at Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 64 a. 7, and I don't understand how the classic doctrine of double effect is supposed to help with the dumping-the-knife case. Here's the passage that's tripping me up, with my professor's translation:

> Nothing prevents one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is outside the intention. Now moral acts take their species according to what is intended, not according to what is outside the intention, since this is accidental to them. Therefore from the act of self-defense two effects can follow: one the saving of one's own life, the other the killing of the aggressor. Such an act, since its intention is to save one's own life, is not on that account have the character of the unlawful, since it is natural to anything to preserve itself in being as far as possible. And yet an act that is not rightly intended may become unlawful, if a man slay in order to defend his own life: for one has to preserve one's life, so the doing of it may be rendered unlawful, if it be not proportionate to the end. Hence if a man, in defending his own life, uses more violence than necessary, it will be unlawful: whereas if he repels force moderately, his defense will be lawful. Nor is it necessary for salvation that a man omit the act of moderate self-defense in order to avoid killing the other: since one is bound to take more care of one's own life than of another's.

My professor says the official reading is: the killing of your assailant is a *foreseen side effect* of the act whose intended end is saving your own life, so it isn't murder, as long as the force is proportionate to the threat. Fine. But here's my issue. It seems to me that on this reading, the person chasing after your runaway ore cart would *also* have to be off the hook, not the textbook example of someone who gets condemned. If the only difference between the two cases is what you *intend*, and intention is just whatever description of your act you mentally settle on before pulling the trigger, then what stops me from redescribing any killing as self-defense with a foreseen consequence? The mugger's death was just the price of keeping my wallet safe; the rival's death was just the regrettable byproduct of my plan to win the election. Does Aquinas have a principled answer, or is "intended vs. merely foreseen" doing less moral work here than my professor wants it to? If he *does* have an answer, why do so many modern ethicists say the doctrine of double effect can't distinguish the standard trolley cases — and does that same objection apply to the runaway cart?
