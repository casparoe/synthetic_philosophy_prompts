For years I have read adversarial-collaboration write-ups in the rationalist blogosphere, and I have wanted to see the method applied to a philosophical disagreement that is usually run less carefully than, say, debates about AI forecasting. Please draft the public document that a committed Searlean about original intentionality and a committed Dennettian about the intentional stance could jointly issue after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, and that neither would later repudiate.

Frame the two positions as follows. The Searlean holds that biological brains possess original, intrinsic intentionality in virtue of their causal powers, whereas symbols, computers, and other artefacts possess only derived intentionality, borrowed from the minds that designed or interpret them; the Chinese Room is taken to show that manipulating syntax cannot by itself yield semantics. The Dennettian holds that the distinction does no explanatory work: all attributions of intentionality are made from the intentional stance, adopted when an entity's behaviour is usefully predicted by treating it as a roughly rational agent; the appeal to original intentionality is the target of Dennett's two-bitser challenge. Reconstruct the two-bitser as an argument rather than a slogan: state what it charges the Searlean with, what it takes the extra ingredient to be doing, and what test it offers for distinguishing a real explanatory distinction from a relabelling of the boundary one already wanted.

The document should include the following sections.

First, a numbered section of claims both parties can sign. These should be substantive rather than empty: they should cover, at minimum, what the Chinese Room does and does not establish, what the predictive successes of the intentional stance do and do not establish, what both accept about the differences between how we talk about brains and how we talk about artefacts, and what evidence would count against each position.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should state the Searlean view in wording the Searlean would accept, state the Dennettian view in wording the Dennettian would accept, and then isolate the narrowest sub-claim on which they part ways.

Third, a cruxes section. Identify three or four sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete experiment, observation, or thought experiment that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts.

Fourth, a closing assessment of the dispute's status rather than of who wins: say which disagreements are substantive, which are empirical, and which are verbal or trade on different senses of intrinsic, aboutness, or derived.

Force the collaboration into territory where both positions become genuinely strained. Consider, for example, whether an uploaded emulation of a human brain would possess original intentionality on each view, whether a system that maintained and revised its own symbols without any human interpreter would still count as merely derived, and whether evolution is best described as having installed original intentionality in organisms or as a process whose products we interpret from the intentional stance. Where the two camps would make the same prediction, say so; where one side would treat an outcome as a refutation while the other treats it as a clarification of their own view, make that explicit.
