In the preface to The Picture of Dorian Gray, Wilde pretty much says there's no such thing as a moral or an immoral book—only well-written or badly written ones—and that an artist's having ethical sympathies is at best irrelevant and at worst a kind of artistic flaw. In "Of the Standard of Taste," Hume leans the other way: if a poem depicts vicious behavior without any mark of blame or disapproval, that must be allowed to disfigure the poem and rightly lowers our opinion of it. Are these two actually in tension, or are they talking past each other? Give the strongest readings on which they're compatible—different senses of artistic value, evaluating the work versus the content versus the implied author, maybe a difference between moral and aesthetic defects—and then say which reconciliations are honest and which are special pleading. Then apply it to the hard case: a work whose immorality is less in what it depicts than in the attitude it seems to take toward it. Which of the two does that case put more pressure on, and is there any stable rule left after the attempted reconciliation?
