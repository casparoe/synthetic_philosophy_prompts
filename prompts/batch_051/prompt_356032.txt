I'm increasingly sold on the idea that rationality is best trained the way a martial art is trained — you dont get better by memorizing lists of biases or decision-theoretic principles; you get better by drilling small, repeatable moves (the equivalent of katas) until they fire on their own, and then seeing whether they transfer to messy, high-stakes situations. I mean this fairly literally, not as a vibe: the way a boxer drills footwork, I think someone can drill a pre-mortem, a move for catching themselves rationalizing, an outside-view correction, until these become reflexes rather than things they remember to do.

What would count against this view? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they'd actually be, not an even-handed survey. Which empirical findings — say, from the transfer-of-training literature, or about whether rationality is even stable across contexts — should genuinely move me? Which arguments from expertise research or dual-process theory would be fatal as opposed to merely awkward? And which are just generic skepticism I can mostly set aside? I'd also like to know which of these a defender of the martial-art view would themselves admit counts against it, not just what a critic would throw at it. Finally, give me an honest answer about whether the view has been padded into unfalsifiability: if every failed transfer gets explained away with "the training was done wrong" or "transfer is slow and context-bound but real," does the view still risk anything? If it can't be killed by evidence, is that a flaw here or just how claims about skills normally work? Assume I'd only change my mind on genuinely decisive stuff, so give me the hierarchy, with the one or two things that should make me abandon the whole analogy called out clearly.
