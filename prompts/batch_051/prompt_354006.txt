A friend who recently read a popular ethics book sent me this summary of a famous argument, and I want to know how much of it I can safely repeat:

> "Total utilitarianism says we should maximize the total sum of well-being. But Parfit showed this leads to the repugnant conclusion: for any world full of, say, ten billion people living wonderful lives, there must be some much larger world—overcrowded, with everyone living just barely above the threshold of a life worth living—that total utilitarians are committed to calling better, because the sum of well-being is larger. The route there is the mere-addition paradox. Start with a well-off population A. Add a second group B of equally many people whose lives are less happy but still worth living, while leaving A untouched. Since total welfare has gone up, A+B is better than A. Then redistribute welfare evenly across A+B to create a single population at the average level. This doesn't make anyone worse off overall, and it removes inequality, so the new population is at least as good as A+B. By transitivity, this equal-sized, lower-average population is better than A. Repeat this operation many times and you eventually reach a huge population Z whose members have lives barely worth living, and Z is better than A. Since everyone agrees that Z is not better than A, the mere-addition step must be wrong—and with it, total utilitarianism. In fact, any consequentialist view that says adding happy people is an improvement gets driven to the same absurd result."

Something about the phrasing feels off to me, but I don't know population ethics well enough to say where. Could you audit this summary for me? Specifically: (1) which claims in it are accurate, and which are subtly wrong or overstated? (2) Does the mere-addition paradox really run the way he describes—especially the step where the two groups are merged into one population at the average level? (3) Is it fair to say Parfit treated the repugnant conclusion as a straightforward refutation of total utilitarianism, and that everyone agrees the larger population is worse? (4) Does the final sentence follow from what the argument actually shows, or does it overstate the scope of the difficulty? I'd like both a correction of the summary and a sense of what a careful defender of the relevant view would say in response.
