In most legal systems, a private person may lawfully use force, up to and including lethal force, to stop an attack that is unfolding in front of her, but with narrow exceptions she commits a crime if she tracks the attacker down an hour later, seizes him, or hurts him to make him answer for what he did. A police officer may lawfully do all three after the fact and in the state's name. Yet the same state claims a monopoly on legitimate violence even as it permits private self-defense and licenses armed private security. What theory of legitimate violence or political authority does this package of practices presuppose? Does the split between permitted private defense and forbidden private vindication strictly require the view that responding to already-committed wrongs is something only the state may own, or is the practice compatible with several rival theories? And what would have to change in the law if one held instead that the justification of force depends only on what the target has done, not on whether the person using force holds an office?
