We're doing the "fall or transformation" debate in my late antiquity course, and a friend has this argument that seems to show no state ever falls. I dont think the conclusion can be right, but all the steps looked fine when he said them.

(1) A state falls only if there is some feature F that was essential to that state's continuing to exist, and the state lost F at a determinate time.
(2) For Rome, every candidate for F looks bad. The western imperial office was already a puppet of military strongmen by the 460s, the army had been heavily barbarized for decades before 476, and trade and urban life had been thinning out for centuries. Yet Roman law, the Church, the Senate, and the cities all carried on after 476, and the eastern half just kept going.
(3) So Rome never lost any essential feature at a determinate time; Rome didn't fall, it just transformed.
(4) But the same move works for any state you name. The USSR was followed by a Russian state with much of the same bureaucracy, security apparatus, and elite. After the Third Reich there was still a German state, a German civil service, and a German legal order. After 1521 the Aztec noble houses, languages, and populations didn't vanish.
(5) By this reasoning no state has ever fallen: every so-called collapse is just a transformation where some things continued.
(6) But obviously the USSR fell, the Third Reich fell, the Aztec empire fell. That can't seriously be denied.
(7) So one of (1) through (5) has to go. Which one, and what do I pay if I reject it? I'd rather keep (6), and I keep going back and forth about which earlier step is doing the work. I cant shake the sense that Rome and the USSR are different cases, but I can't say how without it sounding like I've already picked a side.
