I'm a PhD student in political theory (so this is sort of my home turf), but I've been reading enough about AI alignment that I want to stress-test an argument I used to find persuasive. No reading lists, please—just the argumentative work.

The argument is the standard liberal-neutrality one. In a pluralistic society, the state shouldn't justify its coercive policies by appeal to any contested view of the good life, because citizens who don't share that view couldn't reasonably be expected to accept those reasons. So the state is supposed to stay neutral between comprehensive doctrines—Christian, utilitarian, Confucian, whatever—and only enforce principles that don't require taking sides on what makes a life go well. I can identify three background premises the argument leans on. First, there's a bounded demos sharing institutions, so the people whose values lose out are fellow citizens rather than distant users. Second, the state is special because it coerces people who can't easily exit. Third, staying neutral is actually feasible: for the genuinely coercive decisions, there are usually nonsectarian reasons available.

Now take a general-purpose AI assistant deployed by a private company to a billion people worldwide. It mostly can't dodge the "should I" questions: should I take this job, how strict should I be with my kid, should I tell my partner this thing, is it okay to cut off this friend. Whatever advice it gives, somebody tuned the model so that some of these answers count as good advice and others don't. "Just defer to each user's stated values" is itself a stance, and "roughly average across users" is majoritarianism about the good life. There's no shared political community the dissatisfied can petition, no public reason they had a hand in shaping, and the company is arguably coercive in effect—try living without algorithmic mediation—even though it isn't backed by law.

So here's the question. Does the liberal neutrality argument survive this change in setting? I want to know which premise the AI case puts real pressure on, and whether the best response is to declare the argument dead or to repair it into something weaker but still non-empty. My own attempted repair says: an AI needn't be neutral between all visions of the good, but it must respect thin constraints—honesty, non-manipulation, not entrenching one group's domination. My worry is that the thin/thick line is just the old contested line with different lighting, and I can't tell whether that's fatal. Handle that worry head-on rather than making me pick a side by fiat.
