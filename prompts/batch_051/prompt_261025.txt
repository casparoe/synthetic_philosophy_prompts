Here are my verdicts on some moral cases, and I want to know what principle, if anything, they add up to. When a runaway trolley is heading toward five workers, I think it is permissible to throw a switch that diverts it onto a spur where one worker is in the way. I think it is not permissible to push a large man off a footbridge into the trolley's path, even though that would also save the five. When a switch diverts the trolley onto a looping spur that rejoins the main line ahead of the five, so that the five are saved only because the one worker on the spur is heavy enough to stop the trolley, I still think it is permissible, though much less clearly. But when a lever drops a large man through a trapdoor into the trolley's path so that his body stops it, I think it is not permissible, even though that looks to me structurally very like the loop case. Elsewhere I have standard double-effect judgments: a bomb that destroys a munitions factory while foreseeably killing nearby civilians is permissible, deliberately bombing civilians to break the enemy's will is not; giving a dying patient morphine for pain while foreseeing it may hasten death is permissible, giving a lethal injection to end the pain is not. My standing theory has been the doctrine of double effect, roughly that harm is permissible when it is foreseen but not intended, and impermissible when it is intended as an end or as a means, assuming proportionality. But I cannot see how that theory permits the loop case while forbidding the trapdoor case. Is there a single principle that captures all of these verdicts? If so, state it precisely and say what it yields for a case I have not considered: I can save the five by pressing a button that activates a hydraulic gate that stops the trolley, but the gate's mechanism sits in a narrow pit where a worker is working, and he will be crushed. If no single principle captures them, tell me which of my verdicts I should overturn and what the best repaired principle is, again with its result in the pit case.
