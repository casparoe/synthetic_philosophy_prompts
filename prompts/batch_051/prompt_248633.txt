An adversarial collaboration has been arranged between two camps in the foundations of decision theory, and you are to draft the joint statement they would both sign. The first camp, the Ensemble Camp, defends the standard expected-utility framework: a rational agent chooses the act with the highest expected utility, where the expectation is taken over all possible outcomes weighted by their probabilities, and where utility is a representation of the agent's own preferences, with concavity of utility available to represent risk aversion. The second camp, the Time-Average Camp, associated with the ergodicity economics program, holds that for multiplicative quantities such as wealth, the relevant measure of how a single agent actually fares over time is the time average, not the ensemble average. Where the two diverge because the process is non-ergodic, they argue, maximizing the ensemble expectation can recommend choices that cause an individual agent's wealth to fall almost surely over time, and the curvature of utility should therefore be fixed by the dynamics of the decision problem rather than left as a free psychological parameter.

Both camps have agreed to work through a single running example throughout the document: a gamble which, in each round, multiplies an agent's bankroll by 1.5 with probability one half and by 0.6 with probability one half. They jointly accept the arithmetic: the per-round arithmetic mean multiplier is 1.05, while the per-round geometric mean multiplier is the square root of 0.9, which is less than 1. Thus a large ensemble average grows, while a single agent who repeatedly reinvests everything sees wealth tend downward.

Draft the document these two camps would produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. It should be written in the first person plural, as a text both parties endorse. Structure it as follows.

First, a numbered list of agreed claims. These should cover the mathematical facts both sides accept, the distinction between ensemble averages and time averages, the conditions under which the two coincide, what the running example shows, what each framework is designed to explain, and what neither framework by itself settles without further normative assumptions. Avoid vacuous agreements, and avoid wording either side would reject as slanted.

Second, a disagreements table. For each disagreement, give the camp, then the formulation of its position as that camp would itself accept, then the strongest version of the opposing worry it acknowledges. Ensure the formulations of the two positions make contact with one another rather than talking past each other.

Third, a section on cruxes. Address at least the following three candidate cruxes. (a) Whether the time-average critique carries normative force only for gambles that are genuinely repeated, or also for a one-shot choice. (b) Whether the time-average prescription is simply expected-utility maximization with a particular concave utility function, such as logarithmic utility, so that the camps differ only over how utilities are fixed and not over the decision rule itself. (c) Whether the ensemble should be interpreted as contemporaneous copies of one agent, successive stages of one agent through time, or something else, and why that matters. For each crux, specify a concrete argument, case, or empirical observation both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about what that test would show. Also state what each side has acknowledged would count as evidence against its own position, and whether there is any observation both agree would be genuinely probative.

Finally, close with a short note identifying any part of the original dispute both camps now regard as merely verbal, if one exists, and distinguish it sharply from the residual disagreement that is substantive. Do not adjudicate the dispute; produce the document both parties could sign.
