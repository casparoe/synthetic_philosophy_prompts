Been reporting a piece on whether "democratic backsliding" names a real, trackable process or is just a polite way of saying "governments I don't like," and one political scientist I interviewed kept reaching for the same analogy: backsliding is like tooth decay. You don't feel the enamel going; a cavity is well advanced before it hurts; and by the time it hits the nerve the tooth is already structurally compromised and might need a root canal or an extraction. His punchline was that we shouldn't fixate on the dramatic endpoint — the coup, the tanks, the guy refusing to leave office — because by then the damage is mostly done, and instead we should sweat the boring, painless, early stuff: court-packing, capture of the media, election-law fiddling. The analogy clearly did a ton of work for him, and it's a good one rhetorically, but I kept wondering how much of the argument was actually being imported from dentistry rather than earned from evidence about Hungary or Poland or Turkey or the US.

Can you audit it for me? I'd want the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the enamel, the dentin, the bacteria, the sugar, the nerve, the dentist, the filling? Then the disanalogies, with the ones that matter for his conclusion separated from the ones that are just cosmetic. The ones that worry me most: decay is basically irreversable past a point, while at least some democracies snap back (or do they?), and decay has an uncontroversial diagnosis and a whole profession licensed to fix it, whereas here the diagnosis is the very thing in dispute and the would-be dentist is... who, exactly — voters? courts? the EU? And a tooth doesn't get a vote on whether it wants the filling. So, bottom line: once the disanalogies are on the table, does his conclusion — treat it early, don't wait for the symptom of pain — actually survive, or was the analogy carrying the argument the whole time? If it's a bad analogy, give me the closest better one, and say whether the metaphor framing itself is doing something we should be suspicious of.

Also, tell me what would change your verdict here: what kind of evidence — say, a country that slid far and then genuinely recovered, or a bunch of cases where the "painless decay" signs never led to a real crisis — would make you more or less confident that the analogy earns its conclusion. I want to know where your reasoning is most likely to break, not just where your confidence is highest.
