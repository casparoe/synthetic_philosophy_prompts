I'm an undergraduate taking a political philosophy seminar this term on realist and idealist traditions in public life, and I also sit as the student member of my university's Sustainability Projects Committee. The committee has to allocate a small annual fund, and it is deadlocked in a way I need to write about before Thursday's meeting. This year we received two competing proposals and can fully fund only one.

The first proposal is modest and technical: a retrofit of the refrigeration units in two dining halls, with emissions reductions that would be independently verified within the year. The second proposal is a campaign fund for a student group asking the Board of Trustees to commit to complete fossil-fuel divestment by the end of the decade. The board has rejected a nearly identical proposal twice, and the group's own planning document puts the probability of success in this cycle in the single digits. The group argues that the campaign would nonetheless build a durable advocacy organization, shift the terms of campus debate about the university's investments, and make later, smaller reforms easier. Opponents on the committee reply that this would spend student money on a moral spectacle: the campaign's public messaging would encourage students to expect a change the committee knows is almost certainly not going to happen this year, and the proposal treats the gap between that hope and the likely outcome as a reason to fund it.

The philosophical issue I want the memo to address is the one from my seminar. One line of argument says that hope which persistently outruns what an agent has good reason to expect can be morally criticisable, and not merely epistemically sloppy: in collective decisions it can substitute feeling serious about a crisis for actually doing something about it. The opposing line says that if an institution never funds anything until success is already likely, it lets current feasibility set the ceiling on what the community may aspire to, and it conflates hope with optimism or expectation. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday. It should give a concrete recommendation (fund the retrofit, fund the campaign, or reframe the choice if you think that is the real solution), keep hope, optimism, and expectation distinct, and treat the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously rather than as a paragraph to wave away. The committee's rules bar partial funding this cycle but allow us to invite a revised proposal next semester.
