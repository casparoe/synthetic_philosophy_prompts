I've been trying to get an asymmetry between lying and misleading to sit still in my head, and Grice's stuff just makes it weirder. Everyday practice and a lot of law draw a sharp line between saying the false thing and merely inviting the audience to infer it. If you say something literally false you're a liar, full stop. If you say something true that carries a false implicature, you're slimy, maybe, but people treat 'he didn't actually lie' as a real defense, and perjury statutes usually require a false statement, not a false implication. Take a witness asked whether she saw the defendant on the night of the 12th. She answers 'I saw him that morning and went straight home after work,' knowing she also saw him at 11pm. The literal content is true; she's exploiting the assumption that she's being cooperative to generate a false belief — a cancellable, calculable conversational implicature, as Grice would have it. What I can't figure out is whether that Gricean line is doing any real moral work. It gives us a tidy taxonomy — what is said vs. what is implicated, cancelling by adding 'but I didn't say I didn't see him that night,' the whole apparatus. But it doesn't yet explain why the two speakers should be blamed differently when their intentions, expected consequences, and available evidence are identical and the only difference is wether the false content travels by way of the words or by way of the inference. In fact Grice's intention-based account of speaker meaning seems to push the other direction: if speaker meaning is a matter of intended effects on an audience, then the liar and the misleader are performing the same act in different packaging. So which is it? Is the ordinary asymmetry between lying and misleading defensible, or is it a superstition about literal wording that law and blame should drop? If you defend it, defend it against the identical-intentions case. And if Grice's distinction is too thin to bear the moral weight, tell me whether the law should be expanded, or whether our ordinary blame practices should be shrunk to match it.
