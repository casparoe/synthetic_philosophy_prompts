I am a retired physicist who came to philosophy of mind late, and I have become stuck on a puzzle from the empirical literature. In the color-phi display, a red spot is flashed on the left and a green spot shortly afterward on the right; subjects report a single spot gliding across and changing from red to green roughly midway—before the green flash itself was physically present on the screen. Dennett frames two rival readings of such cases. On the Orwellian reading, the subject first consciously sees the moving spot as red, and the later green stimulus then rewrites the memory of that experience before it can be reported; on the Stalinesque reading, conscious awareness is itself postponed until the second stimulus can help construct a single, edited experience, so there never was a conscious red-moving phase to overwrite. Write a dialogue between two informed and equally capable defenders of these positions. The Orwellian should press the Stalinesque on why the brain would impose such a delay rather than edit the record afterward; the Stalinesque should press the Orwellian on what evidence could ever tell an overwritten memory from a delayed experience, and should bring in backward masking, where a later stimulus prevents an earlier one from being consciously seen at all. Then have both address a thought experiment I keep returning to: an observer trained to narrate continuously throughout the trial. Would the running commentary contain a red-moving phase that is later unreportable, or would the commentary itself be delayed and edited, and can that question be made empirically intelligible? The two should respond to each other's actual arguments rather than deliver set speeches; they may end in partial agreement, but neither may be a strawman. Keep the dialogue under 900 words.
