Take two tests for what is real in a gauge theory. Invariance: a quantity is real only if it is invariant under every gauge transformation, and anything that changes under gauge transformations while all observational content stays the same is surplus structure. Ineliminability: a quantity is real if some gauge-invariant function of it must appear in the theory's predictions, so that removing that structure would leave an observable phenomenon unexpressed. Now apply these tests to electrodynamics treated as a U(1) gauge theory. The vector potential A transforms as A → A + ∇λ, so the invariance test labels it surplus; the magnetic field B = ∇×A is invariant and passes. But in the Aharonov-Bohm effect, an electron travels through a region where B = 0 along its whole path, and its interference pattern shifts by a phase proportional to the gauge-invariant loop integral ∮A·dl around the excluded flux. That loop integral is built from the very potential the invariance test dismisses. Is the vector potential real, surplus, or does this case show that the tests themselves are too blunt? Work through each test, say where they conflict or underdetermine the verdict, and then say whether the real content of the theory should be located in holonomies rather than in either the local field or the local potential.
