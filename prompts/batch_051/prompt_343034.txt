I've been reporting a lot on political polarization, and I keep getting stuck on the same question: when someone on the other side is wrong in a way that conveniently matches their side's line, am I looking at bad faith or at someone honestly reasoning from a different picture of the facts? There's a passage in Walter Lippmann's *Public Opinion* that makes me think this is harder than most of my colleagues admit:

"We insist, because of our superior hindsight, that the world as they needed to know it, and the world as they did know it, were often two quite contradictory things. We can see, too, that while they governed and fought, traded and reformed in the world as they imagined it to be, they produced results, or failed to produce any, in the world as it was."

I want a working definition of "arguing in bad faith" (or "good faith," if that's cleaner) that classifies these cases: a candidate who privately admits the election was clean but keeps claiming it was stolen because his voters expect it; a voter who sincerely believes the stolen-election claim because that is what his news sources keep showing him; a cable host who sticks to true facts that support her side and never corrects the false impression they create; a debater who shifts his definitions mid-argument whenever he starts losing, without noticing hes doing it; an activist who cites a statistic she hasn't checked but would drop immediately if shown the correction; and a pundit who argues for a position he doesn't hold just to see whether his co-panelists can handle it. Run the definition through all six, tell me where it outputs something you'd want to reject, and say whether Lippmann's point should change any of those verdicts.
