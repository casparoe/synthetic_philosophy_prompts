I sit on the Board of Zoning Appeals for a mid-sized city, and next month we must decide an application that I suspect will force us to say out loud what principle we have actually been applying. I'd like you to think about this as a legal philosopher who works on precedent and analogical reasoning. Please begin by laying out the distinct principles that could explain our recent decisions, before you apply them to the new case.

Our recent decisions, all from the past two years:

1. We approved the conversion of a detached two-car garage into a one-bedroom accessory dwelling unit. The only exterior changes were on the alley side. Neighbors objected to parking. We granted the permit on the condition that the lot retain one off-street parking space.

2. We denied a request to demolish a two-flat and build a single-family house roughly half again as large as anything on the block. The owner argued that the new house would improve the street. Our written decision cited both the scale of the proposed house and the loss of two rental units.

3. We approved a second-story addition, set back so that it is not visible from the street, over neighbors' objections that it would reduce their afternoon light. The addition created a separate apartment for an extended family. Our decision said that the addition "preserves the street-facing character of the block."

4. We denied an application to use a single-family house as a short-term rental for up to 150 nights per year. Neighbors documented noise and parking problems during a trial period; the owner said the house would otherwise sit empty while he traveled. Our decision described the use as "commercial and transient" and noted the loss of a long-term home.

5. We approved a church's application to operate an overnight cold-weather shelter for up to twelve adults in its basement from November through March, over neighbors' objections about safety. Our decision said the shelter was "contained within an existing institutional building" and served "a pressing need that cannot be met elsewhere in the ward."

6. We approved the conversion of a vacant ground-floor storefront into a two-bedroom apartment, on the condition that the original storefront windows and cornice be retained. The applicant provided no off-street parking.

The case before us next month: a property owner asks for a special permit to combine the two upper-floor apartments of a century-old mixed-use building into a single three-bedroom unit. The street facade would be unchanged; the owner, who lives on the ground floor, says the merger would restore the building to its original configuration and match the scale of neighboring buildings. The two apartments are both occupied, one by a tenant who has lived there for eleven years. A tenants' organization opposes the application, noting that the ward has been losing rental units for a decade and that the city's own housing plan calls for preserving existing units. Under our code, any reduction in the number of dwelling units on a lot requires our special permit.

If we want to be consistent with our own precedents, how should we vote? Which of our past cases is the closest analog, and under which principle? What principle would we be adopting with each possible decision, and what further hypothetical case would most cleanly tell those principles apart? I care less about the legal answer than about which principle actually fits our record.
