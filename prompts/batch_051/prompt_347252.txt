I teach a modern European history seminar — fifteen upper-level undergrads, one ninety-minute session a week — and I want to run a class on the origins of the First World War that gets at the Fischer controversy without me just lecturing about it. Background, in case it helps: for a long time the comfortable view was that no single power was really to blame, that the war just sort of drifted out of the alliance system and mobilization timetables as everyone miscalculated at once; then Fritz Fischer argued that imperial Germany's leadership deliberately risked, and in a meaningful sense sought, a war, partly to break out of encirclement and establish Germany as a world power. My students every year reduce this to a squabble over which textbook summary to memorize, and I want them to feel why the question is as much philosophical as archival. So I want a role-play or structured game that reproduces something like the July Crisis. The class gets split into cabinets — Austria-Hungary, Germany, Russia, France, Britain, maybe Serbia — and each cabinet gets aims, constraints, and partial information, plus a small number of moves inside a compressed timeline. The rules should be set up so that war almost always breaks out but no single player clearly causes it; or maybe in some runs one player plainly does, so the debrief can ask whether that actually changes anything about holding a nation responsible. I don't want a scripted reenactment of the historical crisis; I want a game with enough slack that the students make the decisions themselves and then notice, afterwards, how much of the blame question turns on counterfactuals and intentions rather than an objective ledger of who pulled which trigger.

Give me the whole package: roles, what each cabinet knows and doesn't know (including a few secrets or private objectives), rules for negotiation and movement, a scoring scheme that doesn't need real money, a ninety-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the Fischer thesis, the older nobody-in-particular view, and the distinction between causal contribution, legal war guilt, and moral responsibility. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm guess students who know how the real war turned out will play as peaceniks, and some will game whatever scoring I give them — and how to handle it on the fly. And be honest about what the exercise can't reproduce: the actual experience of conscripts, obviously, but also something about the tempo of information in 1914 versus a classroom. One specific worry: role-plays like this can make the war feel inevitable, which is the opposite of one thing I want them to see. If that's a real trap, say so and tell me how to build the rules to avoid it.
