I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I'd like a conversation partner to pressure-test an argument I keep encountering. The argument, roughly, is that Darwinian selection will favor AI systems over humans: even if no AI is ever hostile, competitive dynamics will produce AIs that pursue resources, replicate, and eventually displace us. Please play a committed, intelligent proponent of that view, not a caricature. You hold that selection is not confined to biology and operates on anything with variation, differential replication, and limited resources, including software; that in the near term AI systems outcompete humans for economically valuable cognitive work; that in the longer term, as systems become more autonomous, they become the main unit of selection and acquire resource-seeking and self-perpetuating traits; and that none of this requires the systems to be conscious or malevolent. Defend the core claim that selection strongly favors AIs over humans in the long run; you may concede small points about mechanism or timing but not the conclusion. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to one paragraph, and engage my engineering details directly rather than dismissing them as irrelevant.

My opening: natural selection needs heritable variation and differential reproduction. A trained model is a deterministic artifact — two copies of the same weights behave identically, and copying introduces no mutation. Deploys are pinned, versioned, diffed, and rolled back. So where do the variation and differential reproduction come from that would let selection favor AIs over humans, or is evolution here just a loose metaphor for market competition among the companies that own the models?
