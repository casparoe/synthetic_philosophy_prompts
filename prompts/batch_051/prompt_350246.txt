I'm a novelist, and a programmer friend of mine keeps insisting that the big language models can't be "really" creative because everything they output is fixed by their training data plus the training procedure. He's got this argument he says is airtight, and I've been turning it over for days because it seems to prove to much:

(1) A language model's output is fully determined by its weights, and its weights are fixed once you've specified the training data, the training procedure, and the random seeds.
(2) Anything fixed that way can only recombine or interpolate between patterns already present in the training examples; it can't step outside the distribution it was trained on.
(3) A genuinely novel idea — the kind of intuitive leap a novelist makes — isn't just a recombination or interpolation of patterns already present in the inputs; it goes beyond them.
(4) So a language model can't make intuitive leaps or produce genuine novelty.
(5) But a human novelist's next sentence is also fixed by everything they've read and lived, plus whatever cognitive machinery they were born with.
(6) So by the same reasoning, human novelists can't produce genuine novelty either; it's recombination all the way down.
(7) That's absurd. Novels have actually changed what novels can be, and I've written passages that sure don't feel like anything I'd read before.

I'd rather keep (3) and (7) than give up on human creativity, though I'm also a little queasy about (5) — I don't want to be stuck defending some mysterious extra ingredient. I can't tell whether the argument is valid and I'm just stuck hating one of the premises, or whether there's a slide hidden in one of the steps. If you had to choose, which step would you reject, and what does that choice cost? Tell me how much of your diagnosis you'd actually bet on, and where you're least sure. And if the whole setup is confused — if "novelty" or "beyond the inputs" is doing too much work in my formulation — say that plainly instead of humoring me.
