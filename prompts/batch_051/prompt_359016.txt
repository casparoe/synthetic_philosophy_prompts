I've been going in circles on van Fraassen's Reflection principle and I'd like help sorting out whether it or my gut should give way. As I understand it, the principle says my present credence in a proposition has to equal a weighted average of the credences I might have at some later time: P_now(A) = Σ P_now(P_later(A) = r) · r. Informally, I should treat my future self as an expert in the sense that learning what my future self will believe should move me to that belief right now. What attracts me to it is that it's a single tidy constraint, it seems to string together a whole lot of otherwise scattered intuitions about not going in for predictably irrational changes of mind, and it's backed by a Dutch book argument (if your present and future selves both bet at their own odds, a bookie can clean you out iff you violate it, or so I've seen it presented). At the same time, I have an intuition I can't get rid of: if I know that tomorrow I'll be running on three hours of sleep, or will have been given a drug that makes me massively overconfident about whatever I happen to be thinking about, or will have been manipulated into high confidence without any new evidence, then that future version of me seems like exactly the person I should not defer to. It feels not just unwise but crazy to let a credence I'll hold only because somone slipped me something set my credence today.

People I talk to split into two camps. Some say "no problem, just restrict the principle to future selves you expect to be rational," and act like that settles it. Others say my sleep-deprivation and drug intuitions are just scope insensitivity or some other bias, and I should bite the bullet on the unrestricted principle because the Dutch book argument is a real coherence requirement. I'm not satisfied with either one-liner, and I want a negotiated verdict.

Act as an expert in formal epistemology and decision theory. Start by steelmanning both sides as hard as you can — give the strongest version of the Reflection principle and the strongest version of my counterexamples — and take your time before you take a position. Then walk me through what each possible move actually costs: giving up the unrestricted principle, restricting it (and whether "rational future self" can be made non-circular and non-empty), debunking the intuition as a bias, or some other reading on which both are right. Also please separate what's genuinely settled among people who work on this (for example, what the formal principle says and what the Dutch book argument actually establishes) from what's still contested, and don't present a contested position as the consensus. Please write your answer in American English.
