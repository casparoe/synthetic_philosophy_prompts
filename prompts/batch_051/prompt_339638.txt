Over the last few months I've been defending a particular thesis in conversations about self-knowledge, and I'd like to know where it is actually vulnerable rather than merely where it is criticized. The thesis is this: the familiar evidence that self-examination can be harmful — the finding that dwelling on one's symptoms and their causes lengthens depressive episodes, the demonstrations that searching for the reasons behind certain preferences can worsen later satisfaction with the choice, and the everyday cases in which self-monitoring disrupts skilled performance — does not really indict self-examination. It indicts rumination: passive, repetitive, distress-driven self-focus that circles without revising anything. Genuine self-examination, by contrast, I understand as active, question-driven, and aimed at changing some understanding, plan, or behavior. So stated, the position is that genuine self-examination is close to an unqualified good, and that its apparent failures are failures of a different process.

I want a map of what would count against that position. Specifically: what evidence, possible cases, or conceptual arguments would have to be present for the rumination/self-examination distinction itself to fail, or for the supposedly healthy kind of self-scrutiny to turn out harmful in ways that matter — worsening mood, decisions, motivation, or self-concept even when it is active and structured? Which of those would be decisive and which merely awkward, and which would a committed defender of my view have to concede as counting against it? I'd be most persuaded by experimental or clinical work showing downstream costs of question-driven self-scrutiny that cannot be attributed to pre-existing distress or to the passive counterpart, or by a conceptual argument showing that the very features I use to separate the two kinds — activity, structure, goal-directedness — are themselves the mechanism of harm. What I'd find much less persuasive is the bare observation that both processes correlate with negative affect, since the view predicts they will co-occur. Finally, give me an honest verdict on whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability, because any bad outcome could be reclassified as rumination — and whether, if it has been, that is a defect in this kind of view or just the price of drawing the distinction cleanly.
