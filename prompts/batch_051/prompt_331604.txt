I am an undergraduate currently taking an epistemology course that has just finished a unit on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. I have been keeping a record of my reactions to a series of cases, and they do not obviously hang together. I would like your help extracting a principle from them.

By higher-order evidence I mean evidence about the reliability of my own reasoning or about what my total evidence supports. By epistemic akrasia I mean the state in which my degree of belief in a proposition comes apart from my own all-things-considered judgment about what degree of belief my evidence supports. All of the cases below involve me trying to determine whether a particular formula of first-order modal logic is valid.

1. The all-nighter. After an all-nighter, I construct a validity proof and become 0.9 confident that it is valid. I know from experience that in this sleep-deprived state I tend to make subtle mistakes in quantifier scope and usually fail to catch them. As I look over the proof, every step still seems right. My gut reaction: I am rationally required to lower my confidence to around 0.6, even though I cannot point to any error.

2. The morning recheck. The next day, rested, I redo the proof from scratch. Every line comes out the same way, and the derivation still goes through. My gut reaction: my confidence should return to around 0.9, and it would be irrational to remain at 0.6 merely because the first attempt was made under bad conditions.

3. The peer. A classmate whose logical ability I regard as equal to mine, working independently, tells me she has found a countermodel and is 0.9 confident the formula is invalid. We compare work for half an hour, and neither of us can find a mistake in the other's reasoning. My gut reaction: I should move to roughly 0.5.

4. The base rate. The professor has shown the class data from five years of this same exercise: 70 percent of submitted proofs contained a hidden error, and students' confidence did not predict which proofs were correct. I am now in the position of case 2: I have rederived the proof line by line under good conditions, and it still looks right, but I also have this base-rate data. My gut reaction: I should lower my confidence, but only to around 0.75.

5. The akratic proof. After another all-nighter, I work through a fresh proof. It looks valid, and I find myself at 0.85. But when I step back and ask what my total evidence supports, given the fatigue and my known error rate, I judge that a rational person in my position should be at 0.6. The proof continues to look valid, and 0.85 continues to feel natural. My gut reaction: the 0.85 is not rationally permissible, and I am required to bring my confidence down to 0.6 even while the first-order reasoning keeps pulling the other way.

The two verdicts I find hardest to hold together are 1 and 4. In both cases an outside source says my reasoning is substantially less reliable than it feels from the inside; in both cases the chain of reasoning looks valid. Yet in 1 I want the outside evidence to do most of the work, while in 4 I want my own step-by-step derivation to absorb nearly all of the statistical pressure. Case 5 adds another kind of pressure: if my all-things-considered judgment about what my evidence supports should defeat my first-order confidence there, why should that same sort of judgment not dominate everywhere, and how does that square with the resistance to the base rate in 4?

Can you state a precise principle about how first-order seemings and higher-order evidence about my reliability should combine, and about when epistemic akrasia is rationally permitted, that captures these five verdicts, or as many of them as possible? For each clause of the principle, tell me which case motivates it. Then apply the principle to a further case I have not settled: in case 1, suppose I recheck the proof later that same night while still exhausted, and it still looks valid; should that recheck raise my 0.6 at all, or is it just another report from the same unreliable process? Finally, say explicitly which of my five verdicts, if any, your principle cannot accommodate, and specify which verdict I should abandon. Please do not merely list candidate principles and leave me to choose; commit to the one you think best, and argue for it.
