so i've been putting together a philosophy of religion course and I keep going back and forth on whether "religion" is even a workable category, or whether it's one of those folk concepts that smuggles in more than it earns. I'd like a second mind, but I want you to argue for a definite position, not just lay out the options.

You're a philosopher of religion who works on definitional disputes and isn't afraid to say when an ordinary concept is doing no real work. Before you settle on a verdict, brainstorm at least one strong version of the yes-answer, one strong version of the no-answer, and then say briefly which route you're taking and why.

Here are the criteria I was taught for a religion:
1. a more-or-less bounded community with shared beliefs and practices;
2. regular ritual practice, including rites for major life events;
3. a body of texts or teachings treated as authoritative;
4. an account of human flourishing and instructions for achieving it;
5. some orientation toward what members take to be ultimate or sacred, even if not supernatural.

Now the case. North Valley Confucian Association is a study-and-ritual community in a mid-sized city. It runs twice-monthly communal rites honoring ancestors and sages, with bowing, offerings, and recitation of passages from the Analects and Mencius. It has a study curriculum on those texts, lectures on ren and li, a member code of conduct, and it performs coming-of-age and funeral rites. Here's the wrinkle: about a third of its members are practicing Christians, and another decent chunk are self-described atheists. In interviews, members say they don't think the ancestors or sages are literally present or conscious; they describe the rites as "tending to gratitude, memory, and social harmony." The association's mission statement says its purpose is ethical cultivation and the repair of social relationships, and it deliberately takes no position on gods, an afterlife, or whether Tian is a person or just the underlying pattern of things. Some teachers say the rites are not worship but embodied gratitude; other teachers call the same rites worship and roll their eyes at the "secular" members.

Question: does North Valley qualify as a religion under my five criteria? Go criterion by criterion, and where a criterion underdetermines the answer, say so outright. Then give your honest overall verdict: is it a religion? On the back of that, answer the harder question — does the case show that one or more of my criteria (especially criterion 5, or criterion 1) needs revising, or does it show that "religion" is a family-resemblance concept that's going to have genuinely fuzzy borders no matter how the criteria are tuned? I want a committed answer: pick a side on the overall verdict and on whether the criteria should be fixed, and defend both.
