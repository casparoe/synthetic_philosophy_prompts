I am currently taking a course on the philosophy of induction, and we have recently been working through the no-free-lunch theorems from computational learning theory. I have found myself drawn to a particular reading of those results, and I want it stress-tested rather than simply rehearsed or dismissed. The position I am considering is this: because every learning algorithm performs equally well when averaged over all possible target functions, no inductive rule can be justified except by assuming in advance that the world belongs to some preferred class of regularities; and since that assumption is itself just an induction, the no-free-lunch results do not solve the problem of induction but give it a sharper formal expression. On this reading, the theorems are not merely a practical constraint on algorithm selection in machine learning; they are a precise statement of why induction cannot be justified from a neutral starting point.

I do not want a survey of the literature; I want a systematic account of what would count against this position. Specifically: which features of the theorems themselves would a critic emphasize — the uniform average over all possible functions, the treatment of off-training-set error, the finite-data formulation, the fact that an average over all possible worlds may tell us nothing about any particular world — and which of these would a committed defender concede as real limits rather than dismiss as beside the point? Which philosophical considerations, possible cases, or results in adjacent areas such as formal learning theory, algorithmic information theory, or ordinary scientific practice would count as evidence against the skeptical reading? Please be explicit about what the position predicts that rival readings do not, and then rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, including where a defender of the skeptical reading would admit she has paid a price for the conclusion.
