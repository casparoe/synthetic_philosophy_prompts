I'm a retired physical chemist who's been reading philosophy seriously for a couple years, and my reading group has been stuck on Berkeley's idealism for months. Two regulars, Jan and Priya, have settled into opposing camps. Jan leans toward a secular, phenomenalist version of Berkeley: physical objects are, at bottom, stable patterns of actual and possible perceptions, and a claim that something exists utterly unperceived by anyone is either empty or means something like "there is a rule about what anyone would perceive in the right circumstances." Priya is a scientific realist: a mountain or an electron has its own nature whether or not any mind perceives it, and perception samples but does not exhaust what is there.

They have been argueing past each other so consistently that the group suggested an adversarial collaboration. They agreed, provided someone drafts it for them. You are that someone.

Act as a professional philosopher who knows Berkeley's texts, especially the master argument where he challenges you to conceive of a tree existing unconceived, and the later phenomenalist descendants of that view. Think carefully; before you produce the final version, brainstorm the candidate agreed claims and the likely cruxes.

Please write the document Jan and Priya should have produced, with these parts:

1. Numbered agreed claims: substantive things both can sign about perception, the master argument, and ordinary talk about unperceived objects. Not just truisms. I suspect both can sign that the quick version of the master argument (you always catch yourself conceiving the tree, so the tree is only an idea) is not by itself decisive against realism, and that the real fight is about what follows from that fact. Put that in their own words.

2. A disagreements section, in neutral wording, that says exactly where they still differ. Each statement of a position should be phrased so the person holding it would accept it, without the other side feeling strawmanned. Use a table if that helps: the question, Jan's answer, Priya's answer.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. I have a hunch the deepest one is the distinction between "not actually perceived" and "not possibly perceived" -- Jan may be able to admit the first while denying the second has any sense, and Priya has to explain what the second adds over possible experiences. There may be other cruxes too.

4. For each crux, a concrete test, case, or argument both would agree bears on it, with each person's prediction about the outcome recorded in advance. For example, they have both mentioned the "last perceiver in the universe" case and the old perceptual relativity cases (water feeling warm to one hand and cold to the other). You can use those or better ones.

5. A short note on any apparent disagreements that turn out, on inspection, to be only about words.

Write the final statement in American English.
