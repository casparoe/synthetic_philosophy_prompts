Here's a puzzle that's been eating at me. A colleague in my department is in a book club with 16 people. Each meeting, the facilitator randomly splits them into four groups of four. At the first meeting of the year, her group is her plus three people she's never met, and during introductions it comes out that all three of the others were born on June 8—which is also her birthday. She's spooked. Her husband, who knows just enough math to be dangerous, waived it off: "that's just the birthday paradox." But that's not obviously the birthday paradox, is it? The birthday paradox is about *any* two people in a room sharing *some* birthday, not three strangers matching a fixed date.

Anyway, I want your credence that the group assignment actually was random—that the facilitator wasn't sorting by birthdays or anything correlated with them. Give me a number, but then decompose it. In particular: (i) what's the probability of that exact observation if the sorting really was random? (ii) how much of the husband's birthday-paradox point genuinely applies here, as opposed to the usual any-pair-in-a-room case? (iii) how heavily should we discount the spookiness because she's been in small groups like this for years and would have noticed and retold any equally striking birthday cluster she happened to land in—and is that the better argument for not being spooked even if the husband got his math wrong? (iv) what prior would you put on there being some non-random mechanism, and do you need a concrete story about what the mechanism would be before that hypothesis is worth taking seriously? Then give me the final number, say which term is doing the most work, and tell me what single piece of evidence would move it most.
