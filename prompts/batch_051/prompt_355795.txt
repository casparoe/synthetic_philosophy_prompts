Does induction rest on our heads, not on the world?

I got into this because of the standard claim that the future resembles the past. Goodman's 'grue' example is supposed to show that this platitude hides a real problem. Define 'grue' like this: something is grue if it is green when examined before 1 January 2050, and blue when examined after that date. Every emerald we have checked so far counts as both green and grue. So the evidence confirms 'all emeralds are green' and 'all emeralds are grue' to the same degree. But the first rule predicts next year's emeralds will look green, and the second predicts they will look blue. Same observations, two different futures.

The obvious comeback is that 'grue' is a made-up, gerrymandered predicate, so we should not project it. But this reply is not as free as it looks. Define 'bleen' as blue before 2050 and green after. Then a community that started with 'grue' and 'bleen' as its basic colour words could define 'green' as grue-before-2050-and-bleen-after, and 'blue' the other way round. To them, 'green' would look just as artificial as 'grue' does to us. So appealing to naturalness or simplicity seems circular: which words count as simple depends on which language you are already using.

Goodman's own solution was entrenchment. 'Green' has been used over and over in successful predictions, and 'grue' hasn't, so 'green' is the projectible predicate. I think this is circular in a sneaky way. 'Stay with the predicates that have worked so far' is just another induction: it takes the past reliability of projecting 'green' as evidence that projecting 'green' will keep working. That is the very thing that grue put in doubt.

Maybe the real difference between 'green' and 'grue' is not about language or history but about the kind of minds we have. Green and blue are perceptually basic for humans: an infant can discriminate green from blue long before it can tell grue from bleen, because doing that requires knowing a date. Our visual system comes with a built-in similarity space that makes some groupings easy and others almost impossible. 'Grue' cuts across that space: it groups some green things with some blue things, and separates green things from each other on the basis of when they are examined. So induction might work when its predicates match the grain of our perceptual systems, and fail when they don't. That would explain why 'all emeralds are green' feels genuinely projectible and 'all emeralds are grue' feels like a trick.

I realise this only pushes the question back one step. A differently wired creature might find 'grue' perfectly natural. But I don't think that ruins my point, because I don't need induction to be justified from nowhere; I only need it to be justified for us. My teacher says this makes induction "anthropocentric" in a bad way, and maybe she's right, but I can't see the alternative. What I'm now tempted to say is that induction cannot be justified at all and is just something our kind of mind cannot help doing. That ending might be to strong for what the argument actually shows.
