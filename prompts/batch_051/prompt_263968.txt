I've been reporting a piece on how history departments carve up their survey courses, and I keep running into the same stale fight. One side treats the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution as real joints in European history: something actually changes in Italian art and letters around 1400, and something actually changes in how knowledge gets made around 1600 — print, experiment, mathematics as a tool, the first scientific societies. The other side says these are retrospective brand names. The "middle" ages got their name from humanists who needed a boring stretch between themselves and the ancients; a Victorian Swiss historian turned the Renaissance into a neatly packaged cultural moment you can teach in a term; and the Scientific Revolution is basically a mid-twentieth-century course-catalog category. One of the most assigned books on the Scientific Revolution opens by flatly denying that such a thing ever happened, then spends a few hundred pages describing it.

That's the thing that confuses me, though. The two sides seem to agree on nearly all the underlying facts. Everyone conceedes there was a Carolingian renaissance and a twelfth-century renaissance long before 1400. Everyone conceedes late-medieval thinkers had roughly momentum-shaped theories of motion before Galileo. Everyone conceedes the break looked less clean if you were a peasant, a woman, or a merchant in Bruges. And still nobody moves. It's been decades.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't a debate about the past at all but about what periodization is supposed to do — whether a label is a claim about a sharp break, about how people back then understood themselves, about what later centuries made of them, or just a pragmatic filing system for courses and journals. But I don't want to flatten it into a tired "it's just semantics" verdict.

So diagnose it: is this disagreement verbal or substantive, and substantive about what? Are they fighting about facts, about how big a break has to be to count, about standards of evidence, or about bedrock temperaments — splitters versus lumpers? And once you've diagnosed it, tell me what would count as progress: better quantitative work on institutions and output, a cleaner taxonomy of period terms, an explicit decision to treat the labels as pure convention, or the admission that this one is permanent and I should just say so in the feature.
