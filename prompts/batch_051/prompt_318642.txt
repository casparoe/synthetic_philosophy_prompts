I am a doctoral student in philosophy working on the concept of death, and I have been worrying about the irreversibility condition inside the standard definition. I have built an argument that seems to show this condition is either too strong to be usable or too weak to keep death a biological fact, and I would like a diagnosis of which step to give up and what it would cost.

(1) The standard criterion says: a human being is dead when there is irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, or irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain. Consider the circulatory-respiratory prong.

(2) "Irreversible" most naturally means that the cessation cannot be undone by any intervention that could be applied to the body.

(3) In the first minutes after a cardiac arrest, the clinician commonly cannot rule out that an intervention such as cardiopulmonary resuscitation or mechanical circulatory support would restart the heart. What the bedside examination actually reveals is that circulation has stopped and is not resuming on its own, and that no attempt at resuscitation will be made.

(4) If death required irreversibility in the sense of (2), then the ordinary hospital declaration would be premature: the patient would not yet be dead, because the cessation could still be reversed. If we instead take ordinary declarations to be generally correct, the operative criterion must be weaker — permanence: the cessation will not, in fact, be reversed.

(5) But permanence in that sense is partly a social fact. Whether a cessation will actually be reversed depends on whether anyone attempts resuscitation, and that depends on whether a do-not-resuscitate order is in place, on institutional policy, and on a judgment of futility — all of which can vary without any change in the patient's underlying biology.

(6) So the permanence reading yields this: the same patient in the same biological condition could be dead under one hospital policy and alive under another, and the moment of death would shift according to whether a form was signed.

(7) That seems unacceptable. Death is a biological condition of an organism, not an artifact of institutional decision; a DNR order does not determine when the organism dies.

(8) Hence the dilemma. Read irreversibility strictly, and timely death declarations are systematically premature; read it as permanence, and whether someone is dead is partly settled by hospital policy.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I want to preserve (7) and the claim that many ordinary declarations are objectively correct at the time they are made. Please put the bottom line first: state in a sentence or two which step or premise should be rejected, then explain the diagnosis and what rejecting it costs, and say whether the damage carries over to the brain-death prong. Keep the entire response under 600 words.
