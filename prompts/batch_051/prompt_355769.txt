I have been working with a distinction that I have not seen drawn in exactly this form, and I would like an honest assessment of it. I distinguish between a descended tradition, which survives through an actual, continuous chain of transmission from person to person and owes its authority to that chain, and a designated tradition, which is selected and labeled by an institution as "our tradition" after the fact and owes its authority to that labeling rather than to any continuous transmission. My claim is that most national traditions are designated rather than descended, and that this is part of what makes them politically mobilizable. This clearly overlaps with the familiar ideas associated with Hobsbawm and Anderson that many traditions are invented and that nations are imagined communities, but I think the cut is different: a tradition invented in 1850 can by now have become descended, since people have genuinely handed it down for generations, while an imagined community can contain genuinely old descended practices. Is this a real distinction, does it already exist under another name, and where does it break — for instance, what does it classify wrongly, or what cases fall between the two categories? Please respond in American English.
