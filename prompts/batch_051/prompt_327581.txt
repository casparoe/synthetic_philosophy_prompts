In my history class we've been stuck on the same argument for weeks now and I'm honestly not sure what kind of disagreement it even is. We read about how dueling died out in the 1800s (we also touched on slavery, but dueling is the one everyone got worked up about). The timeline isn't really contested: dueling used to be this whole honor ritual among upper-class men, with seconds and satisfaction and all that, and over the 1800s it slowly went from something a respectable guy basically had to do, to something people made fun of, and then it was mostly gone. But nobody can agree on what actually killed it.

The first camp in my class says it was arguments and changing beliefs: critics who ridiculed dueling as stupid and barbaric eventually persuaded enough men that it didn't actually restore their honor, so they genuinely stopped believing in it. The second camp says that's too flattering — it was interests. The world changed so that dueling got bad for your career and your business, and the law started actually punishing it, so people stopped because it cost to much, and the arguments were just stuff people told themselves afterward to make it look principled. The third camp says both are missing the point: dueling was always about status and honor, so it ended when respectable opinion turned against it. Once refusing a challenge stopped looking cowardly and accepting one started looking ridiculous, the whole thing collapsed, and the real question is how that status shift happened — not just what people said or calculated.

We've gone around this circle like five times now. Everyone knows the same examples, nobody has moved, and I can't tell if it's because one side is wrong about the facts or because the question itself is mushy. So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Are we disagreeing about a hidden factual question (like whether the men who stopped dueling were mainly persuaded, mainly calculating, or mainly following their social set), or about what counts as a real explanation of a norm ending — whether stated reasons, private interests, or social standing is the "true" cause? Or are we just answering three different questions under one heading, about why the public norm collapsed, why individual compliance dropped, and why the whole practice lost its honor? And given how you diagnose it, what would count as actual progress — what kind of evidence or way of reframing the question should I bring to class next week?
