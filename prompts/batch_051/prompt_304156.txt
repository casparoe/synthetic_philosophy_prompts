Something I keep noticing and I don't know if there's a name for it. A city council decides, for good moral reasons, to rename a street or a square — say the street named for some 19th-century colonial administrator gets changed to honor a local poet. Overnight the maps, the signs, the official registry all change. But in everyday speech the old name just hangs around, sometimes for a whole generation. People use it to give directions, cab drivers still say it, older residents use it without any particular edge in their voice. Its not protest and it's not nostalgia exactly — they just never switched. So you end up with a situation where the official act of commemoration has been formally cancelled, but a kind of de facto commemoration keeps running in ordinary talk, purely by inertia. Is there an established concept for this gap between the official and the vernacular life of a name, or for the way a cancelled honor keeps being performed informally? And I don't just want a label. I want to know what it implies for whether renaming actually does what it's supposed to. Maybe the point of renaming was never to change how people talk, only to change what the state is willing to say in its official voice. Or maybe the persistence shows that after a while a name stops being an honor at all and becomes just a noise that points at a place, the way bakery street points at where the bakeries are; in that case there's nothing left to object to. But then how do you tell when a name is still doing commemorative work and when it's been bleached into a pure label? And is the gap itself a problem for the ethics of renaming, or only for people who expected a quick fix?
