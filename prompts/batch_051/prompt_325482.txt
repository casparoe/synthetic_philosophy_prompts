I'm a software engineer, and I've never studied philosophy formally, but I keep running into a pattern at work that I've since learned has a name in game theory. Two teams, or two companies, both understand that a race is destructive for everyone involved, yet each also knows that the side that first makes its own course irreversible often wins, because the other side is then the one left deciding whether to back down. I gather this is called a commitment race. What puzzles me isn't so much why these races start, but what actually makes a commitment credible once both sides know the other has every reason to bluff. In the settings I know, people tend to reach for five devices: (1) burning a bridge, for example actually deleting the old code path or announcing that a legacy system will be retired so that reversing course becomes expensive; (2) audience costs, such as publishing a dated public roadmap so that missing it would damage a person's or team's reputation; (3) escrow or forfeiture, such as a contract under which a payment is made to the other side if a deadline is missed; (4) delegation, such as handing the decision to an executive or agent whose own metrics depend on seeing the commitment through; and (5) automation, such as a system that will proceed on schedule unless a human actively intervenes to stop it.

Please rank these five from most to least credible as commitment devices in a race between two roughly rational, roughly informed rivals who cannot directly observe each other's resolve. Give an explicit ordering, with ties only if you genuinely cannot separate them and a sentence on why, and then say for each in one or two sentences why it lands where it lands. I'd then like a short second pass: would your ordering change if the cost of backing down falls primarily on an individual decision-maker's own reputation rather than on the organization's balance sheet, and if so, which items move and why? I'm less interested in which device is sensible business practice and more in which one actually constitutes a credible commitment, so I'd ask you to keep those two questions apart.
