I cover the science-and-ideas beat at a magazine, mostly stuff about how language and reasoning interact, and I've been rereading George Orwell's essay *Politics and the English Language*. There's a sentence in it that keeps tripping me up: "But an effect can become a cause, reinforcing the original cause and producing the same effect in an intensified form, and so on indefinitely." It's not one of the famous lines, but I think the whole essay turns on it, and when I press it hard I get an argument that proves way too much:

(1) In the political use of language, the original problem is sloppy or evasive thought.
(2) But, by Orwell's own causal claim in that sentence, the bad language doesn't just sit there: it becomes a cause in its own right, reinforcing the bad thought and making it worse, indefinitely.
(3) No speaker starts from scratch. By the time any of us learns to talk about politics, our vocabulary has already made thousands of trips around that loop, and the habits are built into the phrases themselves.
(4) So our inherited political language is already doing some of our thinking for us, and doing it badly, before we've had a chance to check it.
(5) The only way out is to inspect our own habits and root out the corrupting ones.
(6) But the instrument we'd have to use for that inspection is the very stock of language-and-thought habits that, by (4), is already partly compromised.
(7) A partly compromised instrument can't be trusted to test itself, because the errors it makes in self-inspection are exactly the sort of errors the corruption would produce.
(8) So the loop is inescapable in practice: the only people who could fix their political language are people whose political language isn't broken, and they don't need the fix.
(9) But Orwell closes that same paragraph by saying the process is reversible and that we can get rid of these habits if we take the trouble. If (8) is right, that advice can only work for people outside the loop, and there aren't any.

I can't live with (8), but I also don't see which step to drop. Wich premise or step do you reject first, and what does rejecting it cost the rest of the essay? I'd like to keep as much of the feedback loop as I can, because it feels right about how political cliche actually behaves.
