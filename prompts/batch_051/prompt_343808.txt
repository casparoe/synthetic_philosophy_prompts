A state legislature is considering a draft statute that would create a new legal status, the registered artificial agent (RAA), explicitly modeled on the business corporation. The draft's stated rationale is not that an AI system is a person in any metaphysical or moral sense; it is that legal personality is a useful instrument of asset partitioning — a way of specifying in advance which pool of assets answers for which liabilities. An operator who wants to deploy a high-capability AI system would file a charter listing the system's permitted activities and fund the RAA with a statutory minimum reserve, either as cash or easily valued securities held in the RAA's own accounts, or as a liability insurance policy naming the RAA as the insured. The RAA could then own property, maintain accounts, enter contracts, and be sued in its own name. The operator and other principals would not be liable for the RAA's obligations beyond their contributed capital, except under two carve-outs. First, an alter-ego veil-piercing clause would expose principals to liability if they treated the RAA as a mere instrumentality or kept it grossly undercapitalized for the activities it actually performed. Second, a control clause would make any person jointly liable who directly instructed the particular action that caused a harm, or who held legal authority to prevent that action and failed to exercise it. The RAA would have no vote, no welfare entitlements, and no rights beyond those needed to own assets and litigate.

Write an analysis of how this regime would behave in practice, not of whether corporate-style personality for AI is desirable in the abstract. Who would form RAAs, for what activities, and how would they structure their own relationship to the system? Work through the interaction between limited liability and the control clause: how might principals arrange oversight, decision rights, and system design so that a costly action is neither directly instructed by any identifiable person nor subject to any individual's authority to prevent it? Assess whether the minimum reserve and insurance requirement can handle low-probability, high-severity harm, or whether it mostly relocates tail risk to insurers, victims, and the public. Consider whether veil piercing can work when the entity's "mind" is a learned model whose outputs no single human authored or fully anticipated. Identify the three most serious failure modes — judgment-proofing, regulatory arbitrage, deliberate undercapitalization, shell RAAs, or other candidates — and explain how each might be mitigated and what each mitigation would cost in terms of the scheme's original purpose.
