A friend and I argued late into the evening about whether natural selection is, as he kept saying, a stupid optimizer, and he left me with an argument that I keep failing to refute even though I am sure something in it must be wrong. I would like to know exactly where it gives, and what I would be committed to if I gave it up.

His chain of reasoning, as I reconstruct it, ran like this. (1) If natural selection explains complex adaptation, then it is in the same business as gradient descent in a neural network: it is a search through an enormous, high-dimensional space of possibilities for a point at which some quantity — fitness, or the negative of a loss — is high. (2) The moves available to natural selection are local and greedy. Mutation and recombination produce variants that are close to their parents in genotype space, and selection retains a variant only if it does not reduce expected reproductive success under current conditions; it has no foresight and never accumulates a sequence of net-deleterious changes now in exchange for a greater gain later. (3) On a rugged landscape this is fatal, because the way from one peak to a higher one must pass through a valley of lower fitness, and a process that rejects every net loss can never make the crossing; it is trapped in whichever basin it first occupies. (4) Real fitness landscapes are rugged in just this way, because widespread epistasis means the effect of a mutation depends on the genetic background in which it appears. (5) Complex adaptations — eyes, ribosomes, flagellar motors, metabolic pathways — look like high peaks, and they were certainly not present in the first living systems. If (1) through (4) are right, natural selection could at most fine-tune whichever basin life happened to start in; it could not have climbed to those peaks. (6) Therefore natural selection cannot be the explanation of complex adaptation, and the whole picture of selection as an optimizer is false.

(6) strikes me as unacceptable; I would give up a great deal before accepting it, and yet each premise seemed individually plausible while he was arguing for it. He added, with some satisfaction, that the same reasoning proves too much if pointed at machine learning, because the loss surfaces of deep networks are also non-convex and gradient descent is also greedy and local — yet gradient descent evidently works. So either the argument fails somewhere, or there is a disanalogy between fitness and loss that itself needs to be stated. Where does the argument go wrong? Which premise should I reject, what exactly does rejecting it cost, and how much of the biological worry is actually defused by what we have learned about why gradient descent so often finds good solutions despite its greediness? I would rather have a precise diagnosis than a reminder that the metaphors are loose.
