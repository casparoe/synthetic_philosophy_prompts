I cover privacy and technology for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a piece about the legal structure of blackmail. For background I have been reading the published hypothetical rulings of a retired state appellate judge who now writes a regular column. In her first column she endorsed a reading of Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis’s 1890 Harvard Law Review article The Right to Privacy: on that reading, the law already recognizes an interest in personal inviolability that is not exhausted by property or reputation, and it is this interest that makes it wrongful to treat the details of someone’s private life as bargaining assets. She has applied that framework in a series of columns.

Her published verdicts so far:

- In the ex-spouse case she found blackmail where a former spouse threatened to send intimate photographs to the target’s new partner unless the target sold him a vintage car below market value.
- In the auditor case she found no blackmail where an auditor threatened to send evidence of a CFO’s embezzlement to the board unless the CFO repaid the money and resigned.
- In the wedding photographer case she found blackmail where a photographer threatened to post a video of a couple’s private argument unless they paid an extra fee beyond the contract.
- In the zoning journalist case she found no blackmail where a reporter told a zoning commissioner that an article documenting a favor for a donor would run unless the commissioner voted to rescind the approval.
- In the concierge case she found blackmail where a hotel concierge threatened to sell a celebrity’s bill showing a psychiatric treatment to a tabloid unless the celebrity paid him more than the tabloid offered.
- In the environmental activist case she found no blackmail where an activist threatened to publish evidence of illegal dumping unless the factory funded an independent cleanup and monitoring program.

The new case I want to use to test her consistency: a home health aide discovers that a hospital has repeatedly failed to log medication errors, a patient-safety failure that regulators and families would find significant. The aide tells the administration: either promote me to a supervisory position, or I will take the medication-error logs to a health-care reporter. The promotion would mean higher pay and would move the aide off the affected ward.

If the judge wants to be consistent with her own precedents, what should she rule in this new case, and which earlier case is the closest analog? I also want to know which competing principles fit her published rulings so far—at least three look live: a private-gain principle, a public-accountability principle, and an intimate-sphere principle drawn from her Warren and Brandeis reading—and what each predicts about the new case. Finally, if her existing rulings genuinely underdetermine the new case, what principle would she be committing herself to in each direction, and what further case would cleanly separate the public-accountability principle from the intimate-sphere principle, since the new case sets the private-gain principle against both?
