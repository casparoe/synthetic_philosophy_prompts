For a short essay in a course I am currently taking on statistical and causal reasoning, I need to construct a worked example of Simpson's paradox to a precise specification. Please build the example to order rather than reproducing the standard textbook cases, so do not use the Berkeley graduate admissions tables or the kidney-stone treatment example.

Construct a realistic example, set in either a workplace or a hospital, comparing two groups (for instance, two treatments or two hiring policies) on a binary outcome, such that the overall success-rate comparison favors one group but within every level of a single covariate the comparison favors the other group. Constraints: the example must fit in one 2×2×2 table whose cell counts could plausibly occur in an ordinary study; the covariate must be chosen so that the aggregate comparison is the misleading one; and there must be no optional stopping, unequal follow-up, or any other cherry-picking of the data.

After presenting the table, do the following. First, show the arithmetic of the aggregate comparison and of the covariate-stratified comparisons, and state exactly where the reversal comes from. Second, identify the structural feature doing the work — the imbalance in group sizes or in stratum-specific rates that is actually responsible. Third, show that if you remove only that feature while holding the stratum-specific rates fixed, the reversal disappears. Fourth, explain how a defender of the aggregate analysis would criticize the conclusion that the stratified comparison is automatically the correct one, and say what additional background information would settle the disagreement. The essay's point is that Simpson's paradox does not license the rule "always trust the finer-grained analysis."
