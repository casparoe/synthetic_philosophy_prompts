I'm teaching a grad seminar on decision theory, and for the last session on the Pasadena game I want to close with a poem. I've been toying with a sestina because the form itself reorders the same six end-words from stanza to stanza, which feels exactly like the trick where reordering the terms of a conditionally convergent series changes what it sums to. So here's the ask: write a sestina about the Pasadena game—fair coin tossed until the first heads, payoff alternating in sign and growing like 2^n/n, so the expected value series is conditionally convergent and the natural ordering sums to about ln 2. The six end-words should do double duty as both the poem's repeating vocabulary and the variables of the decision problem: maybe sign, order, heads, tails, sum, fair. The first stanza should present the natural ordering with its nice finite-looking sum, and each subsequent stanza, just by following the sestina's permutation, should enact a different rearrangement, so that by the end the reader feels the same materials producing a different total. The envoi should bring all six end-words back in a final compressed order and then make the philosophical point that there is no well-defined fair price—not merely that we can't compute it, but that the expectation depends on the order in which the outcomes are listed. Dont name-drop Riemann or the rearrangement theorem in the poem; let the form do that work. Someone who knows the paradox should recognize it without a footnote. After the poem, give me a short note saying how confident you are in the decision-theoretic rendering, where the poem takes liberties, and single out the point you're least sure of.
