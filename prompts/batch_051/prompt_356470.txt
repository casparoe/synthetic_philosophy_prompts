For the retired-scientist reading group I lead, I am preparing a handout on the Münchhausen trilemma — Albert's point that any attempt to justify a claim by giving reasons seems to terminate in infinite regress, a circle, or an arbitrary stopping point. Please write a decision tree that takes a reader from their first honest response to the trilemma to a named position in epistemology. Each branch node should be a concrete question someone can answer without already knowing the labels — for instance, whether an infinite chain of reasons could justify even if no one can traverse it; whether a circle of mutual support can transmit justification or only test coherence; whether stopping at a basic belief is anything more than arbitrary; whether the demand for further reasons is ever misplaced. Build the tree from the logical space of answers, not from the standard three-option fork, and then locate the named positions within that space. The leaves must include at least classical foundationalism, modest foundationalism (beliefs default-justified until challenged), coherentism, infinitism, Pyrrhonian skepticism, and at least one position that rejects the trilemma's demand for reasons from the start — externalist reliabilism, or a hinge- or contextualist-style view. Where a combination of answers has no defender, say so and briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any named positions the tree cannot separate and any pair of leaves your questions do not actually pull apart, and keep the whole thing usable as a two-page handout for people trained to treat axioms, asymptotic limits, and calibration loops as ordinary working tools.
