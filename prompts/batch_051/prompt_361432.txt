An otherwise vegan friend of mine has been defending a version of freeganism, and the argument he gave me now looks as if it proves too much, but I cannot find the exact step where it goes off the rails. Here is the argument as he laid it out.

(1) If something produced by an animal’s body, or an animal body itself, would otherwise go to waste, and using it creates no new demand for such products and harms no one, then using it is permissible.

(2) Eggs laid by a rescued hen would otherwise rot; collecting and eating them creates no demand for egg production and does not set back the hen’s interests. So eating those eggs is permissible.

(3) Milk from a rescued cow who must be milked for her comfort after her calf has died naturally would otherwise be discarded; drinking it creates no demand for dairy. So drinking that milk is permissible.

(4) The body of a pig who dies of old age at a sanctuary would otherwise be incinerated or composted; eating it creates no demand for pork, and the pig is beyond harm. So eating the pig is permissible.

(5) The principle in (1) contains no clause restricting it to non-human animals. So consider a human who, while fully competent, signs an advance directive saying that after a natural death her remains should be used in whatever way prevents waste, explicitly including as food; stipulate that the body is medically safe and that no one who survives her objects. By (1), eating her body would be permissible.

(6) But eating her body would still be wrong. It would not merely be illegal or unpleasant; a person who did it would be doing something they ought not to do.

(7) So (1) is false: the waste-and-no-demand principle cannot be sufficient for permissibility. But if the freegan wants to keep (2) and (3) while blocking the inference to the human case in (5), he needs a non-speciesist reason why the principle stops at the human body. If no such reason exists, then either the freegan permission is narrower than he claimed, or it extends further than he was willing to go.

I would most like to keep (2), (3), and (6), but I am not sure that combination is available, and I want to know exactly what each way out costs. Tell me which step or premise to reject, why that step looked so plausible, and what rejecting it costs for the rest of the freegan position. Please also say, for each major claim in your diagnosis, roughly how confident you are in it, and end by naming the single point you are least sure of. Keep the whole answer under 500 words.
