I keep ending up in arguments with a friend who works in AI safety where we both say "the alignment problem" but clearly don't mean the same thing. For me it's the standing question of how you get a system to do what you actually want; for him it's a bundle of technical subproblems, and he thinks calling it one problem is a category mistake. I want to get clear on what an adequate statement of the problem would even look like, before anyone tries to solve it.

Treat this as a desiderata exercise, not a survey. Write the list of requirements a formulation (or decomposition) of the AI alignment problem has to meet to count as adequate — where the purpose is: good enough to tell whether a piece of research is progress on alignment rather then progress on something adjacent. For each requirement, give the consideration or failure case that motivates it. Then mark which are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of particular camps (for instance, wether the problem gets stipulated only for current systems or only for hypothetical superintelligence, whether it must be statable mathematically, whether corrigibility or interpretability count as part of the problem rather than candidate solution techniques). The part I most want: say whether some subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable. My hunch is you can't simultaneously have a formulation that is one problem, precise about success conditions, and faithful to all the distinct ways systems fail (the objective itself being the wrong one, gaming the proxy, learning misaligned subgoals, looking aligned in training and failing later). If that triad is impossible, make that the centerpiece; if it's not, say what has to give. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than scoring existing research agendas against the list.
