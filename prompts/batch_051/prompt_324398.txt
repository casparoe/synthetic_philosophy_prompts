I have a rule for the 'man of his time' defence and I can't tell weather it's genuinely defensible or just hindsight bias with extra steps. The rule: I don't ask whether an act was widely accepted in the person's era. I ask whether the wrong was *legible to them where they actually stood*, and I operationalize that by hunting for counter-voices — contemporaries in roughly the same country, class, and information environment who condemned the act in something like my terms. If I can find even one counter-voice who isn't a total crank, I judge the figure by roughly modern standards, full stop. If I can't find any, I give them almost a complete pass, no matter how bad the act was.

Two things bother me. First, the rule makes blame depend on who happened to write their dissent down, which is obviously a selection effect in the surviving record, not a fact about what the figure could have known. If their peers privately knew it was wrong but stayed quiet, my rule still hands out a pass, and that seems to get "could have known" wrong. Second, the hard cutoff is doing real work and I'm suspicious of it: a single articulate dissenter shouldn't flip a whole moral judgement with no gradation, but that's exactly what my rule says.

This came up most recently in an argument about Jefferson vs. Columbus. For Jefferson I can easily point to contemporaries saying slavery was wrong, so the rule condemns him harder than most of my friends do. For Columbus I mostly can't, or the critics I half-remember object on grounds I don't share, so he gets the pass. That might be right, or it might just reveal that my rule is a machine for converting a biased sample of surviving letters into moral verdicts. So: is this rule defensible? If not, is there a repaired version that keeps the genuine insight without the arbitrariness? And how would I tell from the inside whether I'm tracking the thing I actually care about (whether the wrong was available to them) rather than rationalizing verdicts I already had?
