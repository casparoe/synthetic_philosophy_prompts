I want to work through a modification of Kavka's toxin puzzle that I have not seen treated explicitly.

First, the familiar setup, restated so we are working from the same base. A reliable billionaire offers to wire you one million dollars just after midnight if, at midnight, you genuinely intend to drink a vial of toxin at two o'clock the following afternoon. The toxin will make you nauseated for a day but has no lasting effects. The money is unconditional once wired: you need not actually drink to keep it. You know all of this, and you know that after the money is wired there will be no reason to drink; you will predictably decline at two o'clock. Kavka's question is whether you can form the intention at midnight, and whether doing so is rational. The standard take I have absorbed is that a rational agent cannot simply decide to intend to do what she knows she will not do, even when the intention would be extremely useful, and that this exposes a gap between what it would be good to intend and what you can bring yourself to intend.

Now change one thing. Suppose that at eleven o'clock tomorrow morning, a public random draw with probability q, known to you tonight, determines whether the billionaire opens a second, independent offer: a further sum large enough to make drinking clearly net-positive for you if offered — for concreteness, call it two hundred thousand dollars — payable only if you drink the toxin at two o'clock. If the draw succeeds, drinking at two o'clock is clearly the rational choice, given the extra payment. If the draw fails, you are exactly back in the original position: no reason to drink, and every reason to skip it. The midnight condition is unchanged: the original million is wired only if, at midnight, you hold a genuine, categorical intention to drink at two o'clock, not merely a conditional intention to drink if the second offer materializes. You know all of this before midnight.

Here is what I want to know. At q = 0, we have the original puzzle. At q = 1, the future reason to drink is present with certainty, so the standard difficulty seems to vanish. For 0 < q < 1, which of the following happens?

First, does any q greater than zero suffice to make the required categorical intention available to a rational agent, or is there a clean threshold q* at which the ability to form it switches on? What would set that threshold?

Second, or does the puzzle merely relocate? That is: at most you can form the conditional intention to drink if the draw succeeds, but a conditional intention does not satisfy the midnight condition, and a categorical intention remains unavailable because you know that with probability 1 − q you will not drink. Does that mean the paradox survives at every q below 1?

Third, which premise of the original puzzle is carrying the real weight: the certainty that you will not drink, or the demand that the intention be categorical rather than conditional? What does the modified case reveal about that distinction?
