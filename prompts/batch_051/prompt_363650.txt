I teach seventh-grade science, and the thing that's been nagging at me is that wrong answers on my tests are never random. The same ones come back year after year: plants eat soil, heavier things fall faster, the seasons happen because the Earth is closer to the sun in summer. When I press a kid on it, it's not just a fact they got wrong — they've got a whole little system behind it, with it's own logic, and they can defend it. I've started thinking of their wrong answers as theories they're actually running, not just holes where the correct fact should be. I'm not any kind of education researcher, just a teacher who's been staring at the same wrong answers for a while.

So I'm putting together a proposal for my department, and I want to think through how it actually runs before I pitch it. The rule would be: every unit test starts with a section called 'What I Used to Think,' where students write out the explanation they held before the unit and why it seemed right, and that section gets graded only on how fully and honestly they reconstruct the old theory — never on whether it was correct. On top of that, the class keeps a 'Wall of Dead Theories' where the recurring wrong systems get named (like 'Soil-as-Food Theory' or 'Force-Carried-Inside-the-Ball Theory'), and at the end of the term students get a small number of points for being able to explain an old theory, say what evidence finally killed it, and where it still sort of works.

I'm trying to picture the school year in advance. What does this do to which kids speak up, and which ones figure out they can game the honesty grading by inventing a wrong theory that sounds good? Does writing the old theory down cement it for some students instead of killing it? What happens by February, when the wall has twenty dead theories on it and the early ones are old news? I'd rather you commit to a picture: tell me how you think this mostly plays out over the year, what the one or two worst failure modes are, and where your own prediction is shakiest. Don't just give me a list of things that could happen.
