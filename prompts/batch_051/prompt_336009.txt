I am a high school student trying to think carefully about mental privacy and cognitive liberty as neurotechnology becomes more common, and I would like help extracting a principle from my own judgments. I have gone back and forth on a list of cases, and I would like to know whether there is one coherent view behind them, or whether some of my verdicts have to go.

Here are my verdicts on eight cases.

Case A. I buy a consumer EEG headband that detects when my attention drifts while I do homework and plays a soft tone. I set it up myself, and the data never leaves the device. My verdict: acceptable.

Case B. My school requires students in some classes to wear similar headbands that send a real-time focus score to the teacher. The school says it helps adjust instruction. My verdict: wrong.

Case C. Police test a theft suspect with a brain-imaging method to see whether he has a recognition response to details of the crime scene that were never made public. The result would not be used in court, only to decide whether to keep investigating him. My verdict: wrong.

Case D. A hospital uses the same kind of brain imaging to let a locked-in patient answer yes-or-no questions about pain and room preferences. The patient asked for the system and can stop at any time. My verdict: acceptable.

Case E. A market-research firm recruits adults with written consent, records their EEG while they watch advertisements, and publishes only aggregated results. My verdict: acceptable, though it leaves me slightly uneasy.

Case F. An insurance company offers a discount to customers who play focus-training games through a BCI device. The company receives only an overall engagement score, not raw brain data, and nobody is required to participate. My verdict: wrong, but I find it hard to say why.

Case G. A parent uses a mood-reading headband on a twelve-year-old to anticipate emotional meltdowns. The child is embarrassed and asks to remove it, but the parent says it helps the household. My verdict: wrong, though less wrong than the police case.

Case H. A healthy adult sells recordings of their own brain activity to a data broker that trades in neural information. My verdict: tolerable, since it is their own body and their own information, but I am not confident.

The pattern I see is something like: it is acceptable when a person controls the device and the information it produces, and wrong when a powerful institution reads the brain to evaluate, sort, or pressure the person. But that rough rule does not explain why E is acceptable while F is wrong, and it says nothing about children or medical necessity. I also notice that B resembles A in the signal being measured, and C resembles D in the technology being used, and yet my verdicts differ. I hope I have good reasons for the differences.

Is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures my verdicts? Where do mental privacy, understood as protecting the contents of my mind from outside access, and cognitive liberty, understood as protecting my freedom to think and feel on my own terms, actually come apart? Please state the principle precisely, say which case motivates each part of it, and then apply it to cases I have not settled: (1) a judge orders a brain-imaging memory test before sentencing a defendant, (2) an employer offers a BCI wellness program that employees may decline, but declines are remembered informally when promotions are decided, (3) a university uses focus-tracking to study whether online lectures hold attention, with students notified in advance, and (4) a parent agrees to an implanted device for an infant that detects brain activity before a seizure and alerts the parent. Finally, please tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether the best repair is to complicate the principle or to overturn one or two of my verdicts.

Please write your answer in American English.
