I chair the institutional review board of a mid-sized community hospital. We review roughly sixty interventional protocols a year, and our committee is deliberately mixed — a cardiologist, an oncologist, a hospital pharmacist, two staff nurses, a biostatistician, a community representative, and me — but none of us has formal training in research ethics, and we have about twenty-five minutes per protocol. A recent experience has made this inadequacy feel acute: we approved a randomized trial that a visiting ethicist later criticized for confusing the absence of hard evidence with genuine clinical uncertainty, and I now think the criticism was substantially fair. Since then I have been trying to understand clinical equipoise properly. My working understanding is that a randomized trial is ethically acceptable only when the relevant community of clinical experts is in a state of honest, professional disagreement about which arm is better, so that no participant is knowingly assigned to a treatment the community already regards as inferior. But I have not been able to turn that understanding into something my committee can actually run.

I would like you to write a decision procedure for us, not an essay about the history of the concept. It should be a sequence of questions to ask, in order, when we review a randomized trial. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer to continue. Mark the points at which the procedure returns a no, and the points at which it stops and hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than evidence. The procedure must be usable by intelligent non-ethicists in twenty-five minutes, and it must produce a decision we can document and defend when a sponsor or our administration pushes back.

Please build the procedure so that it is actually forced to handle at least the following hard cases rather than gliding over them:

- A single investigator sincerely believes arm A is better than arm B, while the wider expert community is genuinely divided. Should the investigator's private belief carry weight, and if so, what kind?
- The experts who disagree may be responding to different outcome measures, or to local resistance patterns, rather than to any genuine divergence about which treatment is better for patients overall. How do we tell the difference?
- A trial uses a placebo control when a known effective treatment exists, and the sponsor argues the existing treatment is too burdensome or insufficiently effective.
- A non-inferiority trial proposes a margin that some committee members regard as clinically meaningful. Who should set that margin, and on what basis?
- An interim analysis shows a trend that is not yet statistically persuasive but would change how we ourselves would consent if we were patients. Does watching data accumulate alter the equipoise judgment, and if so, how?

Then apply the procedure to the two protocols below. Show the path through the questions for each, where each stops or continues, and what the procedure directs us to do. At least one of the two applications should produce an answer I will not enjoy delivering, because the sponsor — and probably parts of our hospital leadership — will dislike it.

Protocol A: A 400-patient randomized trial comparing two oral antibiotics for complicated urinary tract infection in hospitalized adults. Both antibiotics are widely used; international guidelines list either as acceptable and describe the comparative evidence as weak. Some local urologists have strong preferences, but those preferences are based on anecdotal experience and local resistance data.

Protocol B: A 900-patient randomized non-inferiority trial of a new oral anticoagulant against warfarin in patients with mechanical heart valves. The new drug is taken without routine monitoring, which would be convenient, but pilot data suggest its annual thromboembolic-event rate may be higher than warfarin's. The sponsor proposes a non-inferiority margin of 2.5 percentage points absolute difference in annual thromboembolic events, a difference several cardiologists on our committee would regard as clinically important. The hospital would receive trial revenue, and the sponsor's main argument to patients is avoiding the burden of INR monitoring.

Finally, mark each step as either a finding we are checking about the world or a normative judgment we are making, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do. If you think I am asking the wrong question — because clinical equipoise cannot be operationalized this cleanly, or because it does not actually do the normative work I am assuming it does — tell me so directly and give me the most honest workable alternative rather than a checklist that merely redistributes the confusion.
