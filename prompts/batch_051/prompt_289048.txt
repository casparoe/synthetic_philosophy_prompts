Act as a professional philosopher who works on anthropic reasoning and Bayesian confirmation theory, and think through the whole argument carefully before you settle on a diagnosis. I've been trying on my own to work out whether the sheer number of observers a theory predicts should make us more confident in that theory, and I've hit an argument that seems to prove far too much. I want to know exactly which step to reject and what it costs to reject it.

Here is the argument.

(1) Suppose there are two live theories, Small and Large. They agree on everything we could ever observe about the universe except one point: Small says the universe will contain 200 billion conscious observers in total across all of space and time, while Large says it will contain 200 trillion. On all other grounds — simplicity, fit with our best physics, everything else — they are equally good, so before I take my own existence into account I should split my credence 50/50.
(2) If Small is true, there are 200 billion observer "seats"; if Large is true, there are a thousand times as many seats.
(3) My finding myself in one of those seats is more probable the more seats there are. If one theory says the universe contains exactly one conscious observer and another says it contains a million, and I know I am a conscious observer in one of the two, it is a million times more likely that I am in the observer-rich universe.
(4) That I exist is a piece of evidence I have, and rational updating requires updating on my total evidence.
(5) By (2) and (3), my existence is a thousand times more likely given Large than given Small.
(6) By Bayes' rule, my credence in Large should therefore rise from 50% to roughly 1000-to-1.
(7) But that is absurd. I would become nearly certain that the universe contains a thousand times more observers than Small says, without doing any experiment or making any new observation — just by reflecting on my own existence. And the argument generalizes: for any pair of theories that differ only in how many observers they predict, I should always become nearly certain of the one that predicts more. Something has to give.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Explain why that step looked so natural, state the main alternative positions, and spell out the most awkward commitment each one has to accept. I most want to hold onto the idea that a theory is not confirmed merely by predicting more people. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of the question.
