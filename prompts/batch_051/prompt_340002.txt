ok so i'm a senior and the student rep on my district's dress-code review comittee. We meet Thursday and I need to circulate a short memo before then, and I honestly don't know how to get the two sides to talk to each other instead of past each other.

The rule under review bans basketball shorts during class hours. The defenders keep saying the rule is justified because "students who wear basketball shorts treat the school day like gym and are more likely to goof off." The discipline data does show an association: the kids written up for being off-task were more likely to be wearing athletic shorts that day. But about 85% of the kids who wear them never get written up. Half the committee says that's fine, a rule can rest on a true generalization even if most cases are exceptions, the way "ducks lay eggs" is true even though most ducks never lay an egg. The other half says that's exactly the problem: "mosquitoes carry malaria" is true even though a tiny fraction of mosquitoes carry it, but you can't run a school like that when the 'kind' is human students and the policy punishes the majority for a minority.

I've been reading about generics because my philosophy club did a unit on them, and it's messier than either side admits. What makes a generic true isn't just "most Xs have the property." Some accounts say generics latch onto properties that are characteristic or attention-grabbing for the kind, rather than statistically common — which would turn the question into whether "goofs off in class" is characteristic of wearing basketball shorts the way "lays eggs" is characteristic of ducks. But other accounts say social generics about people behave differently from biological ones: they don't just describe a pattern, they reinforce it and encode a stereotype. I want the memo to actually put that distinction to work.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate. It should have a real bottom line — keep the ban, repeal it, or keep it with specific conditions — plus a time frame. It should give the committee a test for when a generic claim about students may justify a blanket rule, and it should treat the defenders' strongest point seriously rather than just calling it stereotyping. Also, please write the memo itself in British English, spelling and phrasing and all, even though I'm writing you in American English. Keep it something a committee would actually read in ten minutes.
