In a graduate seminar on Habermas's communicative rationality, a classmate dismissed my worry that even formally open deliberation can be quietly skewed by status, fluency, and confidence. He said the ideal speech situation and the rest of the theory all come down to a single requirement: "no force except that of the better argument is exerted." On his reading, if someone's point loses ground because of background advantages rather than the strength of the reasons, then the discourse has simply violated the requirement; the theory's job is only to note that violation, so there is nothing further to explain. I suspect this one-liner is carrying far more weight than he realizes, but I am having trouble saying exactly which weight.

I would like you to lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, from the trivial to the substantive to the probably false, and to say for each whether it actually supports my classmate's move. In particular, I want the readings of better and force made explicit: is a better argument the one that would persuade participants under idealized conditions, or the one that is in some independent sense stronger, such as better supported or more truth-conducive? Is its force motivational, causal, or something normative? For each reading, state whether it is plausible and whether it licenses dismissing a concern about power, or instead changes the subject. A list I can take back to the seminar would be ideal.
