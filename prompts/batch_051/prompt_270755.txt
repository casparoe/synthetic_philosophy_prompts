I'm a high school student, and I've been reading excerpts from Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot, the book built around his many attempts to render a short fixed-form poem by Clément Marot; my French class is doing the same exercise right now. A friend and I put together an argument that unsettles us because its conclusion seems to prove too much — that a fixed-form poem cannot genuinely be translated. Please answer as a professional philosopher of language who also translates poetry under formal constraint. Think carefully, and before you settle on a diagnosis, brainstorm the places where the reasoning below could come apart.

(1) A translation of a poem is faithful only if it preserves both what the poem says and how the poem works. (2) In a fixed-form poem, much of how the poem works consists in its formal machinery — rhyme, meter, line length, stanza shape — and the felt necessity of each word's place in that machinery. (3) That machinery is a feature of one language's sounds and grammatical resources: an English word cannot rhyme in French, and a French alexandrine works by French syllable-counting rather than by English stress, so the same meter does not simply transfer over. (4) A translation is written in a different language, so it cannot reproduce that same machinery while also carrying the literal sense; the words that carry the sense will not, except by luck, fall into the same patterns. (5) Therefore every translator of a fixed-form poem faces a forced choice: preserve the formal machinery or preserve the literal sense. (6) Yet translators keep publishing what they call translations of such poems, and readers keep receiving them as versions of the original. (7) Therefore either all those versions have silently abandoned the poem's form — making them paraphrases or new poems rather than translations of the original — or they have abandoned the sense — making them imitations rather than translations. Either way, translating a fixed-form poem is impossible.

I'm suspicious of this conclusion, but each step looked fine as we wrote it. Where does the argument go wrong, and what would I have to give up to escape it? If I must choose, I'd rather keep the thought that form is part of what a poem means (steps 1 and 2) than the thought that all those published translations are faithful, but I want to know exactly what keeping that costs.
