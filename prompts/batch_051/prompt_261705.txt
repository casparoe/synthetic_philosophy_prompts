A friend showed me this as a quick refutation of the principle of indifference, and I can't find the mistake. The principle of indifference says that if you have no reason to favor one of several mutually exclusive, exhaustive possibilities, you should assign them equal probability. Now take Bertrand's paradox: an equilateral triangle is inscribed in a circle, and we want the probability that a "randomly chosen" chord is longer than a side of the triangle. The argument runs:

(1) There are three natural ways to generate a random chord, and each one is just the indifference idea spread over a different set of equally plausible possibilities: pick two endpoints uniformly on the circumference; pick a radius and a point uniformly along it, then take the perpendicular chord; or pick the midpoint uniformly in the disk.

(2) Those three procedures give probabilities 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 for the chord being longer than the triangle side.

(3) Since the phrase "randomly chosen" doesn't pick out any one procedure, all three are equally legitimate representations of the same state of information.

(4) So the principle of indifference assigns three different probabilities to the same proposition from the same information.

(5) Therefore the principle is not a sound rule for turning ignorance into probabilities, at least once continuous spaces are involved.

Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and whether the conclusion survives once the failing step is repaired.
