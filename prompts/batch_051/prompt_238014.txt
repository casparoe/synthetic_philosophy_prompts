I run the grants program at a small effective-altruism-style family foundation. We make about forty grants a year, ranging from roughly $20,000 to $2 million, mostly in global health, animal welfare, and AI governance, with the board technically approving every docket but relying on our recommendations. We keep hitting the same wall. Every proposal has a first-order story: this intervention has a clean randomized trial behind it; this organization has a credible theory of change and a competent team. But the effects that actually seem to matter are second- and third-order: what the grant does to field norms, which careers it enables or forecloses, what precedent it sets for other funders, what happens if the project half-succeeds or fails quietly. Those effects are large, and I cannot honestly say we can bound them. The team oscillates between building spreadsheets that imply a precision we do not have and falling back on whether a proposal feels important.

I have followed the philosophical discussion of consequentialist cluelessness, roughly that for many decisions of this kind the expected value is dominated by downstream and far-future consequences that we can neither enumerate nor assign well-grounded probabilities to, so straightforward expected-value reasoning simply gives no usable answer. I want that difficulty translated into a reusable procedure my program officers can actually run before each grant recommendation. The deliverable should be a checklist or decision tree in words, usable by intelligent non-philosophers. A program officer has about half a day per proposal; I cannot ask for formal models of Knightian uncertainty, and I cannot accept "just do the robust thing" unless the procedure says what counts as robust and how we would know. The procedure should specify when first-order evidence is enough and when cluelessness genuinely bites; how to choose among hedging, funding a pilot, funding a small portfolio rather than one bet, or declining on uncertainty grounds; which distinctions from the philosophical discussion must survive translation and which can be dropped; and every step that is a judgment call dressed up as procedure should be marked as such.

Apply the procedure to two or three worked cases, using realistic proposals from our grantmaking area. Include at least one case where the procedure tells us to decline a grant we are emotionally and ideologically inclined to make, and be specific about what that proposal is. Include at least one case where the procedure tells us to go ahead despite encountering the same broad species of uncertainty. End by saying what the procedure cannot do: in particular, what it cannot tell us about actual long-run consequences, and the situations in which no procedure will remove the need to sit with unresolved uncertainty.
