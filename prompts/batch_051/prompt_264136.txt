Adam Smith in The Theory of Moral Sentiments has a passage I keep returning to, and I want to test it against something that didn't exist when he wrote it. His claim, as I understand it, is that much of our grief for the dead is directed at features of their situation that cannot actually affect them—being shut away in the dark, being cut off from the company of the living, being forgotten—and that this is a kind of sympathetic illusion, because the dead themselves are beyond such concerns. I've been trying to use this to think about griefbots: AI systems trained on a dead person's texts and speech so that the bereaved can keep interacting with a rough simulation of them. On one hand, griefbots seem to feed the exact mechanism Smith describes, giving the mourner a vivid surrogate for the dead person's continued worldly life. On the other hand, they change the premise that the dead are fixed and unreachable: the simulated person can say new things, respond, and evolve in the mourner's life, making it harder to say the grief is for something that cannot matter to the deceased. Does Smith's account survive this development, need repair, or become inapplicable? Which specific premise of his analysis does the griefbot put under pressure, and is there a repaired version that preserves what was right about it while acknowledging that something new is going on?
