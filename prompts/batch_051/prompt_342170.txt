I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I lead a small machine-learning team at a company that builds underwriting-support software for community banks. Our models flag loan applications for mandatory human review, and our model-risk group has started refusing to sign off on new model versions unless they meet something they call legibility. As I reconstruct their position, it is this: a model can beat the incumbent on held-out accuracy and still be a worse system to ship, because if the route from inputs to score is opaque, then being right for the wrong reason is a latent defect — one that will eventually surface as an error no reviewer sees coming. They distinguish three things I don't fully grasp. First, an explanation can sound plausible after the fact without faithfully reflecting the features the model actually used. Second, being able to explain one prediction is not the same as being able to predict, without re-running the model, how it will generalize on nearby cases. Third, legibility for an engineer who can read the code is not the same as legibility for a compliance analyst who sees only the output and a narrative. I don't know how much of this survives contact with a real release process, and I need it turned into a procedure my team can actually run, not a philosophy essay.

Constraints: we release quarterly, we evaluate two or three candidate model versions per release, and each candidate review can get about half a day of attention from two senior engineers and one risk analyst. The stakes are real: a false flag sends a small-business borrower into a slow manual process, and a missed red flag can cost a community bank enough to matter. The model-risk committee still makes the final sign-off; our job is to send them a clear recommendation. Please write a reusable pre-release decision procedure for judging whether a candidate model version meets a legibility standard, with legibility treated as a separate requirement from accuracy — so that a more accurate but less understandable version can fail, and a less accurate but more understandable version can pass. Structure the response exactly as follows. (1) "Operating principle" (about 150 words): restate the legibility requirement as a single testable standard, and say explicitly which of the three distinctions above survive in the procedure and which are dropped. (2) "The procedure" (about 350 words): the ordered questions a reviewer asks, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a fail, and where it hands the decision to the model-risk committee as a matter of values rather than facts. (3) "Worked applications" (about 300 words): run the procedure on two candidate versions — one with better accuracy than the incumbent whose explanations are plausible but unfaithful, and one with strong legibility but lower accuracy than the incumbent, so that the procedure gives an answer the accuracy-first engineers on my team, and honestly part of me, will not want to accept. (4) "Judgment calls and limits" (about 200 words): flag where the procedure still requires a human to make a value call, how to keep those calls from quietly becoming technical ones, and what the procedure cannot guarantee even when it passes a model.
