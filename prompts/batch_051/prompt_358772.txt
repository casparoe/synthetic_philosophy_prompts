I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and the debate I keep returning to is whether artificial intelligence will arrive as a discontinuity or as an acceleration. I find the cleanest argument points one way while my background sense of how technologies actually spread points the other, and I want a verdict rather than a survey.

The position that attracts me is the software-loop form of the intelligence-explosion argument. Once a system becomes capable of doing AI research — identifying promising architectures, writing and debugging training code, designing and interpreting experiments, and implementing its own improvements — the thing being improved is the improver. Each round raises the quality of the next round's work, so the binding constraint moves off human research labor and onto the system's own recursive self-improvement. The usual hardware objection is met by noting that a jump in ability can be spent on software that wrings far more performance from the same chips before any new fabrication is needed. The theory is appealing exactly because it is nearly parameter-free: it does not depend on a particular chip, firm, or architecture, and it explains a vertical takeoff as what happens when the scarcest input to AI progress stops being scarce.

The intuition I cannot shake is that the binding constraints are in the world rather than in the model. Better software does not fabricate chips, permit data centers, lay power infrastructure, collect calibration data, or change the practices of the institutions that would have to adopt these systems. Those processes have long, incompressible lead times and remain capital-intensive no matter how intelligent the researcher is. And even a model that is superhuman at proposing improvements must still have those proposals validated empirically, which means running them, which means the same physical and organizational bottlenecks. So my intuition says the loop should be fast but bounded — perhaps shockingly fast by historical standards, but not a vertical takeoff, because the world does not compound the way software does.

I want you to settle this from a definite position, not lay out the options and leave me to pick. Say whether the theory should yield, the intuition should yield, or the two are compatible under a better description of what the loop actually improves, and argue for that verdict. Then make clear what evidence or argument would justify each of the moves you are rejecting, so I can see what your position costs. Lead with your bottom line in your first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
