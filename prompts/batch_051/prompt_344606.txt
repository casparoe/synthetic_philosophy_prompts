I have recently started designing Bongard-style problems—the format in which a solver sees six left-hand panels that all obey an unstated rule and six right-hand panels that all violate it, and must produce the rule rather than merely classify the panels—because I want to use them with students to study how a concept can actually be fixed from a handful of examples instead of merely recognised against a known label. Before I build the set any further, I want the standard against which I can judge any account of what makes such problems solvable at all. Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of few-example concept induction of this kind, and give the case or consideration that forces each requirement. Be exact about the following, among others: the underdetermination problem, since infinitely many rules are consistent with any twelve panels, so a theory must say how a unique intended rule is recovered, or else give an account of intendedness; the role of the negative panels, which are not noise but seem to delimit the rule; the selection of the right level of abstraction, since a rule may concern colour, shape, number, orientation, symmetry, or a relation; the avoidance of spurious or accidental regularities; and the difference between inducing a concept and merely classifying examples. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps. If some subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable—I suspect that recovering a unique rule from six examples, without prior constraints on the hypothesis space, while covering arbitrary rules cannot all be had at once—say so, and explain what the list itself therefore commits any adequate theory to. End by indicating which requirement or pair of requirements does most to exclude otherwise attractive candidates. Please write the answer in British English.
