I've been trying to turn a recurring forum argument into a credence, because the disagreement keeps hiding in a few load-bearing sub-questions that nobody separates. The argument is about whether video-game characters can matter morally. Consider a concrete, buildable game rather than a philosophical zombie. In Oakhollow, a single-player settlement simulator, each settler is a model-based reinforcement-learning agent: it carries an internal model of its environment and learns actions that keep its bound-states for injury, hunger, fear, and social attachment. A pain-prediction stream fires when that model anticipates damage, triggering moans, limping, flight, and generalized avoidance of novel threats. Settlers also have long-term episodic memory and stable dispositional preferences; they bind one another's wounds, mourn their dead, and change multi-day plans to avoid future harm. In blind comparisons, observers cannot reliably sort clips of settlers under threat from clips of real animals in comparable distress. The architecture, however, has no global workspace, no metacognition, and no language-based self-report; the developers explicitly decline to claim there is anything it is like to be a settler, describing them as welfare-tracking agents rather than conscious subjects.
