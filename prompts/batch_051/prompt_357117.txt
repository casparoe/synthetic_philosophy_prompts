I got into a holiday argument with my cousin, who works in data science, about whether “ducks lay eggs” is true. He kept saying it’s false because drakes never lay eggs, and that any careful statement would have to be “female ducks lay eggs.” I tried to explain that generics work differently, and I brought up the standard case of “mosquitoes carry malaria”—the sentence seems accepted even though only a tiny proportion of mosquitoes actually transmit the parasite. He conceded that people do talk this way, but then said thats just evidence that generics are loose, practical shorthand with no real truth conditions, so you can’t safely reason from “Ks are F” to any particular K or to any precise statistic.

Thats been bugging me because I use these sentences all the time, and I dont want to give up on them being truth-evaluable. There has to be one principled rule for when “Ks are F” is acceptable, something that covers both the ducks case and the mosquito case and that also tells me what, if anything, the generic commits me to. What is the rule? Take a definite position: give me the version you would actually defend, and explain why it handles the hard cases better than the alternatives.
