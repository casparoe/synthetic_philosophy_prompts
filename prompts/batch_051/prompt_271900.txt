I've been using a distinction in arguments about veganism outside rich countries and I want it stress-tested, because I might just be dressing up a cop-out. I call them 'ambient omnivory' and 'elective omnivory'. Ambient omnivory is when someone eats animal products because the food system around them makes those the obvious or only reliable source of calories, protein or fat: think fish for a coastal village that can't get pulses cheaply, or milk in a region where the land won't grow much else. The eater isn't really choosing against the animals; the menu they were born into is already carnivorous. Elective omnivory is when decent alternatives are available and affordable and you eat the animal stuff anyway because it's tastier or easier. My claim is that a lot of western vegan argument treats the ambient kind as if it were elective, and that in ambient cases the moral failure belongs to the food environment — subsidies, infrastructure, the absence of alternatives — rather than to the default eater, who mostly isn't exercising anything worth calling a choice. I know this is somewhere near the ought-implies-can principle and non-ideal theory, and probably near structural-injustice talk too. What I want to know: am I making a real distinction, or is it already on the books under a name I don't know, and does it do real work or just produce a nicer word for excuse? What does it misclassify? Are there ambient omnivores who should still count as choosing, and elective omnivores the distinction wrongly lets off? And is there a danger that making the food environment into the villain turns it into nothing in particular, so no actual person is ever responsible?
