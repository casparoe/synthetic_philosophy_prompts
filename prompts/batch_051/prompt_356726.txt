I've more or less accepted the Gibbard–Satterthwaite way of looking at voting: for any rule that takes everyone's ranked preferences over three or more candidates and returns a single winner, if the rule isn't dictatorial, some voter will sometimes have an incentive to misstate their preferences. I treat that not just as a formal result but as a practical lesson, something like "every real election method is gameable, so stop looking for the ungameable one."

What I'd like from you is a map of what would count against that position, or against the practical lesson I'm drawing from it. Which assumptions of the theorem are doing the actual work, and what would the world (or the voting rule) have to be like for each to fail? I know some of the standard escape routes exist in the literature—restricting preferences to single-peaked ones, allowing a random winner rather than a fixed one, moving to cardinal ballots like score voting or approval voting—but I want you to sort them by how live they are rather than just listing them. For each: is it a realistic way to get a non-dictatorial method that's strategy-proof, or is it just a logically possible world that buys strategy-proofness by giving up something I'd actually care about?

Also tell me whether the theorem itself is exposed or whether only my practical lesson is. I suspect the formal result is unassailable and the real question is whether the practical reading is overtrading—maybe manipulability mostly doesn't matter because voters can't identify a beneficial misreport in practice, or because the manipulation is a tie or a tiny shift in outcome, or because norms and ignorance do the deterring. If that's the best objection, say so and tell me how much it should move me.

I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including which ones a defender of my position should concede as counting against it. And be honest about whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability: if no observation about actual elections could move me, say so and say whether that's a problem for this kind of claim.

I'll personally find an argument most persuasive if it points to a concrete mechanism—a real rule, or a realistic property of preferences—rather than a pure possibility result. Keep it under 500 words and write in American English.
