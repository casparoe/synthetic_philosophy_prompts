I cover science and ideas for a magazine, so every January I end up re-reading the same ritual: during the State of the Union, one cabinet secretary gets hidden away at an undisclosed location, on the logic that if the Capitol were attacked and the entire elected leadership died at once, that one person would become president. I'm trying to pin down what this practice is quietly assuming about political legitimacy and about what a state even is. It seems to treat government a bit like a machine that keeps running as long as one authorized part survives — regardless of wether anyone voted for that person, or whether they'd be any good at running a country. But the rest of the time we talk as if authority comes from elections and the consent of the governed. What would have to be true for that survivor to be the rightful president the next morning, and is that consistent with how we normally justify authority? Does the practice also assume that mere continuity — keeping the same legal entity going — matters more than whatever the surviving public might choose in the chaos afterwards? I'm less interested in the constitutional mechanics and more in the theory of authority it commits us to, if it commits us to anything at all.
