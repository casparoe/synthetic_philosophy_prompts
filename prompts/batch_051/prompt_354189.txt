You are an expert in the philosophy of language and philosophical logic, with a specialist's command of the sorites paradox and the main theories of vagueness. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming all the objections this audience could plausibly raise before you order them.

I am an undergraduate in a philosophy of language seminar, and next week I am giving the seminar presentation defending epistemicism about vagueness. My thesis is that a vague predicate such as "heap" has, at each moment of use, a sharp cut-off that no speaker is in a position to know. The sorites argument is valid; the tolerance premise (for every n, if n grains make a heap then n minus one grains make a heap) is simply false, but we cannot identify the counterexample. I defend this because it allows us to keep classical logic and bivalence without revising semantics. The price is accepting unknowable sharp facts.

The audience is my seminar. The professor is a supervaluationist, who thinks vague predicates are governed by admissible sharpenings and that sentences about borderline cases are neither true nor false. One graduate student works on degree-theoretic accounts and will insist that borderline cases form a genuine continuum. Two classmates have argued in earlier weeks, respectively, that vagueness is really a form of context-sensitivity, and that the right lesson of the sorites is to abandon classical logic rather than the tolerance premise.

Please draft a Q&A preparation sheet for me. For each objection, phrase it roughly as the relevant person would actually put it, say who is most likely to raise it, estimate how damaging it is, and give a reply I could deliver in about two minutes. For each reply, note where it is softest — where a sharp follow-up would do the most damage. Order the entries by some combination of likelihood and damage, and end with the one question I am hoping nobody asks. I do not need citations; I need the dialectical moves themselves.
