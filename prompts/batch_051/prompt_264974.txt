I've been teaching myself premodern mathematics alongside its modern reinterpretations, and I can't settle a standing dispute with myself. On reflection I'm strongly drawn to the anti-anachronism view: to present Archimedes' determination of a parabolic area as an application of the definite integral is to import the concepts of limit, function, and the real continuum into a proof that had none of them. The method of exhaustion, as historians describe it, works through a double reduction to impossibility over inscribed and circumscribed polygons, and reading it as taking a limit turns a distinct mathematical practice into a clumsy preview of calculus. That view has genuine systematic virtues: it gives a clear rule about when a reading is legitimate, it explains why so much history of mathematics looks whiggish, and it respects the historical individuation of concepts. Yet I also have a firm intuition I cannot dislodge: if a modern translation is formally faithful, and if it exposes exactly the same dependence among quantities as the original proof, then there is something true and nontrivial in saying Archimedes proved a result about the area under a curve. The mathematical object does not seem to care whether the author would have recognized our vocabulary for it.

Present each side at its strongest, then give me a negotiated verdict. Specifically, I want to know what exactly would justify giving up the intuition (for example, showing that the faithful translation is doing hidden work only a modern concept can supply), what would justify amending or abandoning the anti-anachronism principle, and whether there is a stable reading — perhaps distinguishing proof-content from concept-possession, or distinguishing explaining an author from exploiting a result — on which both can survive. Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
