I have been reading through the Hart–Devlin debate in preparation for a thread on legal moralism, and I keep hitting a compressed version of one of Devlin's moves. The move is that a society is held together by a shared positive morality, something like the way masonry is held together by mortar; that the criminal law is one of the main instruments through which a society repairs and preserves that mortar; and that to tolerate private immorality is therefore like declining to repair cracks in the structure, or, in Devlin's stronger comparisons, like declining to act against subversion. The conclusion this is meant to license is that there is nothing illiberal in principle about using the criminal law to enforce a society's morality as such, even for conduct with no obvious direct harm to non-consenting others, because what the law protects is not the conduct's immediate effects but the structural integrity of the society.

I would like an audit of this analogy rather than a point-by-point restatement of Hart's replies. Please give me an explicit correspondence table between the masonry picture and the social reality it is supposed to illuminate: what plays the role of the stones, the mortar, the cracks, the weather, the owner who carries out the repairs, and the failure point of the whole. Then do the analytical work: identify the load-bearing disanalogies and separate them from the ones that are merely incidental color.

Here is the angle I want the audit organized around. A significant part of rationalist-adjacent discussion now treats social norms as coordination equilibria stabilized by common knowledge: a norm is stable because enough people act on it and enough people know that enough people act on it. That model suggests a society can absorb a great deal of private, low-visibility immorality, because what matters for the equilibrium is not the raw number of violations but the public state of the norm and the mutual assurance around it. So the central question is whether the cement analogy quietly imports a material-substance picture into a domain that is really about shared expectations and assurance: damage accumulates mechanically, integrity degrades continuously, and even invisible cracks weaken the whole. Does that picture distort the mechanism by which conduct, especially private conduct, threatens or fails to threaten a society's integrity? And which version of Devlin's conclusion, if any, survives once the mechanism is corrected: the strong disintegration thesis, the moderate conservation thesis that a society may legitimately steer its moral climate through law, or only the much weaker claim that law may suppress conduct that directly attacks shared trust?

If something survives, I would also like a better analogy for whatever does survive, if one exists, and a brief note on where its own stress points are. The deliverable should let me say, in a debate, exactly which parts of the familiar rhetoric about morality holding society together are doing real argumentative work and which parts are vivid phrasing.
