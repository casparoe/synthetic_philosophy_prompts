Since a friend in emergency medicine described her triage training to me, I have been trying to figure out whether the numbers should count. The protocol she learned directs responders to the incident with more victims when the injuries are equally severe, and I found that instruction obviously right. But when I tried to articulate why, I built an argument that now seems to show the protocol is merely a permissible convention, and I cannot find the exit. Here it is.

(1) Suppose you hold the only dose of an antidote and must choose between saving one stranger and saving five strangers. None of the six has any stronger claim to the dose than anyone else, and no third party would be differently affected by your choice. (2) If you save the five, the one loses an entire life; if you save the one, each of the five loses an entire life. The one's loss is exactly as serious as any one of the five's losses, so the one has a claim as strong as any one of the five. (3) But the five's claims cannot be added together into a single claim with five times the weight. There is no person who suffers five deaths and no person who enjoys five saved lives; the number five merely counts how many separate claims there are, and a number of claims, apart from the claimants themselves, is not the kind of thing that can carry a stronger reason. (4) Therefore the fact that five are at stake gives you no reason to prefer saving the five over saving the one. You may flip a coin, and if it comes up for the one, you have done nothing wrong. (5) If that is right, the same license extends wherever the numbers are the only difference: a triage officer may settle a tie between one waiting patient and five by flipping a coin, a rescue crew may flip a coin between a building with one person trapped and one with five, and an aid agency may fund a program that prevents one death rather than an equally costly program that prevents five.

But those extensions strike me as false. The triage officer who coin-flips and lets the five die while treating the one seems to me to have made a mistake, not a permissible choice; the rescue crew that flips while five are trapped seems wrong, not merely unconventional. So one of the premises must go, or the move from them to the conclusion must fail somewhere. Which premise or step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it commit me to? I would most like to keep (1) and (2); if I cannot, I want to know what I am paying for the view that replaces them. Lead with your bottom line in your first sentence, and then give your reasoning; do not begin with a preamble or restate the question.
