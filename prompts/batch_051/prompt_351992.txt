I keep coming back to epiphenomenalism. The physicist in me wants to accept the standard package: the physical domain is causally closed, every bodily movement has a complete physical cause, and so adding a mental cause on top would be either overdetermination or a violation of closure. Qualia end up as by-products — caused, but causing nothing. The view is tidy, parsimonious, and I honestly dont know how to deny the closure premise.

But then I burn my hand on the stove and the pain sure seems to be what makes me pull it back and later tell you I'm in pain. More generally: if phenomenal experience is causally sealed off from my own speech and judgment, how is it that I'm so reliably able to talk and reason about my experience? It would be a pretty wild coincidence if my reports of pain were caused entirely by the underlying neural machinery while the pain itself never once nudged a syllable. The intuition that my experience does something is about as firm as any I've got.

So here's the clash. I dont want to hear that epiphenomenalism is obviously false or that I should just trust the argument. What I want to know is what it would actually take for the intuition to be the thing to go, what it would take for the theory to go, and whether there's a reading on which they're compatible — maybe theyre only incompatible with a bad gloss on what causing my report requires, or on what it means for a judgment to be explained by the very experience it's about. Give me the strongest version of each move, then tell me which you'd give up and why.
