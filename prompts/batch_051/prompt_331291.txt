I keep reading discussions about when someone deserves moral credit for doing the right thing, and there's one kind of case that comes up over and over that I don't have a stable name for. Two examples. An adult hands in a wallet with cash in it because, growing up, her mother drummed into her that keeping what isn't yours is what bad people do, and she just trusts that; if you asked her why handing back a wallet matters, she couldn't say much beyond that she was raised right. A second person gives money to a disaster-relief charity because a friend who reads a lot of moral philosophy told him it was the ethically right thing to do; he hasn't looked into what the charity actually does and couldn't defend the choice himself. Both acts come out right, and neither person seems selfish. But when people argue about whether these two acted for the right reasons, it feels like there's a specific difference in play: they're doing the right thing on somebody else's say-so, without seeing for themselves what makes the act right. Does that setup have an established name, and does the moral-worth literature have a settled view on whether acts like these get full moral credit? I don't just want the label; I want the borders. How is this different from (a) doing the right thing for selfish reasons, (b) doing it because you see and are moved by the considerations that make it right, and (c) doing it from a bare sense of duty without seeing why either? Also, please seperate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
