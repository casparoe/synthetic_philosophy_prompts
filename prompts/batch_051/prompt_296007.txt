I've been going around in circles in a discussion about what, exactly, makes an assertion appropriate. One camp keeps saying the rule is simple: don't assert what is false. Once what you said is true, they argue, further complaints (that your evidence was thin, that you reasoned badly, that you got lucky) are really complaints about your evidence gathering or your reasoning — not about the assertion itself. The other camp says the rule has to be: assert only what you know. That, they say, is why we criticize someone who blurts out a lucky guess that happens to be true, and why "my ticket lost" seems improper even when the odds are overwhelming. I'd like you to write a dialogue between two proponents of these positions — the truth norm and the knowledge norm — set somewhere concrete: maybe they're debriefing after a talk in which a speaker made a bold, true, but poorly grounded claim, and half the room objected that the claim wasn't known while the other half objected only to the false things that were said.

Both characters should be genuinely competent, and each should have to do real work against the other's strongest data: the lucky-but-true assertion, the lottery ticket, the true claim made from a scrambled but accidentally accurate memory, and first-person sentences like "p, but I don't know that p" or "p, but I shouldn't assert that p." Have them respond to each other's actual moves rather than restating their positions, and allow them to concede small points while holding the main line. After the dialogue, include a brief editorial note, separate from the dialogue itself, distinguishing the points in this debate that are reasonably settled from those that are still live among philosophers, and make clear that neither the truth norm nor the knowledge norm is the consensus view.
