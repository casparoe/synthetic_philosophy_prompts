Here's something I keep going back and forth on. I'm trying to get clear on whether treating non-human things as historical actors is actually a useful shorthand or just loose talk. Consider three candidates: pathogens (e.g. the Black Death, the smallpox epidemics after 1492), climate shifts (e.g. the Little Ice Age, the Medieval Warm Period), and individual volcanic eruptions (e.g. Tambora, Laki). I want you to rank them by how much explanatory work they can do as actors in human history, not merely as background conditions.

Before you rank, score each of the three from 1 to 10 on each of these five dimensions:

a) how event-like and bounded it is, rather than a diffuse condition;
b) how legible its effects are in sources people left behind;
c) how much its historical impact depends on the human institutions it happens to hit;
d) how strongly historians are tempted to personify it;
e) how well the actor treatment survives when you shift from a ten-year to a two-hundred-year timeframe.

Then produce a tier list (S, A, B, C) of the three as historical actors, and give two or three sentences per candidate justifying its tier. Finally, give me one concrete example where treating one of these as an actor would probably mislead, and one where refusing to treat it that way would miss something real.
