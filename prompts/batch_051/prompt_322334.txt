I have been asked to deliver the eulogy at the memorial for my uncle, who spent forty years maintaining and rebuilding the pipe organ at a large Episcopal church and, before that, tuned pianos for a living. The room will be mostly family and fellow parishioners who knew him simply as a quiet, dexterous, almost invisible presence; but three or four friends from his student years will also be there, and they still tell a story about the semester he tried to teach them Heidegger by taking apart a broken metronome on a pub table. I want the eulogy to be built, from the ground up, on Heidegger's distinction between the ready-to-hand and the present-at-hand, and on the way a tool reveals itself and its whole network of references only when it breaks. The speech should never explain this vocabulary or name Heidegger—at most it may contain one glancing phrase that only the old friends will catch—because I want the idea to do structural work rather than sit on the surface as decoration.

Practically, that means the eulogy should be organized as a series of remembered breakdowns: the console that stalled during the Easter Vigil, the swollen wooden tracker that seized in the middle of a wedding processional, the drowned blower motor after the sacristy flooded, the gap in the choir loft on the Sunday he was in the hospital. Through each story, the man himself should become visible only in the way he restored the seamless, unnoticed flow of liturgy and rehearsal; he should be rendered as the person whose daily work was to keep the equipment from ever becoming conspicuous. His death itself should function as the final breakdown, the one he is not there to repair, which suddenly makes explicit the entire background of care and attention he had been holding together. Write it so that the family hears grief and gratitude, and the old friends hear one long, unspoken piece of Heidegger.
