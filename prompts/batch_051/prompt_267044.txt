Trying to get my head around when I should distrust my own read on an argument because of facts about my read, rather than facts in the argument itself. My gut reactions to a bunch of cases keep pulling in different directions, and I'd like to know whether there's one principle behind them.

1. Two drinks and an airtight proof. I work through a statistical argument after two drinks. Every line still looks right when I go through it slowly. But I know my slip rate goes up after two drinks, even when I can't spot the slips. Reaction: I should hold the conclusion at maybe 0.6, not the 0.9 my read of the argument would suggest.

2. Disagreement with an equal. A friend whose exactly as good as me at this stuff looks at the same data and reaches the opposite conclusion. We walk through each other's reasoning and neither of us finds an error in their own. Reaction: I should move to roughly 0.5 between the two answers, even though my reasoning still looks right and hers still looks wrong.

3. The error hint. A colleague who's a better proof-checker than me tells me she's found a real flaw in my proof but wants me to find it myself first. Until she shows me, I should lower my confidence a lot, even though every line checks out on my end.

4. The jury base rate. I'm on a jury. Having sat through the whole trial, I put guilt at about 0.85; I can point to specific moments in cross-examination that I don't see an innocent reading of. Then I'm told that juries from my county convict at a much higher rate than juries from the next county over, on evidence of the same type. Reaction: barely moves me — down to maybe 0.82. The coarse base rate just doesn't feel like it says much about whether my particular assessment was sound.

5. The overconfidence gas. Someone I fully trust tells me the room my group has been reasoning in has an odorless gas that makes arguments feel stronger than they are; everyone exposed gets a warm 'oh, that has to be right' feeling. I re-read my argument, still get the feeling, still find no flaw, and end up 0.9 in P. Reaction: I should drop a lot, maybe to 0.35, just because the gas gives me a standing explanation for why the argument feels strong apart from how strong it actually is.

6. The underconfidence gas. Same setup, but the gas is known to make arguments feel weaker than they are — a nagging 'eh, there's probably a problem somewhere' feeling. I read an argument, can't find a specific flaw, and put P at 0.25. Reaction: I'd barely move, maybe to 0.3. Something in me refuses to swing up to 0.65 in the mirror-image way; trusting a conclusion because a chemical might be suppressing my doubts feels weirder than withholding when a chemical might be inflating my conviction.

Cases 5 and 6 are mirror images and I treat them asymmetrically — is that defensible, or is it just a glitch in my epistemic gut? More generally, is there one principle about how to weigh higher-order evidence against your first-order take that gets 1–5, and does it say my reaction in 6 is wrong? If no single principle gets them all, tell me which of my reactions I should abandon, and why that one rather than the others. Then run the principle you settle on through a case I haven't listed: a pharmacist does a dosage calculation three times and gets the same number each time; a decision-support app that is 90% reliable at catching exactly this kind of arithmetic slip says she's wrong, without showing where. What should her confidence in her number be, and why?
