I'm putting together a unit on coordination problems for my political philosophy course, and a colleague sent me this summary of the Moloch idea from a blog post he likes. I have a rough sense of the game theory underneath, but I don't want to pass a garbled version on to my students. Here's the summary:

"Moloch is the name for a pattern in which competition forces everyone to do something none of them actually want. The standard example: two gas stations would both prefer to close at 10pm, but each knows that if it closes early the other will steal its customers, so both stay open all night and end up no better off, just more exhausted. Scott Alexander's point is that this is not a special feature of capitalism but a general feature of any situation where individual incentives point one way and collective interests point another. It shows up in arms races, beauty standards, grade inflation, and the way tech platforms are forced to make their products worse. The upshot is that when you see a bad outcome that nobody chose, the cause is competition, and the fix is to remove the competitive pressure, usually by handing the decision to a single authority that can act in everyone's interest. The essay calls the force Moloch because we've been sacrificing what we value to a false god of competition. None of this requires irrationality or bad intentions; it's just what happens when rational people respond to local incentives."

Before I build a lecture around this, can you give me an accuracy audit? Go through it and separate what's right, what's a simplification, and what's wrong — and in particular say whether the leap from 'this trap exists' to 'the fix is to centralize' actually follows. I'm also trying to sort out weather Moloch is just a vivid name for stuff game theorists already had (tragedy of the commons, prisoner's dilemmas, arms races) or whether the idea of a multipolar trap picks out something more specific, and where a plain race to the bottom fits in. I'd like the lecture to be fair to the view even if I end up pushing back on it.
