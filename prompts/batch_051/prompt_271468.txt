I work in a small city policy shop and we're redrafting the local estate tax exemptions. We keep coming back to whether passing down a family farm, a rental building, or shares of a family company should all be treated the same as passing down cash. A board member keeps pushing this passage from Thomas Paine's Agrarian Justice at me and says it settles it, but I honestly cant tell what it settles. The passage is: "And as it is impossible to separate the improvement made by cultivation, from the earth itself, upon which that improvement is made, the idea of landed property arose from that inseparable connection; but it is nevertheless true, that it is the value of the improvement only, and not the earth itself, that is individual property."

I have no philosophy background and neither does anyone else here, so I need you to turn whatever principle is actually in that passage into a decision procedure someone in my office can follow. The goal we care about is equal opportunity: not making every kid start with exactly equal wealth, but not letting inherited claims to land and other people's need for it pile up across generations for free. The procedure should say what questions to ask in order, what counts as the "earth itself" versus the "improvement" in modern cases, what to do about land that's been sold many times, mineral or zoning value, leases, family trusts that mix land and investments, and whether the tax should hit at death or at the moment the heir takes control. Give me two or three worked examples, one where the procedure says a family owes something even though the family will hate it. Then flag the places where the procedure stops and a person has to just decide. And please keep the theory part straight with what's settled vs what's contested; dont present a contested view as the consensus.
