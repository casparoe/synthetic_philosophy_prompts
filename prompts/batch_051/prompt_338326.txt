Okay so I've been reading Fabre's *The Hunting Wasps*, and the famous bit about the yellow-winged Sphex keeps bugging me. For background if you don't know it: the wasp catches a cricket, stings it, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it outside on the threshold, goes down alone to check the burrow, comes back up, and only then drags the cricket inside. Fabre found that if you move the cricket a few inches away while she's down below, she'll come back up, drag it back to the threshold, and go down to inspect all over again — as many times as you move it, each time repeating the whole entrance ritual, even though nothing changed inside. The usual reading is that she's a rigid automaton locked into a fixed stimulus-response chain; the absence of the cricket from the threshold doesn't register as "already done." Hofstadter called this sphexishness and used it as the opposite of flexible, free-ish behavior.

I've been thinking this is actually backwards, or at least half-wrong. My theory: the wasp isn't failing to notice anything. She's operating with what I call a threshold-verification norm — the rule that the cricket must be on the threshold at the moment the burrow check is completed, where the check isn't really a fact about the burrow but a fact about the ritual's completion conditions. If the cricket moves, the condition hasn't been met, so she re-runs the check. That sounds like a dumb technicality, but I think it's the same structure as human cases: people redoing a proof because they changed a variable's name, re-reading a sentence after a single typo was fixed even though the meaning didn't change, a committee re-voting because the wording changed though the substance didn't. The rigidity isn't the absence of a norm; it's having a norm so precise that it treats irrelevant changes as relevant. And if that's right, then sphexishness and free will aren't opposites — free will might just be having enough norms, or high-enough-order norms, to decide which changes are irrelevant. A computer running a fixed program is sphexish, but so is a person who can't stop checking whether they locked the door; the difference is in what counts as a failed verification, not in whether there's a verification at all.

Is this a real distinction or am I just re-describing something obvious? I know Dennett made sphexishness famous, and there's a whole clinical literature on compulsive checking, but I'm not aware of anyone claiming the wasp's behavior is norm-governed rather than merely automatic. Give me the three strongest objections, and tell me which one is fatal. Also: wouldn't a simpler model — the wasp has a variable for 'cricket at threshold' that resets to false whenever the cricket moves, and that variable gates the entry procedure — explain the same thing without my norm talk? If so does my theory actually add anything, or is "norm" just a word I'm layering on top of a variable?
