I've got a standing rule for my own thinking: anything I'd put below about a 1% chance, I treat as zero. Not because I think it's actually impossible, but because if I gave every low-probability idea a seat at the table I'd never finish deciding anything. Lately this has been bugging me though. I've been reading about insect sentience, and the scale argument goes roughly like this: suppose you think it's only 1% likely that insects can feel pain. There are quintillions of them alive right now, so a 1% chance of sentience still means an expected number of possibly-suffering lives that's, I dont know, enormous. My friend says my 1% rule is just a way to ignore high-stakes outcomes whenever they're uncomfortable, and that the whole point of expected-value reasoning is that a tiny probability times a huge number can matter. But I also don't want to end up taking Pascal's mugging seriously, where some guy claims he'll give me infinite utility if I hand over my wallet and I'm supposed to chase a tiny probability times infinity. Is my rule defensible? Is there a principled version of it that keeps me from being paralyzed by every wild scenario but still lets the scale argument through? Or is my friend wrong that I have to care about low-probability moral catastrophes at all?
