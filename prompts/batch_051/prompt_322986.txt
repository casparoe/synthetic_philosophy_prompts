In my animal minds class we've been talking about the distribution question — how far down the tree of life consciousness goes and how you could ever tell. A friend who took a different philosophy of mind course sent me a paragraph from a popular science article he'd saved, and I can't tell how much of it is fair. Here's the paragraph:

"Scientists can't settle which animals are conscious the way they settle whether an animal has nociceptors, because consciousness is private and can't be directly observed in another creature. So we have to fall back on behavior — writhing, escape learning, trade-offs between food and shock — and behavior is always consistent with more than one interpretation. Since animals can't tell us what their experience is like, the inference will always be somewhat underdetermined, and there's no single decisive test for where consciousness begins. Still, the evidence doesn't make every attribution equally good: behavioral and neurobiological markers make it reasonable to think mammals and birds are conscious, less clear for fish and octopuses, and pretty doubtful for insects. But since the underdetermination never fully goes away, the distribution question is ultimately not a factual question but a practical decision about how much risk of suffering we're willing to accept. The natural conclusion is the precautionary principle: since we can't prove that any given animal isn't suffering, we should treat any animal that behaves as if it's in pain as if it really is."

I'm inclined to think the "not a factual question" bit overstates things — like, the uncertainty is an epistemic problem, not the absence of a fact — but I cant articulate exactly where the article goes wrong and I'm also not sure the precautionary bit actually follows. Could you go through it and tell me which parts are accurate, which are common simplifications, and what someone working on animal consciousness would object to? And does the precautionary conclusion survive once the summary is corrected?
