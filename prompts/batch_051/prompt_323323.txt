I'm an archaeology PhD student (bioarchaeology, early Holocene Near East), and our department has a running reading group on the whole 'was farming a mistake' debate. We've done the classic revisionist line: hunter-gatherers as the original affluent society, less work, better nutrition, taller and healthier than the early farmers; farming as a health and equality catastrophe, enamel hypoplasia and cribra orbitalia and all that. And we've done the pushback: fine, but agriculture is why there are billions of us rather than a few million foragers, so by any population-level standard it comes out ahead, and calling it a 'mistake' smuggles in a counterfactual nobody can evaluate—no group ever sat down and chose it with alternatives in view. Everyone can now predict the other side's moves and we haven't shifted in two terms. I honestly don't think it's just a case of 'more evidence needed', and it clearly isn't a fight about whether early farmers had worse teeth. It feels like a disagreement about what would even make it a mistake: average individual welfare, total wellbeing, equality, long-run trajectory, or the quality of the decision at the time. Maybe it's a hidden population-ethics dispute, or maybe the question is ill-posed because there was no agent and no available alternative. My supervisor keeps saying its 'not really empirical', but that feels too quick. Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, from what each side says, and say what would count as progress—a distinction, a formal model, a different question—or whether we should just stop expecting the debate to move?
