I keep coming back to Hofstadter's 2018 Atlantic essay about Google Translate, and I'm trying to figure out whether any of the argument survives large language models. His core charge, compressed, was that the system doesn't actually understand anything: it's exploiting statistical regularities in a big pile of parallel text, so it can produce fluent-looking output while having no grip on the meanings the words are supposed to carry. His favorite kind of test was a French possessive trap. You give it a passage about a couple's house where everything comes in pairs — his car and her car, his towels and her towels — and the translator has to make the possessives agree with the grammatical gender of the French nouns, not the sex of the owner. A machine that leans on the surface words his and her will mangle it, while a human who understands the scene won't.

Modern LLMs usually pass that kind of test, or near variants, and they handle more pragmatic stuff too. What nags at me is this: they're trained on a ridiculous amount of web text that almost certainly includes Hofstadter's essay, blog posts discussing it, reproductions of the example, and close translations of the same passage. So the exact test he used is probably sitting in the training data, and a model could pass it through shallow memory rather than through the kind of understanding he was after. I'm not sure which way that cuts. It might mean the argument's evidential base is spoiled and needs a memory-proof replacement. It might actually strengthen his point, since a pass on the original example would prove nothing, and the failure should just reappear on genuinely novel tests. Or it might kill the argument, if the new systems also handle examples that weren't in his essay and the word understand was never precise enough to do real work.

So my question: does Hofstadter's argument survive this specific development — the fact that the systems got better partly by ingesting the text of the argument and its examples? I want you to say which premise the development bears on, whether the argument can be restored in another form, and what a memory-proof version of his test would have to look like if he's still right. And if you take him to have been making an in-principle argument about statistical systems regardless of test performance, spell out wether that argument still applies to LLMs or whether the architectural change escapes it.
