Consider Denis Villeneuve's film Arrival, adapted from Ted Chiang's story 'Story of Your Life', as a thought experiment about indexicals. A standing question in philosophy of language is whether expressions like 'I', 'here', and 'now' are essential — whether what they contribute can be paraphrased without loss into context-free, objective descriptions, or whether some thoughts are irreducibly self-locating (Perry's essential-indexical cases, Kaplan's character/content distinction, and Lewis's centered worlds are the usual landmarks). The film gives us an unusually concrete test case: Heptapod B is a written language with no linear order, its logograms convey an utterance all at once, and as Louise Banks learns it she comes to experience time non-linearly, so that future events are available to her the way memories are. Read the film through the indexicality debate. Does Heptapod B actually lack indexicals, or does it just change what its indexicals pick out — a different kind of 'now', an 'I' that names a whole temporal span rather than a moving point? Where does the film conflate grammatical tense, the linearity of a writing system, and the semantics of indexicals — and where does it dramatize something those distinctions cannot capture? I want an honest, two-way reading: do not reduce the film to an illustration of the philosophy, and do not bend the philosophy to fit the plot. End by saying what the film adds to the Perry–Kaplan–Lewis debate that argument alone misses — or, if the film's lesson resists that framing, say so plainly.
