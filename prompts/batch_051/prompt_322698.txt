I've been arguing with a friend about higher-order evidence—that is, evidence about how reliable your own judgment is, or about what your evidence really supports—and he thinks epistemic akrasia is just a philosopher's fiction: you can describe it, but no rational person ever actually ends up there. I suspect there's a real case to be made, and I want a thought experiment built to order for a paper I'm outlining. The target: an agent believes p, and also believes, about that very belief, that her total evidence doesn't support p or that she ought to suspend on p—yet the whole akratic package is rationally held all things considered, not merely blameless or understandable. The case should split two camps. The no-akrasia camp says rationality requires first-order and higher-order attitudes to cohere: if you rationally think you should suspend on p, you can't rationally believe p. The process camp says a first-order belief can stay rational because it's produced by a normally reliable process, even while the agent rationally distrusts that very process on this occasion.

Constraints: ordinary adult life—no drugs, alcohol, brain lesions, hypnosis, neural implants, or anything science-fictional; no disagreement with a recognized epistemic peer on the same evidence; p should be a humdrum external-world proposition, not a report of the agent's own current mental state; the higher-order evidence has to be the kind a rational person would genuinely be moved by, not a transparently bad source; and the rationality has to be epistemic, not practical (no "I had to believe it to get through the day"). Since the case is for a paper defending the possibility of rational epistemic akrasia, its crucial that it generate both verdicts from the evidence and the reliability facts rather than just stipulating that both attitudes are rational.

After the scenario, do the check: state the belief and the two bodies of evidence; say why the first-order belief is rational on the first-order evidence and why the second-order judgment is rational on the higher-order evidence; explain why the combination counts as genuinely akratic rather than merely conflicted. Then identify the structural feature of the case that does the work to pull the camps apart, what would happen to the verdicts if that feature were removed, and the strongest reply the no-akrasia camp could use to block the case. Close with a definite verdict: does the best version of this case succeed, or does it only work if you weaken one of the constraints? I'd rather have a committed answer than a menu of options.
