I am a novelist planning a book about a small self-governing island whose constitutional order is built on the conviction that envy and resentment should be treated as evidence. The islanders' founding text draws a line between excusable envy—resentment at an advantage that cannot be justified to the person who lacks it—and invidious envy, which is merely private rankling. The institution meant to draw that line in practice is a standing body called the Resentment Tribunal, and I want help thinking about how it would actually function, because I want the novel to show the institution's costs and not just its theory.

Here is the mechanism as I have designed it for the book. Any adult resident may file a petition against one named resident, identifying a specific advantage the respondent holds: wealth, land, office, formal honor, a place in the island's selective school, and similar scarce goods. The petition must allege that the advantage violates at least one of two constitutional principles: the equality-of-opportunity principle, under which no advantage may be traced to inherited wealth, inherited office, or family connection; or the least-advantaged principle, under which inequalities are permitted only if they can plausibly be shown to leave the worst-off better than they would be without them. A panel of thirteen citizens, drawn by lot, hears the petitioner and the respondent in public. The petitioner must describe the resentment itself—its object, its source, and how it relates to one of the two principles—and the respondent must answer. The panel then deliberates in private and decides two questions separately. First, whether the resentment is well-grounded: would a reasonable person in the petitioner's position, comparing the petitioner's circumstances with the respondent's, be warranted in treating the identified advantage as an injustice under the two principles? Second, whether the advantage in fact violates one of those principles. Only if both questions are answered yes may the panel order a remedy.

If the petitioner wins, the panel orders the requested remedy within constitutional limits—typically a transfer of part of the respondent's advantage to the petitioner where it is divisible, and otherwise a loss or sharing of the post or honor—and the petitioner receives a public honor. If the respondent wins, the petitioner must pay the respondent's reasonable hearing costs and is barred from petitioning against that respondent for five years. A respondent may end the matter before the panel sits by granting the remedy; the record then reads consented remedy, and the constitution expressly says this is not an admission that the advantage was unjust.

Treat this as an institution to be analyzed, not as a proposal I am endorsing. I care less about whether it is just in the abstract than about how it would behave in a community of a few thousand people over a generation. I do not need a verdict on whether the institution is morally justified; I need a map of its behavior. Walk through what it does to who speaks, about what, and to how people hold, display, or conceal advantages. Where does it get gamed first? What second-order effects—on friendship, on the selective school, on the shape of the economy, on how people talk about their own and others' success—should I build into the novel? Does the tribunal mostly track injustice, or does it produce professional claimants, strategic settlements, and a cult of conspicuous modesty? Can any of the failure modes be fixed without gutting the institution?

Please approach this as a political philosopher with a mechanism designer's eye, and begin by working through the incentives facing petitioners, respondents, panelists, and bystanders before you commit to a view. In your written response, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. Do not preamble, do not restate the question, and do not provide citations or a reading list.
