I've been chewing on this for a while and I want an honest read, not the usual both-sides shrug. I've started calling something the backdated optimum, and I keep going back and forth on weather it's a real mechanism or just sour grapes with a new sticker.

The claim is this: a lot of anti-life-extension argument is post hoc. People anchor on the longest lives actually available in their society, treat that number as roughly the right amount of life, and then go looking for reasons why a longer life would be bad — boring, selfish, undignified, unnatural, whatever. The reasons get presented as the grounds for the conclusion, but I suspect the reasoning runs the other way: the current horizon sets the conclusion, and the reasons are filled in backwards. That's the backdated optimum — the ideal length of a life gets updated to sit somewhere near whatever is currently possible, then written backwards as if that was always the right amount of life.

A few things make me think the phenomenon is real. First, the arguments are weirdly reversible. Someone says they wouldn't want to live to 120 because they'd watch everyone they love die. If you change the scenario so their whole cohort lives to 120 too, that objection mostly disappears, and it's not clear they have another one loaded. Same with the it-would-just-be-decades-of-decline line: that's an objection to bad extra years, not to extra years. Second, the same people who insist they'd never want an extra thirty years tend to fight very hard for an extra six months once the six months are actually on the table. The preference seems to track availability rather than any stable view about how much life is enough. Third, the death-gives-life-meaning move — I've never seen somebody apply it to their own kid. Nobody says they'd prefer their healthy child die at 80 because the extra forty years would make the whole life feel pointless. That asymmetry is the part I can't shake.

What I want to know: is this one coherent mechanism, does it already exist under a better name I don't know, and where does it break apart? I'm aware there are anti-extension positions that don't look like horizon effects — worries about inequality, about who gets the extra life first, about social stagnation, about what extremely long lives do to birth rates and care structures. I don't want to pretend those are all just rationalization; some of them might be doing the real work. But I also don't want to grant that they're as cleanly seperated from the sour grapes thing as people like to claim. So give me the strongest version of my idea, then the strongest objections, and be candid about which objections are fatal and which are just uncomfortable. And at the end, tell me what would change your verdict: which argument or bit of evidence would move you the other way, and where your own reasoning is least certain.
