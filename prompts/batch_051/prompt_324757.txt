Consider two transactions that are structurally similar yet receive radically different moral treatment.

A buyer offers a stranger $60,000 for one of her kidneys. The seller is healthy, fully informed, and badly in need of money; she accepts. Most people regard the buyer as having done something repugnant, regard the sale as the commodification of a body part or as exploitation, and the transaction is illegal in almost every legal system.

A fishing company offers a stranger the same sum for a season of work on a notoriously dangerous crab boat. The work is brutal, the company knows that some share of its crew will be injured or killed, and the worker accepts mainly because he needs the money. Most people regard this as an ordinary labor contract with hazard pay, not as bodily commodification, and it is legal.

In both transactions, money is exchanged for the use of another person's body under conditions of economic need, with a serious risk of death or permanent bodily loss. Yet one is repugnant and the other is just a job.

What, if anything, justifies this asymmetry? I want the strongest candidate principle you can find—one that tracks the distinction without being tailored after the fact—tested against nearby cases: compensation for blood plasma (routine and widely accepted), paid sale of a liver lobe (the lobe regenerates, yet many still find the practice repugnant), paid participation in an early-phase drug trial (bodily risk for money, legal and ordinary), and post-hurricane price increases for bottled water or plywood (condemned as price gouging and illegal in many jurisdictions, though both parties consent). If your principle handles these cases, say so; if one of them breaks it, say what that failure reveals.

Then give an honest verdict: is the original asymmetry justified, or should it be revised—and if revised, in which direction? Should hazardous bodily labor look more like repugnant commodification, or should paid organ transfer look more like a legitimate hazardous contract?
