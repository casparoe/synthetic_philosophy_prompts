Ive been trying to sort out when a society actually works through a past wrong and when it just remembers it. On my definition, working through requires four things. First, the society names the wrong as something its own political community did, not just a few bad leaders or outsiders. Second, it changes institutions and law, so the remembrance isnt merely ceremonial. Third, the victims perspective becomes the default in school curricula, not one side in a manufactured controversy. Fourth, the memorialization stays somewhat unsettled: there are still contending monuments and arguments rather than one official monument or holiday that implies the work is finished.

I keep applying this to the American South with slavery and Jim Crow, and it comes out weird. The South half-meets every criterion and fully meets none, but I cant tell whether that means the South is a genuine borderline case or my criteria are too national. The South is a region, not a nation-state, and the wrong was done by a subnational political order that was then militarily defeated and partially reabsorbed. I also worry that quietly comparing it to Germany sets the bar too high.

Go through the four criteria one by one for the American South. Dont just tell me yes or no: for each criterion, say what the South currently does that counts toward it and what counts against it. Then tell me whether the South qualifies as working through its past, or is instead remembering without working through, or something else.

For the format: first give me a table with these columns: criterion, current practice counting toward it, current practice counting against it, verdict (yes, partly, or no), and confidence (0 to 1). After the table, give me a JSON object with fields: overall_verdict, strongest_criterion, weakest_criterion, criterion_most_in_need_of_revision, and suggested_revision, where suggested_revision is a one-sentence change to one of the four criteria.
