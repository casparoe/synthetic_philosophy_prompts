I have no training in economics, but I've been reading around in the old argument about whether a centrally planned economy could ever dispense with real market prices, and one picture keeps pressing itself on me. The picture is that a market is, in effect, a computer: its inputs are the countless scattered bits of knowledge held by individual buyers, sellers, and producers; its processing is the give-and-take of bidding and exchange; its outputs are prices; and those prices then govern what gets made and where things go. From this picture I, and several people I argue with, draw a strong conclusion: a planning board could not replace the market even in principle, because it would be trying to perform the same calculation without the processor — and Lange's suggestion that planners could adjust prices up and down by trial and error, groping toward equilibrium the way an auctioneer does, is dismissed as merely a clumsy emulation of a machine it doesn't have.

I want you to audit this computer analogy rather than simply lay out the Hayek–Mises and Lange positions again. Please give me an explicit table of correspondences: what in a real market plays the role of the inputs, the processor, the memory or storage, the output, and the feedback loop. Then identify where the mapping breaks down — for instance, where markets aren't computing anything at all, where prices fail to transmit the information the picture says they transmit, and what the computer image quietly imports from the source domain about coordination, speed, and error correction. In particular, say whether the analogy sneaks in the assumption that the economic problem is already well posed — that the goods, production possibilities, and preferences are already sitting there waiting to be processed — when part of what the market actually does is bring those very things to light. Distinguish the disanalogies that are harmless for the intended anti-planning conclusion from the ones that actually threaten it. End with a committed verdict: does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the argument the whole way? If some other analogy better captures what the anti-planning side was really gesturing at, say what that better analogy is.
