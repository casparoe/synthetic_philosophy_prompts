I am an undergraduate currently taking a course in epistemology, and next week I am chairing a student Q&A with a visiting philosopher who defends an accuracy-first approach to epistemic norms. On their view, the sole fundamental epistemic value is accuracy—the closeness of a doxastic state to the truth—and the central norms for graded belief, including probabilism and conditionalization, are to be derived from accuracy considerations alone. Please draft nine interview questions for this session.

The questions should not merely invite the speaker to restate the position. They should press on the places where accuracy-first epistemology is under genuine strain. Ensure that the set includes questions on at least the following, though you may order and combine them as you see fit:

- the worry that the accuracy-dominance argument for probabilism depends on strictly proper scoring rules, and that strict propriety may already encode the probabilist verdict it is meant to establish;
- the objection that there are many candidate measures of accuracy, so that dominance relative to one measure need not carry over to another;
- whether a framework developed for graded credence can say anything principled about outright belief;
- the expected-accuracy argument for conditionalization and whether it is genuinely non-circular;
- the immodesty of probabilistic credences, and whether this is a virtue or a defect;
- whether accuracy is really the only fundamental epistemic value, or whether values such as evidential fit, coherence, or understanding resist an accuracy-first treatment.

For each question, provide a follow-up keyed to the answer the speaker is most likely to give. Where the speaker has a standard defensive move, the follow-up should get behind that move rather than accept it.

After each question and follow-up, add a brief annotation saying which argument or consideration from the speaker would most lead me to revise my current, moderately favourable view of accuracy-first epistemology, or where the question itself is most likely to fail. I would rather have a sharp sense of what would change my mind than a set of questions that merely flatter my present sympathies.

Please write the questions, follow-ups, and annotations in British English.
