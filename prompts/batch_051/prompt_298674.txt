I am committed to a version of what has come to be called Friedman's F-twist about idealized models, and I extend it well beyond economics: if a model outpredicts its rivals in its intended domain, then the falsehood of its assumptions—frictionless planes, infinite populations, homogeneous mixing, perfect rationality, no spatial structure—is not in itself a reason to reject or demote the model. On my view, criticism should target predictive performance, never the descriptive accuracy of assumptions. I want a frank map of what would count against this position, sorted from decisive to merely awkward. Specifically, address these candidate defeaters: (a) models that predict well inside a known range and fail just outside it, where the false assumptions are exactly what keep the model from revealing the boundary of its own validity; (b) the objection that some unrealism only omits negligible factors while other unrealism distorts the causal structure being studied, and that Friedman's argument is plausible only for the first kind; (c) the line of argument exemplified by the Lucas critique, that parameters estimated under one regime may not be invariant when the model is used to change the regime, so a model with false behavioral assumptions can predict well right up until we act on it; and (d) the charge that without realistic assumptions a model does not explain, so predictive success alone cannot distinguish a theory from a well-tuned curve fit. For each, tell me whether the view can absorb it with qualifications, whether a committed Friedmanian would concede it as a genuine cost or dismiss it as changing the subject, and which remaining version of the view still risks something. End with an honest assessment of whether the position can be qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a problem for a methodological commitment of this kind or simply the mark of a regulative norm rather than an empirical claim. I expect (c) to be the most damaging, but I want that expectation tested.
