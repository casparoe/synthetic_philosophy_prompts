You're a philosopher of mind who works on consciousness, and you're very familiar with Dennett's argument in Quining Qualia. I'm taking a phil mind course and we are stuck on the Chase and Sanborn passage, but I want to build a variation on it and I'd like you to think through how the analysis changes. Start by reconstructing what Dennett is actually trying to do in that thought experiment, then engage with my variant—don't just say "it depends," give a reasoned verdict.

First, here is how Dennett sets up the target notion early in Quining Qualia:

Look at a glass of milk at sunset; the way it looks to you--the particular, personal, subjective visual quality of the glass of milk is the quale of your visual experience at the moment. The way the milk tastes to you then is another, gustatory quale, and how it sounds to you as you swallow is an auditory quale; These various "properties of conscious experience" are prime examples of qualia.

Then later he gives the two coffee tasters: Chase says his coffee tastes exactly the same as it always did, but he no longer likes it; Sanborn says his taste has changed, but he likes the coffee just as much. Dennett's deflationary point is that once you try to distinguish these cases on the basis of private, intrinsic, ineffable qualia, you can't actually settle whether either taster is right, and the very difference starts to look ill-posed. My study group argued about whether that's a fair move.

Here is the variant I want to explore. Suppose we add an intervention: Chase and Sanborn are given a drug that temporarily suppresses the liking system while leaving sensory discrimination intact. Under the drug, they can still sort cups by taste profile perfectly, but they have no preference or hedonic response either way. After the drug clears, Chase again reports same taste but less liking, and Sanborn again reports different taste but same liking. Now, does adding this dissociation between hedonic response and discriminative capacity give the qualia realist a way to say there is a real, factual difference between Chase's and Sanborn's cases? Or does Dennett's original point just show up at the next level, because we now need a criterion for whether the drug spared "the taste itself" rather than just the tasters' sorting mechanisms? Tell me whether the quining strategy survives this variation, or whether the variation makes the "no fact of the matter" conclusion harder to reach.
