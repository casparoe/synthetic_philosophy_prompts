My robotics club has been arguing about what it would actually mean for a machine, or any agent, to be flexible rather than merely running a fixed program. A club member introduced us to the Sphex wasp, which performs a famous rigid sequence: it drags a paralyzed cricket to the edge of its burrow, leaves it there, goes inside to inspect, comes back out, and then drags the cricket in. If someone moves the cricket a few inches away while the wasp is inside, the wasp does not simply pull the cricket in when it comes back out; instead it drags the cricket to the entrance again, goes inside to inspect again, and can be made to repeat the whole cycle many times. We found this striking because the wasp looks as though it has a plan, but the plan cannot be revised when the situation changes in a way that should make the task easier.

That discussion led us to propose a certification standard. Suppose an industry body adopted an interruption test for any system advertised as genuinely flexible or autonomous. The test would take a routine the system normally completes, interrupt it at the point where a rigid system would restart from the beginning even though the goal had not changed, and certify the system as flexible only if it recognized the shortcut and completed the task without re-running the routine. Analyze how such a standard would behave if it became widely used: what it would incentivize engineers to build, how manufacturers would game or overfit to the test, what kinds of real flexibility it would fail to detect, whether its failure modes could be fixed while keeping the test's goal, and whether passing would tell us anything about free will in the system being tested or only about observable flexibility.
