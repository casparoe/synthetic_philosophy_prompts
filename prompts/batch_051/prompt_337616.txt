I'm taking a global health ethics course and we've spent the last couple weeks on the methods big aid agencies use to decide which health programs to fund. They rely on summary measures—I think they're called QALYs or DALYs—that put every health state on one scale: full health for a year is 1, dying is 0, and a year with a given illness is some decimal in between. A year with severe depression gets coded as a loss of some fraction of a healthy year, and a program that keeps a thousand people from getting a mild parasitic infection can end up scoring the same as a program that saves one child's life, depending on how the weights shake out. The whole logic is that all health losses and gains are commensurable, so funding decisions become arithmetic.

What's bugging me is that these same agencies describe their mission as reducing suffering, or helping the worst-off. But in the metric, suffering has no special status: it is just another entry on the scale, and a sufficiently large pile of small health gains for other people can always outweigh one person's extreme, unrelenting pain. My professor said this looks like it presumes a particular picture of value, where there is no deep difference between preventing something bad and adding something good, and where no amount of misery is off-limits to trade. I'm not sure if that's an actual commitment of the practice or if the metric is just a rough heuristic that could be defended from lots of different ethical positions.

So my question is: what philosophical commitments does this whole way of measuring health presuppose? Does it presuppose that reducing suffering and adding positive well-being are fully interchangable, or that intense suffering can be traded away like any other number, or is the practice underdetermined so that several rival theories could license it? And if you took a suffering-focused ethic seriously—the kind where preventing severe suffering has a different, maybe lexical kind of priority—what would have to change in how these organizations build and use their numbers? I'd prefer the answer in American English, and I'm less interested in the history of these measures than in the assumptions they quietly build in.
