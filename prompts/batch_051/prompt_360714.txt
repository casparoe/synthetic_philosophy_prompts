At a picnic last weekend I ended up listening to two friends argue about the self-torturer case, and I'm still not sure who had the better of it. The setup, as they ran through it: a person gets a sequence of offers, each one slightly more pain in exchange for a fixed amount of money, where no single step feels noticeably worse, but after a few hundred steps he's in agony he'd pay to escape. They agreed on the setup and still fought.

Sofia said the case shows rational preferences don't have to be transitive. At each step, the person is reasonable to prefer the next setting, since the extra pain is imperceptible and the money is actual. If each single step is rational, then taking the whole series is what rational steps add up to, and the fact that he hates where he ends up just shows that "better than" isn't transitive for creatures like us.

Dev said that's backwards: rationality has to be transitive, and what the case really shows is that the step-by-step reasoning is the thing to reject. "Imperceptible" doesn't mean "indifferent"; it means you can't tell, which is a limit on your evidence, not on value. A rational person has to place a cutoff somewhere, even if any particular cutoff feels arbitrary, because treating one more step as never mattering is a sorites-style mistake.

Sofia answered that the arbitrariness is exactly the point. If every possible cutoff is arbitrary, then a theory of rationality that requires one is telling the person to invent a reason he doesn't have, while the local reasoning never made up anything. So transitivity, not the self-torturer, is what has to go.

Dev said that's worse, because without transitivity you can be money-pumped, and anyway the person can see the whole staircase from the start. Each step isn't purely local: it's a step taken while knowing the plan leads to a state he'd refuse. The rule to reject is "take every step that seems fine on its own," not transitivity.
