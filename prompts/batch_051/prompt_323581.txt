Suppose we say that an entity counts as a body only if it satisfies all of the following: (a) it has a determinate spatial location and bounded extension; (b) it has a characteristic set of sensible qualities—colour, texture, temperature, mass—by which competent observers can identify and reidentify it over time; (c) its parts are arranged in a characteristic three-dimensional structure; and (d) it persists through change in the continuous, gradual way typical of ordinary material objects, rather than being wholly generated or wholly destroyed in an instant.

Now consider the consecrated host as described by a particular tradition of theology. According to that description, at a certain act of consecration the whole substance of the bread—substance understood as the underlying what-it-is, as opposed to its perceptible features—is converted into the substance of a particular human body. What was bread ceases to be bread, yet every sensible quality and measurable property of the object remains exactly as it was, down to its taste, texture, and mass. What is present is the whole body and not merely a part of it, and it is present not in the manner of an extended body in a place, with limbs and organs arranged spatially, but in the manner of a substance, whole in the whole host and whole in each of its parts. The description is not meant metaphorically.

Does the consecrated host, so described, qualify as a body under the four criteria? Work through each criterion in turn. Where the answer is no or unclear, distinguish carefully between a result that follows from the criteria as written and a result that follows from the character of this particular case. Where the criteria fall silent or underdetermine the verdict, say so. Finally, say whether this case shows that the criteria themselves need revising; if they do, explain how they should be revised without simply gerrymandering them so that the host comes out as a body.

Please write your answer in British English.
