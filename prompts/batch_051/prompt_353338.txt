I have been trying to extract a single position on quotas and group preferences from my own case-by-case verdicts, and the verdicts keep pulling in different directions. I would like to know what principle, if any, captures them—or which of them I should give up. Consider these cases, each simplified only enough to make the comparison clean.

1. Norway-style boardroom quota: a country requires public limited companies to have at least 40 percent of each sex on their boards. I approve, though with genuine unease when the rule reaches small or family-controlled firms.

2. Indian-style reservations: a country permanently reserves a substantial share of public university seats and government posts for members of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. I approve in broad outline, even though I think open-category applicants pay a real price.

3. Race-conscious US admissions: a selective university gives applicants from underrepresented racial groups a boost, but the boost functions as one factor among many rather than as a fixed number of set-aside seats. I approve, but only because I accept the end of a visibly integrated student body; I would stop approving if the policy became a permanent, rigid entitlement apportioning places by group.

4. Legacy preference: the same university admits children of alumni at a higher rate than otherwise identical applicants. I disapprove, and I cannot see why this is more defensible than some race-based boosts merely because the beneficiaries are privileged.

5. Rural service quota: a country reserves half of its medical school seats for applicants who commit to practice for five years in underserved rural areas, and the policy sunsets after ten years. I approve, more comfortably than I approve of 2 or 3.

6. Individual tie-break: a company with a stated gender-balance target hires the slightly lower-scoring female candidate over a male candidate for a particular software role. I disapprove of this particular decision while still approving of the company's having the target, and I cannot fully explain why the two judgments go together.

7. Veteran preference: a civil service adds a fixed number of points to the exam scores of military veterans. I approve, but only mildly, and I cannot articulate a principle that counts this as legitimate while excluding things I dislike.

8. Reserved legislative seats: a country reserves half of its parliamentary seats for women, with the reservation reassigned anew each election cycle so that no incumbent is protected. I am torn, but lean in favor.

The nearest I have come to a principle is this: preferences are justified when they operate at the level of an institution aiming at a visible structural imbalance, and not when they decide which particular individual receives a post. But that principle would make me oppose the Indian reservations and the veteran points, which I do not; and a boardroom quota cannot be met without individual appointments that sometimes pass over otherwise stronger candidates. A second candidate—preferences are justified only as remedies for deliberate exclusion, not for mere underrepresentation—handles some cases but not my easy approval of the rural quota.

Is there one principle behind these verdicts, or a small set of principles? What does the best principle say about three cases I have not yet settled: (a) a university that charges richer students more and uses the surplus to fund free places for poorer students, with no quota at all; (b) a quota for first-generation university entrants; (c) a permanent caste-based quota in private-sector employment? And which, if any, of my eight original verdicts does the principle force me to abandon?
