I am a doctoral student working on the foundations of decision theory, and I keep running into a conflict in my own views about risk that I cannot settle. One side of me is strongly drawn to geometric rationality. The policy of staking, on each favourable gamble, the fraction of one's wealth that maximises the expected logarithm of terminal wealth—usually called Kelly betting—has exactly the theoretical virtues I am trained to respect. It is unified and simple; it returns a definite stake rather than a range of permissible ones; it maximises the long-run growth rate of wealth across sequences of gambles; and it eventually overtakes, with probability tending to one, any policy that systematically bets a different fixed fraction. When I think about repeated choice, it strikes me as the right way to be.

But I also have an intuition I cannot dislodge. Suppose I am offered a single, unrepeatable favourable bet, and my utility for final wealth is more concave than the logarithmic function. It does not feel like a mistake to wager less than Kelly prescribes. The criterion's virtues are all long-run or asymptotic, and in a one-shot case they seem to be answering a question I am not asking: not how to grow wealth quickly over many trials, but how to trade off the possible final outcomes of one trial in accordance with my actual attitude to terminal wealth. The dispersion of outcomes matters to me in a way that the expected logarithm does not fully register.

I do not want a stand-alone verdict. Give me the strongest version of each side. In particular, tell me what would justify treating my finite-horizon intuition as a debunkable artefact and dropping it, what would justify amending or abandoning the Kelly/geometric-rationality view, and whether there is a stable reading on which they are compatible—say, one principle for long-run policy selection and another for one-off expected-utility evaluation—or whether such a reconciliation merely relocates the problem. The response should be written in American English.
