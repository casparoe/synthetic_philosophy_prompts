Consider a variant of Thomson's lamp in which the switch is unreliable. In the original puzzle the lamp is on at t = 0, and the switch is flipped at the times 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, ... (that is, at 1 - 2^{-n} for n = 1, 2, 3, ...). Because the state after each flip alternates on, off, on, off, ..., the standard analysis concludes that the lamp has no determinate on/off state at t = 1. Now modify the setup: at each scheduled flip the mechanism succeeds in toggling the lamp only with probability p, where 0 < p < 1, and fails with probability 1 - p, independently at every attempt. The original puzzle is the limiting case p = 1.

(a) Let a_n be the probability that the lamp is on immediately after the n-th scheduled attempt, with a_0 = 1. Show that a_n = p + (1 - 2p)a_{n-1}, solve for a_n, and find lim_{n→∞} a_n. If the limit exists, does it entitle us to say that at t = 1 the lamp is on with probability lim a_n and off otherwise, or does something of the original underdetermination survive?

(b) Follow a single run of the unreliable mechanism. Does the actual on/off state after the n-th attempt settle down to some final state as n → ∞, with probability one? If it does not, explain in what sense (if any) the probabilistic limit from (a) resolves the puzzle, and say precisely what the noise has changed in the original analysis.

(c) Now let the success probability depend on the attempt: at the n-th scheduled flip the mechanism toggles with probability p_n, independently, where 0 < p_n < 1/2 for every n. Derive a formula for a_n in terms of the finite product ∏_{k=1}^{n}(1 - 2p_k), and use it to compare the cases Σ p_n < ∞ and Σ p_n = ∞. In which of these cases, if any, does an individual history of the lamp settle down almost surely, and why?
