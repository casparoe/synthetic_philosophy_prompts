I am a retired cell biologist who has come to philosophy of biology late, and I want help extracting the principle behind my own judgments about what is alive. Here are my honest verdicts on cases, which I have never managed to unify:

- A single E. coli cell actively growing and dividing: alive.
- A forest fire: not alive.
- A crystal growing in a supersaturated solution: not alive.
- A tobacco mosaic virus, desiccated and crystallized for years and then infectious again: not alive.
- A prion, which propagates by inducing other proteins to misfold: not alive.
- A long-dormant seed that has been metabolically inert for decades but germinates when planted: alive.
- A tardigrade in the tun state, with no detectable metabolism and every capacity suspended, which revives when rewetted: alive.
- A rickettsia, an obligately intracellular bacterium that cannot reproduce outside a host cell: alive.
- A mitochondrion inside a cell, with its own DNA and clear bacterial ancestry: not alive.

I am not asking for a survey of textbook definitions. I want to know whether one criterion of life, or a small set of jointly necessary and sufficient conditions, can accommodate all of these verdicts as they stand. Please say what that account is, or what the leading candidates are, which of my verdicts it would force me to revise or reinterpret, and what the resulting account says about cases I have deliberately left out: a membrane-bound vesicle that continuously synthesizes and repairs all its own parts but never divides, a self-copying computer program that mutates and is subject to selection, and a honeybee colony considered as a whole.
