I've been trying to reconstruct Pascal's wager without appealing to infinite payoffs, because as soon as a payoff becomes infinite, expected-utility arithmetic stops behaving like the real-valued function I'm used to from modeling decisions. I put together a finite-payoff version that seems to reach the same conclusion, and I can't tell whether it's sound or whether I've just hidden the infinity in a parameter rather than removing it. Here's the argument:

(1) Let the two options be committing to a specific religious practice or not committing. Let c be the finite net cost of committing over a lifetime, and let g be the finite net gain of not committing.

(2) Suppose there is a possible being who, after your death, gives a finite reward R to those who committed and gives nothing to those who didn't.

(3) Let p be your subjective probability that this specific being exists. Since the hypothesis promises only a finite reward, it isn't unreasonable to assign it some p greater than 0.

(4) The expected value of committing is pR − (1 − p)c. The expected value of not committing is g.

(5) Because the scenario is a stipulation and R is finite, we can choose R large enough that committing comes out ahead for whatever positive p we settled on: any R > (g + (1 − p)c) / p works.

(6) So, for that stipulated being, committing has greater expected value than not committing.

(7) Rationality requires taking whichever option has greater expected value.

Conclusion: for the practice corresponding to that stipulation, rationality requires committing. So the wager can be rebuilt with finite payoffs.

I keep feeling the defect is somewhere around step 5, like a variable-scoping bug: I bind p to one hypothesis, and then I quietly swap in a different hypothesis with a larger R while leaving p bound to the first one. But step 4 is just ordinary expected value, and step 6 looks like it follows from 5, so I can't tell whether I've actually found the error or whether I'm just resisting an unwelcome conclusion. Is this argument valid? If it isn't, which step is the first to fail, and exactly what goes wrong in it? Please say how confident you are in your answer, and single out the point you're least sure of.
