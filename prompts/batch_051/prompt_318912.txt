I am taking an upper-level seminar on free will, and the instructor has a way of halting the standard debate that I want to be able to assess rather than simply inherit or resent. Whenever someone starts pressing the familiar question whether free will is compatible with determinism — by invoking Frankfurt-style cases, the consequence argument, or disputes over the ability to do otherwise — he waves it off and says that the important question is which varieties of free will are worth wanting, and that until we answer that, the compatibility debate is chasing a phantom. His suggestion, as I understand it, is that once we inventory the kinds of agency we actually value, nearly all of them will turn out to be compatible with determinism, and the remaining disagreement will concern only a technical sense of being able to do otherwise that nobody had reason to want in the first place.

What I would like is a careful disambiguation of that slogan. What distinct, precise claims could the saying that the important question is which varieties of free will are worth wanting be taken to express? I want the readings set out as a menu, with each one labeled as something like trivial or definitional, substantive and defensible, or false or misleading, and with a note on which reading is doing the real argumentative work when someone uses the slogan to demote or dissolve the question whether free will is compatible with determinism. Finally, tell me which reading I should hold the instructor to if I want to engage with the strongest, least caricatured version of the position.
