I don't have an academic background, but I've been trying to think carefully on my own about why two reasonable people can look at the same thing and nevertheless seem to see different things. A chapter in a popular science book I read recently gave this a name, cognitive penetration of perception, and, as I understood it, summarized the research something like this:

Cognitive penetration is now the well-established finding that what we see is shaped directly by what we believe, want, and expect. Experiments show that a hill looks steeper when you are carrying a heavy backpack or feeling tired, that a desirable object looks closer than an undesirable one, and that an experienced birdwatcher literally sees field marks that a novice's eyes pass over. Since perception is always filtered through our concepts and expectations, observation is inherently theory-laden: we never get an unfiltered view of the world, so no observation can be a neutral referee between rival theories. An older view held that perception was cognitively impenetrable, walled off from belief, but the evidence against that view is now overwhelming, and the debate is essentially settled.

The chapter's punchline was that, because there is no neutral observation, the idea that we can settle a factual dispute by just looking is naive. Something about the move from the experiments to that punchline feels too fast to me, but I am not sure which step is doing the work. Could you go through the summary and tell me which parts a researcher in this area would accept, which parts are common simplifications, which parts are overstatements or just wrong, and whether the punchline survives the corrections? I would also like to know where the real debate currently stands between people who think perception is cognitively penetrable and people who think it is not.
