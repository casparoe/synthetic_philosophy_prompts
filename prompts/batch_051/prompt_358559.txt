In a graduate seminar on quantum gravity, the conversation turned to whether the notion of a point—or of local geometry at all—is meaningful below the Planck scale, roughly the length at which quantum and gravitational effects become comparable. A postdoc ended the discussion by saying "spacetime is emergent," and the room treated this as decisive. I can tell the sentence is doing real argumentative work, but I don't know which of several possible claims it was being used to make. Please lay out the different precise claims "spacetime is emergent" could be expressing, organized along these axes: (1) what is being said to be emergent—the metric field, the smooth manifold, the number of dimensions, the topology, the causal structure, or something else; (2) what "emergent" is being said to mean—a low-energy effective description that should not be taken literally at short distances, a collective or composite phenomenon analogous to thermodynamics, a structure that can be derived from or reduced to more basic nonspatiotemporal degrees of freedom, or something weaker; and (3) what spacetime is said to be emergent from—a lower-dimensional boundary theory, a discrete combinatorial structure, the entanglement structure of a quantum state, or the dynamics of quantum matter. For each reading, say whether it is (a) a platitude that most working physicists accept, (b) a substantive and defensible programmatic claim, (c) a contested or speculative claim, or (d) something misleading or strictly false. Then say which reading, if any, actually licenses shutting down a question about whether local geometry is meaningful below the Planck scale, as opposed to merely postponing or reframing that question.
