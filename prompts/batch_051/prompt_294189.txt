Here is an argument I assembled for a seminar on vagueness, and I distrust its conclusion because it would erase the difference between supervaluationism and epistemicism.

(1) Supervaluationism holds that a sentence is true — call it supertrue — just in case it is true on every admissible precisification of the language.
(2) Each admissible precisification is a classical interpretation, so it draws a sharp boundary for a vague predicate: on any admissible precisification, there is a least number n such that n grains make a heap and n−1 grains do not (assuming, as usual, that one grain does not make a heap and a million grains do).
(3) Therefore, the sentence 'there is a number n such that n grains make a heap and n−1 grains do not' is true on every admissible precisification.
(4) By (1), that sentence is supertrue: supervaluationism is committed to the truth of the sentence.
(5) If that sentence is true, then there must be some particular number — 47, or 321, or wherever the boundary falls — such that exactly that many grains make a heap and one fewer does not; otherwise the words 'there is' are doing no work.
(6) So supervaluationism is committed to a sharp cutoff for 'heap'. But that is precisely the thesis of epistemicism: vague predicates have sharp cutoffs, and our inability to say where they fall is a kind of ignorance, not semantic indecision.
(7) Hence the dispute between supervaluationism and epistemicism is only about whether we can know which number is the cutoff, not about whether a cutoff exists — and supervaluationism has conceded its rival's central claim.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I would rather keep supervaluationism than accept the collapse, but I want to know what keeping it costs. Please identify the first step that should be rejected or amended, explain why it looks compelling as stated, and then say precisely what the two theories actually disagree about once the problem is fixed.
