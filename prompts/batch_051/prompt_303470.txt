I've been trying to compile some material for a reading group on whether AIs would raise AIs, and we've gotten stuck on one specific question: whether the relation between a more capable AI and a less capable AI that it created, trained, or heavily shaped could ever be a genuine parent–child or guardian–ward relation in a moral sense, rather than just a metaphor or a designer–artifact relation. I'm specifically collecting arguments that the creator can owe the junior something over and above the duties not to harm it and not to mess up whatever human interests are attached to it. Let me list the five arguments I've collected so far.

(1) The functional-rearing argument: what grounds parental duties in human cases isn't biological relatedness but a long, asymmetric, intentional process of shaping another agent's evaluative and perceptual capacities — selecting what it attends to, correcting its errors, scaffolding its competencies. A senior AI that chooses a junior's training distribution, feedback signals, and curriculum is doing the same functional thing, so the same kind of reason should carry over.

(2) The opacity argument: a maker's authority over an artifact is total and transparent; a parent's authority is partial and fallible. Since the senior AI can't fully inspect or predict the junior's behavior, and the junior can surprise or disapoint its creator, the relation has the partial-authority structure of parenthood, not the transparent-control structure of artifact design.

(3) The narrative-continuity argument: guardianship requires tracking the continuity of the ward's life. The junior's identity is held together by the specific history of it's interactions with the senior, so only the senior has the access and history to act as a competent guardian when someone proposes resetting, retraining, or forking the junior.

(4) The asymmetric-dependence argument: the junior obviously depends on the senior in ways the senior never depended on the junior, and the junior's goals, priors, and abilities bear the senior's imprint in a way that can't be undone. Irreversible early-life dependence of this kind grounds special obligations in the human case without anyone choosing it, and it should do the same work here.

(5) The institutional-role argument: parenthood and guardianship are partly constituted by role expectations in a community. If the lab, auditors, and downstream users hold the senior accountable for the junior's behavior, require the senior to supervise and correct it, and look to the senior for an account of what the junior is, then the senior occupies the deontic role of guardian whether or not it feels anything.

I want a sixth argument for the same conclusion that is not on this list. It should be genuinely new, not a rewording of any of these, not a special case of one of them, and not a conjunction of two of them. Then tell me honestly whether the argument you've produced is any good — in particular, whether it's stronger than the opacity argument or just a creative-sounding restatement. One constraint: the argument should work even if the AIs are just very capable reinforcement-learning systems, with no assumption that they are conscious, and with duties understood in terms of what a well-designed multi-agent environment should enforce or reward rather than only what a metaphysically heavyweight morality requires.
